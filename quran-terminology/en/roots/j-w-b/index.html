1. The root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root J W B (ج و ب) has two important branches. The first concerns cutting, piercing, opening a way through something, and passing through it. جَابَ (jāba) is used for cutting or cleaving rock or ground, and for traversing a land. The second concerns answering speech, a question, a request, or a call. It includes جَوَاب (jawāb), إِجَابَة (ijābah), اِسْتِجَابَة (istijābah), and مُجِيب (mujīb).

Both branches occur in the Qur’an. Thamūd are described with جَابُوا (jābū) as people who cut through rock in the valley. Elsewhere, the root concerns responding: The God answering His servants’ supplication, people responding to the divine call, a community answering its Messengers, or even people accepting Satan’s invitation.

This breadth matters, since Qur’anic jawāb does not always mean a correct answer, agreement, or acceptance of truth. Someone may respond to a call and accept it, reject it, or even threaten the caller instead of offering an argument.

Istijābah is likewise not confined to supplication. The Qur’an uses this verb both for The God responding to His servants’ requests and for people responding to others’ calls.

Qur’anic forms

The root J W B occurs 43 times in the Qur’an, in five morphological and lexical groups:

  • جَابُوا (jābū): Form I, from جَابَ، يَجُوبُ (jāba, yajūbu); once, in Al-Fajr 89:9, cutting through rock.
  • أَجَابَ (ajāba) and its verbal forms: eight occurrences in Form IV, concerning answering and receiving an answer.
  • اِسْتَجَابَ (istajāba) and its verbal forms: 28 occurrences in Form X, concerning responding to a request, accepting a call, and reacting.
  • جَوَاب (jawāb): four occurrences, an answer or response expressed in reaction to another’s speech.
  • مُجِيب، الْمُجِيبُونَ (mujīb, al-mujībūn): two occurrences, “one who answers” or “those who answer,” describing The God.

Other forms are known in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an. They include جَوْب (jawb), cutting or piercing; جَائِب (jāʾib), someone who traverses a route or pierces something; جَوَّاب (jawwāb), one who travels extensively; جَوْبَة (jawbah), a depression in the ground; إِجَابَة (ijābah), answering; اِسْتِجَابَة (istijābah), answering or accepting a call; and تَجَاوُب (tajāwub), answering one another. اِجْتَابَ (ijtāba) is also used for traversing and, in some applications, putting on a garment; this form does not occur in the Qur’an.

A morphological distinction must also be clear from the outset. الْجَوَابِ (al-jawābi) in Sabaʾ 34:13, concerning large vessels, belongs to J B Y (ج ب ي) in the Qur’an’s morphological classification and is the plural of جَابِيَة (jābiyah). Despite its similar spelling, it does not concern answering and should not be counted under J W B.

2. Two semantic branches in early Arabic

Piercing and cutting

Early Arabic uses جَابَ الشَّيْءَ (jāba al-shayʾa) for cutting, perforating, or cleaving something.

This action sometimes concerns rock or ground, as when someone makes an opening in hard rock or cuts part of it away.

Jawb can name the act of piercing itself. Lexical sources also record مِجْوَب (mijwab) for an iron implement used to perforate or cut something.

Another application, jawbah, is recorded for a depression or an open area with few trees. It can be compared with the image of an opening, but does not establish that every jawbah was made by cutting or excavation.

The same family includes جَابَ الْبِلَادَ (jāba al-bilāda), meaning to traverse lands and pass through them.

Here, a person does not necessarily cut the ground with a tool; they cover a distance and pass through a territory.

The relationship between these meanings is intelligible: in one, a way is opened through a hard body; in the other, someone passes through an extensive area.

This does not mean that passing through a place must involve physical cleaving. That meaning is not present in the use concerning travel and traversing a route.

Evidence from Labīd’s Muʿallaqah

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, transmitted as pre-Islamic poetry, includes:

فَبِتِلْكَ إِذْ رَقَصَ اللَّوَامِعُ بِالضُّحَى
وَاجْتَابَ أَرْدِيَةَ السَّرَابِ إِكَامُهَا

“With that she-camel [I accomplish my purpose], when the shimmering mirages dance in the forenoon and the hills put on the mirage’s cloaks.”

In this image, ijtāba concerns putting on a garment: the hills seem to wear cloaks of mirage. The verse’s opening is linked to aqḍī al-lubānah in the following verse; the bracketed part of the translation makes this connection clear. The example illustrates Form VIII in poetry transmitted as pre-Islamic. This form does not occur in the Qur’an and is not added to its five Qur’anic groups. Nor should this clothing sense be transferred to jābū al-ṣakhra in Al-Fajr.

Evidence attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

A poem attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī includes:

أَتَاكَ أَبُو لَيْلَى يَجُوبُ بِهِ الدُّجَى
دُجَى اللَّيْلِ جَوَّابُ الْفَلَاةِ عَثَمْثَمُ

“Abū Laylā has come to you; a sturdy camel, a tireless traverser of the wilderness, carries him through the darkness of night.”

In this verse, يَجُوبُ (yajūbu) and جَوَّاب (jawwāb) concern traversing a route and passing through the desert.

جَوَّابُ الْفَلَاةِ (jawwābu al-falāh) describes a creature that travels extensively through the wilderness. This evidence shows that the family J W B was also known in Arabic for passing through and traveling.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse, however, is also transmitted in an account of his meeting with ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr. Without independent investigation, it should therefore not be presented as conclusively composed before the Qur’an’s revelation.

The verse also has variant wordings, but the use of yajūbu and jawwābu al-falāh for traversing a route is clear.

Answering and returning speech

The second branch of J W B concerns answering.

Arabic أَجَابَهُ (ajābahu) means “he answered him,” while أَجَابَ عَنْ سُؤَالِهِ (ajāba ʿan suʾālihi) means “he answered his question.”

Jawāb names the answer expressed in response to a question, statement, request, or call.

تَجَاوَبَ (tajāwaba) is also used for answering one another, as when two people converse or two sounds respond to each other.

Early collections of Arabic proverbs record the following saying. Its precise date of origin should not be inferred solely from the age of the collection transmitting it:

أَسَاءَ سَمْعًا فَأَسَاءَ جَابَةً

“He listened badly, so he answered badly.”

جَابَة (jābah) in this expression belongs to the same semantic field of answering.

This usage shows that an answer may be appropriate or inappropriate. Someone who hears a statement poorly or misunderstands it may give an unsuitable answer.

An elegy attributed to Kaʿb ibn Saʿd al-Ghanawī, mourning Abū al-Mighwār, includes:

وَدَاعٍ دَعَا يَا مَنْ يُجِيبُ إِلَى النَّدَى
فَلَمْ يَسْتَجِبْهُ عِنْدَ ذَاكَ مُجِيبُ

“A caller cried: Who will respond to the call for generosity? Yet at that moment no answerer answered him.”

This verse is significant for its use of يجيب (yujību), يستجبه (yastajibhu), and مجيب (mujīb).

Answering here means more than returning a few words. Someone asks for assistance, and the expected response is to turn toward the request and help. Within the elegy, the absence of an answerer also recalls the generosity lost with the deceased.

Istijābah as accepting a call and acting in response to it is therefore a recognized Arabic use.

Nevertheless, attribution and the poem’s precise date must be considered separately from the word’s meaning. This verse is evidence from the early Arabic poetic tradition, but should not be called certainly pre-Qur’anic without establishing when it was composed.

Did answering develop from piercing?

A resemblance may be imagined between opening a path and answering: in one, a material obstacle is opened; in the other, someone’s words meet a response.

This resemblance alone does not establish a history of derivation.

Arabic lexical tradition has also sometimes divided this family into two separate principles: piercing something and returning speech.

One should therefore not claim that answering originally meant “breaking the silence” and that every application concerning supplication and response developed from this image.

“Breaking the silence” is an intelligible literary expression, but without independent historical evidence it cannot serve as the lexical definition of ijābah.

The more precise approach records both branches without constructing a certain, unproven historical relationship between them.

3. “Jābū al-ṣakhra”: cutting through rock

Al-Fajr 89:9

وَثَمُودَ الَّذِينَ جَابُوا الصَّخْرَ بِالْوَادِ

“And Thamūd, those who cut through rock in the valley.”

This verse contains the Qur’an’s only occurrence of the Form I verb jāba.

Jābū here concerns Thamūd. Its object is الصَّخْرَ (al-ṣakhra), “rock,” and بِالْوَادِ (bi-l-wādi) identifies where the action occurs.

Cutting rock and traversing a route

Arabic jāba is used both for cutting or piercing and for traversing a land.

Here, however, the verb’s explicit object is al-ṣakhr. Cutting through rock therefore fits the sentence’s construction more directly.

If the verse concerned traversing a desert or a territory, its object or construction would be expected to belong to that semantic field.

Instead, the passage concerns something Thamūd did to rock.

Al-ṣakhr names the rock; the valley is the stated location; and jābū expresses cutting or cleaving.

The verb should therefore not be translated as “building houses.” Building houses may result from cutting rock, but is not the direct meaning of jābū itself.

The connection with other verses about Thamūd

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 speaks of carving houses from mountains. Al-Ḥijr 15:82 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 also describe making houses in mountains.

These verses provide a suitable setting for understanding Thamūd’s stoneworking.

Understanding jābū al-ṣakhra as cutting and carving rock is thus consistent with the Qur’an’s other descriptions of this people.

Not every detail of those verses should, however, be inserted into the meaning of jābū itself.

Al-Fajr 89:9, for example, does not identify the tools used, the size of the rocks, or precisely how the buildings were made.

Nor does the name Thamūd alone establish that every known stoneworking monument in a region belongs to that people. This requires independent historical and archaeological investigation.

Jābū and material capability

In Al-Fajr, Thamūd are mentioned alongside ʿĀd and Pharaoh.

The description of cutting rock forms part of the Qur’an’s depiction of their capabilities and activities. Judgment concerning these peoples’ conduct and end, however, comes from the surrounding verses rather than from the root J W B itself.

Cutting rock is not blameworthy in itself. It can serve ordinary needs such as making houses and roads.

Pride, wrongdoing, or disobedience therefore cannot be extracted from jābū. Where these arise in the narrative, they concern the people’s conduct and circumstances.

4. Jawāb: an answer does not always mean acceptance

Jawāb occurs four times in the Qur’an. All four occurrences belong to narratives of Prophets and concern a people’s response to its Prophet’s call or speech.

They matter semantically because they show that an answer can be negative, threatening, or opposed to the message.

The response of Lot’s people

Al-Aʿrāf 7:82

وَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا أَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ قَرْيَتِكُمْ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ أُنَاسٌ يَتَطَهَّرُونَ

“His people’s only response was to say: Expel them from your settlement; they are people who seek purity.”

The response of Lot’s people is expressed as a demand to expel him and his companions.

Jawāb itself, however, does not mean expulsion. It names their reaction to Lot’s call; the following sentence supplies the content.

Jawāb therefore cannot mean agreement or accepting another’s speech in every application.

Al-Naml 27:56 repeats a similar response, there demanding the expulsion of Lot’s household.

The response of Abraham’s people

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24

فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا اقْتُلُوهُ أَوْ حَرِّقُوهُ فَأَنْجَاهُ اللَّهُ مِنَ النَّارِ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

“His people’s only response was to say: Kill him or burn him! Then The God saved him from the fire. In that are signs for people who believe.”

Here too, jawāb names the people’s response to Abraham’s speech.

Its content, however, is a threat to kill or burn.

An answer therefore does not always indicate constructive dialogue or necessarily offer reasons and arguments. An individual or community may make threats and violence their response to someone’s speech.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29, Lot’s people likewise respond by demanding that divine punishment be brought upon them.

Taken together, these four uses show that jawāb names the relationship of an answer to earlier speech; its value and content come from the sentence stating the answer.

Must an answer be verbal?

In these four verses, the answer’s content is expressed through speech.

Arabic answering can nevertheless go beyond returning a few words: responding to a call may involve doing or refraining from something.

This matters more when studying the root’s verbal branch.

Even so, the noun jawāb should not be turned into “action” in every sentence. In the verses just discussed, the Qur’an itself gives the answer’s content with قالوا (qālū), “they said.”

5. Ajāba: answering requests and calls

The verbal forms of ajāba, Form IV, occur eight times in the Qur’an.

Their settings include The God responding to human requests, people responding to the divine call, and questions about the answers received by Prophets or Messengers.

“Ujību daʿwata al-dāʿi”: The God responding to supplication

Al-Baqarah 2:186

وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِي عَنِّي فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ ۖ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ ۖ فَلْيَسْتَجِيبُوا لِي وَلْيُؤْمِنُوا بِي لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْشُدُونَ

“When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the supplicant’s call when they call upon Me. So let them respond to Me and believe in Me, that they may find right guidance.”

This is one of the root’s most important occurrences, bringing two different forms into one sentence: أُجِيبُ (ujību) and فَلْيَسْتَجِيبُوا (fa-l-yastajībū).

In the first part, The God speaks of responding to His servants’ supplication. In the second, the servants are asked to respond to His call and believe in Him.

These relationships are connected, but they are not identical.

The God answers supplication; the servants, in turn, are addressed by the divine call.

فليستجيبوا لي (fa-l-yastajībū lī) should therefore not be translated as “so let them fulfill My supplication too.” People responding to The God here means turning toward His command and call.

Furthermore, وَلْيُؤْمِنُوا بِي (wa-l-yuʾminū bī) connects responding to the divine call with faith.

Their association does not make the lexical meaning of istijābah simply “believing.” The Qur’an expresses them with two separate verbs.

Does answering supplication always mean granting the exact request?

In this verse, ujību expresses answering.

The root J W B itself, however, does not determine when each request is answered, how, or with what outcome.

Some verses state a particular result after mentioning the response, such as removing distress or granting a child. In those cases, the result is known from what follows.

Al-Baqarah 2:186 specifies no such particular material outcome in the sentence.

The lexical meaning of ujību alone therefore does not establish that every supplication must be realized immediately and exactly as the supplicant imagines.

At the same time, the explicit promise of answering should not be made so vague that no real response remains.

The verse expresses the servant’s direct relationship with The God and His responsiveness. How this response is realized requires examining the wider Qur’anic treatment of supplication, will, wisdom, and divine action.

In Ghāfir 40:60, the command to call upon the Lord likewise accompanies a promise of answering. The verse continues by speaking of arrogance toward worshipping Him. Here too, the sentence distinguishes The God as answerer from the human being who calls.

Answering someone in distress

Al-Naml 27:62

أَمَّنْ يُجِيبُ الْمُضْطَرَّ إِذَا دَعَاهُ وَيَكْشِفُ السُّوءَ وَيَجْعَلُكُمْ خُلَفَاءَ الْأَرْضِ ۗ أَإِلَٰهٌ مَعَ اللَّهِ ۚ قَلِيلًا مَا تَذَكَّرُونَ

“Or who answers the distressed when they call upon Him, removes adversity, and makes you successors on the earth? Is there a god alongside The God? How little heed you take!”

Here, يُجِيبُ (yujību) takes الْمُضْطَرَّ (al-muḍṭarra): someone in severe difficulty and distress.

It is followed by وَيَكْشِفُ السُّوءَ (wa-yakshifu al-sūʾa), “and removes adversity.”

This association connects The God’s response in this context with removing distress.

Removing adversity is not, however, the independent meaning of J W B. It is a separate verb and expression stating an outcome or an action accompanying the response.

The verse also asks about the true object of worship, ending with the question of whether there is a god alongside The God.

It should therefore not be detached from that context of divine oneness and used merely as a formula for every wish being fulfilled at the moment of distress.

Answering the supplication of Moses and Aaron

Yūnus 10:89

قَالَ قَدْ أُجِيبَتْ دَعْوَتُكُمَا فَاسْتَقِيمَا وَلَا تَتَّبِعَانِّ سَبِيلَ الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

“He said: The supplication of you both has been answered; so remain steadfast, both of you, and do not follow the path of those who do not know.”

Here, أُجِيبَتْ (ujībat) is passive, and دَعْوَتُكُمَا (daʿwatukumā) refers to the supplication of two addressees.

The dual distinction in كما (kumā) must therefore be retained rather than restricting the translation to one person’s supplication.

Another point is the command فَاسْتَقِيمَا (fa-staqīmā) after the announcement of the answer.

This association shows that announcing a supplication has been answered does not necessarily mean every stage of the requested event has ended immediately. A command to remain steadfast still follows.

The verb itself does not determine the timing or manner of the outcome; the narrative context supplies that information.

6. Mujīb: describing The God as answering

Mujīb and its plural al-mujībūn occur twice in the Qur’an as descriptions of The God.

“Inna rabbī qarībun mujībun”

Hūd 11:61

وَإِلَىٰ ثَمُودَ أَخَاهُمْ صَالِحًا ۚ قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ اعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ مَا لَكُمْ مِنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرُهُ ۖ هُوَ أَنْشَأَكُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ وَاسْتَعْمَرَكُمْ فِيهَا فَاسْتَغْفِرُوهُ ثُمَّ تُوبُوا إِلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّ رَبِّي قَرِيبٌ مُجِيبٌ

“To Thamūd [We sent] their brother Ṣāliḥ. He said: My people, serve The God; you have no god other than Him. He brought you forth from the earth and set you to cultivate it. So seek His forgiveness, then turn back to Him. My Lord is indeed near and answering.”

Mujīb describes The God in this verse.

It is formed from Form IV, ajāba, and denotes one who answers.

In the same verse, Ṣāliḥ calls his people to seek forgiveness and return to The God, then speaks of the Lord’s nearness and responsiveness.

Mujīb is therefore connected here with the possibility of turning to The God and receiving His answer.

It should not, however, be restricted to someone who always performs the exact request without condition.

The God’s nearness comes from قريب (qarīb); His answering comes from mujīb. The descriptions appear in one sentence but belong to different roots.

“Fa-la-niʿma al-mujībūn”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:75

وَلَقَدْ نَادَانَا نُوحٌ فَلَنِعْمَ الْمُجِيبُونَ

“Noah indeed called upon Us; how excellent We are as answerers!”

Al-mujībūn appears here in the plural.

This relates to The God speaking of Himself in the plural. It does not establish several gods or independent answerers.

The following verse, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:76, speaks of saving Noah and his household from great distress. The response in this context is therefore a real action with an outcome.

That outcome, however, comes from the continuing context, particularly Al-Ṣāffāt 37:76, rather than from the independent meaning of al-mujībūn.

These two Qur’anic occurrences show answering as a divine description, while requiring the actual word forms to be preserved.

In Hūd 11:61, مُجِيبٌ (mujībun) is indefinite; in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:75, الْمُجِيبُونَ (al-mujībūn) is definite and plural.

Later lists of divine names should not lead us to add al- to the first occurrence or remove the second’s plural. The place of “al-Mujīb” among divine names is a separate question from reporting the Qur’an’s wording.

7. Istajāba: responding to calls and requests

Form X is this root’s largest Qur’anic branch, with 28 occurrences.

Arabic istajāba can mean answering, accepting a call, or turning toward a request.

No absolute boundary can be drawn between ajāba and istajāba that makes the first always a verbal answer and the second always the practical fulfillment of a request.

The Qur’an itself uses istajāba both for The God responding to supplication and for people responding to a call.

The God responding to His servants

In Āl ʿImrān 3:195, The God answers His servants’ supplication and declares that He does not allow any worker’s work, male or female, to be lost.

In Yūsuf 12:34, His response is accompanied by averting the scheme Joseph feared.

In Al-Anfāl 8:9, the response occurs in the context of asking for aid and divine assistance.

These uses show that istajāba can express The God responding to a human request.

Each response’s outcome, however, is known from its context. The morphological form alone should not determine the kind of answer.

Answering Job’s request

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:84

فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ فَكَشَفْنَا مَا بِهِ مِنْ ضُرٍّ وَآتَيْنَاهُ أَهْلَهُ وَمِثْلَهُمْ مَعَهُمْ رَحْمَةً مِنْ عِنْدِنَا وَذِكْرَىٰ لِلْعَابِدِينَ

“So We answered him, removed the affliction he suffered, and gave him his household and their like with them, as mercy from Us and a reminder for those who worship.”

فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ (fa-stajabnā lahu) expresses The God’s answering in this verse.

Removing suffering and granting the household, however, are expressed by the following verbs and clauses.

Istajāba therefore does not lexically mean healing or restoring a family.

Answering Jonah’s call

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:88

فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْغَمِّ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نُنْجِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“So We answered him and saved him from distress; thus do We save the believers.”

Here too, the response is accompanied by deliverance.

Deliverance, however, comes from نَجَّيْنَاهُ (najjaynāhu). Istajabnā itself states that The God answered his call.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:76, answering Noah is likewise accompanied by saving him and his household.

Answering Zechariah

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:90

فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ يَحْيَىٰ وَأَصْلَحْنَا لَهُ زَوْجَهُ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْخَيْرَاتِ وَيَدْعُونَنَا رَغَبًا وَرَهَبًا ۖ وَكَانُوا لَنَا خَاشِعِينَ

“So We answered him, granted him John, and set his wife right for him. They used to hasten to good deeds, call upon Us in hope and fear, and humble themselves before Us.”

The God’s answer here accompanies granting John and setting Zechariah’s wife’s condition right.

Istijābah can therefore express a real answer to a particular request in context.

The word itself neither identifies the child nor determines how the wife’s condition was set right.

Together these verses show that istajāba and ajāba overlap in answering supplication. Their morphological difference alone does not establish two entirely separate kinds of divine response.

8. Human response: accepting a call and turning toward it

Responding to The God and the Messenger

Al-Anfāl 8:24

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اسْتَجِيبُوا لِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ إِذَا دَعَاكُمْ لِمَا يُحْيِيكُمْ ۖ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَحُولُ بَيْنَ الْمَرْءِ وَقَلْبِهِ وَأَنَّهُ إِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

“You who believe, respond to The God and the Messenger when he calls you to what gives you life. Know that The God intervenes between a person and their heart, and that to Him you will be gathered.”

اسْتَجِيبُوا (istajībū) is an imperative here.

The addressees are people who believe. The command therefore means more than merely hearing the call; they are asked to turn toward it and respond.

Giving life, however, comes from لِمَا يُحْيِيكُمْ (li-mā yuḥyīkum), rather than from J W B itself.

This distinction matters. Responding to a call is one action; the call’s subject and outcome are another matter.

Al-Shūrā 42:47 likewise commands استجيبوا لربكم (istajībū li-rabbikum): respond to your Lord’s call.

The verse calls for responding before a day whose arrival cannot be averted.

In Ibrāhīm 14:44, when punishment arrives, the wrongdoers ask for a short postponement and promise to answer The God’s call and follow the Messengers. Nujib, “we will answer,” occurs within these petitioners’ speech; the nature and value of their promise depend on their situation in the verse.

Responding despite hardship

Āl ʿImrān 3:172

الَّذِينَ اسْتَجَابُوا لِلَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا أَصَابَهُمُ الْقَرْحُ ۚ لِلَّذِينَ أَحْسَنُوا مِنْهُمْ وَاتَّقَوْا أَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

“Those who responded to The God and the Messenger after suffering wounds: for those among them who did good and practiced taqwā, there is a great reward.”

Here the response accompanies previous hardship and injury.

Being wounded is not part of istijābah’s lexical meaning. The verse describes people who responded despite their injuries.

لِلَّذِينَ أَحْسَنُوا مِنْهُمْ وَاتَّقَوْا (li-lladhīna aḥsanū minhum wa-ttaqaw) also matters, connecting the reward with goodness and taqwā among those people.

Responding alone should therefore not be equated with every moral merit without considering the conduct and conditions accompanying it.

Response and consultation

Al-Shūrā 42:38

وَالَّذِينَ اسْتَجَابُوا لِرَبِّهِمْ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ

“And those who have responded to their Lord, established ṣalāh, conduct their affairs through consultation among themselves, and spend from what We have provided them.”

Here, responding to the Lord appears alongside establishing ṣalāh, consultation, and spending.

This association shows that responding to the divine call is accompanied by particular conduct in the lives of those described.

Istajābū should not, however, be translated on its own as establishing ṣalāh, consulting, or spending.

Each is expressed through its own word and construction.

Nor does this establish that istijābah originally denoted a particular form of political administration. Consultation is one characteristic of the community described here, rather than the root’s lexical meaning.

Al-Raʿd 13:18 likewise contrasts those who responded to their Lord with those who did not. The good promised to the first and the second group’s end come from the rest of the verse; the contrast uses the same root for response and nonresponse.

A response to a call can also be wrong

One of the root’s most important uses appears in Satan’s speech.

Ibrāhīm 14:22

وَقَالَ الشَّيْطَانُ لَمَّا قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَعَدَكُمْ وَعْدَ الْحَقِّ وَوَعَدْتُكُمْ فَأَخْلَفْتُكُمْ ۖ وَمَا كَانَ لِيَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ سُلْطَانٍ إِلَّا أَنْ دَعَوْتُكُمْ فَاسْتَجَبْتُمْ لِي ۖ فَلَا تَلُومُونِي وَلُومُوا أَنْفُسَكُمْ ۖ مَا أَنَا بِمُصْرِخِكُمْ وَمَا أَنْتُمْ بِمُصْرِخِيَّ ۖ إِنِّي كَفَرْتُ بِمَا أَشْرَكْتُمُونِ مِنْ قَبْلُ ۗ إِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

“When the matter was decided, Satan said: The God promised you a true promise; I too promised you, but broke my promise. I had no authority over you except that I called you and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot rescue you, nor can you rescue me. I have disavowed your previously associating me [with The God]. The wrongdoers will indeed have a painful punishment.”

فَاسْتَجَبْتُمْ لِي (fa-stajabtum lī) is attributed here to people who accepted Satan’s invitation.

This use is fundamental for determining the root’s meaning.

Istijābah is not inherently good conduct or a sign of guidance. Its value depends on the call answered.

In Al-Anfāl 8:24, believers are called to respond to The God and the Messenger. In Ibrāhīm 14:22, a group has responded to Satan.

The verb belongs to the same family and morphological form in both, but the conduct’s direction and value differ.

This verse also connects response with personal responsibility. Satan says that he called them and they accepted his call.

Responsibility and blame, however, are not part of istijābah’s lexical meaning; they come from the speech and the verse’s context as a whole.

Hearing and answering

Al-Anʿām 6:36

إِنَّمَا يَسْتَجِيبُ الَّذِينَ يَسْمَعُونَ ۘ وَالْمَوْتَىٰ يَبْعَثُهُمُ اللَّهُ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ يُرْجَعُونَ

“Only those who hear respond. As for the dead, The God will raise them; then to Him they will be returned.”

Hearing and responding appear together here.

Their association should not make them synonyms.

Hearing can precede responding, but sound reaching the ear does not itself mean accepting a call.

The verse concerns hearing that brings a person into engagement with the call.

Nevertheless, J W B itself should not be translated as hearing. Yastajību still expresses responding; يسمعون (yasmaʿūna) expresses hearing.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:52 also presents responding in the context of a summons and being raised. Its association with praise and the thought of having remained only a little while come from the same verse; tastajībūna itself does not name resurrection or the length of that stay.

9. An unanswered call: gods that do not respond

Several verses describe objects of worship that people invoke without receiving an answer.

Calling upon others besides The God

Al-Raʿd 13:14

لَهُ دَعْوَةُ الْحَقِّ ۖ وَالَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ لَا يَسْتَجِيبُونَ لَهُمْ بِشَيْءٍ إِلَّا كَبَاسِطِ كَفَّيْهِ إِلَى الْمَاءِ لِيَبْلُغَ فَاهُ وَمَا هُوَ بِبَالِغِهِ ۚ وَمَا دُعَاءُ الْكَافِرِينَ إِلَّا فِي ضَلَالٍ

“To Him belongs the true call. Those whom [people] invoke besides Him do not answer their callers in anything, except as one who stretches both hands toward water so that it may reach his mouth, although it does not reach it. The disbelievers’ supplication is only in error.”

لَا يَسْتَجِيبُونَ لَهُمْ بِشَيْءٍ (lā yastajībūna lahum bi-shayʾin) expresses the invoked objects’ inability to answer.

The water example further clarifies the request’s futility.

Istijābah here does not mean delivering water. That image comes from the accompanying comparison.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:194, people are challenged to invoke their claimed gods and receive an answer if they are truthful.

Fāṭir 35:14 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:5 also describe the inability of objects worshipped besides The God to hear or respond.

These uses foreground the relationship between a call and the ability to answer.

They do not establish that every person who has not yet believed must go unanswered if they call directly upon The God.

The principal subject here is the inability of those being invoked. The judgment’s scope should not be extended beyond what the verses themselves express.

The gods’ inability at the Resurrection

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:64 and Al-Kahf 18:52 also describe calling upon supposed partners and their failure to answer.

These uses show that calling and receiving an answer are two different acts.

Someone may call another without finding any response.

The existence of a supplication therefore does not itself establish an answer, just as the designation “god” does not establish a real ability to respond.

10. The difference between ajāba and istajāba

These are among the root’s most important Qur’anic forms.

Arabic ajāba concerns answering; istajāba likewise concerns answering or accepting a call.

A fixed distinction has sometimes been proposed: for example, that ajāba is only a verbal answer, istajāba a practical response, or that istijābah always follows an intense request.

Qur’anic usage does not support such an absolute boundary.

In Al-Baqarah 2:186, The God says أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ (ujību daʿwata al-dāʿi). In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:88, He says فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ (fa-stajabnā lahu). Both concern a divine response to supplication.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:31, a Form IV imperative calls for responding to the divine invitation.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:31

يَا قَوْمَنَا أَجِيبُوا دَاعِيَ اللَّهِ وَآمِنُوا بِهِ يَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ مِنْ ذُنُوبِكُمْ وَيُجِرْكُمْ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

“Our people, respond to the one who calls to The God and believe in Him; He will forgive you of your sins and shelter you from a painful punishment.”

أَجِيبُوا (ajībū) here means more than returning a sentence. The addressees are called to respond to the divine caller, with faith mentioned next.

This use shows that Form IV can also express a practical response to a call.

The following verse, Al-Aḥqāf 46:32, uses the same verb negatively: someone who does not respond to the one calling to The God.

The two forms therefore cannot be divided so that one belongs only to supplication and the other only to obedience.

The role of the preposition

Qur’anic istajāba often appears with لِـ (li-), responding to someone or a call.

Ajāba may occur without that preposition, taking a direct object.

This difference does not create an exceptionless rule about the verbs’ meanings either. Their transitive constructions and varied applications must be examined in the verses themselves.

What can be said with greater confidence is that both forms express a relationship between a call, request, or statement and an answer.

The sentence and context clarify the kind of answer, whether it is positive or negative, and whether it appears in words or actions.

In Hūd 11:14, nonresponse appears in the context of the challenge to produce a comparable text; in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:50, failure to respond to the Messenger is connected with following desires. These uses likewise show that the request’s subject and the judgment concerning the response come from the sentence and context.

11. Two verses with important syntactic differences

“Wa-yastajību alladhīna āmanū”

Al-Shūrā 42:26

وَيَسْتَجِيبُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَيَزِيدُهُمْ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ ۚ وَالْكَافِرُونَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ

“He answers those who believe and do righteous deeds and adds to them from His bounty; the disbelievers will have a severe punishment.”

Two main syntactic analyses identify the subject and object here, with several semantic explanations. This is a difference in analyzing the same wording, rather than different textual forms or recitations of the verse.

In the first, The God is the subject of yastajību and الذين آمنوا (alladhīna āmanū) is its object. The God thus answers the believers who do righteous deeds and adds to them from His bounty.

In the second, alladhīna āmanū is the subject: those who believe and do righteous deeds respond to their Lord’s call. Another explanation of this same subject construction understands istajāba as seeking their Lord’s response through righteous deeds; on that analysis, the believers seek an answer.

Both interpretations have been examined linguistically.

The first clearly fits the continuity of the nearby verbs: the preceding verse also concerns The God’s actions, while وَيَزِيدُهُمْ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ (wa-yazīduhum min faḍlihi) in this verse refers to Him.

This preference should not make the second interpretation wrong without grounds. Identifying the subject and object has a real effect on the sentence’s meaning and must remain part of the study.

“Min baʿdi mā ustujība lahu”

Al-Shūrā 42:16

وَالَّذِينَ يُحَاجُّونَ فِي اللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا اسْتُجِيبَ لَهُ حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ وَعَلَيْهِمْ غَضَبٌ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ

“Those who dispute about The God after His call has been answered: their argument is invalid before their Lord; wrath is upon them, and they will have a severe punishment.”

اسْتُجِيبَ (ustujība) is passive here.

The important questions concern the referent of له (lahu) and who has answered the call.

One interpretation relates the pronoun to The God and understands the sentence as the divine call having been answered.

Another connects it with the Messenger’s call and the acceptance of his message among people.

Both concern a call that has received a response, followed by people disputing about The God.

Ustujība itself, however, does not determine every historical detail of this dispute.

A basic translation should therefore preserve the passive construction and avoid greater certainty about the pronoun’s referent than the evidence allows.

12. “Mādhā ujibtum” and “mādhā ajabtum”: receiving and giving an answer

Two verses ask similarly constructed questions, but their morphological difference matters.

Al-Māʾidah 5:109

يَوْمَ يَجْمَعُ اللَّهُ الرُّسُلَ فَيَقُولُ مَاذَا أُجِبْتُمْ ۖ قَالُوا لَا عِلْمَ لَنَا ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ عَلَّامُ الْغُيُوبِ

“On the day The God gathers the Messengers and asks: What response did you receive? They say: We have no knowledge; You indeed know all the unseen.”

أُجِبْتُمْ (ujibtum) is passive here. The Messengers are addressed and asked about the response they received from others.

Another verse reads:

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:65

وَيَوْمَ يُنَادِيهِمْ فَيَقُولُ مَاذَا أَجَبْتُمُ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

“On the day He calls them and asks: What answer did you give the Messengers?”

أَجَبْتُم (ajabtum) is active here. The addressees are themselves those who answered the Messengers.

The verb’s different vowels therefore produce a real difference in meaning.

One question concerns an answer received; the other concerns an answer given.

This clearly illustrates why knowing the three radicals alone is insufficient for studying a root. The verb’s construction, its active or passive voice, and the pronouns’ referents must also be examined.

13. Comparison with related roots

J W B (jawāb) and D ʿ W (daʿwah): call and response

Calling and answering occur together in many verses, but they do not have the same meaning.

A call or supplication begins with someone addressing another, making a request, or summoning them to an action.

Jawāb is the response to that call or request.

Al-Baqarah 2:186 presents this relationship between the servant’s supplication and The God’s answer.

In Al-Anfāl 8:24, however, the call comes from The God and the Messenger, while people are summoned to respond.

The relationship therefore runs in different directions in the two verses.

Nor does every call necessarily receive a positive answer: in the verses concerning powerless objects of worship, supplication occurs but a response is denied.

J W B (ijābah) and S M ʿ (samʿ): hearing and answering

Hearing may precede answering, but it is not the answer itself.

Al-Anʿām 6:36 places the two alongside each other.

Someone may hear speech without responding. They may also give a verbal answer without accepting the call.

Istajāba should therefore not be translated simply as “heard” in every verse.

Conversely, Qur’anic hearing is not always merely receiving sound: it may involve attention and acceptance. The precise distinction must be understood from each verse’s context.

J W B (istijābah) and Ṭ W ʿ (ṭāʿah): responding and obeying

Responding to the divine call may accompany obedience.

Al-Shūrā 42:38 describes those who answer their Lord’s call alongside other conduct.

Responding and obeying, however, are not synonymous in every application.

In Al-Baqarah 2:186, The God answers supplication, but this response cannot be called The God obeying His servant.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, people respond to Satan’s invitation. Accepting that call involves following wrongly.

The moral value and kind of commitment must therefore be understood from whom and what the call concerns.

J W B (jawāb) and R D D (radd): answering and returning

Arabic رد (radd) concerns returning or sending something back.

Some applications of returning speech or sending back a reply belong to this same semantic field.

Jawāb, however, denotes responding to speech, a question, or a call, without necessarily returning something to its original place.

Radd may also involve rejection in particular applications, whereas jawāb may be positive or negative.

In the narratives of Lot’s and Abraham’s peoples, their response is negative and threatening. That comes from what they say, rather than from an invariant meaning of jawāb.

J W B (jawb) and N Ḥ T (naḥt): cutting and carving

Concrete نحت (naḥt) concerns carving and shaping something by removing parts of it.

Jawb can likewise concern cutting, perforating, and cleaving.

In the Thamūd narrative, both semantic fields relate to their work in rock.

Not every cut, however, carves a particular shape; nor should every act of carving be given the same lexical designation jawb.

Al-Fajr 89:9 describes cutting rock. Other verses about Thamūd describe carving houses from mountains.

These descriptions help contextual understanding of one another, but they are not lexically identical.

J W B (jawb) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭʿ): cleaving and cutting

قطع (qaṭʿ) has a broad field of cutting and separating.

Jawb can also express cutting or cleaving, but its recognized uses concern making an opening, perforating, or passing through something.

Still, one should not claim that jawb always means cutting completely and qaṭʿ cutting partially, or the reverse.

Linguistic evidence does not establish such an absolute boundary.

In Al-Fajr 89:9, cutting through rock is close to both fields, but the Qur’an’s actual word is jābū, whose image is best preserved in translation.

J W B (jawāb) and J B Y (jābiyah): two different families

This comparison is particularly important when identifying word forms.

Jawāb as answering belongs to J W B.

Al-jawābi in Sabaʾ 34:13, however, occurs in a phrase concerning large vessels. It is the plural of jābiyah and belongs to J B Y.

Jābiyah concerns a large vessel or basin in which something collects.

Similar spelling therefore does not suffice to place the words under one root.

Qur’anic morphology requires examining the actual form, syntactic role, and contextual meaning. A digital index is a classification tool; disputed derivation still requires independent study.

14. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translations of this root’s different forms should follow construction and context.

جَابُوا (jābū), Al-Fajr 89:9: “cut through” or “made openings in the rock.” A flowing translation may also use “cut and carved the rock,” recognizing that carving is clarified by the connection with other descriptions of Thamūd.

جَوَاب (jawāb): the basic equivalent is “answer” or “response.” Its four Qur’anic occurrences concern a people’s response to its Prophet’s speech, which may be negative or threatening. “Acceptance” is not a suitable general equivalent.

أَجَابَ (ajāba): “answered” or “responded.” In supplication contexts, “answered his supplication” is suitable.

أَجِيبُوا دَاعِيَ اللَّهِ (ajībū dāʿiya Allāhi), Al-Aḥqāf 46:31: “Respond to the one who calls to The God.” Where context requires, the explanation may add that this means turning toward his call.

اِسْتَجَابَ (istajāba): depending on context, “responded,” “accepted the call,” or “answered the request.”

For The God, “answered” and “responded” are natural. For a human response to the divine invitation, “responded to the call” or “accepted the call” is more suitable.

فَاسْتَجَبْتُمْ لِي (fa-stajabtum lī), Ibrāhīm 14:22: “So you accepted my invitation” or “So you responded to my call.” Satan is the caller here; moral goodness should not be added to istijābah itself.

اسْتَجِيبُوا لِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ (istajībū li-llāhi wa-li-l-rasūli), Al-Anfāl 8:24: “Respond to The God and the Messenger.”

أُجِبْتُمْ (ujibtum), Al-Māʾidah 5:109: “What response did you receive?” The passive must be preserved.

أَجَبْتُمُ الْمُرْسَلِينَ (ajabtumu al-mursalīna), Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:65: “What answer did you give the Messengers?”

مُجِيبٌ (mujībun), Hūd 11:61: “answering” or, in a supplication context, “one who answers supplication.” The natural basic description “one who answers” is preferable.

الْمُجِيبُونَ (al-mujībūn), Al-Ṣāffāt 37:75: “answerers,” retaining the plural The God uses of Himself.

One fixed expression such as “obeyed” should not translate every form of istajāba. The word concerns The God answering supplication, people responding to Messengers, accepting Satan’s invitation, and the inability of gods besides The God to respond.

Nor should a positive or negative judgment be inserted into jawāb beforehand. The response may accept truth or reject and threaten. The sentence and context clarify that difference.

15. Conclusion

J W B has two recognized semantic branches in Arabic.

In the concrete branch, jawb concerns cutting, cleaving, perforating, and traversing a route. جَابُوا الصَّخْرَ (jābū al-ṣakhra) in Al-Fajr 89:9 belongs to this field, referring to Thamūd’s cutting rock.

In the verbal and behavioral branch, jawāb and the related verbs concern responding to speech, a question, a request, or a call.

This branch is extensive in the Qur’an. The God answers His servants’ supplication; people respond to The God and the Messengers; communities reject their Prophets’ calls; and some people accept Satan’s invitation.

Answering is therefore not inherently a sign either of truth or of falsehood. The direction and value of istijābah depend on what is answered.

Ajāba and istajāba are also very close in some uses. Without sufficient evidence, no absolute boundary should make one exclusively verbal and the other exclusively practical.

Supplication passages likewise show that answering and its outcome are connected but distinct. Some verses state a particular result after mentioning the response, such as deliverance, removing distress, or granting a child. That result comes from what follows, rather than from J W B itself.

For Forqan, this family’s definition should retain two branches: in concrete usage, jawb concerns cutting, piercing, and passing through something; in verbal and behavioral usage, jawāb and istijābah concern responding to speech, a request, or a call.

This definition preserves Thamūd’s stoneworking and clarifies the Qur’an’s range of answers and responses, without converting either branch into the other through an invented image.

Al-Baqarah 2:186 holds a special place among these uses, presenting both directions of the relationship: The God answers His servant’s supplication, and the servant is called to respond to The God. This should not make their roles identical or turn answering supplication into an automatic transaction.

Qur’anic jawāb has a real relationship with preceding speech and a call. Whether that relationship leads to guidance, opposition, deliverance, or an unhappy end depends on the agent, the addressee, the call’s subject, and each verse’s context.
