The Qur’anic Root W Y L

From Cries of Grief and Warning to the Announcement of a Dire Outcome

وَيْل (wayl) is a powerful word in the Qur’an, but unlike many Qur’anic roots, it does not have an extensive morphological family. The Qur’an does not form a variety of verbs and derivatives from it; most occurrences are the same وَيْل (wayl), appearing sometimes as وَيْلٌ لِـ (waylun li-), sometimes as وَيْلَكَ / وَيْلَكُمْ (waylaka / waylakum), and sometimes in cries of lament such as يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ، يَا وَيْلَتَنَا (yā waylatā, yā waylatanā) and يَا وَيْلَنَا (yā waylanā).

Early lexicographers likewise examined wayl more as an independent word for lamentation, distress, destruction, punishment, or the arrival of evil than as an ordinary verbal root.

The early lexical tradition also records an interesting explanation of the relationship between وَيْل (wayl) and nearby words such as وَيْح (wayḥ) and وَيْس (ways). Some lexicographers connected this family with the element وَيْ (way), a word used for regret, calling attention, and emotional reaction. Forms such as وَيْح، وَيْس (wayḥ, ways) and وَيْل (wayl) then came to be recognized as independent words in the language.

This view shows that the exclamatory and emotional character of wayl attracted attention early on, but it does not establish the precise history of the word’s formation. In particular, we cannot confidently claim that wayl necessarily arose by combining “way” with the preposition “li-.”

Qur’anic usage supports the following core meaning: a word for lamentation and intense reaction to something unwelcome, or for warning and announcing a dire outcome and severe distress. Whether wayl in an ayah is a person’s cry concerning themselves or a warning concerning someone else becomes clear from the sentence construction and context.

Forms Examined

وَيْل (wayl) occurs 40 times in the Qur’an. Its principal forms are:

وَيْلٌ، فَوَيْلٌ، وَوَيْلٌ (waylun, fa-waylun, wa-waylun)

الْوَيْلُ (al-wayl)

وَيْلَكَ، وَيْلَكُمْ (waylaka, waylakum)

يَا وَيْلَتَا / يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ (yā waylatā)

يَا وَيْلَتَنَا (yā waylatanā)

يَا وَيْلَنَا (yā waylanā)

All these forms return to the same basic word. Their differences arise mainly from syntax, the attachment of pronouns, and the form of lamentation, rather than from independent semantic derivatives.

There is therefore no need to construct a “material image” for this word. The clearer evidence is its use in living Arabic speech and in the Qur’an itself.

1. Usage in Pre-Islamic Arabic

وَيْل (wayl) was known in Arabic before the Qur’an and was used for lamentation, anxiety, distress, and intense emotional reaction.

Al-Aʿsha’s poetry includes:

قَالَتْ هُرَيْرَةُ لَمَّا جِئْتُ زَائِرَهَا
وَيْلِي عَلَيْكَ وَوَيْلِي مِنْكَ يَا رَجُلُ

Qālat Hurayratu lammā jiʾtu zāʾirahā / waylī ʿalayka wa-waylī minka yā rajulu.

In this verse, وَيْلِي (waylī) does not name punishment in the Hereafter or a particular place. In a moment of intense emotion, the speaker says “my woe over you” and “my woe from you.”

This usage itself shows that wayl could already be a cry of grief, anxiety, or intense reaction before Islam.

Early sources also compare وَيْل (wayl) with وَيْح (wayḥ). One well-known distinction is that wayl is more often addressed to someone in distress, facing destruction, or on a path to ruin, with a warning or reproachful tone; wayḥ, by contrast, can more readily convey pity and compassion toward the person in distress.

This distinction is useful but not absolute. The early lexicographers themselves did not fully agree on the precise boundary between wayl and wayḥ, and sometimes considered them close in meaning.

2. وَيْلٌ لِـ (Waylun Li-): Announcing a Dire Outcome

A large proportion of Qur’anic occurrences use the construction وَيْلٌ لِـ (waylun li-):

وَيْلٌ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ

Waylun li-l-muṭaffifīna.

Woe to those who give short measure.

Al-Mutaffifin 83:1

And:

وَيْلٌ لِّكُلِّ هُمَزَةٍ لُّمَزَةٍ

Waylun li-kulli humazatin lumazatin.

Woe to every faultfinder and taunter.

Al-Humazah 104:1

And:

فَوَيْلٌ لِّلْمُصَلِّينَ

Fa-waylun li-l-muṣallīna.

So woe to those who perform salat...

Al-Maun 107:4

In such constructions, wayl does more than express the speaker’s feelings. The sentence announces a dire outcome and delivers a severe warning concerning a group whose behavior or condition is then explained.

This is particularly clear in Al-Baqarah 2:79:

فَوَيْلٌ لِّلَّذِينَ يَكْتُبُونَ الْكِتَابَ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ ثُمَّ يَقُولُونَ هَٰذَا مِنْ عِندِ اللَّهِ

Fa-waylun li-lladhīna yaktubūna al-kitāba bi-aydīhim thumma yaqūlūna hādhā min ʿindi Allāhi.

So woe to those who write the Book with their own hands, then say: This is from The God.

Al-Baqarah 2:79

It then says twice more:

فَوَيْلٌ لَّهُم مِّمَّا كَتَبَتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ

Fa-waylun lahum mimmā katabat aydīhim.

So woe to them for what their hands have written.

And:

وَوَيْلٌ لَّهُم مِّمَّا يَكْسِبُونَ

Wa-waylun lahum mimmā yaksibūna.

And woe to them for what they earn.

Repeating wayl intensifies the warning, but the word itself still does not specify a particular kind of punishment. The reason for the dire outcome is understood from the continuation of the ayah.

In Al-Mursalat, too, the expression:

وَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ

Waylun yawmaʾidhin li-l-mukadhdhibīna.

is repeated many times, each time closing a new part of the surah’s argument with a warning about the outcome awaiting those who deny.

3. وَيْلَكَ and وَيْلَكُمْ (Waylaka and Waylakum): Warning, Not Necessarily a Curse

When wayl is attached directly to a pronoun referring to the addressee, it can take the form of a warning or strong reproach.

Moses says:

وَيْلَكُمْ لَا تَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا فَيُسْحِتَكُم بِعَذَابٍ

Waylakum lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhābin.

Woe to you! Do not fabricate a lie against The God, lest He destroy you with punishment.

Ta-Ha 20:61

Here وَيْلَكُمْ (waylakum) comes before a prohibition and warning. Moses is not wishing punishment upon his hearers; he is restraining them from an action that can bring punishment.

In the account of Qarun, those who have been given knowledge likewise say:

وَيْلَكُمْ ثَوَابُ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ لِّمَنْ آمَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا

Waylakum thawābu Allāhi khayrun li-man āmana wa-ʿamila ṣāliḥan.

Woe to you! The reward of The God is better for whoever believes and acts righteously.

Al-Qasas 28:80

Here too, the utterance is not a curse. They are discouraging people from infatuation with Qarun’s wealth.

In Al-Ahqaf, parents say to their child:

وَيْلَكَ آمِنْ إِنَّ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ حَقٌّ

Waylaka āmin inna waʿda Allāhi ḥaqqun.

Woe to you! Believe; the promise of The God is indeed true.

Al-Ahqaf 46:17

Thus وَيْلَكَ / وَيْلَكُمْ (waylaka / waylakum) can express reproach, warning, or an attempt to restrain the hearer from a dangerous path. Translating it as “a curse upon you” in every instance erases this capacity.

4. يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ and يَا وَيْلَنَا (Yā Waylatā and Yā Waylanā): A Person’s Cry in Shock and Grief

Another branch of Qur’anic usage is a person’s lament concerning themselves:

قَالَ يَا وَيْلَتَا أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَٰذَا الْغُرَابِ

Qāla yā waylatā a-ʿajaztu an akūna mithla hādhā al-ghurābi.

He said: Woe is me! Have I been unable to be like this raven?

Al-Maidah 5:31

Here the reaction is accompanied by shame and regret.

Al-Kahf also states:

وَيَقُولُونَ يَا وَيْلَتَنَا مَالِ هَٰذَا الْكِتَابِ لَا يُغَادِرُ صَغِيرَةً وَلَا كَبِيرَةً إِلَّا أَحْصَاهَا

Wa-yaqūlūna yā waylatanā māli hādhā al-kitābi lā yughādiru ṣaghīratan wa-lā kabīratan illā aḥṣāhā.

They say: Woe to us! What is this Book that leaves nothing small or great without counting it?

Al-Kahf 18:49

And in Ya-Sin:

قَالُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا مَن بَعَثَنَا مِن مَّرْقَدِنَا

Qālū yā waylanā man baʿathanā min marqadinā.

They said: Woe to us! Who has raised us from our resting place?

Ya-Sin 36:52

In these instances, wayl is a reaction to the disclosure of a terrifying reality.

But one of the clearest examples of this word’s range appears in the words of Abraham’s wife:

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا

Qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan.

She said: Alas! Shall I bear a child when I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man?

Hud 11:72

This usage shows that يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ (yā waylatā) is not uttered only in response to punishment and destruction. Abraham’s wife encounters the unexpected news of a child’s birth and cries out in intense astonishment.

Wayl can therefore accompany intense wonder, shock, anxiety, regret, or grief. The kind of emotion is determined by the continuation of the sentence.

5. Is وَيْل (Wayl) the Name of a Valley in Hell?

Some later exegetical reports and transmitted narratives describe wayl as a valley, well, or place in Hell.

These reports, however, should not determine the word’s lexical definition.

Clear Qur’anic usages prevent wayl from being the name of a particular place in every instance. If it were, an expression such as:

يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ

Yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun.

or:

وَيْلَكُمْ ثَوَابُ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ

Waylakum thawābu Allāhi khayrun.

would be difficult to explain.

What can confidently be drawn from the Qur’an itself is that wayl can announce punishment, a dire outcome, and severe distress; the word itself does not specify the form, location, or particular name of that punishment.

In وَيْلٌ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ (waylun li-l-muṭaffifīn), therefore, “wayl” should not be translated as “a valley in Hell.” At its direct level, the ayah announces a dire outcome for those who give short measure.

6. Wayl’s Different Roles in the Sentence

Placing the Qur’anic usages side by side reveals three main directions.

In وَيْلٌ لِـ (waylun li-), the word mainly announces a dire outcome or warns about what lies ahead:

وَيْلٌ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ

Waylun li-l-muṭaffifīna.

In وَيْلَكَ / وَيْلَكُمْ (waylaka / waylakum), direct warning and reproach are more prominent:

وَيْلَكُمْ لَا تَفْتَرُوا

Waylakum lā taftarū.

And in يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ / يَا وَيْلَنَا (yā waylatā / yā waylanā), the person cries out in response to the situation that has arisen:

يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ

Yā waylatā.

These are not three completely separate meanings. All belong to the field of something unwelcome, danger, distress, or intense reaction; but the positions of the speaker and addressee change the sentence’s function.

7. Comparison with Nearby Words

W Y L (وَيْل, Wayl) and W Y H (وَيْح, Wayḥ)

These two words are close in the early language, but lexicographers often recognized a difference in their tendencies.

وَيْل (wayl) appears more often where distress, destruction, reproach, or serious warning is at issue.

وَيْح (wayḥ) can more readily accompany compassion and pity for someone afflicted by calamity or on the verge of danger.

Put simply, in wayḥ the speaker may feel compassion for the addressee, while wayl often has a weightier and more warning tone.

But this difference is not absolute, and some lexicographers regarded the two as close or even synonymous in certain uses.

W Y L (وَيْل, Wayl) and H S R (حَسْرَة, Ḥasrah)

Ḥasrah concerns more particularly the grief arising from something lost, something done, or something that has passed beyond reach.

Wayl can accompany ḥasrah, but is broader. A person can warn with وَيْلَكُمْ (waylakum) before a calamity has fully occurred, or say يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ (yā waylatā) in intense astonishment.

Not every wayl, then, is ḥasrah.

W Y L (وَيْل, Wayl) and TH B R (ثُبُور, Thubūr)

Al-Furqan states:

لَا تَدْعُوا الْيَوْمَ ثُبُورًا وَاحِدًا وَادْعُوا ثُبُورًا كَثِيرًا

Lā tadʿū al-yawma thubūran wāḥidan wa-dʿū thubūran kathīran.

Do not call today for a single thubūr; call for much thubūr.

Al-Furqan 25:14

Thubūr is closer to destruction and ruin.

Wayl can announce such an outcome or react to it, but the word itself does not necessarily mean “destruction that has already occurred.”

W Y L (وَيْل, Wayl) and H L K (هَلَكَ, Halaka)

Halāk is destruction and perishing itself.

Wayl is more a verbal reaction or an announcement of a condition associated with destruction, punishment, evil, or calamity.

Halāk therefore expresses the event; wayl can be a warning about that event or a person’s cry in response to it.

8. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

The most important point is not to restrict wayl to a fixed referent such as “Hell” or “a valley in Hell.”

On the other hand, it should not be reduced to a neutral exclamation such as “oh.” Even in Hud 11:72, the word carries intense astonishment and emotional reaction.

English renderings such as “woe” and “alas” should be chosen with the flexibility of Arabic wayl in mind. “Woe to you” can express warning or reproach, “woe is me” regret or anxiety, and an exclamation such as “alas!” can convey an intense reaction to sudden, astonishing news when the surrounding sentence makes that emotion clear.

Preserving this exclamatory function can convey the Arabic word’s capacity in many ayahs better than a cumbersome explanatory paraphrase.

Nevertheless, the precise kind of emotion or warning should be explained from the sentence, rather than packed entirely into a single English equivalent.

9. Suggested Translations for Forqan

وَيْلٌ لِـ (waylun li-):
“Woe to...”

In explanation: announcing a dire outcome or delivering a severe warning.

فَوَيْلٌ لِّلَّذِينَ... (fa-waylun li-lladhīna...):
“So woe to those who...”

وَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ (waylun yawmaʾidhin li-l-mukadhdhibīn):
“Woe that day to those who deny.”

وَيْلَكَ (waylaka):
“Woe to you!”

Depending on context, a warning or strong reproach; not necessarily a curse.

وَيْلَكُمْ (waylakum):
“Woe to you all!”

In Ta-Ha 20:61 and Al-Qasas 28:80, warning and restraining the hearer are prominent.

يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ / يَا وَيْلَتَا (yā waylatā):
“Woe is me!” or “Alas!”

The emotion can be regret, fear, shame, or intense astonishment.

يَا وَيْلَنَا / يَا وَيْلَتَنَا (yā waylanā / yā waylatanā):
“Woe to us!”

الْوَيْلُ (al-wayl):
A dire outcome, distress, or punishment, according to context, without turning the word itself into the name of a particular place.

Conclusion

وَيْل (wayl) in the Qur’an is an old, independent Arabic word more than a member of an extensive morphological family. The Qur’an uses it 40 times, but does not form a variety of verbal and nominal derivatives from it.

In the early lexical tradition, wayl was compared with a family of words such as وَيْح (wayḥ) and وَيْس (ways), and some lexicographers connected all of them with the element وَيْ (way), a word used for regret, calling attention, and emotional reaction. This view helps explain the word’s exclamatory character, but does not establish the precise history of wayl’s formation.

Wayl was also known in pre-Islamic Arabic for lamentation and intense reaction. Al-Aʿsha’s poetry provides a clear example of this usage.

The Qur’an employs this capacity in several directions. وَيْلٌ لِـ (waylun li-) mainly announces a dire outcome and warns about what lies ahead. وَيْلَكَ / وَيْلَكُمْ (waylaka / waylakum) can express direct reproach and warning. In يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ / يَا وَيْلَنَا (yā waylatā / yā waylanā), a person cries out in the face of calamity, shock, regret, or intense astonishment.

The usage of Abraham’s wife in Hud 11:72 is one of the clearest examples of the word’s range. On hearing the news of a child, she says يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ (yā waylatā). Wayl therefore cannot mean “the punishment of Hell” or name a particular valley in every instance.

The difference from وَيْح (wayḥ) further clarifies this picture. In many early uses, wayḥ accompanies pity and compassion, whereas wayl usually carries a more intense tone of warning, distress, or a dire outcome, although this boundary is not absolute in every case.

A concise and cautious definition of the word is therefore:

Wayl: a word for lamentation and intense reaction to something unwelcome, and, when addressing others, for warning, reproach, or announcing a dire outcome and severe distress. Its precise meaning is determined by the sentence construction and context; the word itself does not specify the name of a particular punishment or place.
