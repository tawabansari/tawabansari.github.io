The Arabic root ح ر س concerns guarding, watching over, and protecting someone or something. حَرَسَ الشَّيْءَ means guarding something and seeking to prevent harm, encroachment, or unwanted access. حَارِس is a guard, and حَرَس denotes a group of guards.

In the family’s ordinary uses, guarding involves attention and vigilance. A guard may protect a house, property, a person, a road, or a particular place. Sometimes the aim is to prevent entry; sometimes it is to prevent the theft of, or damage to, something entrusted to the guard.

Early Arabic also has another branch: حَرْس can mean time or an age, with أَحْرُس used for periods of the past. A use concerning the nighttime theft of livestock is also recorded, though lexicographers have debated its precise relationship to guarding.

The root ح ر س occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَرَسًا in Al-Jinn 72:8, in the expression حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا.

The verse names guards in a description of the sky reported by a group of jinn. It also mentions شُهُب alongside them.

Understanding this Qur’anic use therefore requires distinguishing the guards, the act of guarding, associated means or phenomena, and what is protected.

On the preferred reading here, حَرَس names the guards, rather than the sky, the shooting flames, or the act of guarding. The alternative verbal-noun analysis is considered separately below.

1. Forms and derivatives

The root ح ر س consists of ḥāʾ, rāʾ, and sīn. It is a sound, regular triliteral Arabic root.

Important forms in the family include:

  • حَرَسَ ـ يَحْرُسُ / يَحْرِسُ: Guarded, watched over, and protected against encroachment or harm.
  • حَرْس: A verbal noun of حَرَسَ for guarding and protection; in another use, time or an age.
  • حِرَاسَة: Guarding, watching over, and protection.
  • حَارِس: A guard: someone responsible for watching over and protecting something.
  • حُرَّاس: A plural of حارس, guards.
  • حَرَس: A collective noun or designation for a group of guards, such as the guards of a ruler or place.
  • أَحْرَاس: A recorded plural for guards, with an alif after rāʾ.
  • أَحْرُس: The plural of حَرْس for periods of time, without an alif after rāʾ and with ḍammah on it.
  • حَرَسِيّ: A member of a body of guards, especially the guards of a court or ruler.
  • مَحْرُوس: Someone or something being guarded.
  • اِحْتَرَسَ: Took precautions against a danger or person and protected oneself; in a different use, stole livestock from its grazing place.
  • اِحْتِرَاس: Vigilance and caution; in a particular early use, livestock theft.
  • تَحَرَّسَ: Looked after oneself and took precautions to remain safe from danger.
  • تَحَرُّس: Vigilance and caution to prevent harm.
  • حَرِيسَة: In early usage, a sheep remaining outside its shelter at night or stolen from its grazing place; in another explanation, the theft itself.
  • حَرَائِس: A plural of حريسة in reference to livestock.
  • أَحْرَس: In the time-related branch, old or ancient, as of a building over which many ages have passed.
  • أَحْرَسَ: In a lexical use, stayed in a place for a period.
  • مِحْرَاس: In one recorded use, a large arrow.

Lexicons also record proper place names with these consonants, including حَرْس and الحَرْسَان for particular places or mountains. Their existence does not prove that each name historically derives from guarding.

Only حَرَسًا occurs in the Qur’an.

It is a collective noun, occurring with accusative nunation in the sentence.

The verb حَرَسَ, the singular active participle حَارِس, the verbal noun حِرَاسَة, and the family’s other derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an.

Thus, one Qur’anic occurrence belongs to a broad lexical family; there is not a series of different derivatives across multiple verses.

2. Semantic centers in early Arabic

Two branches in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two bases for ح ر س:

أَحَدُهُمَا الْحِفْظُ وَالْآخَرُ زَمَانٌ

aḥaduhumā al-ḥifẓu wa-al-ākharu zamānun

That is: “One is preservation, and the other is time.”

This distinction matters: unlike his treatment of some roots, Ibn Fāris does not reduce every use in this family to one meaning.

The first branch concerns guarding and vigilance.

The second concerns time and the passing of ages.

One can imagine a connection between prolonged watching and the passage of time, but that resemblance does not establish a common historical origin.

The two branches should therefore be identified separately in research.

The Qur’anic حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا belongs to the first branch, guarding.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: preservation and time

حَرَسَ الشَّيْءَ: protecting against danger

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī writes:

حَرَسَهُ يَحْرُسُهُ حِرَاسَةً، أَيْ حَفِظَهُ

ḥarasahu yaḥrusuhu ḥirāsatan ay ḥafiẓahu

That is: “He guarded it, meaning he preserved it.”

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains حَرَسَ الشَّيْءَ through حَفِظَهُ.

Guarding and preservation are therefore close in this use.

In practice, however, ḥaras often brings out the image of guarding against an external danger or unwanted access.

Someone responsible for guarding a house, for example, must watch for people not authorized to enter.

Someone guarding livestock may seek to prevent its theft or injury.

In such circumstances, guarding means more than being present beside something: it involves responsibility for vigilance and preventing danger.

But the word itself does not specify the means by which the guard prevents danger.

Presence alone may suffice; the guard may give a warning or block an entrance, or may have defensive equipment in another situation.

The action and its means are known from context, rather than the root alone.

حَرَس: a group of guards

In early Arabic, حَرَس denotes a group of guards.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports from Al-Azharī that حَارِس and حَرَس are both established in referring to guards.

As with خَادِم and خَدَم, one form can name the person performing a task and another from the same family a group of such people.

حَرَسُ السُّلْطَانِ likewise denotes the group responsible for guarding a ruler or his court.

Here ḥaras names a group, rather than one particular person.

This matters for Al-Jinn, where حَرَسًا refers to guards.

Their precise number is not established by the word itself.

حرس can identify a group of guards without specifying their number, ranks, or organization.

حَارِس and حَرَسِيّ

Arabic حَارِس is an active participle: someone who guards.

In historical usage, حَرَسِيّ denotes a member of a body of guards.

The difference primarily concerns naming.

حارس describes the person’s role.

حرسي can indicate membership in a guard or protective establishment.

But the distinction is not identical or absolute in every period and dialect of Arabic.

The Qur’an uses حَرَسًا, not حَرَسِيًّا or حَارِسًا.

The verse’s translation should therefore not identify the guards, without justification, as officers of a modern administrative or military organization.

Guarding and caution against danger

Early Arabic اِحْتَرَسَ مِنْهُ and تَحَرَّسَ مِنْهُ concern taking precautions and protecting oneself against danger.

This differs importantly from حَرَسَهُ.

In حَرَسَ الشَّيْءَ, a person guards something.

In اِحْتَرَسَ مِنْهُ, the person takes precautions to protect themselves against something.

In the first, the object of care may be someone or something else.

In the second, caution and care to ward off danger from oneself are prominent.

The verb’s construction and preposition establish this distinction.

In this use, iḥtirās does not simply name a guard standing by a door; it can describe a person’s vigilance and alertness to danger.

An Arabic saying about an untrustworthy guard

The saying is recorded in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab. Here it is given with the passive-participle vocalization transmitted in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and other sources:

مُحْتَرَسٌ مِنْ مِثْلِهِ وَهُوَ حَارِسٌ

muḥtarasun min mithlihi wa-huwa ḥārisun

That is: “One guards against the likes of him, although he is himself a guard.” The vocalization مُحْتَرِس with kasrah is also found in some transmissions; the rendering here follows مُحْتَرَس with fatḥah, so the two readings are not conflated.

The expression is applied to someone entrusted with protecting something whose betrayal is nevertheless feared.

Its point is that the title حارس does not itself guarantee a person’s integrity.

Someone may hold a guarding responsibility yet betray the trust.

The entrusted responsibility and the guard’s actual conduct are distinguished.

The saying attests the root’s social and ethical use in Arabic.

Its precise origin is not established; without independent evidence, it should not be assigned to a particular poet or period.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the passive muḥtaras and the saying about a guard

حَرِيسَة and the nighttime theft of livestock

Lexicons record a different use of this family.

حَرِيسَة can denote a sheep that remains outside its shelter when night falls.

In another explanation, it denotes a sheep stolen from its grazing place.

اِحْتَرَسَ الشَّاةَ is also used to mean stealing a sheep.

At first sight, this seems incompatible with guarding.

How can one word family refer both to guarding and to theft?

Ibn Fāris reports this disagreement.

He explains that some lexicographers take ḥarīsah as guarded livestock, and others as the theft itself.

For theft, he suggests that the thief also watches the object before taking it, as though lying in wait for it.

But Ibn Fāris considers the explanation through guarded livestock more sound.

Livestock theft is thus recorded, but should not be presented as the root’s certain original meaning.

Nor does this restricted use establish that حَرَس in Al-Jinn 72:8 means thieves.

The verse’s context clearly fits guards.

Ibn Fāris: the dispute over ḥarīsat al-jabal and preference for guarded livestock

حَرْس: time and passing ages

The root’s second branch in Maqāyīs al-Lughah and other lexicons concerns time.

One early use of حَرْس denotes a span of time.

Lisān al-ʿArab records it for a period of an age, described as shorter than a حُقْب.

أَحْرُس is its plural for periods and ages.

This use differs from guarding.

When something is said to survive from earlier ages, the issue is passing time, rather than watching a doorway or protecting against an enemy.

Shared consonants and the presence of two meanings in a lexicon do not suffice to establish that one historically derives from the other.

A witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays concerning passing time

The collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

لِمَنْ طَلَلٌ دَاثِرٌ آيُهُ
تَقَادَمَ فِي سَالِفِ الْأَحْرُسِ

li-man ṭalalun dāthirun āyuhu / taqādama fī sālifi al-aḥrusi

That is: “Whose ancient campsite is this, its traces worn away, grown old through ages past?”

Here الْأَحْرُسِ denotes periods of time.

The poet describes the worn traces of a dwelling site over which much time has passed.

أحرس does not refer to guards here.

The witness matters for the second branch because it shows time and passing ages in poetic language.

The verse is transmitted in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and in lexicons.

But the precise attribution and dates of all poems ascribed to Imruʾ al-Qays are not uniformly certain. The witness should be considered within those limits.

أَحْرَس: an old building

Lexicons record بِنَاءٌ أَحْرَسُ for an ancient building.

Here the reference is to a structure over which many ages have passed.

أحرس does not mean that the building had many guards.

Its age belongs to the branch concerning the passage of time.

This shows why resemblance to forms of guarding should not cause preservation to be imposed on every use.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: aḥrus, passing ages, and an ancient building

A pre-Islamic poetic witness concerning guards

In the Muʿallaqah of Imruʾ al-Qays, in describing reaching a woman difficult to approach, the poet says:

تَجَاوَزْتُ أَحْرَاسًا إِلَيْهَا وَمَعْشَرًا
عَلَيَّ حِرَاصًا لَوْ يُسِرُّونَ مَقْتَلِي

tajāwaztu aḥrāsan ilayhā wa-maʿsharan / ʿalayya ḥirāṣan law yusirrūna maqtalī

That is: “To reach her, I passed guards and a group eager for my death, wishing to conceal my killing.” This rendering follows the explanation of يُسِرُّونَ as concealment; other explanations of the reading are noted below.

Here أَحْرَاسًا refers to actual guards.

The poet speaks of passing people who watched the approach to his destination.

The use concerns guarding and preventing access.

أحراس does not mean past ages here.

The two plurals must be distinguished: أَحْرَاس, with an alif after rāʾ, denotes guards; أَحْرُس, without that alif and with ḍammah on rāʾ, denotes periods of time. They are not one identical plural form with two meanings.

Early transmissions vary, including أَحْرَاسًا إِلَيْهَا وَمَعْشَرًا versus أَحْرَاسًا وَأَهْوَالَ مَعْشَرٍ, and يُسِرُّونَ versus يُشِرُّونَ. Al-Tabrīzī records concealment and disclosure as explanations of the former, while the latter is explained as disclosure. These differences should not be silently collapsed into one translation.

These textual differences should be retained when recording the witness.

Nevertheless, أَحْرَاس as guards is clear in this verse.

Together, the two witnesses show that guarding and time were both established branches in Arabic poetic usage.

Al-Tabrīzī’s Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: the Imruʾ al-Qays witness and yusirrūna/yushirrūna variants

3. Qur’anic use of the root

Al-Jinn 72:8: strong guards and shooting flames

Arabic verse:

وَأَنَّا لَمَسْنَا السَّمَاءَ فَوَجَدْنَاهَا مُلِئَتْ حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا

wa-annā lamasnā al-samāʾa fa-wajadnāhā muliʾat ḥarasan shadīdan wa-shuhuban

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“And we sought out and examined the sky, and found it filled with stern guards and shooting flames.”

حَرَسًا is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The speech is reported from a group of jinn describing a situation connected with the sky.

فَوَجَدْنَاهَا مُلِئَتْ indicates that they found the sky in a state described through guards and shooting flames.

حَرَسًا names the guards.

شَدِيدًا qualifies them.

وَشُهُبًا introduces another category of what the verse names.

The verse thus brings two designations together: guards and shooting flames.

The two should not be collapsed from the outset into a single word or object.

حَرَسًا: a noun with collective meaning

Arabic حَرَس is a collective noun.

One apparently singular form can identify a group of guards.

In Al-Jinn 72:8, حَرَسًا likewise refers to the body of guards.

Accordingly, شَدِيدًا appears in the masculine singular.

Had the individuals within the group been considered, another expression with a plural qualification could have been used.

But the qualification in the verse follows the wording of حَرَس.

This construction is natural in Arabic and does not establish that only one guard was present.

Early grammatical and exegetical sources describe حَرَسًا as a collective noun or a plural of حَارِس.

The collective-noun explanation suits حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا.

The role of شَدِيدًا

شَدِيدًا comes from ش د د.

It concerns intensity, firmness, and strength.

Here it qualifies the guards.

But شديد in this expression does not mean moral cruelty or injustice.

In Persian, “stern guard” may suggest someone who mistreats others.

Here the intensity of guarding and its deterrent force are prominent.

Equivalents such as “strong guards” or “guards maintaining firm, strict protection” can therefore clarify the concept.

But direct translation must not merge the independent meaning of شديد into حرس itself.

حرس denotes the guards; intensity and firmness come from the following qualification.

Can حَرَسًا be a verbal noun?

Some grammatical explanations also propose that حَرَسًا is a verbal noun concerning guarding, rather than a name for the group of guards.

On this analysis, the expression can be understood as the sky being filled with intense guarding.

Most lexical and exegetical explanations, however, take حرس here as guards.

حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا also fits that reading, since guards and shooting flames are named together.

“Guards” therefore has stronger support for direct translation in this study’s assessment.

The verbal-noun possibility can nevertheless be recorded in grammatical research without imposing it on the principal translation.

Al-Qurṭubī: collective meaning, singular qualification, and the verbal-noun analysis

لَمَسْنَا السَّمَاءَ: touching, or seeking access?

The verse begins:

وَأَنَّا لَمَسْنَا السَّمَاءَ

wa-annā lamasnā al-samāʾa

In its physical sense, لَمَسَ concerns touching.

But this family can also relate to seeking and testing in other Arabic uses.

Early explanations interpret لمسنا السماء as seeking access to the sky or seeking its news.

This relates to the next verse, where the same speakers describe positions in which they sat to listen.

Seeking access and listening therefore matter in this context.

But لمسنا alone does not establish that the jinn touched the sky with a hand or bodily organ in the way that a person touches a wall.

Conversely, the entire image of access to the sky should not be removed without justification.

The verse does not explain the precise character of the experience.

The subject of this study is حَرَسًا; a full treatment of لمس belongs to the corresponding root study.

Al-Ṭabarī: lamasnā as seeking the sky and access

4. Guards, shooting flames, and listening

Al-Jinn 72:9: positions for listening

Arabic verse:

وَأَنَّا كُنَّا نَقْعُدُ مِنْهَا مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ فَمَنْ يَسْتَمِعِ الْآنَ يَجِدْ لَهُ شِهَابًا رَصَدًا

wa-annā kunnā naqʿudu minhā maqāʿida li-al-samʿi fa-man yastamiʿi al-āna yajid lahu shihāban raṣadan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“And we used to sit in positions there to listen; but whoever listens now finds a shooting flame lying in wait for him.”

This verse explains why the guarding in the preceding verse matters.

The speakers describe positions where they previously sat to listen.

They then say that whoever seeks to listen now encounters a shooting flame lying in wait.

Together, the two verses connect guarding with preventing a particular kind of access and listening.

But ح ر س itself does not mean listening or a shooting flame.

Guarding expresses the protective function that prevents access.

The positions for listening are established by مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ.

The shooting flame is likewise named with a separate word and root.

The difference between حَرَس and شُهُب

Al-Jinn 72:8 joins the two with وَ.

حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا

ḥarasan shadīdan wa-shuhuban

Lexically, حرس denotes guards.

شهب is the plural of شهاب.

Thus the verse names guards and shooting flames.

This construction alone does not establish the guards’ nature, their number, or their precise operational relationship with the shooting flames.

Early commentaries identify the guards as angels.

This reading relates to the protection of the sky described in other verses.

But the verse’s direct wording must be distinguished from identifying its referents through interpretation.

Al-Jinn 72:8 itself does not use the word مَلَائِكَة.

Translating حرس as “guardian angels” thus introduces an exegetical identification.

“Guards” is more faithful to the word itself.

Al-Qurṭubī: identifying the guards as angels, distinguished here from the verse’s wording

Other verses concerning the sky’s protection

Al-Ḥijr 15:17, Al-Ḥijr 15:18, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:6, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:7, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10, and Al-Mulk 67:5 also describe the sky’s protection and shooting flames in connection with devils.

These verses matter for thematic comparison.

They use ح ف ظ and other words such as شهاب.

But ح ر س itself does not occur in them.

This distinction should be preserved.

The thematic resemblance does not justify merging all meanings of preservation, pelting, shooting flame, and guarding into a single root definition.

Comparison shows a thematic connection between protection and preventing access, rather than identical words.

Do the guards protect the sky against destruction?

The immediate subject in Al-Jinn 72:8 and Al-Jinn 72:9 concerns listening and access.

Without evidence, the guards’ task should therefore not be extended to protecting the sky against every natural harm or cosmic destruction.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:32 describes the sky as a protected roof.

But that verse treats a separate matter and uses ح ف ظ.

The verses may be compared in a broader study of the sky.

Yet حرس in Al-Jinn should first be understood through its context of listening and restricted access.

The word alone does not establish a mission to guard every star, planet, or celestial body.

Is شهاب a meteorite in today’s scientific terminology?

Modern usage scientifically distinguishes a meteor, a meteoroid, a meteorite, and other celestial phenomena.

But Qur’anic Arabic شهاب names a bright, fiery phenomenon in the sky’s description.

The word alone does not define every physical detail of that phenomenon.

حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا therefore does not establish that the Qur’an specifies the material, astronomical origin, path, or numerical speed of shooting flames.

Conversely, this caution does not deny the existence of actual bright phenomena in the sky.

The point is the limited information conveyed by the expression itself.

Scientific knowledge of celestial phenomena should rest on empirical evidence and should not replace the Qur’anic words’ lexical meanings.

Have all jinn been prevented from hearing?

In Al-Jinn 72:8 and Al-Jinn 72:9, a group of jinn speaks about access for listening.

But the beginning of the same surah describes jinn hearing the Qur’an and believing in it.

The difference matters.

The jinn hearing the Qur’an in Al-Jinn 72:1 and Al-Jinn 72:2 differs from seeking to listen from celestial positions in Al-Jinn 72:9.

These verses do not establish that jinn are fundamentally excluded from hearing any divine message.

The restriction concerns the particular access described in Al-Jinn 72:8 and Al-Jinn 72:9.

Does حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا mean unchanging, perpetual guarding?

حرس itself names the guards.

شديد emphasizes their strength and firmness.

Neither word alone specifies an unlimited duration.

The next verse distinguishes the former situation from الْآنَ, now.

The account therefore speaks of changed access.

This distinction does not establish the precise date when guarding began or ended without further evidence.

حرس should therefore not be made to mean eternal, everlasting guards.

5. حَرَس and رَصَد: guarding and lying in wait

Al-Jinn 72:9 uses رَصَدًا.

This comes from ر ص د and concerns watching, monitoring, and lying in wait.

Al-Jinn 72:27 also uses رَصَدًا in the context of conveying revelation and knowledge of the unseen.

The uses are thematically close to حرس.

But رصد and حرس are not the same root.

حرس concerns guarding and protection.

رصد can place greater emphasis on watching, waiting, and monitoring in anticipation of an event or person.

For example, a guard may protect a gate.

Someone lying in wait may also watch people’s arrival.

One person may sometimes perform both tasks, but the concepts are not linguistically identical.

In Al-Jinn 72:8, حرس identifies guards.

In Al-Jinn 72:9, شِهَابًا رَصَدًا describes a shooting flame lying in wait for the listener.

This thematic proximity does not make حرس and رصد synonymous in every use.

6. Differences from related roots

Ḥ R S and Ḥ F Ẓ: guarding and preservation

حَفِظَ has a broad Qur’anic range.

The root concerns care, preservation, and preventing something from being lost or wasted.

A person may preserve property, a covenant, words, or knowledge.

Preservation can also concern the sky, a book, a person, or an action.

حَرَسَ likewise relates to preservation, but its familiar use brings the guard’s role and vigilance against encroachment or unwanted access more to the foreground.

Guarding can thus be a kind of preservation, but preservation does not always mean a guard standing against an external danger.

For example, retaining words in memory belongs to ح ف ظ, but is not ordinarily called حراسة.

The guards in Al-Jinn 72:8 relate to preventing access and listening.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

Ḥ R S and Ḥ R Z: guarding and security

Arabic حَرَزَ concerns protection and keeping something in a secure place.

حِرْز can denote a safe place for keeping property.

اِحْتَرَزَ concerns taking precautions and protecting oneself.

These meanings approach حرس.

Ibn Fāris reports that some lexicographers considered the zāy in ح ر ز a replacement for the sīn in ح ر س.

But that is a lexical hypothesis, rather than conclusive proof that the roots were historically identical.

Qur’anic research should examine their root consonants separately.

Moreover, حرس in Al-Jinn 72:8 does not mean a secure place or container; it identifies guards.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ḥ R Z: the proposed interchange of sīn and zāy

Ḥ R S and R Ṣ D: guarding and watchful waiting

رَصَدَ concerns monitoring, watching, and lying in wait.

حَرَسَ concerns guarding and protection.

The two may accompany one another in security and preventing access.

But رصد can concern waiting and observation, whereas حرس primarily expresses the guarding role.

Both families occur in Al-Jinn, and their independent meanings should be preserved.

Ḥ R S and K L ʾ: guarding and care

Arabic كَلَأَ concerns preserving and watching over.

Its family includes كِلَاءَة for guarding and protection.

The Qur’an uses this root in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:42, asking who guards people by night and day.

This is close to حرس.

But Qur’anic حرس concerns guards within a particular context of access to the sky.

The meanings of divine care in other verses should therefore not all be merged into حرس without attention to context.

Ḥ R S and ʿ S S: guarding and nighttime patrol

Arabic عَسَّ concerns going on patrol and inspecting conditions at night.

عَسَس also denotes people who patrol at night.

This use can relate to guarding.

But عسس emphasizes nighttime movement and inspection.

By contrast, حرس can identify a group guarding a person or place, by night or by day.

حرس in Al-Jinn 72:8 therefore does not itself mean nighttime patrol.

Ḥ R S and Ḥ R Ṣ: guarding and eagerness or concern

حَرَصَ comes from ح ر ص, with ṣād.

This family occurs in the Qur’an for strong desire, concern, and effort to obtain something.

حَرَسَ, with sīn, concerns guarding.

The words sound similar but differ in root and semantic range.

For example, حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:128 concerns the messenger’s intense concern and attention toward his addressees.

But حَرَسًا in Al-Jinn 72:8 refers to guards.

Similarity in writing should therefore not lead eagerness or intense seeking to be assigned to حرس.

Study of Ḥ R Ṣ

Ḥ R S and Ḥ J B: guarding and a barrier to sight or access

حِجَاب comes from ح ج ب and can denote a curtain or barrier between two sides.

حَرَس is the group of guards.

Guards may prevent entry to a place, but حرس itself does not name a curtain or wall.

Al-Jinn concerns access to the sky, yet the verse does not say that the guards must themselves be a curtain or barrier.

The shared function of preventing access should therefore not cause the words to be equated.

7. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does حَرَس always mean angels?

No.

Arabic حرس names a group of guards.

It was also used in human affairs, such as guards of a house, court, or ruler.

Al-Jinn 72:8 has a celestial context, and early commentators identified the guards as angels.

But the lexical meaning of حرس is not itself angels.

It is therefore preferable to retain “guards” in translation and examine their identity in reflection on the verse.

Does guarding always mean warfare and attack?

No.

Guarding may consist simply of vigilance, warning, or restricting access.

Defense may be necessary in some situations, but حرس itself does not specify the means or intensity of confrontation.

Al-Jinn 72:8 includes شديد, and the following verse mentions a shooting flame.

These indications show substantial deterrent force in this scene.

But the full method of deterrence cannot be derived from ح ر س alone.

Must حرس mean an organized force in today’s military sense?

In modern language, حرس may name a military or security institution.

But the word itself in early Arabic denoted a group of guards.

They could guard a person, a place, or property.

The Qur’an specifies no administrative title, command structure, ranks, or number of guards.

Modern security and military organizations should therefore not be projected onto the word’s meaning in Al-Jinn.

Is حَرْس for time certainly connected with guarding?

Ibn Fāris distinguishes preservation and time as two branches.

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays likewise shows أَحْرُس for past ages.

But this does not establish that time must have developed from prolonged watching, or vice versa.

The question remains one for historical linguistics.

In Al-Jinn, حَرَسًا does not mean periods of time: it accompanies شديد, شهب, and the following verse’s explanation of listening.

Does guarding the sky mean that guidance is inaccessible?

No.

The beginning of Al-Jinn describes the same group who report the guarding of the sky as having heard the Qur’an and believed in it.

The guarding described in Al-Jinn 72:8 and Al-Jinn 72:9 therefore does not close every path to receiving guidance.

Context distinguishes seeking access to listening positions in the sky from hearing the guidance conveyed to humans and jinn.

The subjects should not be confused.

Can the verse reveal the complete structure of the unseen world?

The verse names the sky, guards, and shooting flames.

The next verse also describes listening positions and encountering a shooting flame.

But their dimensions, shape, precise location, and full manner of operation are not explained.

حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا alone therefore cannot yield a complete map of the heavens, the angels’ positions, or every unseen phenomenon’s mechanism.

Nor should later exegetical and transmitted explanations be treated without examination as part of the word’s direct meaning.

What is lexically clear is the presence of strong guards in the report under discussion.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For حَرَسًا in Al-Jinn 72:8, the most suitable short equivalent is “guards.”

The Persian pāsdārān also fits semantically, but may suggest a particular military or political body.

Negahbānān, guards, is the more general and natural Persian equivalent.

For حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا, possible renderings include:

  • “Strong guards”
  • “Stern guards”
  • “Guards maintaining firm, strict protection”

Among these, “strong guards” is fluent in Persian and avoids the possible negative moral coloring of “stern.”

Nevertheless, شديد may also emphasize the intensity and firmness of guarding. The contextual explanation can clarify this.

For the complete verse, the proposed translation is:

“And we sought out the sky, and found it filled with strong guards and shooting flames.”

لَمَسْنَا can be discussed through seeking access, but it belongs to ل م س and should not be added to the definition of ح ر س.

The Forqan word box should distinguish two independent fields:

Brief root meaning: “Guarding, watching over, and protecting something against danger or unwanted access.”

Meaning in this verse: “A group of strong guards introduced in the jinn’s report alongside shooting flames in connection with the protection of the sky.”

A further explanation may note that early commentaries identify these guards as angels, but حرس itself does not name angels.

The non-Qur’anic senses of time, passing ages, and livestock theft should also be recorded in the root study, but not imported into the verse’s translation.

9. Conclusion

Arabic ح ر س has two principal branches: guarding and preservation, and time or passing ages.

In the first, حَرَسَ denotes guarding and care, حَارِس a guard, and حَرَس a group of guards.

In the second, حَرْس concerns a span of time and أَحْرُس past ages.

Lexicons also record a particular use concerning nighttime livestock theft. Its relationship to guarding is disputed, so this rare use should not alone determine the root’s principal meaning.

Poetic witnesses attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays show both guarding and passing ages.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَرَسًا in Al-Jinn 72:8.

Here a group of jinn reports finding the sky furnished with strong guards and shooting flames.

The next verse makes clear that the subject relates to access to positions for listening.

Thus, the direct meaning of حَرَسًا in the Qur’an is the group of guards; their role in preventing access is known from the context of Al-Jinn 72:8 and Al-Jinn 72:9.

The word itself is not an angel, a shooting flame, the sky, or a particular scientific mechanism.

Conversely, guarding should not mean merely being ineffectually present beside something. Arabic ḥirāsah concerns care and protection, which may involve preventing access or harm.

For Forqan, “guards” suits حَرَسًا and “strong guards” suits حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا.

This preserves the word’s meaning without adding the guards’ exegetical identification or unknown details of the unseen world to the verse’s direct translation.
