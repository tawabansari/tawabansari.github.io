Rising, exceeding a measure, overstepping limits, and excess in belief and conduct

The Arabic root غ ل و concerns rising, increasing, and exceeding a measure or limit. In physical uses, غَلَا can concern rising prices, plant growth, or forceful movement upward or far away. غُلُوّ concerns exceeding a proper limit in human belief, speech, or conduct.

The family includes غَلَاء for high or rising prices, غَلْوَة for an arrow's range, مُغَالَاة for excessive valuation or increase, and غُلَوَاء for the intensity or height of a condition.

These uses share associations with increase and rising but are not identical. Plant growth, expensive goods, an arrow's flight, and excessive religious claims concern different subjects.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both as تَغْلُوا: Nisa 4:171 and Maidah 5:77.

Both address the People of the Book with لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ: do not exceed the bounds of truth in your religion.

Their contexts show that religious excess is not simply more worship or stronger religious affection. It can involve attributing to God, Prophets, or religion something inconsistent with their true position.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic lexicons identify two principal themes: rising and exceeding a measure.

A physical thing can increase in height, quantity, price, or intensity.

In human conduct and judgment, someone can exceed the appropriate measure or limits of a subject.

If a commodity's price rises from ten units to a hundred, its price has increased. Growing plants and lengthening stems likewise suggest rising. An arrow forcefully sent far away can also be described through this family.

The three situations do not have the same value.

A price rise may be unwelcome to a buyer. Plant growth may indicate health and favorable conditions. A long shot may reflect an archer's skill.

Increase or height alone therefore carries no necessary positive or negative moral judgment.

When belief or conduct crosses a proper limit, however, غلو takes the sense of excess.

An early witness: plant growth in Labid

One reading of the Muʿallaqa attributed to Labid ibn Rabiʿa says:

فَغَلَا فُرُوعُ الْأَيْهَقَانِ وَأَطْفَلَتْ

fa-ghalā furūʿu al-ayhaqāni wa-aṭfalat

بِالْجَلْهَتَيْنِ ظِبَاؤُهَا وَنَعَامُهَا

bi-l-jalhatayni ẓibāʾuhā wa-naʿāmuhā

Translation:

“The branches of the ayhaqan plants rose and grew, and on the valley's two sides its gazelles and ostriches brought forth young.”

أَيْهَقَان names a plant associated in early accounts with wild-growing vegetation.

غَلَا here concerns plants rising and growing.

The subject is neither moral transgression nor religious excess. The poet describes land where plants flourish and animals reproduce.

The witness attests the physical branch of increase and rising in early poetic tradition.

The line also has another familiar reading:

فَعَلَا فُرُوعُ الْأَيْهَقَانِ

fa-ʿalā furūʿu al-ayhaqān

“The branches of the ayhaqan rose.”

That reading uses ع ل و, not غ ل و.

Early commentaries record both. فغلا is therefore an attested version, not the only certain reading of the line.

Labid lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, so attribution alone does not precisely date every verse.

Another witness: غلواء in Tufayl al-Ghanawi

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Tufayl al-Ghanawi says:

فَمَشَوْا إِلَى الْهَيْجَاءِ فِي غُلَوَائِهَا

fa-mashaw ilā al-hayjāʾi fī ghulawāʾihā

مَشْيَ اللُّيُوثِ بِكُلِّ أَبْيَضَ مُذْهَبِ

mashya al-luyūthi bi-kulli abyaḍa mudhhabi

Translation:

“They advanced toward battle at its fiercest, walking like lions, with every bright, gilded sword.”

غُلَوَاء here concerns the intensity and height of the situation.

The poet describes battle at a fierce stage.

The word does not itself name war; it describes its intensity.

The verse is recorded independently in the collection attributed to Tufayl. He is known as a pre-Islamic poet, though the precise date of this individual verse has not been established here.

It nevertheless attests the family's use for the intensification of a condition in early poetic tradition.

It does not make every intense condition necessarily wrong or excessive.

Battle, speed, or plant growth can be described without the word alone judging whether they are right or wrong.

غَلْوَة: an arrow's range

غَلَا بِالسَّهْمِ describes shooting an arrow forcefully to a great distance.

غَلْوَة denotes the range or distance of the shot.

The distance an archer sends an arrow can be reckoned as a غلوة.

Some early uses also employ it as an approximate distance measure.

It was not intrinsically one precise, universal distance: range varies with bow, arrow, force, and other conditions.

The arrow's rise and distant flight are prominent here.

مُغَالِي is also recorded for someone shooting an arrow forcefully over a long distance.

A high or distant shot is not inherently moral transgression. This use concerns movement and range, not the archer's wrongdoing.

غَلَاء: rising prices

غَلَا السِّعْرُ means the price rose or became high.

غَلَاء contrasts with رُخْص: dearness against cheapness.

For example, a wheat price may rise when the harvest is scarce.

The root here concerns amount and exchange value.

A high price does not alone prove that the seller acted unjustly or deceptively.

Prices can change with supply, demand, production costs, or other factors.

Someone might instead raise a price unfairly through hoarding, deception, or exploiting need.

The causes and responsibilities differ.

Lexically, غلاء concerns high or rising prices; the conduct's legitimacy depends on circumstances and actions.

Lexicons also record غالى في الثمن and أغلى الشيء for raising a price or valuing something highly.

Such uses connect increase and exceeding a measure without making every expensive item the result of blameworthy excess.

2. Core meanings and important forms

Rising and exceeding a measure

The principal semantic field can be understood through two situations.

First, an increase in quantity, height, or intensity.

Second, going beyond a limit or measure appropriate to a subject.

The first is not necessarily undesirable.

A tree's growth may indicate health.

Where a proper limit exists and someone exceeds it, the second becomes excess.

Respect for a teacher or parent is appropriate. Believing that they may command against truth and justice and must be obeyed without examination gives them authority beyond their right.

The issue is the position attributed to the respected person, rather than the strength of affection.

This distinction is central to the Quranic use.

Important forms and derivatives

Recognized Arabic forms include:

  • غَلَا / يَغْلُو: rose, increased, or exceeded a limit.
  • غُلُوّ: exceeding a limit; excess in belief or conduct.
  • غَلْو: shooting far; in other uses, increase or exceeding a measure.
  • غَلْوَة: an arrow's range.
  • غَلَاء: high or rising prices.
  • غَالٍ: expensive; in another context, someone exceeding a limit.
  • غَالٍ / الْغَالِي: in archery, one shooting far; these are forms of the same active participle, not independent derivatives.
  • غَالَى / يُغَالِي: raised a price or quantity, or went to excess.
  • مُغَالَاة: excessive valuation or intensification.
  • تَغَالَى: rose, increased, or went to excess.
  • غُلَوَاء: intensity or the height of a condition; youthful ardor in human use.
  • غُلْوَان: intensity and the vigorous beginning of youth, in some lexical uses.
  • اِغْتَلَى: moved rapidly or vigorously, particularly of an animal.
  • اِغْلَوْلَى: rose or increased in growth and extension.

Some lexical accounts also record غالية as an aromatic preparation. Its familiarity in later Arabic should not make it the root's central Quranic meaning or an independently established pre-Islamic perfume name.

Only تَغْلُوا occurs in the Quran.

The other forms illuminate the lexical family but do not themselves occur in the Quranic text.

The morphology of تَغْلُوا

It is an imperfect form of the basic triliteral غَلَا يَغْلُو.

It addresses a masculine plural and follows prohibitive لَا in both verses.

Before the prohibition, the form is تَغْلُونَ. Prohibitive لا makes it jussive, deleting the final نون.

لَا تَغْلُوا is thus a negative command: do not exceed the limit or go to excess.

فِي in فِي دِينِكُمْ identifies the field of that excess.

The Quran is not prohibiting every rise or increase, but overstepping bounds in religion.

This distinction determines the translation.

3. لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ in Nisa

Nisa 4:171

يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ وَلَا تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ إِنَّمَا الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ وَكَلِمَتُهُ أَلْقَاهَا إِلَىٰ مَرْيَمَ وَرُوحٌ مِنْهُ فَآمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِ وَلَا تَقُولُوا ثَلَاثَةٌ انتَهُوا خَيْرًا لَكُمْ إِنَّمَا اللَّهُ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ سُبْحَانَهُ أَن يَكُونَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ وَكِيلًا

yā ahla al-kitābi lā taghlū fī dīnikum wa-lā taqūlū ʿalā Allāhi illā al-ḥaqqa innamā al-masīḥu ʿĪsā ibnu Maryama rasūlu Allāhi wa-kalimatuhu alqāhā ilā Maryama wa-rūḥun minhu fa-āminū bi-Allāhi wa-rusulihi wa-lā taqūlū thalāthatun intahū khayran lakum innamā Allāhu ilāhun wāḥidun subḥānahu an yakūna lahu waladun lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi wa-kafā bi-Allāhi wakīlā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171

“O People of the Book, do not go to excess in your religion, and say nothing about The God except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, is only the Messenger of The God, and His word which He conveyed to Mary, and a spirit from Him. So believe in The God and His Messengers, and do not say ‘Three.’ Desist; that is better for you. The God is only one deity. Exalted is He above having a child. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth. The God suffices as trustee.”

The prohibition is immediately accompanied by:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ

wa-lā taqūlū ʿalā Allāhi illā al-ḥaqq

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171

That is, say nothing about God except the truth.

The connection clarifies the excess at issue.

It concerns attributing claims to God and religion beyond the bounds of truth.

Excess concerning Jesus' position

After the prohibition, the Quran states Jesus' position.

He is the Messiah, Mary's son, God's Messenger, His word conveyed to Mary, and a spirit from Him.

These descriptions mark his distinctive mission and creation.

The verse then commands faith in God and His Messengers and prohibits saying “Three.”

It emphasizes:

إِنَّمَا اللَّهُ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ

innamā Allāhu ilāhun wāḥid

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171

That is, The God is only one deity.

It also denies that God has a child.

Excess here therefore concerns raising Jesus beyond the position the Quran assigns him.

Jesus is God's Messenger, but being a Messenger does not mean sharing divinity.

He is God's word, but that description does not by itself establish that he is God or part of God's essence.

Nor should وَرُوحٌ مِنْهُ be understood without evidence as dividing God's essence and transferring a portion to Jesus.

That interpretation must be measured against the same verse's explicit divine oneness.

Nisa 4:172 also identifies Jesus as God's servant.

The Quran recognizes his position and qualities while rejecting their conversion into divinity.

Miracles and excess

Excess can arise when someone draws from a real quality a conclusion beyond what it establishes.

An exceptional intellectual or practical ability may rightly prompt respect and wonder.

It does not establish that its possessor never errs, knows every truth, or has absolute control over human destinies.

The Quran describes Jesus' distinctive creation and divine signs without making them evidence of his divinity.

Al Imran 3:59 compares Jesus' creation with Adam's.

An unusual human creation therefore does not itself make that human an object of worship.

Prohibiting excess does not mean denying miracles or diminishing the true position of Prophets.

It means retaining the position established by truth and revelation.

Respect and divinity are different

One may love a Prophet, follow him, believe in his mission, and respect his sacrifices.

None of that is inherently excess.

Attributing exclusively divine qualities to a Prophet goes beyond respect and following.

Believing Jesus to be God's Prophet differs from claiming he shares God's divinity.

Likewise, respect for Muhammad and following the revelation he received differ from worshiping him or attributing independent, unlimited knowledge and authority to him.

In Kahf 18:110, Muhammad is commanded to state his humanity and reception of revelation.

Preserving the distinction between Messenger and God therefore matters beyond Jesus alone.

It can inform reflection on all Prophets and religious leaders, though Nisa 4:171 directly addresses the People of the Book and especially claims concerning Jesus.

Excess and claims about “Three”

The verse commands وَلَا تَقُولُوا ثَلَاثَةٌ.

Maidah 5:73 also criticizes a claim involving divinity and “three.”

These verses place God's oneness and incomparability against such beliefs.

Historical Christian formulations should nevertheless not all be assumed identical without examination.

Mainstream Christian theology commonly explains the Trinity through Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, while Maidah 5:116 presents a dialogue about taking Jesus and his mother as deities. Trinitarian Christianity itself professes one God, not three independent gods.

The two descriptions should not be treated as precise explanations of a single formula without historical and theological study.

The Quranic principle is clear: God is one deity, has no child, and no Prophet or creature should be elevated to His exclusive position.

This is the boundary against which excess in Nisa is measured.

4. The prohibition in Maidah

Maidah 5:77

قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ غَيْرَ الْحَقِّ وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا أَهْوَاءَ قَوْمٍ قَدْ ضَلُّوا مِنْ قَبْلُ وَأَضَلُّوا كَثِيرًا وَضَلُّوا عَنْ سَوَاءِ السَّبِيلِ

qul yā ahla al-kitābi lā taghlū fī dīnikum ghayra al-ḥaqqi wa-lā tattabiʿū ahwāʾa qawmin qad ḍallū min qablu wa-aḍallū kathīran wa-ḍallū ʿan sawāʾi al-sabīl

Al-Māʾidah 5:77

“Say: O People of the Book, do not go to excess in your religion contrary to the truth. Do not follow the desires of a people who went astray before, led many astray, and strayed from the even way.”

The same prohibition is repeated.

Two accompanying expressions stand out.

First, غَيْرَ الْحَقِّ.

Second, وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا أَهْوَاءَ قَوْمٍ.

They connect religious excess with departing from truth and following misleading desires and interpretations.

غَيْرَ الْحَقِّ: beyond the truth

The qualifier identifies the wrongfulness of the prohibited excess.

Grammatically, it can be explained as qualifying an omitted verbal noun or as a circumstantial expression.

Either way, the sentence prohibits overstepping truth in religion.

Strong conviction or earnest action alone does not suffice to identify excess.

Someone may defend an oppressed person's rights with great dedication. If that defense remains just and respects others' rights, intensity alone is not excess.

Someone equally ardent might conceal the truth or excuse injustice to defend a beloved leader.

Then the issue is overstepping truth and justice, not merely strong feeling.

Religious excess must be measured against truth and proper limits, rather than enthusiasm, attachment, or effort alone.

Following misleading desires

The verse continues:

وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا أَهْوَاءَ قَوْمٍ

wa-lā tattabiʿū ahwāʾa qawm

Al-Māʾidah 5:77

That is, do not follow a people's desires.

Excess may arise from unexamined adherence to others' interpretations and wishes.

Someone may grow up amid grand claims about a religious leader, group, or tradition.

Accepting them solely from family or social loyalty, without testing them against truth, may perpetuate unsupported beliefs.

Two situations must be distinguished.

Someone may sincerely believe something because clearer knowledge has not yet become available.

Another may persist after encountering clear evidence, merely to protect status, reputation, or interests.

Their knowledge, intentions, and responsibilities differ.

غ ل و alone does not determine intentions; the conduct and claims must be examined against truth.

Being misled and misleading others

Maidah 5:77 does more than describe a group's own error.

It says they strayed before, misled many, and departed from the even way.

Personal error and influence upon others are distinguished.

Someone may hold a mistaken religious view as a personal belief.

An influential person might instead teach it to thousands as God's certain ruling and demand unquestioning acceptance.

The second situation expands the error's social consequences.

Influence or teaching alone does not prove conscious lying. Responsibility relates to knowledge, access to reasons, and engagement with clear evidence.

This does not change the lexical meaning, but clarifies its social implications in context.

Who is addressed?

Both verses address يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ.

Nisa 4:171 then particularly concerns Jesus' position and the claim of “Three.”

The preceding context of Maidah 5:77, including Maidah 5:72, Maidah 5:73, and Maidah 5:75, also concerns Jesus and divine oneness.

The immediate setting therefore involves beliefs among some People of the Book, especially concerning Jesus.

The designation does not justify attributing identical excess to every adherent of earlier scriptures in every age, with equal knowledge.

Al Imran 3:113 expressly says the People of the Book are not all alike.

Criticizing a doctrinal claim differs from attributing it to every individual in a religious community.

5. Excess among related Quranic concepts

Excess concerning Prophets and leaders

A clear Quranic instance is elevating a human beyond the position God has assigned.

A Prophet may guide people and receive revelation; a Messenger is commissioned to convey the message. Neither title makes its bearer divine.

Being a Messenger does not mean being an object of worship or possessing independent, unlimited divine authority.

A group might regard every statement of a beloved leader as certain truth even when it conflicts with clear Quranic verses.

Loyalty to the person then displaces loyalty to truth.

That can be examined as excess because a human receives a position beyond what is justified.

Respect, affection, and following a sound guide should not be labeled excess solely because they are strong.

The criterion is crossing the bounds of truth.

Excess in constructing religious rulings

Excess is not confined to magnifying Prophets or leaders.

Someone may exceed divinely established bounds concerning what is permitted and forbidden.

Maidah 5:87 commands believers not to forbid good things God has permitted, and not to transgress.

Nahl 16:116 prohibits attributing unsupported declarations of permitted and forbidden to God.

Religious severity is therefore not always evidence of piety.

Someone may personally choose not to eat a particular food.

Declaring it forbidden to everyone in God's name without Quranic evidence goes beyond a personal choice.

It constructs a religious restriction not established from God.

That can relate to excess because the claim exceeds its authority and valid evidence.

Not every personal precaution or juristic disagreement is automatically excess. First identify the claimed divine ruling and its grounds.

Excess and the position of knowledge

People may consult someone with expertise to benefit from knowledge and experience.

That relationship is not inherently wrong.

If an expert's or religious leader's words are placed beyond testing against evidence and truth, expertise becomes authority beyond its actual scope.

A medical specialist can assist with diagnosis and treatment; medical expertise does not mean absolute knowledge of every subject.

Likewise, knowledge of Arabic or religious history does not make every interpretation of the Quran infallible.

Knowledge and expertise are valuable but have limits.

The Quran's demand for grounds and avoidance of speaking without knowledge provide criteria for examining claims.

Respecting knowledge differs from making its possessor an untestable absolute authority.

Excess and intensity of worship

Intense or sustained worship is not itself the meaning of excess.

Someone may devote much time to prayer, remembrance, Quran study, or good deeds.

If it respects rights and responsibilities, quantity alone does not establish excess.

Violating others' rights in worship's name, or making an uncommanded practice universally obligatory, presents another issue.

Someone may freely spend part of the night in worship.

Another may compel people to remain awake every night and declare anyone who does not do so outside the religion, without Quranic evidence.

The latter concerns attributing an unsupported obligation to God, rather than merely the quantity of worship.

The criterion is crossing truth and valid limits, not simply doing much.

6. Comparison with related roots

غ ل و and ط غ و: excess and rebellion

The family ط غ و concerns rebellion and exceeding bounds in the Quran.

In that respect, excess and طغيان are close.

The Quranic uses of غلو specifically prohibit religious excess.

طغيان occurs more widely, including human rebellion, power, and transgression.

A ruler may act tyrannically by using power to oppress people.

A religious claim about a Prophet may instead exceed the truth and constitute غلو.

They may coincide, but every instance of excess does not possess every characteristic of rebellion.

غ ل و and ع د و: excess and transgression

اعتداء belongs to ع د و and concerns crossing bounds and violating rights.

Quranic contexts include war, rights, and social conduct.

غلو also concerns exceeding a proper limit.

اعتداء can describe an act against another's rights, while the two verses examined concern religious belief and claims.

Someone may make an excessive claim about a Prophet without physically assaulting anyone.

Another may violate property rights without religious excess being at issue.

Both involve crossing a limit, but their fields differ.

غ ل و and س ر ف: excess and wasteful overstepping

إسراف concerns exceeding an appropriate measure.

Its Quranic uses include consumption, conduct, and other matters.

غلو also concerns exceeding limits.

Its Quranic subject, however, is religion and claims beyond truth.

Wasting water is not inherently religious excess.

Attributing divinity to a leader is excessive belief even without financial waste.

Both families can express excess, but their objects and contexts differ.

غ ل و and ف ر ط: excess and exceeding a measure

إفراط concerns going ahead and exceeding a measure in Arabic.

In familiar later usage it sometimes closely resembles غلو.

That does not make every derivative of the roots synonymous.

The Quran uses ف ر ط in other senses involving sending ahead, neglect, or missed opportunity.

The two Quranic غ ل و occurrences concern exceeding truth in religion.

Each family should be defined through its actual uses.

غ ل و and ش د د: intensity and excess

شدة concerns force, hardness, and firmness.

Not everything intense is excess.

Someone may defend justice earnestly or persevere in a difficult duty.

Intensity alone does not establish overstepping.

Religious excess arises when belief or conduct goes beyond truth.

“Intense,” “earnest,” “firm,” and “excessive” should not be treated as identical everywhere.

غ ل و and غ ل ظ: excess and hardness

غ ل ظ concerns thickness, coarseness, and hardness.

غليظ can describe a firm covenant, severe punishment, or a hard-hearted person in the Quran.

غلو concerns going beyond truth in religion.

Some ethical applications may overlap, but the meanings differ.

Firmness in fulfilling a legitimate commitment need not be excess.

A calm, gentle speaker may make an excessive claim of human divinity. The tone does not remove the claim's excess.

Excess cannot therefore be diagnosed solely by harsh speech or violent conduct.

غ ل و and ع ل و: rising and exceeding a limit

علو concerns height, elevation, and superiority.

Physical uses of غلو also concern rising and increase.

The roots remain independent.

Something may stand high without exceeding its appropriate measure.

An excessive valuation may involve no physical height at all.

Not every rise is excess, and not every excess concerns spatial elevation.

غ ل و and غ ل ي: excess and boiling

The written forms of these roots are close.

Some early lexicons discuss rising and boiling near each other in morphological treatments.

Quranic morphological indexing nevertheless distinguishes them.

تَغْلُوا in Nisa 4:171 and Maidah 5:77 belongs to غ ل و.

يَغْلِي in Dukhan 44:45 and غَلْيِ in Dukhan 44:46 belong to غ ل ي and concern boiling.

لَا تَغْلُوا should not be translated as “do not boil” or “do not seethe.”

Boiling may provide imagery for intensity, but the Quranic prohibition concerns exceeding the bounds of truth in religion.

7. Questions of understanding and translation

Is excess simply intense religious affection?

No.

Someone may deeply love a Prophet without making any false claim about his position.

Someone else may calmly and formally elevate a human to divinity without visible excitement.

Excess is not measured by emotional intensity or loudness.

The Quranic criterion is going beyond truth.

Is every strict religious practice excess?

Not necessarily.

A clear command may require serious effort and restraint.

Its difficulty does not make obedience excessive.

Adding unsupported obligations or forbidding what God has permitted can exceed truth.

The difficulty of a real duty must be distinguished from inventing an unsupported one.

Does excess always mean associating partners with God?

No.

A clear instance in Nisa 4:171 concerns elevating Jesus beyond his true position and the claim of “Three.”

Lexically, excess is broader than that single instance.

Someone may overstep in belief, valuation, speech, or conduct.

Conversely, not every religious error or interpretive difference should be labeled associationism or excess without examination.

Identify the violated boundary and the claim that goes beyond it.

Does excess concern only the People of the Book?

Both Quranic occurrences directly address them.

Translation and analysis should preserve that fact.

Other verses also prohibit false attribution to God and placing humans in His exclusive position.

A broader principle for examining religious beliefs can therefore be drawn from these verses.

That extension must not replace the direct addressees or license unsupported accusations between religious groups.

Is inquiry or questioning excess?

No.

Asking about a verse, examining a report, criticizing a translation, or researching Prophets' qualities is not inherently excessive.

The search for truth can involve difficult questions and competing possibilities.

Turning a possibility into certainty without evidence and attributing it to God is a different matter.

The boundary concerns unsupported overclaiming and crossing truth, not a question's difficulty or an interpretation's novelty.

Does avoiding excess conflict with respect for religious figures?

Respect and affection for Prophets, scholars, and worthy people can accord with faith and ethics.

They must not displace truth or create an independent divine prerogative for those people.

Someone may deeply respect a teacher after years of study.

Correcting a clear error later discovered in the teacher's words is not disrespect.

Knowingly denying truth from attachment to the teacher takes loyalty beyond its proper place.

Excess can thus involve reversing the positions of truth and a beloved person.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Both Quranic instances of تَغْلُوا mean exceeding a proper limit.

لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ — Nisa 4:171

Suggested translation:

“Do not go to excess in your religion.”

A more explanatory rendering is:

“Do not exceed the bounds of truth in your religion.”

لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ غَيْرَ الْحَقِّ — Maidah 5:77

Suggested translation:

“Do not go to excess in your religion contrary to the truth.”

Or, explanatorily:

“Do not exceed the bounds of truth in your religion.”

Persian can naturally retain the familiar term غلو in the basic translation. English can use “go to excess,” with a lexical explanation identifying the proper limits and truth being exceeded.

For non-Quranic uses:

غَلَا: rose, increased, or exceeded a limit.

غُلُوّ: excess or exceeding a measure or limit.

غَلَاء: high or rising prices.

غَالٍ: expensive; in another context, excessive or someone going to excess.

غَالَى: raised a price or quantity, or went to excess.

مُغَالَاة: excessive valuation or intensification.

غَلْوَة: an arrow's range.

غُلَوَاء: the height or intensity of a condition; youthful ardor in its youth-related use.

تَغَالَى: rose, increased, or exceeded a limit.

اِغْتَلَى: moved swiftly or vigorously.

اِغْلَوْلَى: rose or increased in growth and extension.

Translation must identify what is increasing.

High prices, plant growth, an arrow's range, and religious overclaiming cannot all be rendered with one fixed equivalent merely because they share a root.

Conclusion

غ ل و concerns rising, increasing, and exceeding a measure.

Physical uses include plant growth, rising prices, an arrow's range, and intensifying movement or conditions.

Early poetic witnesses, including a reading of Labid's Muʿallaqa and the verse attributed to Tufayl, attest growth and intensity. Variant readings and historical limits must remain visible when assessing antiquity.

The form تَغْلُوا occurs twice in the Quran: Nisa 4:171 and Maidah 5:77.

Both address the People of the Book and prohibit religious excess.

In Nisa 4:171, it concerns false attribution to God and elevating Jesus beyond his Quranic position. The verse affirms his mission and qualities while denying his divinity and God's having a child.

Maidah 5:77 couples excess with غَيْرَ الْحَقِّ and the prohibition on following misguided desires. Unexamined imitation and unsupported claims can exceed truth and mislead others.

Neither verse makes every strong affection, prolonged worship, earnest effort, or interpretive disagreement excessive.

The criterion is crossing truth and attributing an unsupported position, authority, or ruling.

Nor should excess be restricted to associationism, although Nisa 4:171 presents such a clear instance.

The root غ ل و concerns rising and exceeding a measure. Its Quranic uses concern crossing the bounds of truth in religious belief and claims.

Excess can arise when someone elevates a Prophet, leader, belief, or purported divine ruling beyond its true standing.

Respect for a Prophet, fidelity to religion, and earnest action are not inherently excessive while they remain within truth. Excess begins where persons and claims exceed their true position and human assertions displace divine truth and judgment.
