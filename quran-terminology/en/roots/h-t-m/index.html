Qur’anic root Ḥ T M

The certainty of a decree, firmness of a decision, and inevitability of what has been ordained

In Arabic, the root ح ت م concerns making a matter definite, establishing a decree firmly, and ordaining something so that it no longer remains a possibility or an unfinished decision. حَتَمَ الأَمْرَ means to settle a matter firmly and decisively; حَتَمَ عَلَيْهِ emphasizes making it necessary or binding upon someone.

حَتْم can denote a definite decree, a binding matter, or something whose occurrence has been ordained. Within the same family, حَاتِم describes someone who pronounces a decisive judgment, while مَحْتُوم describes what has been settled and ordained.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَتْمًا in Maryam 19:71, in the expression كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا.

The expression states the certainty of the decree mentioned in the verse. Understanding it requires distinguishing the meaning of حَتْم, the meaning of مَقْضِيّ, and the subject of the decree described as certain. Ḥatm itself does not mean entering the Fire, punishment, or deliverance; it emphasizes the certainty of what has been ordained.

1. Forms and derivatives of the root

The root ح ت م consists of the three consonants ḥāʾ, tāʾ, and mīm. It is a sound Arabic root, with no weak, hamzated, or doubled radical.

Its principal Arabic forms include:

  • حَتَمَ ـ يَحْتِمُ (ḥatama–yaḥtimu): pronounced a decisive judgment; ordained and made binding.
  • حَتْم (ḥatm): a definite decree, a binding matter, or something whose occurrence or performance has been ordained.
  • حُتُوم (ḥutūm): plural of ḥatm for definite decrees or matters; also a plural of ḥātim in other uses.
  • حَاتِم (ḥātim): one pronouncing judgment or settling something decisively.
  • مَحْتُوم (maḥtūm): something made definite and ordained, whose occurrence or performance is regarded as necessary.
  • حَتَّمَ ـ يُحَتِّمُ (ḥattama–yuḥattimu): made a matter definite or obligatory.
  • تَحْتِيم (taḥtīm): making a decree definite or an act obligatory.
  • تَحَتَّمَ (taḥattama): became definite and binding; or, in a transitive construction, made something definite.
  • اِنْحَتَمَ (inḥatama): became binding and definite, in recorded Arabic usage.
  • حَتْمِيّ (ḥatmiyy): definite and necessary.
  • حَتْمِيَّة (ḥatmiyyah): certainty or necessity; also a term used in contemporary philosophical and scientific language.

Lexicons also record حَاتِم as a name for a crow regarded in augural beliefs as a sign of separation. One recorded branch also associates حَتَمَة with blackness. These uses require separate examination; without historical evidence, they should not all be derived from the meaning of a definite decree.

Only the verbal noun حَتْمًا occurs in the Qur’an. None of the verbs حَتَمَ, حَتَّمَ, or تَحَتَّمَ, nor the nouns حَاتِم and مَحْتُوم, appears in the Qur’anic text.

In Maryam 19:71, حَتْمًا is the indefinite accusative form of حَتْم. A difference in case does not create a new derivative.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of ḥatm, Maryam 19:71

2. Core meaning and usage in early Arabic

Making a decree definite

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains حَتْم through two related expressions: إِحْكَامُ الْأَمْرِ and الْقَضَاء.

Iḥkām al-amr concerns settling a matter firmly and decisively, so that the decision is neither unstable nor incomplete.

Qaḍāʾ, in one of its uses, concerns judging, decreeing, and bringing a decision to completion.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn Manẓūr cites Ibn Sīdah’s explanation of حَتْم as إِيجَابُ الْقَضَاءِ: making a decree necessary and definite.

The same source explains حَتَمْتُ عَلَيْكَ الشَّيْءَ as “I made the thing binding upon you.”

These uses show that ḥatm does more than report that something might occur. Describing something as ḥatm presents it, in the speaker’s language, as having passed beyond uncertainty and possibility.

A decision’s certainty in the speaker’s words must nevertheless be distinguished from the speaker’s actual power to carry it out.

A human being may declare something definite, yet external circumstances may prevent its realization. The word ḥatm alone does not guarantee that every person pronouncing a decree has unlimited power to implement it.

In the Qur’an, when the certainty of a decree is attributed to God, it is considered within the context of divine power and sovereignty.

Judging and making a judgment definite

In Arabic, someone may judge a matter while implementation of that judgment still depends on other conditions.

حَتْم emphasizes the firmness and binding force of the decree.

For example, حَتَمْتُ عَلَيْهِ الْأَمْرَ does not merely mean that I spoke about a matter; it means that I made it binding upon that person.

In حَتَمَ اللَّهُ الْأَمْرَ, the decree and determination are attributed to God.

Semantically, ḥatm is related to ḥukm and qaḍāʾ, with particular emphasis on certainty and necessity.

This distinction does not mean that qaḍāʾ is always provisional and ḥatm always a subsequent stage. Qaḍāʾ can also refer to a definite decree or something brought to completion.

The words differ in emphasis, rather than necessarily describing a fixed chronological sequence.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: settling firmly, decree, and necessity

Did ح ت م develop from ح ك م?

Ibn Fāris offers an interesting suggestion concerning this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

Unlike his treatment of many roots, for which he identifies an independent semantic foundation, he says that he does not regard ح ت م as independent, and suspects that its tāʾ may have replaced the kāf of ح ك م.

On this account, حَتَمَ is semantically close to حَكَمَ, since both can concern judgment and decreeing.

Ibn Fāris himself, however, presents the relationship as a conjecture, rather than an established fact.

His suggestion therefore does not establish that ح ت م and ح ك م were certainly one original root.

Demonstrating such a relationship requires independent historical and phonological evidence.

What can be stated confidently is that the families are close in some uses: ḥukm concerns judging and determining a ruling, while ḥatm emphasizes its certainty and firmness.

This semantic proximity helps explain the word, but does not establish a historical relationship.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: conjectured substitution of tāʾ and kāf

A poetic witness attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt

The following verse is transmitted in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and the collected poetry attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt:

عِبَادُكَ يُخْطِئُونَ وَأَنْتَ رَبٌّ
بِكَفَّيْكَ الْمَنَايَا وَالْحُتُومُ

ʿibāduka yukhṭiʾūna wa-anta rabbun / bi-kaffayka al-manāyā wa-al-ḥutūmu

Meaning: “Your servants err, while You are Lord; deaths and settled destinies are in Your hands.”

Here الْحُتُوم is the plural of حَتْم.

It accompanies الْمَنَايَا, referring to deaths and the mortal destinies of human beings.

The poet means the decrees and outcomes that lie within the Lord’s control.

In this usage, ḥatm does not concern rope, physical binding, or bodily restraint; it concerns the certainty of destiny and decree.

The witness is lexically useful because it illustrates حُتُوم in Arabic poetry in a context of decree and destiny.

Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt is known as a poet of the pre-Islamic period and the time surrounding Islam’s emergence. The dates and precise attribution of all verses ascribed to him are not certain, however. This verse is therefore a witness attributed to that poetic tradition, rather than conclusive evidence for dating the root’s emergence.

Some transmissions in Lisān al-ʿArab give a different reading of the first hemistich. This variation does not change the meaning of الْحُتُوم in the second.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt

حَاتِم and Arab augural beliefs

Besides a person pronouncing judgment, lexicons record حَاتِم as a name for a black crow.

One early explanation connects this name with some Arabs’ belief that the crow’s call was ominous and signaled separation.

In this use, حَاتِم is a bird believed by those practicing augury to pronounce separation as though it were a certain decree.

A verse attributed to Al-Muraqqish al-Sadūsī reads:

وَلَقَدْ غَدَوْتُ وَكُنْتُ لَا
أَغْدُو عَلَى وَاقٍ وَحَاتِمْ

wa-la-qad ghadawtu wa-kuntu lā / aghdū ʿalā wāqin wa-ḥātim

An approximate rendering is: “I did set out in the morning, though formerly I would not set out in the presence of a wāq or a ḥātim.”

Here وَاق denotes the ṣurad, commonly identified as a shrike, while حَاتِم denotes a crow. Both belong to the context of bird augury and omens of ill fortune; the line does not identify one as a favorable sign and the other as an unfavorable one.

Sources differ over whether the poem belongs to Al-Muraqqish or another poet. Its attribution and exact date therefore require caution.

This usage shows that حَاتِم was not exclusively a title for a human being pronouncing judgment.

The crow’s bearing this name does not mean that the Qur’an endorses bad omens or a bird’s supposed power to determine destiny.

A lexical witness identifies a word’s use and cultural setting; the truth of the associated belief is a separate question.

Ibn Qutaybah, Al-Maʿānī al-Kabīr: wāq, ḥātim, and bird augury

Other usages and caution in etymology

Lisān al-ʿArab also records حَتَمَة for blackness and أَحْتَم for something black.

Some sources use الحُتَامَة for leftovers and fragments of food.

The historical connection between these meanings and حَتْم as a definite decree is unclear.

For the branch concerning food fragments, Ibn Fāris also suggests a relationship with ح ط م and the meaning of breaking and crumbling.

These differences show that placing several forms under one dictionary entry does not always establish that they all developed from a single original meaning.

For understanding Qur’anic حَتْمًا, the meaning of a definite decree or binding matter has more direct and clear support.

Meanings concerning blackness, crows, or food fragments should not enter the translation of the verse.

3. Qur’anic use of the root

Maryam 19:71: a definite and ordained decree

Arabic verse:

وَإِنْ مِنْكُمْ إِلَّا وَارِدُهَا كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا

wa-in minkum illā wāriduhā kāna ʿalā rabbika ḥatman maqḍiyyan

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English:

“There is none of you who will not enter it; this is a binding decree upon your Lord.”

In this verse, حَتْمًا is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The first expression, وَإِنْ مِنْكُمْ إِلَّا وَارِدُهَا, speaks of the occurrence of the arrival or entry.

The following sentence, كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا, emphasizes the certainty of what has been stated.

Ḥatm here is neither the name of a place, nor the name of a punishment, nor the designation of a human group. It concerns the certainty and necessity of the decree reported in the verse.

The grammatical structure of حَتْمًا

حَتْمًا is an indefinite accusative verbal noun.

In one established grammatical analysis, كَانَ is a copular verb; its subject is an implicit pronoun referring to the arrival just mentioned, and حَتْمًا is its predicate.

مَقْضِيًّا is an adjective modifying حَتْمًا.

The two words thus form one expression: حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا.

It describes something presented both as definite and necessary, and as having been decreed.

The verbal noun حَتْم functions here as a nominal predicate; it need not be translated as a separate new verb.

Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān wa-Bayānuh: ḥatman as the predicate of kāna and maqḍiyyan as its adjective

The difference between حَتْمًا and مَقْضِيًّا

حَتْمًا comes from ح ت م and emphasizes the certainty and necessity of the matter.

مَقْضِيًّا is a passive participle from ق ض ي and describes something concerning which judgment has been given or a decree issued.

The words are close in meaning but differ in form and emphasis.

Ḥatm highlights the decree’s certainty.

Maqḍiyy emphasizes its having been ordained and decided.

Together, they show that the subject of the verse is not presented as an open possibility or an unspecified promise.

The expression should nevertheless not be turned into a mandatory chronological process, as though something first becomes maqḍiyy and only later becomes ḥatm.

The verse does not state such a sequence.

The two words emphasize the certainty and ordained character of the same matter in one expression.

عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ: has something been imposed on God?

In كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا, the preposition عَلَىٰ occurs with رَبِّكَ.

In Arabic, this construction can concern something binding or ordained.

It does not follow that a power external to God has imposed a decree upon Him.

In the context of this sentence, God is the one possessing judgment and decree.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an also speaks of things God has prescribed for Himself. For example, Al-Anʿām 6:12 and Al-Anʿām 6:54 speak of His prescribing mercy for Himself.

These constructions show that عَلَى does not necessarily mean compulsion by a higher person or power.

Maryam 19:71 concerns the certainty of a matter in relation to the Lord’s decree.

The appropriate sense is therefore that the matter is definite and ordained by God’s decree, rather than that God has been compelled by an external will.

كَانَ and the certainty of the decree

The verb كَانَ here does not mean that the decree was definite in the past but is no longer valid.

Depending on structure and context, kāna in Arabic can express an established state or an enduring description.

Here the sentence concerns something whose occurrence is presented as certain.

The translation should therefore not suggest that the decree existed only at a past time and subsequently ended.

Ḥatm itself does not specify the event’s exact time of realization either.

The certainty that something will occur differs from knowing when and exactly how it will occur.

This matters for understanding the verse: حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا states the certainty of the decree without explaining every detail of its implementation.

The relationship between ḥatm and وَارِدُهَا

What is presented as certain in Maryam 19:71 concerns the expression وَارِدُهَا.

وَارِد belongs to و ر د and must be examined through that family’s usages.

Lexical and exegetical sources differ over wurūd in this verse. Some understand it as entering the Fire; others emphasize reaching it, being present there, or passing over it.

حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا does not by itself settle this disagreement.

The expression shows that what is called wurūd is certain and ordained. Its exact manner must be examined through و ر د itself, the surah’s context, and other relevant verses.

The preceding verses describe groups being gathered and brought around Hell. The next verse, Maryam 19:72, describes the deliverance of those mindful of God and the leaving of the wrongdoers there.

The certainty of wurūd therefore does not establish that everyone’s final outcome is identical.

The certainty of an event differs from its having the same outcome for every individual.

Ḥatm here emphasizes the occurrence of what has been ordained, while differences between people’s outcomes emerge from the continuing context.

Al-Ṭabarī: ordained decree and disagreement over wurūd

Does ḥatm mean remaining forever in the Fire?

No. The root ح ت م itself has no such meaning.

Ḥatm expresses the certainty of a decree or of a matter’s occurrence.

Questions about duration, length of time, or the ending of a state must be answered through the relevant words for time, continuance, and outcome, and through the verses’ context.

For example, the inevitability of an encounter does not by itself establish that, after reaching a place, someone will remain there forever.

Likewise, the certainty of a decree and the manner of its implementation are two separate questions.

حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا therefore cannot by itself establish that all human beings remain in Hell permanently or share an identical condition.

The expression makes certain only what the verse presents as ordained.

4. Differences from related roots

ح ت م (ḥatm) and ق ض ي (qaḍāʾ): certainty and an ordained decree

Study of Q Ḍ Y

قَضَى has a broad Qur’anic range, including judging, ordaining, commanding, and bringing a matter to completion.

حَتْم emphasizes the firmness and binding character of the decree.

Both families occur together in Maryam 19:71.

This combination shows their semantic proximity; it does not itself prove that every decree always has two distinct stages.

Here both words emphasize the certainty and ordained character of the same matter.

ح ت م (ḥatm) and ح ك م (ḥukm): making definite and judging

Study of Ḥ K M

حَكَمَ concerns judging and pronouncing a ruling.

حَتَمَ can also belong to the field of judgment and decree, with emphasis on certainty.

Ibn Fāris suggests a possible historical relationship between the roots through the substitution of a consonant.

That possibility is not conclusive proof of a historical relationship.

In translating the Qur’an, every use of ḥukm should not be converted into ḥatm. A verse may concern judgment itself, governance, or a ruling, without the particular emphasis on certainty.

ح ت م (ḥatm) and ك ت ب (kataba): a definite matter and what has been prescribed or written

Study of K T B

كَتَبَ in its primary sense concerns writing and recording.

In some Qur’anic contexts, this verb also concerns prescribing and making binding.

For example, كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمْ concerns a prescribed duty or ruling in the passages about fasting, Al-Baqarah 2:183, and proportionate retribution, Al-Baqarah 2:178.

Ḥatm also belongs to the field of binding matters, but does not necessarily contain an image of writing and recording.

The families therefore overlap in the sense of being ordained, while differing in imagery and range of use.

ح ت م (ḥatm) and و ج ب (wajaba): certainty and necessity

Some Arabic uses of وَجَبَ concern becoming established or necessary.

From this family, wājib is used in legal and religious language for an act whose performance is obligatory.

Ḥatm can likewise denote something binding and definite.

The technical legal term wājib should not replace the meaning of every use of ḥatm without regard to its subject.

Maryam 19:71 concerns an ordained event, rather than a command that a person must perform as an act of worship.

Ḥatm here therefore emphasizes the inevitability of what has been decreed.

ح ت م (ḥatm) and ق د ر (qadar): certainty and determination of measure

Study of Q D R

Qadar and its derivatives can concern measure, determining quantity, and the ordaining of matters.

Ḥatm emphasizes certainty, but does not itself specify measure, quantity, or the exact time of occurrence.

Something may be decisively decreed without the same sentence giving the details of its extent or manner of realization.

The overlap between determination and ḥatm therefore does not make them interchangeable throughout their Qur’anic uses.

ح ت م (ḥatm) and ح ق ق (ḥaqq): a definite decree and established truth

Study of Ḥ Q Q

In the Qur’an, ḥaqq concerns truth, rightness, entitlement, and established reality.

Ḥatm is also related to establishment through its sense of certainty.

A human being’s declaring something definite does not, by itself, establish its actual truth.

Someone may pronounce a decisive judgment while being mistaken in that judgment.

When the Qur’an presents something as certain by God’s decree, however, the statement belongs to the context of divine report.

A decree’s claimed certainty must therefore be distinguished from its truth as assessed through its source and evidence.

ح ت م (ḥatm) and ح ط م (ḥaṭm): a definite decree and crumbling

Study of Ḥ Ṭ M

ح ط م concerns breaking, crushing, and smashing.

The Qur’anic word الْحُطَمَة belongs to this family.

This root is distinct from ح ت م.

Some dictionaries also record حُتَامَة for food fragments, but its historical relationship with ح ط م is disputed.

Similarity of words therefore does not make حَتْمًا in Maryam 19:71 mean crushing, breaking, or destruction.

Its meaning in the verse concerns the certainty of the decree.

5. Interpretive challenges and semantic development

Modern Persian “hatman” and Qur’anic حَتْمًا

In contemporary Persian, “hatman” usually functions as an adverb of emphasis, as in “I will definitely come” or “Be sure to do this.”

This use is related to certainty and necessity.

In Maryam 19:71, however, حَتْمًا is more than an independent emphatic adverb: it is an accusative verbal noun with a nominal role in كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا.

Translating the sentence as “it had certainly been ordained” may convey the general idea, but does not fully display the relationship between حَتْمًا and مَقْضِيًّا.

“A definite and ordained decree,” or “a matter whose occurrence has been decisively decreed,” makes the semantic structure clearer.

Does “inevitable” mean natural necessity?

In contemporary philosophy and science, “determinism” sometimes appears in discussions of causal necessity and determination by natural laws.

For example, some philosophical theories discuss causal determinism: events being necessarily determined by preceding conditions and causes.

Qur’anic حَتْمًا does not name a philosophical theory concerning all causes and events in the universe.

The verse speaks of a definite and ordained decree.

This word alone does not establish that all human actions are compelled, that free choice does not exist, or that every detail of every natural event is predetermined in a particular way.

Such broad questions require independent examination of the verses and philosophical evidence.

Can a definite decree change?

In its established sense, ḥatm emphasizes certainty and necessity.

Something properly described as ḥatm is therefore no longer presented as an open possibility within the same relation and conditions.

This meaning does not yield one uniform rule for all Qur’anic promises, warnings, and decrees.

Some Qur’anic statements have conditions, and realization of their outcome depends on fulfillment of those conditions.

In some cases, God speaks of repentance, reform, and changes in human circumstances.

It is therefore necessary first to establish what has been declared certain, under what conditions, by whom, and concerning which audience.

For a conditional decree, certainty of the relationship between condition and outcome does not mean that the condition itself actually occurs.

Maryam 19:71 reports something’s occurrence as certain, while its manner and outcome must be examined in context.

Is حَاتِم always the name of a generous person?

In Persian, the name Ḥātim is commonly associated with Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī and his generosity.

Lexically, however, حَاتِم is the active participle of حَتَمَ, referring to someone who judges or settles a matter decisively.

Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī’s reputation for generosity is a historical and literary characteristic of a person bearing that name, rather than the meaning of ح ت م.

Ḥatm in the Qur’an therefore does not mean generosity, giving, or liberality.

This example shows that the fame of a personal name should not change the lexical meaning of its family.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For حَتْمًا in Maryam 19:71, “a definite decree,” “an inevitable matter,” and “ordained certainty” are semantically suitable possibilities.

In the complete expression حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا, however, the meaning of مَقْضِيًّا should also be retained.

The proposed explanatory rendering of this expression is:

“A definite and ordained decree.”

Or, more expansively:

“A matter whose occurrence has been made certain by the Lord’s decree.”

For the verse’s direct wording, the existing Persian Forqan translation is suitable; rendered in English here, it reads:

“There is none of you who will not enter it; this is a binding decree upon your Lord.”

This translation retains the general meaning of the decree’s certainty. The lexical explanation can add that مَقْضِيًّا also emphasizes that the same decree has been issued and ordained.

For non-Qur’anic derivatives, حَتَمَ can be rendered “made definite and ordained”; حَاتِم, “one pronouncing judgment”; مَحْتُوم, “definite and ordained”; and تَحْتِيم, “making binding and definite.”

Ḥatm should not always be translated as a religious obligation: something certain may be a report about an event’s occurrence, rather than a command to perform an act.

Forqan’s lexical box can present the two distinct fields as follows:

Brief root meaning: “Making a decree definite and binding; settling a matter firmly and ordaining its occurrence.”

Meaning in this verse: “The certainty of the Lord’s decree concerning the arrival just mentioned; an ordained and inevitable matter.”

This distinction keeps the root meaning clear without incorporating questions about the manner of arrival or people’s final outcomes into the definition of ḥatm.

7. Conclusion

In Arabic, ح ت م concerns making a decree firm and definite, and making a matter binding.

حَتَمَ can concern judging and making definite; حَتْم, a binding and ordained matter; and مَحْتُوم, something whose certainty has been established.

Lexicons explain this family through qaḍāʾ, iḥkām, and ījāb: decreeing, settling firmly, and making necessary.

Poetry attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt likewise uses حُتُوم for definite decrees and destinies.

Ibn Fāris suggests a possible historical connection between ح ت م and ح ك م, but the relationship remains a lexical proposal and should not be presented as certain.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَتْمًا in Maryam 19:71.

In حَتْمًا مَقْضِيًّا, the decree’s certainty and ordained character occur together.

The expression emphasizes that the matter mentioned is not an unknown possibility, but a decree whose occurrence is presented as certain.

Nevertheless, the certainty of an event’s occurrence differs from its manner of occurrence and its outcome.

In Maryam 19:71, ḥatm states the certainty of the arrival mentioned. The root alone does not specify the kind of arrival, its duration, the manner of experiencing benefit or punishment, or the eventual outcome for every person.

These questions must be examined through the meaning of wurūd, the verse’s structure, and its continuing context.

The clear and defensible Qur’anic meaning of ح ت م is therefore the certainty and inevitability of an ordained decree.

Understanding this root helps distinguish a definite decree from possibility, condition, command, and an event’s actual realization, without constructing a broad and unsupported theory of compulsion, destiny, or the nature of the hereafter from one limited word.
