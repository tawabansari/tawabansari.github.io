Gentle rain, moisture, and freshness: from drizzle on the ground to a fruitful garden and the fruits of giving

The Arabic family ط ل ل has several branches: very fine rain, surviving traces of old buildings or settlements, and, elsewhere, unredressed bloodshed or loss of a right. Dictionaries group them together, but a single historical origin cannot be asserted without evidence.

In the precipitation branch, طَلّ names gentle rain with small droplets that moisten ground and plants. Some usages approach نَدى, dew or delicate moisture settling on surfaces.

The Quran uses only the noun طَلّ from this family, in Al-Baqarah 2:265, contrasted with وَابِل, heavy, abundant rain.

The verse describes a garden on elevated ground producing twice its yield with heavy rain; if heavy rain does not reach it, light rain is mentioned instead.

The passage concerns giving. Al-Baqarah 2:264 compares giving undermined by display, reminders, and hurt to soil on smooth rock washed away by heavy rain. Al-Baqarah 2:265 then describes rain reaching a productive garden.

The parable concerns both the amount of rain and the condition of the ground receiving it. Heavy rain can wash soil off rock but nourish a garden with a suitable setting; even light rain can benefit it.

Giving, sincerity, blessing, and ethical growth are contextual themes, not definitions of طَلّ. The word itself names fine, gentle rain in this usage.

1. Historical meaning in early Arabic

طَلّ: fine, gentle rain

Early dictionaries describe small droplets and rainfall lighter than ordinary or heavy rain.

One definition mentions small, continuous drops; another calls it the lightest and weakest rain.

Low intensity and fine droplets are prominent.

Imagine tiny droplets settling on leaves and soil instead of large, forceful drops.

The fall may appear slight while still moistening surfaces.

The word does not necessarily mean prolonged rain. Some old definitions mention continuity, but no fixed duration applies to every usage.

For the Quranic context, its gentleness relative to وابل is especially relevant.

Fine rain and dew

Some Arabic accounts bring طَلّ and نَدى close together.

نَدى commonly refers to moisture settling on plants and objects.

In modern meteorological terms, dew generally forms as surfaces cool and atmospheric water vapor condenses. Its mechanism differs from rain falling from clouds.

Older names for slight moisture need not align precisely with modern meteorological categories.

Thus طَلّ is variously explained as very light rain, dew-like moisture, or something between dew and rain.

Its contrast with وابل in Al-Baqarah 2:265 favors fine, gentle rain in this study.

This contextual preference does not deny other recorded Arabic usages.

Arabic examples

طُلَّتِ الْأَرْضُ describes ground reached by light rain or moisture.

أَرْضٌ طَلَّةٌ describes moist ground.

مَطْلُولَة likewise describes ground moistened by fine rain or dampness.

In طَلَّهَا النَّدَى, dew or moisture is the agent moistening it.

These usages connect the word with moisture reaching soil and plants.

They do not inherently mean fertility or fruiting. Moisture can support growth, but production depends on other conditions too.

Moist ground may yield nothing without seed, suitable soil, or other necessary conditions.

The definition should not be rain that invariably makes plants grow.

A rajaz witness

A short line transmitted in dictionaries reads:

مِثْلُ النَّقَا لَبَّدَهُ ضَرْبُ الطَّلَلْ

mithlu al-naqā labbadahu ḍarbu al-ṭalal

“Like a mound of sand compacted by the impact of fine rain.”

النَّقَا refers to a mound or heap of sand.

لَبَّدَهُ belongs to ل ب د and here concerns settling and compacting its parts.

ضَرْبُ الطَّلَلْ describes fine rain striking the sand.

Lexicographers explain الطَّلَلْ as an uncontracted poetic form of الطَّلّ; another transmitted vocalization is explained as a shortened plural.

The line illustrates fine rain in a description of moisture meeting the ground.

Its speaker and exact date were not independently established here, so it should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic.

Dictionaries establish the fine-rain sense, and the Quran directly attests it in the language of revelation. Earlier chronology requires additional independently datable evidence.

The precipitation branch's semantic core

The recorded usages support this definition:

Ṭall is light precipitation or delicate moisture reaching soil and plants in small droplets; in the Quranic passage, fine rain contrasted with heavy rain.

This identifies a kind of precipitation, not an assured result.

Growth, soil fertility, and multiplication of yield are not contained in the word alone.

2. Other branches of ط ل ل

Other semantic branches appear under the same dictionary entry.

They matter for a broad study but should not all be reduced to fine rain.

طَلَل: surviving remains of dwellings

طَلَل describes traces of houses and settlements, especially remains still projecting above the ground.

Its plurals include أَطْلَال and طُلُول.

Standing before deserted dwelling places is a familiar image in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry.

The poet returns to a former settlement where only structures or traces of habitation remain.

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

أَلَا عِمْ صَبَاحًا أَيُّهَا الطَّلَلُ الْبَالِي
وَهَلْ يَعْمَنُ مَنْ كَانَ فِي الْعَصْرِ الْخَالِي

alā ʿim ṣabāḥan ayyuhā al-ṭalalu al-bālī / wa-hal yaʿmanu man kāna fī al-ʿaṣri al-khālī

“Good morning, worn remains of the dwelling! But can one belonging to a bygone age enjoy well-being?”

الطَّلَلُ الْبَالِي identifies the weathered remains of a dwelling.

بالي describes their age and deterioration.

طلل therefore does not itself mean the act of destruction: it names a remaining trace or structure, possibly worn or abandoned.

Some lexical explanations distinguish projecting remains, طلل, from رسم, traces lying close to ground level.

This distinction is useful in some usages but should not become an absolute rule for all poetry.

The witness illustrates a usage attributed to the pre-Quranic period, distinct from طَلّ as fine rain.

أَطَلَّ: overlooking and appearing above

أَطَلَّ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ means overlooking something or looking down on it.

تَطَالَّ can describe stretching the neck or raising oneself to see.

This branch concerns projection and becoming visible.

A semantic connection with visible building remains is understandable: both concern something standing out above its surroundings.

That relationship does not establish a definitive historical derivation for every form.

Nor should overlooking be equated with fine rain.

Later uses of these forms in other languages cannot independently determine the Quranic noun's meaning.

طُلَّ دَمُهُ: unredressed bloodshed

Another branch concerns bloodshed left without redress or compensation.

طُلَّ دَمُهُ describes bloodshed whose associated claim has gone unpursued or uncompensated.

دَمٌ مَطْلُولٌ likewise describes bloodshed left unredressed.

طَلَّ غَرِيمَهُ is recorded for delaying payment to a creditor; some explanations connect it with seeking to defeat another person's claim.

This concerns justice and lost rights, but differs from light rain.

Sharing a root does not make rain inherently a loss of rights or establish that unredressed bloodshed historically derives from moisture.

These social and legal usages do not belong in the interpretation of the rain noun in Al-Baqarah 2:265.

طُلّ: a small quantity

Some expressions use طُلّ for even a little milk.

مَا بِالنَّاقَةِ طُلٌّ means that the she-camel has no milk, not even a little.

Small quantity is foregrounded here.

This can be related semantically to slight rainfall.

It does not make smallness the origin of every branch: visible remains, overlooking, and unredressed bloodshed do not all fit that definition.

Several branches rather than one invented image

Older root analysis distinguishes three principal fields: freshness or moisture, projection or overlooking, and loss or invalidation.

Even early lexicographers therefore did not readily reduce every usage to one image.

The principal branches should remain distinct.

The Quran uses the precipitation and moisture branch.

Early Arabic also has visible dwelling remains and overlooking.

Social and legal usages include unredressed bloodshed or a lost claim.

Their presence in Arabic does not imply their presence in the Quran.

3. فَطَلٌّ: the sole Quranic occurrence

Al-Baqarah 2:265 states:

وَمَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمُ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ وَتَثْبِيتًا مِنْ أَنْفُسِهِمْ كَمَثَلِ جَنَّةٍ بِرَبْوَةٍ أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌ فَآتَتْ أُكُلَهَا ضِعْفَيْنِ فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

wa-mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahumu ibtighāʾa marḍāti allāhi wa-tathbītan min anfusihim ka-mathali jannatin bi-rabwatin aṣābahā wābilun fa-ātat ukulahā ḍiʿfayni fa-in lam yuṣibhā wābilun fa-ṭall wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr

Al-Baqarah 2:265

“Those who spend their wealth seeking the approval of The God and strengthening themselves from within are like a garden on elevated ground: heavy rain reaches it, and it gives twice its yield. If heavy rain does not reach it, then light rain; and The God sees what you do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:265

فَطَلٌّ combines فَـ and the noun طَلٌّ.

The noun is indefinite and nominative, referring to fine, gentle rain.

It contrasts with وَابِلٌ, heavy and abundant rain.

Rainfall intensity is the central contrast here.

وابل is heavier; طل is lighter and gentler.

The short conditional construction فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ contains ellipsis.

Its sense can be expanded: if heavy rain does not reach the garden, light rain reaches it or is available to it.

Arabic commonly omits material understood from what precedes.

Then light rain leaves the relation of reaching the garden to be supplied from the earlier clause.

Ṭall does not mean twice the yield

The verse first mentions heavy rain:

أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌ فَآتَتْ أُكُلَهَا ضِعْفَيْنِ

aṣābahā wābilun fa-ātat ukulahā ḍiʿfayn

Al-Baqarah 2:265

“Heavy rain reached it, and it gave twice its yield.”

It then says:

فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ

fa-in lam yuṣibhā wābilun fa-ṭall

Al-Baqarah 2:265

“If heavy rain does not reach it, then light rain.”

The distinction matters.

Double yield is explicitly mentioned in the first clause.

The second mentions fine rain without independently specifying the yield again.

The word must not be redefined as rain that necessarily doubles production.

Context presents light rain as beneficial to the garden's fruitfulness.

Its exact yield under light rain cannot be derived from طل itself.

This keeps the parable's outcome distinct from the lexical definition.

The garden's setting

The garden is introduced as جَنَّةٍ بِرَبْوَةٍ.

جنة here describes a garden with vegetation.

ربوة names elevated ground.

The picture is therefore a garden on a rise.

That noun alone does not establish that all high ground is more fertile than low ground or produces maximum yields with minimal rain.

The parable describes a particular fruitful garden, not a universal agricultural rule.

It yields abundantly with heavy rain and benefits from lighter rain in its absence.

Rainfall and growing conditions work together.

Rain is not the sole cause of growth, just as land without water and other necessary conditions cannot guarantee it.

4. Heavy and light rain in two adjacent parables of giving

Al-Baqarah 2:264 and Al-Baqarah 2:265 use rain in contrasting pictures.

The first has heavy rain falling on soil lying over smooth rock.

The rain washes the soil away, leaving bare stone.

It illustrates giving deprived of spiritual value through reminders, hurt, and display.

The next verse uses the same word وابل for rain reaching a productive garden.

Here heavy rain accompanies double yield.

One natural phenomenon produces different outcomes in different settings.

Rock and garden

The smooth rock in the first parable cannot retain its surface soil and support plants in the depicted conditions.

The second garden has a setting in which rain supports production.

The contrast relates to the giver's intention and conduct.

A person may spend much to impress others or assert superiority, then remind the recipient of the gift or humiliate them.

Their own conduct damages the gift's spiritual value.

Someone giving for divine approval with inner resolve acts from a different foundation.

Such giving has the capacity to grow and bear fruit.

This is a contextual implication of the contrasting parables, not a definition of طل.

Does slight rain mean a small donation?

A reflection may relate differing amounts of rain to differing resources or donations.

One person may have considerable wealth and meet large needs.

Another may have little yet use it with a sound intention and in an appropriate place.

One might fund an educational center while another buys books and supplies for one disadvantaged child.

Both may help despite their different scale.

That application should not be treated as the certain lexical meaning of وابل and طل.

The literal picture is rain nourishing a garden; reflection may draw attention to suitable conditions, sincerity, and fruitful giving.

The amount given and its value are related but distinct.

A large sum with a wrong intention is not necessarily spiritually superior; a small sum is not automatically good or accepted merely because it is small.

Intention, use of funds, respect for others' rights, and consequences matter too.

This distinction accords with Forqan's discussions of giving without reminders or hurt.

Low intensity does not mean no effect

طل is lighter rainfall than وابل.

Lighter does not mean useless.

Under suitable conditions, fine rain can moisten soil and support continued growth.

The image distinguishes an input's amount from its effectiveness.

A small contribution in a suitable setting may produce a valuable result.

A strong input in unsuitable conditions may fail to produce the desired result.

This does not establish that light rain always suffices for every plant or that every garden can reach maximum yield without substantial rain.

The Quran offers a garden parable, not water requirements for all plants.

5. Distinguishing related roots

ط ل ل and و ب ل: gentle and heavy rain

وابل describes heavy, abundant rainfall.

طل concerns fine, light precipitation.

Al-Baqarah 2:265 places them in direct contrast.

The difference in intensity is clear there.

It does not make heavy rain invariably harmful or light rain invariably beneficial.

Heavy rain washes soil off rock in Al-Baqarah 2:264 but supports increased garden yield in Al-Baqarah 2:265.

Context and the receiving ground determine its effect too.

ط ل ل and ر ذ ذ: fine rain and drizzle

رذاذ concerns very small raindrops.

It consequently overlaps with طل.

Some old lexical classifications distinguish these and heavier rain.

The classifications are not uniform across sources.

Without context, they cannot be treated as meteorological categories with universally fixed boundaries in Arabic.

رذاذ does not occur in the Quran; طل appears once, in Al-Baqarah 2:265.

ط ل ل and ن د ي: fine rain and moisture

نَدى concerns moisture and dampness; this usage is recorded under ن د ي in dictionaries.

Lexical accounts sometimes bring طل and ندى close together.

Modern explanations distinguish dew and drizzle by how moisture forms and reaches surfaces.

The contrast with وابل favors fine rain in this Quranic passage.

That preference does not erase the recorded dew sense.

ط ل ل and م ط ر: light precipitation and rain

مطر is a broad word for rain.

طل here identifies a low-intensity type.

The former can name the general phenomenon and the latter a particular form of it.

Quranic uses of مطر include contexts of punishment and destruction.

Those contexts do not make punishment the root's invariable meaning.

Nor does طل inherently mean merciful rain or divine reward.

Context establishes the rain's value and outcome.

ط ل ل and غ ي ث: rain and relief

غيث concerns rain arriving in need and bringing relief.

طل primarily identifies fine, low-intensity precipitation.

Fine rain may benefit plants, but the two words do not necessarily mean the same thing.

Heavy rain can likewise benefit thirsty land.

No moral boundary makes one family invariably merciful and the other dangerous.

ط ل ل and ر س م: visible remains and surviving traces

طلل and رسم overlap in descriptions of old settlements.

طلل can describe visible, projecting structural remains, while رسم names surviving traces.

They overlap in early poetry without being universally interchangeable.

This branch does not belong to the rain sense in Al-Baqarah 2:265; ruin imagery should not be imported into that verse.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Fine rain must remain distinct from the family's other branches.

طَلّ, in the rainfall sense: fine rain, gentle rain, or drizzle.

طَلّ, in some moisture usages: dew or delicate moisture.

طُلَّتِ الْأَرْضُ: the ground was moistened by light rain or dampness.

أَرْضٌ مَطْلُولَة: moist or dampened ground.

طَلَل: surviving dwelling or building remains, especially visible portions.

أَطْلَال: remains of houses or old settlements.

أَطَلَّ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ: overlooked something or looked down on it.

تَطَالَّ: stretched the neck or raised oneself to see.

دَمٌ مَطْلُولٌ: bloodshed left without redress.

These are recorded Arabic forms, but only the noun طَلّ occurs in the Quran.

Rendering Al-Baqarah 2:265

For the phrase:

فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ

fa-in lam yuṣibhā wābilun fa-ṭall

Al-Baqarah 2:265

A rendering close to the Arabic structure is:

“If heavy rain does not reach it, then light rain.”

A more explicit rendering is:

“If heavy rain does not reach it, fine rain reaches it.”

Reaches it in the second version supplies what is understood from the preceding clause.

Drizzle is also defensible because it expresses small droplets and gentle precipitation.

Dew is recorded lexically but is not this study's first choice for the base translation, given the contrast with heavy rain.

فطل should not be translated as then its yield doubles. The second clause does not independently state that result, and it is not the noun's meaning.

The reflection can explain how a well-situated garden benefits from slight rainfall.

That explanation should not replace direct translation.

7. Summary

ط ل ل is a family with several branches.

طَلّ in its precipitation branch names fine rain or delicate moisture dampening soil and plants, sometimes approaching dew in lexical accounts.

طَلَل elsewhere names dwelling remains, while أَطَلَّ concerns overlooking or appearing above.

طُلَّ دَمُهُ describes unredressed bloodshed.

A common dictionary family does not prove one original historical image behind them all.

The Quran uses only طَلّ, understood here as fine, gentle rain.

Al-Baqarah 2:265 contrasts it with heavy and abundant وابل.

A garden on elevated ground gives twice its yield with heavy rain, while fine rain is mentioned if heavy rain does not reach it.

The picture follows the preceding verse's smooth-rock parable within the discussion of giving.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 has heavy rain wash away the rock's surface soil, exposing its unproductive setting.

Al-Baqarah 2:265 has the same kind of rain reach a fruitful garden; even lighter precipitation can benefit it.

The difference is not merely rainfall amount: the receiving ground and its capacity to benefit also matter.

Reflection can relate this to giving spoiled by display, reminders, and hurt, as opposed to giving for divine approval with inner resolve.

That implication must remain distinct from the lexical definition.

Fine rain does not mean every small donation is automatically accepted or every large gift less valuable. Amount, intention, use, and conduct require contextual examination.

In the Quranic usage studied here, طَلّ is very fine, gentle rain arriving in small droplets at low intensity; other Arabic usages also describe dew-like moisture.

This preserves its contrast with وابل without equating the noun with fertility, mercy, giving, or reward.

The fruitful garden illustrates that effects depend both on the input's intensity and on the conditions receiving it.

Fine rain may be slight without being ineffective; abundant rain alone cannot guarantee good fruit without suitable conditions.
