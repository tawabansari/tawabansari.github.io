The Qur’anic Root K B B (ك ب ب)

Overturning, casting face down, and bending toward something

Qur’anic forms

فَكُبَّتْ
مُكِبًّا

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In early Arabic, ك ب ب concerns overturning something, casting a person face down, and bending toward something. Another branch concerns gathering and accumulation.

Established classical expressions include:

  • كَبَّ الْإِنَاءَ: he overturned the vessel.
  • كَبَّهُ عَلَى وَجْهِهِ: he cast him onto his face.
  • أَكَبَّ عَلَى وَجْهِهِ: he fell face down.
  • أَكَبَّ عَلَى عَمَلِهِ: he turned to his work and occupied himself with it.

A verse attributed to Al-Khansa’, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

يَكُبُّونَ الْعِشَارَ لِمَنْ أَتَاهُمْ إِذَا لَمْ تُحْسِبِ الْمِائَةُ الْوَلِيدَا

They cast down pregnant she-camels to slaughter for their visitors, when even the milk of a hundred camels would not suffice a child.

عِشَار denotes she-camels ten months pregnant. This image praises people who sacrificed valuable animals to host guests even in times of scarcity.

يَكُبُّونَ describes casting down the camels in the process of slaughtering them.

A line attributed to Labid ibn Rabiʿah, another poet spanning the two periods, reads:

جُنُوحَ الْهَالِكِيِّ عَلَى يَدَيْهِ مُكِبًّا يَجْتَلِي نُقَبَ النِّصَالِ

Like a metalworker leaning over his hands, bent down as he polishes rust from blades.

مُكِبًّا describes someone bending over and attending to his work. He need not have fallen to the ground.

These examples illustrate casting down and bending over something in early Arabic poetry. Their precise dates are not independently established, but their meanings agree with classical lexical records and Qur’anic usage.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَبَّ: he overturned or cast down.
  • يَكُبُّ: he overturns or casts down.
  • كَبّ: overturning or casting face down.
  • أَكَبَّ: he fell face down, bent over something, or applied himself to a task.
  • مُكِبّ: someone face down or bending over something.
  • مَكْبُوب: someone or something cast down or overturned.
  • اِنْكَبَّ: he or it overturned, fell down, or bent over something.
  • كُبَّة: a gathered mass, such as a ball of thread or wool; also a group of people.

Forms examined in the Qur’an:

فَكُبَّتْ ـ مُكِبًّا

فَكُبْكِبُوا belongs to the quadriliteral family ك ب ك ب. It is semantically related but is examined separately in morphology.

3. Semantic core and physical image

A clear physical example is overturning a vessel from its ordinary position. Its opening turns downward, and its contents may spill.

The same image applies to a person: one cast face down loses an upright, balanced position, with the face directed toward the ground.

Yet the family is not limited to falling completely. Someone bending over something, such as a metalworker over his tools, can also be called مُكِبّ.

This range makes أَكَبَّ عَلَى عَمَلِهِ, turning to and occupying oneself with work, understandable.

Arabic lexicography also records gathering and concentration. كُبَّة can name a ball of gathered threads or a group of people. Ibn Faris identifies gathering as the family’s shared foundation.

Gathering alone, however, does not explain every use. Both Qur’anic occurrences emphasize a face-down position, overturning, and the loss of an upright stance.

4. Qur’anic uses

فَكُبَّتْ: being cast face down into the Fire

كُبَّتْ is the passive perfect of كَبَّ. وُجُوهُهُمْ is its passive subject, and فِي specifies the destination.

The verb is feminine singular, agreeing with the broken plural وُجُوه.

The verse begins with the singular construction مَنْ جَاءَ, but the following plural pronoun هُمْ refers to the general sense of مَنْ and embraces everyone covered by the description.

كُبَّتْ vividly conveys how they are thrown: the description is not merely entry into the Fire, but being overturned and cast face down into it.

The preceding verse is also relevant:

One side of this contrast offers security and a good reward; the other depicts being cast headlong into the Fire.

The final question in Al-Naml 27:90 connects this outcome to human deeds. Recompense and responsibility emerge from the sentence as a whole, not from the lexical meaning of كُبَّتْ alone.

مُكِبًّا: walking face down

مُكِبًّا is the active participle of Form IV أَكَبَّ. It functions as a circumstantial accusative describing how the person walks.

Its contrast is سَوِيًّا, from س و ي, describing a balanced, upright posture while walking.

The Qur’an sets two images against one another:

A person proceeding face down, and a person moving upright and balanced along a straight path.

Someone facing the ground cannot clearly see the way ahead and has difficulty recognizing the path, obstacles, and destination. An upright person can observe the route and discern the direction of travel.

This physical image expresses the difference between guidance and misguidance. It invites reflection on how one proceeds and in which direction.

مُكِبًّا does not lexically mean “misguided.” That implication comes from the contrast, the question أَهْدَىٰ, and the expression صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ.

5. Morphology: كَبَّ and أَكَبَّ

This family has a notable relationship between its basic triliteral verb and Form IV.

Many Arabic verbs become transitive in Form IV; here, the familiar pattern often works in the opposite direction.

كَبَّ, in the sense of overturning another, is transitive, whereas أَكَبَّ can describe the subject’s own falling face down or bending.

Classical Arabic records:

كَبَبْتُهُ فَأَكَبَّ

I cast him down, and he fell face down.

Some lexical accounts also record transitive أَكَبَّ, so the distinction is not an exceptionless rule for all Arabic usage.

The two Qur’anic uses clearly show the formal difference. In Al-Naml 27:90, passive كُبَّتْ describes an action done to people; in Al-Mulk 67:22, the participle مُكِبًّا describes the walker’s own condition.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك ب ب (كَبّ) and ك ب ك ب (كَبْكَبَ)

كَبَّ denotes overturning and casting face down. كَبْكَبَ can additionally convey tumbling, repeated overturning, and casting together.

The families share an image of being thrown headlong, but the quadriliteral form can emphasize the manner and intensity of overturning.

Their semantic connection is clear, although early grammarians differed about the historical derivation of the quadriliteral from the triliteral verb.

ك ب ب (كَبّ) and ن ك س (نَكْس)

نَكْس concerns inversion, lowering the head, and reversing something’s previous condition.

Al-Sajdah 32:12 describes the guilty with their heads lowered before their Lord.

This concerns lowering the head, not necessarily the whole body falling to the ground.

By contrast, مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ in Al-Mulk 67:22 depicts the person’s face-down condition while moving.

ك ب ب (كَبّ) and ق ل ب (قَلْب)

ق ل ب has a broader range: turning, returning, changing direction, and transforming a condition.

For example, Al-Nur 24:44 speaks of alternating night and day. This concerns change and succession rather than being cast down or losing balance.

The Qur’anic use of كَبّ offers the more specific image of overturning and casting face down.

ك ب ب (كَبّ) and ن ك ب (نَكْب)

One branch of ن ك ب concerns deviating and moving away from a path.

Al-Mu’minun 23:74 describes those who do not believe in the Hereafter as deviating from the path.

Al-Mulk 67:22 concerns how someone walks; Al-Mu’minun 23:74 concerns departing from the path itself.

Both images express misguidance, but from different angles.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Arabic word or expressionSuggested rendering
كَبَّoverturned; cast face down
كُبَّتْwas cast down
أَكَبَّfell face down; bent over something
مُكِبّface down; bending over something
فَكُبَّتْ وُجُوهُهُمْ فِي النَّارِthey are cast face down into the Fire
يَمْشِي مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِwalks face down

Translations should preserve the face-down image. “Falling,” “descending,” or “being misguided” alone does not convey the full meaning.

Conclusion

In bodily movement and posture, ك ب ب concerns overturning, casting or falling face down, and bending over something. Other lexical uses include gathering, concentration, and applying oneself to work.

Al-Naml 27:90 uses this family for being cast headlong into the Fire; Al-Mulk 67:22 contrasts walking face down with proceeding upright on a straight path.

The shared image in these Qur’anic uses is departure from an upright, balanced posture, with the face directed downward.
