Central meaning: pushing away, repelling, driving back, and restraining by force; in human usage, those responsible for repelling, detention, and enforcement.

1. Introducing the root

Arabic Z B N denotes pushing something away from something else. It occurs in descriptions of animal behavior, human encounters, and people empowered to restrain or drive others away.

A familiar example is a she-camel that kicks away someone approaching to milk her. A nāqah zabūn is a she-camel that repels her milker.

Human applications include resistance, defense, preventing approach, and repelling others. Zabāniyah also denotes people who forcibly drive others away or restrain them.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: al-zabāniyah in Al-ʿAlaq 96:18. Powerful agents are summoned against someone who relies on influence and supporters to prevent a servant from performing ṣalāh.

2. Core meaning and principal forms

An early lexical definition states:

الزَّاءُ وَالْبَاءُ وَالنُّونُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الدَّفْعِ

al-zāʾu wa-al-bāʾu wa-al-nūnu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-dafʿ

Meaning: “Zāy, bāʾ, and nūn have a single principle indicating repulsion.”

This definition accords with recorded Arabic usage.

Zabana yazbinu

This basic triliteral verb means pushing away, repelling, or driving one thing away from another.

For example:

زَبَنَ الشَّيْءَ

zabana al-shayʾa

Meaning: “He pushed the thing away.”

زَبَنَ بِهِ

zabana bihi

Meaning: “He repelled it or pushed it aside.”

These expressions involve motion and force that move something away from a position or proximity to something else.

Zabn

The verbal noun denotes the act of repelling or pushing away.

Zabn can describe physical repulsion, as when a camel kicks at its milker. In human encounters it also conveys resistance or restraining someone.

The word does not inherently mean oppression or misconduct. Repelling may serve self-defense or coercion. Its moral character follows from the agent, purpose, and context.

Zabūn

In Arabic this describes a she-camel that kicks away its milker.

The adjective describes an animal’s characteristic behavior, not weakness or incapacity.

Persian also has zabūn meaning abject or weak. That Persian meaning must not be imposed on this Arabic usage.

Zabbūnah

Applied to a person, it refers to defensive strength and resistance.

Rajulun dhū zabbūnah describes a man able to defend himself and prevent others from encroaching upon him.

Some lexical accounts also connect zabbūnah with obstinacy and arrogance.

Repulsion in human relationships can thus accompany strength, obstinacy, or resistance; none alone defines every derivative.

Zābana and tazābana

Zābana, a form III verb, means confronting or repelling another.

Tazābana al-qawmu means that people pushed one another away or engaged in contention.

This usage supports reciprocal action. The morphological form does not, however, establish a universal rule that both parties have equal power or behave identically.

Muzābanah

In commercial usage this names a kind of transaction, including exchanging dates still on a palm for a specified quantity of harvested dates.

The name has been related to zabn through contention between the parties: disagreement over quantity, value, or outcome can produce confrontation and dispute.

This illustrates a commercial application, but muzābanah does not occur in the Qur’an and its meaning should not be transferred to al-zabāniyah.

Zibniyah and zabāniyah

Zibniyah is recorded for a strong or rebellious being, whether human or jinn.

Zabāniyah denotes a group that forcibly drives others away or restrains them.

Its Qur’anic application concerns powerful agents summoned by The God.

The singular of zabāniyah is disputed. Proposed forms include zibniyy, zibniyah, zābin, and zabāniyy.

Another account treats it as an established collective expression without a familiar, definite singular in ordinary usage.

No disputed proposal should therefore be presented without qualification as its sole certain singular.

3. Concrete imagery and Arabic evidence

A she-camel repelling its milker

One clear physical illustration comes from Arab pastoral life.

An attested expression is:

زَبَنَتِ النَّاقَةُ

zabanati al-nāqatu

Meaning: “The she-camel kicked away.”

A camel may repel its approaching milker with its foot, or push its young away from its udder.

A more specific lexical distinction associates zabn with pushing using the calloused parts of the legs, while other verbs describe certain other blows with an animal’s limbs.

This distinction highlights physical force in this concrete application.

Nevertheless, kicking by a camel is only one familiar example, not the entire definition of the root.

Zabn describing war

Another attested expression is:

الْحَرْبُ تَزْبِنُ النَّاسَ

al-ḥarbu tazbinu al-nāsa

Meaning: “War strikes people and drives them back.”

Ḥarbun zabūnun likewise describes war that subjects people to blows, pressure, and violent encounters.

This illustrates the semantic connection between an animal’s physical action and severe human clashes, without proving a historical order of derivation.

War is not a person with limbs; the action is attributed to it to describe the pressure and intensity of conflict.

A poetic example of zabāniyah

A verse attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit reads:

زَبَانِيَةٌ حَوْلَ أَبْيَاتِهِمْ
وَخُورٌ لَدَى الْحَرْبِ فِي الْمَعْمَعَهْ

zabāniyatun ḥawla abyātihim / wa-khūrun ladā al-ḥarbi fī al-maʿmaʿah

Its sense is:

“Around their houses they are forceful enforcers, but amid the turmoil of war they are weak and fearful.”

Here zabāniyah describes people displaying force and repelling others around their own homes.

The contrast with their weakness in battle makes the poet’s criticism clear.

The example demonstrates human application: the word’s lexical range is not restricted to angels. It does not by itself establish the historical priority of its meanings.

Ḥassān lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular verse, it cannot confidently be assigned to the pre-Islamic period. It nevertheless illustrates application to forceful human enforcers or individuals.

Zabāniyah and enforcement officers

Zabāniyah is also recorded for shuraṭ: officers serving ruling authority through detention, restraint, or enforcement.

Their salient feature in this usage is the power to repel and restrain.

This has a clear semantic connection with zabn.

The historical term is not an exact equivalent of modern police in every period. Administrative structures, legal arrangements, and duties vary across societies and eras.

The relevant lexical feature is driving away, restraining, and using force.

4. Qur’anic usage

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18

سَنَدْعُ الزَّبَانِيَةَ

sanadʿu al-zabāniyah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18

Translation:

“We shall summon the stern enforcers.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-zabāniyata is definite and is the object of sanadʿu.

Sanadʿu belongs to D ʿ W and denotes summoning. Summoning itself is therefore not part of the meaning of Z B N.

The verse concerns a group to be summoned whose designation relates to repelling and exerting force.

The surrounding passage

Al-ʿAlaq 96:6 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:7 describe human transgression arising from a perception of self-sufficiency.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:9 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:10 then introduce someone preventing a servant from performing ṣalāh.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:11, Al-ʿAlaq 96:12, Al-ʿAlaq 96:13, and Al-ʿAlaq 96:14 place this behavior against guidance, God-consciousness, denial, and turning away.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:15 warns that unless he desists, he will be seized by the forelock; this image conveys a severe threat.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:16 describes that forelock as lying and sinful.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:17 then states:

فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُ

fa-l-yadʿu nādiyah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:17

Translation:

“Then let him summon his assembly.”

The following verse says:

سَنَدْعُ الزَّبَانِيَةَ

sanadʿu al-zabāniyah

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18

Translation:

“We shall summon the stern enforcers.”

The two verses set two summonses against one another.

On one side, the transgressor may summon his associates and supporters.

On the other, The God speaks of summoning the zabāniyah.

Nādiyahu refers to the assembly or company to which he belongs and whose members he may call upon for support.

The issue is therefore more than individual physical strength: social support and group influence are involved.

Against this display of power stand the zabāniyah, whose ability to restrain and repel constitutes a serious threat to him.

The contrast shows that social influence does not release a person from accountability before The God.

Who are the zabāniyah?

Arabic applies zabāniyah to stern enforcers and people who forcibly drive others away.

In Al-ʿAlaq 96:18, The God speaks of summoning them. That statement alone does not determine their nature; their identification must be considered through context and related verses.

They are commonly understood as angels charged with punishment.

This interpretation also connects with other Qur’anic passages.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:6 describes stern, powerful angels appointed over the fire who do not disobey The God.

Al-Muddaththir 74:30 and Al-Muddaththir 74:31 likewise concern the fire’s attendants and identify them as angels.

In Al-Zumar 39:71, khazanatuhā denotes the guardians of Hell.

These verses identify angelic attendants and guardians who carry out divine commands concerning the fire.

Understanding al-zabāniyah as stern angels charged with punishment is consistent with those passages.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18 itself specifies neither their number nor the manner of their presence and action.

Nor should the nineteen in Al-Muddaththir 74:30 be asserted, without further evidence, as the definite total of all the zabāniyah in Al-ʿAlaq 96:18.

Their number or angelic nature does not define the lexical meaning, which remains connected with repulsion and force.

Does zabāniyah inherently mean torturers?

Translations have used expressions such as torturers, guardians of Hell, or angels of punishment.

These may relate to the interpreted referent, but they are not equivalent.

“Torturer” specifically denotes deliberate infliction of torment. This is not the direct meaning of Z B N.

“Guardian of Hell” describes a custodial assignment, whereas zabāniyah emphasizes repelling and driving.

“Angels of punishment” reflects an interpretation informed by related Qur’anic passages.

A root study should distinguish lexical meaning, contextual function, and identification through other verses.

5. Human power and divine authority

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18 gains particular force from its contrast with the preceding verse.

The person preventing a servant from ṣalāh may summon others for support, showing reliance on personal or collective strength.

Summoning the zabāniyah answers that display of power.

Someone using status, numbers, or strength to obstruct another’s guidance and service faces a power he cannot resist.

This connects with the passage’s opening, where perceived self-sufficiency underlies transgression.

The threat in Al-ʿAlaq 96:18 thus does more than describe a group of agents: it exposes human power’s inability to withstand divine command.

It does not authorize people to call themselves The God’s zabāniyah and suppress others under a claim of divine enforcement.

The verse attributes the summons to The God, not to a human group claiming the right to punish others.

Social strength, supporters, or group membership are not inherently condemned. The condemned conduct here is using power to obstruct a servant’s guidance and ṣalāh.

The passage ends with Al-ʿAlaq 96:19:

كَلَّا لَا تُطِعْهُ وَاسْجُدْ وَاقْتَرِبْ

kallā lā tuṭiʿhu wa-usjud wa-iqtarib

Al-ʿAlaq 96:19

Translation:

“No indeed! Do not obey him; prostrate and draw near.”

This conclusion completes the contrast.

Against threats backed by supporters, the divine command emphasizes refusing the obstructer and continuing prostration and drawing near.

The passage therefore warns the transgressor while also addressing the person under pressure: others’ authority and threats must not prevent service to The God.

6. Distinguishing related roots

Z B N (zabn) and D F ʿ (dafʿ)

Dafʿ means pushing away, removing, or preventing advance and has a broad Arabic range.

Zabn shares this field, but many familiar applications emphasize contact and resistance involving physical force, such as a camel repelling its milker.

This is not an absolute boundary. Dafʿ can involve intense force, and zabn also has social and figurative applications.

Z B N (zabn) and Z J R (zajr)

Zajr concerns driving away, forbidding, and restraining, particularly through voice, command, or deterrent action.

Zabn is chiefly associated with repelling and pushing back.

Their results can overlap because either may stop an action from continuing.

Zajr does not necessarily involve physical pushing, and zabn need not involve shouting or a verbal command.

Z B N (al-zabāniyah) and KH Z N (al-khazanah)

Khazanah denotes guardians in the Qur’an, including guardians of Hell.

KH Z N concerns keeping and preserving.

Zabāniyah, by contrast, belongs to a root of repelling, driving, and restraining.

Both titles may refer to agents associated with the fire, but their lexical meanings differ.

Khazanah emphasizes custody and watchfulness; zabāniyah emphasizes force and repulsion.

This difference alone does not establish two entirely separate angelic groups. Their identities and relationship must be examined through the passages, not inferred solely from distinct roots.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the principal forms. These proposals do not replace the installed verse translations or existing authored explanations.

Zabn: repelling, pushing away, driving back.

Zabana: pushed back, removed, or forcibly drove away.

Zabūn: repelling; of a she-camel, one that kicks away its milker.

Zabbūnah: defensive strength and resistance; firmness against encroachment.

Zābana: confronted or repelled another.

Tazābana: pushed one another away or contended together.

Muzābanah: the name of a particular commercial transaction.

Zibniyah: a strong, stern, or rebellious being, depending on context.

Zabāniyah: stern agents of repulsion and restraint; in the Qur’an, powerful divine agents understood through related verses as angels charged with punishment.

A proposed rendering of Al-ʿAlaq 96:18 is:

“We shall summon the stern enforcers.”

This rendering retains their forceful, restraining role.

An explanation can identify them as divine agents understood through related verses as angels charged with punishment.

Retaining zabāniyah in translation is also possible if accompanied by a short explanation.

Rendering it directly as “torturers,” or restricting the whole root to “angels of Hell,” does not represent its full lexical range.

8. Conclusion

Arabic Z B N denotes repelling, pushing away, driving back, and forceful restraint.

This appears in animal behavior, self-defense, severe encounters, contention, and the role of forceful enforcers.

Its sole Qur’anic form is al-zabāniyah in Al-ʿAlaq 96:18. Set against nādiyahu, it describes the summons of agents against whom the transgressor’s human power is ineffective.

Arabic usage is not confined to angels: it also includes forceful people and enforcement officers. In the Qur’anic context, reading the word as stern angels of punishment is defensible in light of related passages identifying the fire’s attendants as angels. The fact that The God summons them does not alone establish their nature.

The distinction between root meaning and Qur’anic referent remains essential. Zabn means repelling; zabāniyah designates those performing that role; their identification follows from context and related verses.

The passage’s message is clear: someone using power, status, and supporters to obstruct another’s service must not imagine himself beyond divine power and judgment. The servant is commanded not to yield to the obstructer, but to continue serving and drawing near.
