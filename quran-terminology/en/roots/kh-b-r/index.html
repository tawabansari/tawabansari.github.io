Awareness of a thing’s reality, knowledge gained through examination, and information conveyed to others

The Arabic root KH B R concerns awareness, knowledge, and learning about something. Khabar, khubr, khibrah, ikhtibār, and khabīr belong to this family, but each expresses a different aspect of knowing or conveying information.

Khabar can be information about an event or situation reaching another person. Khubr concerns knowledge of the subject itself. Ikhtibār means examining and testing something to learn its characteristics or reality. A khabīr is someone who knows and understands a subject.

These meanings are connected: someone may learn through hearing a report, deepen their understanding through examination and experience, and become knowledgeable after sufficient familiarity. This path is not necessary in every instance, however. Not every report arises from personal experience, and not every knowledgeable person acquires knowledge through direct testing.

The Qur’an uses four main forms of this root: khabar, khubr, akhbār, and khabīr.

In Moses’ story, khabar is information he hopes to obtain at the fire. In the account of Moses and the servant whom God has taught, khubr concerns knowledge without which certain events are difficult to understand correctly. Other verses use akhbār for human circumstances and conduct, and even what the earth will relate.

The root’s most frequent Qur’anic form, however, is khabīr, describing God’s awareness of human actions, what remains concealed, the reality of affairs, and what occurs in the heavens and the earth.

Understanding the family requires distinguishing receiving information, knowing a subject’s reality, testing it, and comprehensive awareness, without wholly separating these related meanings or reducing them to one restricted definition.

1. Qur’anic forms and the root’s semantic field

KH B R occurs 52 times in the Qur’an in four main forms.

Khabar occurs twice, in Al-Naml 27:7 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29.

Khubr also occurs twice, in Al-Kahf 18:68 and Al-Kahf 18:91.

Akhbār occurs three times, in Al-Tawbah 9:94, Muḥammad 47:31, and Al-Zalzalah 99:4.

Khabīr occurs 45 times, in different case forms and with the definite article or attached particles.

The differences between khabīrun, khabīran, khabīrin, and al-khabīru do not create independent derivatives. They are grammatical and combined forms of one word.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 52 occurrences in four forms

Arabic also has other derivatives of this root.

Khabara al-shayʾa means knowing something and becoming aware of its reality. Khabira and khabura are also recorded in uses involving knowledge and awareness.

Khabar denotes information or a report about something that reaches a person; its plural is akhbār.

Khubr and khibrah concern knowing something. Khābir and khabīr also describe a person knowledgeable about and familiar with a subject.

Akhbara and khabbara mean informing another person. Istakhbara concerns asking for news and seeking information.

Ikhtabara means testing and examining something; takhabbara can also concern seeking reports or learning about something.

These forms are established in Arabic, but should not be confused with the derivatives actually occurring in the Qur’an. No verb from this family, such as akhbara or ikhtabara, occurs in the Qur’anic text.

Do all forms have one precise meaning?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for KH B R.

The first is knowledge: awareness and understanding.

The second concerns softness, looseness, and abundance, appearing in words for land, vegetation, agriculture, and some material phenomena.

This division matters because even early dictionaries did not necessarily derive every use of KH B R from informing and learning.

Within the field of knowledge, khabar and khibrah have a clear connection. Someone knows something and can report it; they may also test a subject to discover its reality.

This relationship does not make every report necessarily true or every kind of knowledge dependent on direct experiment.

Nor can the land-related branch establish, without historical evidence, that “knowing about something” originally developed from ploughing or softening soil.

A study of the root should therefore recognize the two recorded lexical branches and then determine the field to which its Qur’anic uses belong.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: knowledge and the separate softness-and-abundance branch

2. Awareness, testing, and conveying information in early Arabic

Khabar: information reaching someone

Arabic khabar denotes something said or conveyed to another person about an event, person, or situation.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn Manẓūr, citing Ibn Sīdah, explains khabar through nabaʾ, news.

Akhbarahu bi-kadhā likewise means informing someone about something.

The principal concern here is conveying information.

A traveller returning from a town might, for example, report on the road, settlements, route safety, or events witnessed. The listener learns through the report about something not directly observed.

Receiving information alone does not guarantee its accuracy.

Someone may convey accurate news, make a mistake, or deliberately pass on false information. A report’s truth and reliability require examining its source and evidence.

Khabar therefore does not itself mean “certain, established truth.” Information may provide a means of knowledge, but its reliability is a separate question.

A pre-Islamic witness: news in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says in his Muʿallaqah:

سَتُبْدِي لَكَ الْأَيَّامُ مَا كُنْتَ جَاهِلًا
وَيَأْتِيكَ بِالْأَخْبَارِ مَنْ لَمْ تُزَوِّدِ

sa-tubdī laka al-ayyāmu mā kunta jāhilan / wa-yaʾtīka bi-al-akhbāri man lam tuzawwidi

Translation:

“The days will reveal what you did not know, and someone whom you supplied with no provisions will bring you news.”

The poet continues by describing reports reaching a person who had made no prior arrangements to receive them.

Akhbār here concerns information and events conveyed through another person.

The distinction between mā kunta jāhilan, “what you did not know,” and akhbār is significant: someone previously lacked knowledge and now receives news of it.

Khabar here is therefore not the listener’s inward knowledge itself, but what conveys knowledge to them.

The witness is recorded in Ṭarafah’s Muʿallaqah and is a well-known example of akhbār in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition.

The poet also describes news arriving without solicitation or expectation. That feature comes from the whole line; khabar does not lexically mean only unexpected information.

Al-Zawzanī, Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Sabʿ: akhbār in Ṭarafah’s Muʿallaqah

Khibrah: knowing through examination and familiarity

Dictionaries record khabartu al-amra as knowing a matter and becoming aware of its reality.

Khibrah also concerns knowledge of something and in some uses includes testing and experience.

Years in a profession may, for example, yield broader understanding through observation, practice, and encountering different situations.

Arabic khabīr is not restricted to someone with many years in an occupation, however. Its basic sense is awareness and knowledge of the subject.

Conversely, practical experience does not always mean correct and complete understanding. Someone may repeat a task for years while failing to recognize errors or remaining poorly informed about some aspects.

Experience itself should therefore be distinguished from the knowledge it may produce.

Early khabara al-shayʾa highlights knowing a thing’s reality; ikhtabarahu highlights testing and examining it.

The two may be related, but sharing a root does not make the verbs identical.

Ikhtibār: a route to knowledge

Ikhtibār, from Form VIII of this root, concerns testing and examination.

Someone may test a product’s quality, assign a task to assess a person’s ability, or compare a report with other data to assess its accuracy.

Testing provides a means of reaching knowledge in these situations.

Ikhtibār alone does not mean certain knowledge of the result, however. A test may be incomplete or its method defective.

The word ikhtibār itself does not occur in the Qur’an. The Qur’an also uses other roots for testing, including B L W and F T N.

Although ikhtibār belongs to KH B R, it should therefore not replace every Qur’anic verb for testing.

Khabīr in human language

In early Arabic, khabīr described someone who knew a subject well.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah states:

فَإِنْ تَسْأَلُونِي بِالنِّسَاءِ فَإِنَّنِي
خَبِيرٌ بِأَدْوَاءِ النِّسَاءِ طَبِيبُ

fa-in tasʾalūnī bi-al-nisāʾi fa-innanī / khabīrun bi-adwāʾi al-nisāʾi ṭabību

Translation:

“If you ask me about women, I am versed in women’s ailments, a physician.”

The poet claims knowledge of the subject under discussion.

An important textual variation affects this witness. Some early sources, such as Ibn Qutaybah’s ʿUyūn al-Akhbār, transmit khabīrun, whereas other versions of ʿAlqamah’s Dīwān and lexical citations have baṣīrun.

Ibn Qutaybah, ʿUyūn al-Akhbār: the khabīr reading of ʿAlqamah’s line

Both convey related senses of awareness, but the variant prevents presenting the line without qualification as certain evidence that the original poem used khabīr.

ʿUmdat al-Ḥuffāẓ: khabīr and the baṣīr reading in Lisān al-ʿArab

The transmission reading khabīr clearly uses it for someone claiming adequate knowledge of a subject.

This witness also shows that Arabic expertise was not restricted to awareness of a recent report.

3. The other branch: soft land, agriculture, and abundance

After discussing awareness in Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces another branch concerning softness, looseness, and abundance.

One form is khabrāʾ: soft, low ground where water may collect or vegetation grow.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records khabār for loose ground into which an animal’s or person’s foot may sink.

This use concerns the ground’s softness, rather than informing or knowing.

In explaining this branch, Ibn Fāris quotes a fragment attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ containing «ثَبْتًا فِي الْخَبَارِ» (thabtan fī al-khabāri): firm on soft, loose ground.

The lexical source attributes the fragment to ʿUbayd. Without independently examining its complete wording and poetic setting, however, it should establish no more than khabār’s use for loose ground.

This branch also records khabīr for a cultivator, who ploughs, softens, and prepares land for planting.

Mukhābarah likewise denotes a kind of agricultural cooperation involving a share of the produce.

Different explanations exist for the precise origin of agricultural mukhābarah. Some relate it to the land and cultivation; others suggest a connection with the place-name Khaybar.

These views should not become a single certain historical origin without decisive evidence.

Dictionaries also record forms concerning abundant camel milk, a large water container, and certain foods within this family.

These meanings show that Arabic KH B R extends beyond reporting and knowing.

Nevertheless, the four Qur’anic forms highlight knowledge, awareness, and conveying information, rather than the branch concerning soft ground, agriculture, or abundant food.

Shared consonants therefore do not justify explaining divine khabīr through ploughing or khabar in Moses’ account through loose soil.

Several lexical branches need not be forced into one shared material image.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: loose ground and differing derivations of mukhābarah

4. Khabar and khubr in the Qur’an: obtaining information and possessing knowledge

Bi-khabarin: Moses seeking information

إِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِأَهْلِهِ إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا سَآتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ آتِيكُمْ بِشِهَابٍ قَبَسٍ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

idh qāla mūsā li-ahlihi innī ānastu nāran sa-ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw ātīkum bi-shihābin qabasin laʿallakum taṣṭalūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“When Moses said to his family, ‘I have perceived a fire. I will bring you news from there, or bring you a burning flame so that you may warm yourselves.’”

Al-Naml 27:7

Moses sees a fire from afar and tells his family he will approach it.

He hopes to return with information or bring a flame to warm them.

Khabar concerns information Moses does not yet possess and hopes to obtain at the fire.

The journey and the family’s stopping suggest that it might concern the route, other people’s whereabouts, or the surroundings.

The verse does not specify the precise subject of the information, however.

Rendering khabar as “directions” may therefore explain a contextual possibility, but should not replace the general word “information” without justification.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29 recounts the same event with a related expression:

فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ مُوسَى الْأَجَلَ وَسَارَ بِأَهْلِهِ آنَسَ مِنْ جَانِبِ الطُّورِ نَارًا قَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ امْكُثُوا إِنِّي آنَسْتُ نَارًا لَعَلِّي آتِيكُمْ مِنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ جَذْوَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

fa-lammā qaḍā mūsā al-ajala wa-sāra bi-ahlihi ānasa min jānibi al-ṭūri nāran qāla li-ahlihi umkuthū innī ānastu nāran laʿallī ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw jadhwatin mina al-nāri laʿallakum taṣṭalūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“When Moses had completed the appointed term and travelled with his family, he perceived a fire beside the mountain. He said to his family, ‘Stay here; I have perceived a fire. Perhaps I can bring you news from there, or an ember from the fire so that you may warm yourselves.’”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29

In both verses, khabar concerns information expected to be obtained somewhere.

The difference between sa-ātīkum, “I will bring you,” in Al-Naml and laʿallī ātīkum, “perhaps I can bring you,” in Al-Qaṣaṣ also shows how accompanying expressions can vary the degree of expectation and manner of expression.

The basic meaning of khabar remains information in both instances.

Khabar does not mean revelation in these statements. Moses is speaking of something he hopes to obtain at the fire.

This example matters because Qur’anic khabar can concern practical, everyday information.

Khubran: knowledge not yet complete

وَكَيْفَ تَصْبِرُ عَلَىٰ مَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ خُبْرًا

wa-kayfa taṣbiru ʿalā mā lam tuḥiṭ bihi khubran

Proposed translation for this study:

“And how will you remain patient with what you do not yet encompass in knowledge?”

Al-Kahf 18:68

Khubran occurs in Moses’ conversation with the servant whom preceding verses describe as having been taught knowledge by God.

Moses asks to accompany him and learn. He is asked in response how he can remain patient with matters he does not yet adequately understand.

Khubr here is more than a report heard from someone.

It concerns understanding events Moses will encounter whose causes and consequences he initially does not properly know.

Tuḥiṭ also matters: the verse concerns knowledge a person has not yet encompassed.

Khubran thus concerns knowing the subject, while lam tuḥiṭ expresses the absence of comprehensive understanding.

Khubr itself does not mean complete encompassing knowledge; that feature comes from its combination with aḥāṭa.

In later verses, Moses observes actions seemingly incompatible with his knowledge and initial judgment. The account ends by explaining the reasons and circumstances behind those actions.

Seeing an event therefore does not always mean knowing all its causes and consequences.

This account does not, however, require accepting every seemingly wrongful act by anyone without questions or evidence merely because hidden knowledge might exist.

The story concerns a specific situation and concludes by reporting explanations. Incomplete knowledge does not authorize attributing truth to every unproven claim.

Wa-qad aḥaṭnā bi-mā ladayhi khubran: encompassing Dhū al-Qarnayn’s circumstances

كَذَٰلِكَ وَقَدْ أَحَطْنَا بِمَا لَدَيْهِ خُبْرًا

kadhālika wa-qad aḥaṭnā bi-mā ladayhi khubran

Proposed translation for this study:

“Thus it was; and We encompassed in knowledge what was with him.”

Al-Kahf 18:91

This verse occurs in Dhū al-Qarnayn’s account.

The same khubran used for Moses’ limited knowledge in Al-Kahf 18:68 now occurs with aḥaṭnā concerning God’s knowledge.

This comparison matters.

Al-Kahf 18:68 describes Moses as lacking encompassing knowledge of what will follow.

Al-Kahf 18:91 describes God’s encompassing knowledge of what is with Dhū al-Qarnayn.

Khubr concerns knowledge in both cases, but its scope differs with the subject, verb, and context.

Moses’ case involves limited understanding.

God’s case expresses encompassing knowledge of what is with the person.

The difference comes from the whole sentence, rather than khubr alone.

Bi-mā ladayhi should also not be restricted to military equipment, property, or particular tools. These may exemplify what Dhū al-Qarnayn possessed, but the expression is broader.

Khabar and khubr distinguished

Qur’anic khabar and khubr share a root but have different roles in their Qur’anic uses.

In Moses’ account, khabar is information he wishes to obtain and bring to his family.

In Al-Kahf, khubr concerns knowledge of the subject itself.

The former highlights information reaching someone; the latter, the extent of understanding and encompassing a subject.

This difference should not become an absolute rule throughout Arabic’s history. Dictionaries also record differently vocalized forms for knowledge and awareness.

The Qur’anic distinction emerges from these particular uses of the two forms.

5. Akhbār: human reports and what the earth relates

Akhbār is the plural of khabar, but its Qur’anic uses extend beyond ordinary news exchanged between people.

Al-Tawbah 9:94: reports of human conduct

يَعْتَذِرُونَ إِلَيْكُمْ إِذَا رَجَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ قُلْ لَا تَعْتَذِرُوا لَنْ نُؤْمِنَ لَكُمْ قَدْ نَبَّأَنَا اللَّهُ مِنْ أَخْبَارِكُمْ وَسَيَرَى اللَّهُ عَمَلَكُمْ وَرَسُولُهُ ثُمَّ تُرَدُّونَ إِلَىٰ عَالِمِ الْغَيْبِ وَالشَّهَادَةِ فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

yaʿtadhirūna ilaykum idhā rajaʿtum ilayhim qul lā taʿtadhirū lan nuʾmina lakum qad nabbaʾanā allāhu min akhbārikum wa-sa-yarā allāhu ʿamalakum wa-rasūluhu thumma turaddūna ilā ʿālimi al-ghaybi wa-al-shahādati fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“They will offer you excuses when you return to them. Say, ‘Offer no excuses; we will not believe you. The God has conveyed to us some of your news. The God and His messenger will see your conduct. Then you will be returned to the Knower of the unseen and the apparent, and He will inform you of what you used to do.’”

Al-Tawbah 9:94

Akhbārikum concerns information about people offering excuses for their conduct.

In qad nabbaʾanā allāhu min akhbārikum, the reports are information of which God has informed us.

The verse then discusses conduct and returning to the Knower of the unseen and the apparent.

Akhbār therefore concerns people’s circumstances and behavior, rather than general reports unrelated to their actions.

Nabbaʾanā comes from N B A and occurs alongside akhbārikum from KH B R in one sentence.

A report can thus provide the content conveyed through the verb nabbaʾa.

Muḥammad 47:31: testing your reports

وَلَنَبْلُوَنَّكُمْ حَتَّىٰ نَعْلَمَ الْمُجَاهِدِينَ مِنْكُمْ وَالصَّابِرِينَ وَنَبْلُوَ أَخْبَارَكُمْ

wa-la-nabluwannakum ḥattā naʿlama al-mujāhidīna minkum wa-al-ṣābirīna wa-nabluwa akhbārakum

Proposed translation for this study:

“We will certainly test you until We know those among you who strive and those who remain steadfast, and test your reports.”

Muḥammad 47:31

Akhbārakum here occurs with nablū, “We test.”

The surrounding verses concern human behavior, fidelity, and positions in difficult circumstances.

“Your reports” may therefore concern actual circumstances, statements, and conduct whose reality becomes apparent through testing.

Early explanations likewise discuss the revealing of truth and falsehood in people’s conduct and claims.

Akhbār should nevertheless not be replaced directly with “actions” in the translation itself: the Qur’an has a separate word for action and uses it elsewhere.

Reports here become connected with people’s conduct and real circumstances through nablū and the verse’s context.

Nor does the verse show God needing to test people to remedy prior ignorance. Other Qur’anic verses explicitly affirm divine knowledge of the hidden and apparent.

Testing here concerns conduct becoming manifest and actual—conduct about which judgment is made.

This matter requires separate examination of B L W and ḥattā naʿlama, “until We know.” Akhbār alone does not resolve it.

Al-Ṭabarī: testing reports and revealing people’s circumstances

Al-Zalzalah 99:4: the earth relates its news

يَوْمَئِذٍ تُحَدِّثُ أَخْبَارَهَا

yawmaʾidhin tuḥaddithu akhbārahā

Proposed translation for this study:

“On that day, the earth relates its news.”

Al-Zalzalah 99:4

Akhbārahā, “its news,” is attributed to the earth.

The preceding verses describe the earth’s shaking, bringing forth its burdens, and the human question about what is happening.

The earth then relates its news.

Akhbār is therefore not confined to ordinary conversation between two people.

In context, the earth possesses information that becomes apparent or is expressed on that day.

The following verse connects this event with the Lord’s communication to the earth, Al-Zalzalah 99:5.

Akhbār itself does not mean “testimony,” although exegetical explanations relate the earth’s reports to witnessing actions performed upon it.

Nor does the verse explain how the earth speaks. Without evidence, it cannot be reduced to a literary metaphor or assigned a specific physical mechanism.

The text states that the earth’s news is related.

In all three Qur’anic uses, akhbār thus concerns information about circumstances and events, while the speaker, subject matter, and conditions of disclosure differ.

6. Khabīr in the Qur’an: awareness of the apparent and concealed

Khabīr is the root’s most frequent Qur’anic form.

Dictionaries use it for someone knowledgeable who understands a subject well.

Its pattern is faʿīl, which in many uses can express a firmly established and strong quality.

The morphological pattern alone does not determine the scope of someone’s knowledge. A human being can be knowledgeable in one subject and poorly informed about others.

Qur’anic khabīr is especially used to describe God, with the scope of His awareness clarified across different verses.

Awareness of outward and hidden actions

A recurring Qur’anic construction is:

«وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ» (wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun).

That is, God is aware of what you do.

This expression occurs in different settings, including family rights, giving, social relations, justice, and human responsibility.

Al-Baqarah 2:271 discusses giving charity openly or secretly, then ends by recalling God’s awareness of human actions.

Divine awareness therefore extends beyond conduct performed publicly before other people.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 commands upholding justice and testifying for God, even against oneself or relatives, then warns that God knows what people do if they distort testimony or turn away.

Khabīr here concerns more than awareness of a testimony’s outward result; within the verse, it relates to actual human responsibility in maintaining justice.

This description does not allow inferring everyone’s particular intentions without evidence. The verse affirms God’s knowledge, rather than a human ability to ascertain one another’s hidden motives with certainty.

Inna allāha ʿalīmun khabīrun: knowledge and awareness

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ ذَكَرٍ وَأُنْثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innā khalaqnākum min dhakarin wa-unthā wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾila li-taʿārafū inna akramakum ʿinda allāhi atqākum inna allāha ʿalīmun khabīrun

Proposed translation for this study:

“O people, We created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes so that you may know one another. Indeed, the most honored of you before The God is the most mindful of Him. Indeed, The God is Knowing, Aware.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

The verse ends by placing ʿalīmun and khabīrun together.

ʿAlīm comes from ʿ L M and concerns knowing.

Khabīr comes from KH B R and concerns awareness and understanding a subject.

Some lexical explanations describe a khabīr as also knowing the inward reality of affairs.

This aspect fits the context, which makes mindfulness of God the criterion of human honor before Him. Its full reality cannot be known merely from appearance, lineage, ethnicity, or social title.

An absolute contrast should not be invented whereby ʿalīm knows only outward matters and khabīr only inward ones.

The Qur’an itself describes God’s knowledge as including the unseen, the apparent, and what hearts conceal.

The paired descriptions express the scope and precision of divine awareness, rather than dividing God’s knowledge into separate domains administered by ʿalīm and khabīr.

Arabic lexicons: knowledge, reports, expertise, and distinct lexical branches

Luqmān 31:34 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:3 also place the two descriptions together.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:3 concerns private speech exchanged between people and awareness of it subsequently becoming apparent. The ending, al-ʿalīmu al-khabīru, directly relates to this subject.

Al-laṭīfu al-khabīru: nothing escapes God’s awareness

لَا تُدْرِكُهُ الْأَبْصَارُ وَهُوَ يُدْرِكُ الْأَبْصَارَ وَهُوَ اللَّطِيفُ الْخَبِيرُ

lā tudrikuhu al-abṣāru wa-huwa yudriku al-abṣāra wa-huwa al-laṭīfu al-khabīru

Proposed translation for this study:

“Eyes do not apprehend Him, while He apprehends eyes; and He is the Subtle, the Aware.”

Al-Anʿām 6:103

Al-khabīru here occurs with al-laṭīfu.

The preceding statement concerns eyes and God: eyes cannot apprehend Him, whereas He apprehends eyes.

Khabīr here relates to God’s awareness of matters beyond the limitations of human observation.

Khabīr itself does not mean seeing with an eye. Knowledge and awareness are broader than visual observation.

Nor should laṭīf mean only kind or gentle, followed by a conclusion that khabīr necessarily concerns awareness of extremely small beings. Each root requires independent examination.

Luqmān 31:16 also places the two descriptions together:

يَا بُنَيَّ إِنَّهَا إِنْ تَكُ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ فَتَكُنْ فِي صَخْرَةٍ أَوْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ أَوْ فِي الْأَرْضِ يَأْتِ بِهَا اللَّهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ

yā bunayya innahā in taku mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin fa-takun fī ṣakhratin aw fī al-samāwāti aw fī al-arḍi yaʾti bihā allāhu inna allāha laṭīfun khabīrun

Proposed translation for this study:

“My son, even if it weighs as little as a mustard seed and lies within a rock, or in the heavens, or in the earth, The God will bring it forth. Indeed, The God is Subtle, Aware.”

Luqmān 31:16

Neither something’s smallness nor its inaccessibility prevents God’s awareness or ability to bring it forth.

This example relates to khabīr, but the word itself does not mean “discovering a seed in a rock.”

The verse’s clauses explain God’s awareness of the most concealed matters; khabīr is one of the divine descriptions at its ending.

Al-Mulk 67:14 likewise follows the question a-lā yaʿlamu man khalaqa, “Does the one who created not know?”, with al-laṭīfu al-khabīru.

This pairing connects God’s knowledge with creation.

It does not imply that God, like a human being, needs gradual testing and experience to know His creation.

In human understanding, experience may provide a route to expertise. In describing God, the Qur’an affirms awareness without expressing a need for such a process.

Al-ḥakīmu al-khabīru: awareness and wisdom

Al-Anʿām 6:18, Al-Anʿām 6:73, Hūd 11:1, and Sabaʾ 34:1 pair khabīr with ḥakīm.

This connects knowledge and wisdom without making the words synonymous.

Ḥakīm belongs to the field of judgment and wisdom; khabīr concerns knowledge and awareness.

Hūd 11:1 describes the Book’s verses as made firm and then explained in detail, attributing this to ḥakīmin khabīrin at its ending.

The detailing of the verses therefore belongs within the network of divine wisdom and awareness.

Khabīr itself does not mean detailing or judging. Separate verbs and roots express those actions.

Wa-lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīrin: information from one who knows

إِنْ تَدْعُوهُمْ لَا يَسْمَعُوا دُعَاءَكُمْ وَلَوْ سَمِعُوا مَا اسْتَجَابُوا لَكُمْ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يَكْفُرُونَ بِشِرْكِكُمْ وَلَا يُنَبِّئُكَ مِثْلُ خَبِيرٍ

in tadʿūhum lā yasmaʿū duʿāʾakum wa-law samiʿū mā istajābū lakum wa-yawma al-qiyāmati yakfurūna bi-shirkikum wa-lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīrin

Proposed translation for this study:

“If you call upon them, they do not hear your prayer; even if they heard, they would not answer you. On the Day of Resurrection, they will disavow your association of partners; and none informs you like one who knows.”

Fāṭir 35:14

Khabīr here accompanies yunabbiʾuka, “informs you.”

Informing is an action; a khabīr is someone who knows the subject.

This pairing shows that information’s reliability and value relate to its source’s knowledge.

God here speaks of the outcome for those calling upon others besides Him. Khabīr has therefore been understood to refer to God as the one who knows that outcome’s reality.

Khabīr itself does not mean “someone who necessarily reports truthfully,” however. Knowledge and truthfulness are related but distinct qualities.

Here the report’s reliability comes from the context of divine speech and God’s knowledge of the subject.

Fa-sʾal bihi khabīran: asking someone knowledgeable

Al-Furqān 25:59 states:

الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ الرَّحْمَٰنُ فَاسْأَلْ بِهِ خَبِيرًا

alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarshi al-raḥmānu fa-sʾal bihi khabīran

Proposed translation for this study:

“The one who created the heavens and the earth and what lies between them in six days, then established Himself upon the Throne—the Entirely Merciful; so ask someone knowledgeable concerning Him.”

Al-Furqān 25:59

This is an important, debated use of khabīr.

Fa-sʾal bihi khabīran places the verb “ask,” the pronoun bihi, and the description khabīran together.

Grammatical and exegetical sources differ over what bihi relates to and whom khabīran denotes.

One reading concerns asking someone who knows about the Entirely Merciful concerning Him.

Another identifies God Himself as the knower: His information is to be consulted on this matter.

Al-Ṭabarī and some other commentators highlight the latter reading.

Lexically, khabīr means the knowledgeable one in either reading.

The difference concerns the description’s referent and relationship to asking, rather than the root’s basic meaning.

A root study need not force one definite referent upon this disagreement. Khabīran in the asking construction concerns knowledge of the subject; identifying the precise addressee or source requires separate grammatical and contextual examination.

Al-Ṭabarī: analysis of fa-sʾal bihi khabīran and its referent

Nor does this expression alone authorize unqualified recourse to every claimant of knowledge or acceptance without evidence.

7. Related roots and translation proposals

KH B R (khabar) and ʿ L M (ʿilm): expertise and knowing

Qur’anic ʿilm has a very broad field of knowledge, awareness, and knowing.

Khabar and expertise also concern knowledge, but some uses highlight understanding a subject’s reality and details.

This is particularly apparent in aḥaṭnā bi-mā ladayhi khubran and the divine pairing ʿalīmun khabīrun.

ʿIlm should nevertheless not be restricted to outward knowledge and khubr to inward knowledge.

The Qur’an itself makes divine knowledge encompass the hidden and apparent. The difference between the roots is therefore a tendency dependent on usage, rather than an absolute division of knowledge into two domains.

Study of ʿ L M

KH B R (khabar) and N B A (nabaʾ): information and informing

Nabaʾ also concerns news and information; many Qur’anic uses involve significant subjects with consequences.

Al-Tawbah 9:94 places nabbaʾanā and akhbārikum in one sentence.

Fāṭir 35:14 also pairs yunabbiʾuka with khabīr.

The families are connected, but the Qur’an also uses them in different grammatical roles.

Some uses of nabaʾ highlight important news, whereas khabar can concern practical information about a route or place, as in Moses’ account.

This should not become an absolute rule that every nabaʾ is necessarily momentous and every khabar minor or unimportant.

Study of N B A

KH B R (khubr) and D R Y (dirāyah): knowledge and knowing

Dirāyah also concerns knowledge and knowing.

Qur’anic verbs from this family frequently occur in constructions such as wa-mā adrāka, “what has made you know?”, or lā tadrī, “you do not know.”

Khubran in Al-Kahf 18:68 and Al-Kahf 18:91, however, accompanies aḥāṭa and concerns the extent of knowledge about a subject.

These fields are related, but not every dirāyah means practical experience and not every khubr means information heard from someone.

KH B R (khabar) and B L W (balw): knowledge and testing

Arabic ikhtibār belongs to KH B R and concerns testing.

Qur’anic ibtilāʾ and balāʾ, however, come from B L W.

In Muḥammad 47:31, nablū akhbārakum places both fields in one sentence.

Nablū expresses testing; akhbārakum identifies what is tested.

This conceptual connection does not make the roots identical.

Nor should every test merely remedy the tester’s ignorance. Its purpose and result depend on context and who performs it.

Study of B L W

Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the root’s four Qur’anic forms.

Khabar — Al-Naml 27:7 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:29

Brief root meaning: Awareness, information, and news.

Meaning in these verses: Information Moses hopes to obtain at the fire and bring to his family.

Proposed translation: “News” or “information.”

Khubran — Al-Kahf 18:68 and Al-Kahf 18:91

Brief root meaning: Knowledge and awareness of something.

Meaning in Al-Kahf 18:68: Knowledge Moses does not yet encompass concerning the subject.

Meaning in Al-Kahf 18:91: God’s knowledge of what is with Dhū al-Qarnayn.

Proposed translation: “Knowledge,” or “encompassing knowledge” when combined with aḥāṭa.

Akhbār — Al-Tawbah 9:94, Muḥammad 47:31, and Al-Zalzalah 99:4

Brief root meaning: Reports and information about people or events.

Meaning in Al-Tawbah 9:94: Information about the addressees’ circumstances and behavior.

Meaning in Muḥammad 47:31: Reports and circumstances revealed and assessed through testing.

Meaning in Al-Zalzalah 99:4: The information the earth relates about itself.

Proposed translation: “Reports,” with a separate explanation of their subject in each verse.

Khabīr — different Qur’anic forms

Brief root meaning: Aware and knowledgeable of a thing’s reality and condition.

Meaning in verses concerning God: God’s awareness of actions, circumstances, and outward and hidden matters.

Proposed translation: “Aware”; in some constructions, “fully aware” or “aware of the reality of affairs.”

In wa-lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīrin, “one who knows” can clarify the role of the knowledgeable person.

In fa-sʾal bihi khabīran, “ask someone knowledgeable” is likewise close to the wording; identifying the source being asked belongs in the verse’s explanation.

“Specialist” is unsuitable for describing God in these instances: in modern Persian it mainly suggests someone trained and experienced in a discipline or profession.

Divine khabīr expresses God’s awareness, rather than a profession, acquired training, or a need for experience.

8. Conclusion

Arabic KH B R concerns awareness, knowledge, becoming informed, and conveying information. In its principal branch, someone may know a subject, receive a report, or deepen their understanding through examination and testing.

Ibn Fāris also records another branch concerning soft ground, agriculture, and abundance. These uses should not be omitted or transformed into knowledge and informing without evidence.

The Qur’an uses four forms: khabar, khubr, akhbār, and khabīr.

Khabar in Moses’ account denotes information he hopes to obtain. Khubr in Al-Kahf concerns understanding a subject and encompassing it in knowledge. Akhbār concerns human circumstances and conduct, and what the earth relates.

Frequent Qur’anic khabīr describes God’s awareness of actions and the reality of affairs. Its pairings with ʿalīm, laṭīf, and ḥakīm place it within a network of divine knowledge, creation, justice, and wisdom.

Khabīr should nevertheless not be confined to knowledge gained through human experience, just as not every khabar is certain truth and not every khubr absolute knowledge.

Final definition: Within its knowledge-related branch, KH B R indicates awareness of a subject, understanding its reality and circumstances, and conveying related information. Khabar mainly denotes information received or conveyed, khubr knowledge itself, akhbār reports collectively, and khabīr the possessor of knowledge. Describing God, khabīr concerns His encompassing awareness without attributing human experiential learning to Him. Early Arabic also has a distinct lexical branch concerning soft ground and cultivation, but this is not the central field of the root’s Qur’anic forms. Brief root meaning: Awareness and knowledge of a thing’s reality and condition; khabar: conveyed information; khabīr: aware.
