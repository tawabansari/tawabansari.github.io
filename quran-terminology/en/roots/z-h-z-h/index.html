The Qur’anic Root Z Ḥ Z Ḥ

Zaḥzaḥa: moving something away from a position and creating distance

Z Ḥ Z Ḥ is an exception among the Qur’anic roots examined here: it is not triliteral, but quadriliteral, with the two consonants zāy and ḥāʾ repeated. Its basic form is زَحْزَحَ, zaḥzaḥa, and the Qur’an uses this root in only two places:

زُحْزِحَ, zuḥziḥa, in Āl ʿImrān 3:185

And:

مُزَحْزِح, muzaḥziḥ, in Al-Baqarah 2:96, in the construction بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ.

This limited Qur’anic usage calls for a careful definition of the root, without extracting more meaning from the word’s outward form than it can support.

The linguistic evidence centers on a clear idea: moving someone or something away from its position, removing it from a point of reference, and creating distance between the two.

In the reflexive form تَزَحْزَحَ, tazaḥzaḥa, the same relationship is realized through the subject’s own movement: moving aside, moving away, or distancing oneself.

Zaḥzaḥah is therefore more than the state of being far away. It involves a change of position: someone or something moves relative to a position or reference point and becomes separated from it.

The reduplicated quadriliteral form: what can and cannot be inferred

Zaḥzaḥa belongs to Arabic’s reduplicated quadriliteral formations: the first two consonants recur in the second half of the word. In some Arabic words, such repetition fits repeated movement, agitation, or continuance, and connections between a word’s sound and the character of certain actions have long been observed.

This observation should not, however, become a universal rule.

The repetition of zāy and ḥāʾ in zaḥzaḥa does not by itself establish that the verb necessarily conveys any of the following:

  • gradual removal,
  • shaking something several times,
  • a very forceful movement,
  • pulling something free of an attraction,
  • or overcoming a resisting force.

None of these is established by the repeated form alone.

Lexical explanations describe zaḥzaḥa in terms of moving aside, moving away, displacing from a position, and creating distance. Some early analyses also connected it with the shorter form zaḥḥa, relating that form to pushing and moving aside; connections with forms such as zāḥa yazīḥu have also been proposed.

This disagreement is itself enough to warrant caution about the word’s historical origin.

The repeated formation of zaḥzaḥa may make the image of movement more tangible than a simple verb would, but the root’s definition should not include more than the direct evidence supports.

The defensible core is therefore:

Moving something away from a position and creating distance.

The physical image: movement away from a position

The root’s basic image is simple and concrete. Someone or something occupies a position, is then moved aside from it, and a distance opens between that person or thing and the point in question.

This feature distinguishes zaḥzaḥa from simply being far away.

Something can be distant from the outset without any movement or displacement having occurred. Zaḥzaḥah, however, focuses on a transition from a position. Something stood in a relationship to a location and has moved, or been moved, away from it.

A poem attributed to Qays ibn al-Ḥaddādiyyah contains the following lines:

فَقَالَتْ تَزَحْزَحْ مَا بِنَا كُبْرُ حَاجَةٍ
إِلَيْكَ وَلَا مِنَّا لِفَقْرِكَ رَاقِعُ

Fa-qālat tazaḥzaḥ mā binā kubru ḥājatin / ilayka wa-lā minnā li-faqrika rāqiʿu.

“She said: Move away; we have little need of you, and none of us will mend your poverty.”

تَزَحْزَحْ, tazaḥzaḥ, is an entirely ordinary command here: move aside; keep your distance.

This evidence matters because it shows that zaḥzaḥah was already a word for moving aside and distancing oneself before its Qur’anic use. It did not begin as a technical expression for salvation, Hell, or the Hereafter.

Al-Baqarah 2:96: can a long life move a person away from punishment?

The root’s first Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Baqarah:

وَلَتَجِدَنَّهُمْ أَحْرَصَ النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَاةٍ وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا ۚ يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ وَمَا هُوَ بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بَصِيرٌ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

Wa-la-tajidannahum aḥraṣa n-nāsi ʿalā ḥayātin wa-mina lladhīna ashrakū yawaddu aḥaduhum law yuʿammaru alfa sanatin wa-mā huwa bi-muzaḥziḥihi mina l-ʿadhābi an yuʿammara wa-llāhu baṣīrun bi-mā yaʿmalūn.

“You will certainly find them the people most eager for life, even more so than those who associate partners. Each of them wishes to be given a thousand years of life; yet being granted a long life will not move him away from punishment. The God sees what they do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:96

The grammar of the verse’s final portion is especially important for understanding muzaḥziḥ:

وَمَا هُوَ بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ

Wa-mā huwa bi-muzaḥziḥihi mina l-ʿadhābi an yuʿammar.

In this construction, مُزَحْزِح, muzaḥziḥ, is an active participle. The attached pronoun -hi refers to the person who would be moved away from punishment, while أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ, “being granted a long life,” is the proposed agent of that removal.

In natural English, the construction means:

Being granted a long life will not move him away from punishment.

This clarifies the relationship between the verse’s two halves. The person wishes for an exceedingly long life, but the Qur’an says that extending life, however greatly, cannot change his position in relation to punishment.

The point is not merely that a thousand years of life is insufficient for salvation. The word’s image is more precise: extending one’s lifespan does not move one away from punishment or create a decisive distance between the two.

Zaḥzaḥa should therefore not be defined directly as “save.” Salvation can be the result of being moved away from punishment, but the verb itself means moving away and creating distance.

Āl ʿImrān 3:185: being moved away, being admitted, and attaining success

The second Qur’anic occurrence is:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ ۗ وَإِنَّمَا تُوَفَّوْنَ أُجُورَكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ ۖ فَمَنْ زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ وَأُدْخِلَ الْجَنَّةَ فَقَدْ فَازَ ۗ وَمَا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا مَتَاعُ الْغُرُورِ

Kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu l-mawti wa-innamā tuwaffawna ujūrakum yawma l-qiyāmati fa-man zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nāri wa-udkhila l-jannata fa-qad fāza wa-mā l-ḥayātu d-dunyā illā matāʿu l-ghurūr.

“Every soul will taste death, and you will be paid your rewards in full only on the Day of Resurrection. Whoever is moved away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has attained success. The life of this world is only a deceptive enjoyment.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:185

Here, زُحْزِحَ, zuḥziḥa, is passive: someone is moved away from the Fire.

The verse expresses three distinct components:

زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ

Zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nār.

He was moved away from the Fire.

The verse continues with:

وَأُدْخِلَ الْجَنَّةَ

Wa-udkhila l-jannah.

He was admitted to the Garden.

And states the result:

فَقَدْ فَازَ

Fa-qad fāz.

He has indeed attained success.

This distinction is decisive for understanding the root.

Zuḥziḥa alone does not mean “was admitted to the Garden,” since admission to the Garden is expressed by a separate verb.

Nor does zuḥziḥa alone mean “attained success,” since fāza states a distinct result.

Zuḥziḥa also does not carry the whole concept of salvation on its own. Within the verse, being moved away from the Fire is part of a situation that, together with admission to the Garden, amounts to fawz, success.

Thus, the rendering:

“Whoever is moved away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has attained success”

preserves the verse’s construction better than a rendering that makes zaḥzaḥa mean “was saved” from the outset.

Does zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nār imply a pulling or attracting force?

One might build an image around zaḥzaḥa in which a person near the Fire is caught in a force and must be pulled free of its attraction with difficulty or by degrees.

Such an image is conceivable as a literary reading, but it should not be introduced into the article as the root’s lexical meaning.

Zaḥzaḥa itself does not express the existence of an attracting force; ʿani n-nār does not say that the person was caught in the Fire’s force field; and the quadriliteral form does not establish that the removal necessarily meets resistance or occurs gradually.

The verse explicitly states only what is sufficient for the root’s definition:

The person occupies a position in relation to the Fire and is moved away from it.

Discussing how this removal occurs, how difficult it is, or the Fire’s physical properties takes us beyond the word’s direct meaning.

ʿAni n-nār and mina l-ʿadhāb

The two Qur’anic uses appear with two different prepositions:

زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ

Zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nār.

And:

بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ

Bi-muzaḥziḥihi mina l-ʿadhāb.

This difference is observable, but should not be turned into a mechanical rule.

In many Arabic constructions, ʿan fits moving aside, distancing oneself, or passing away from something’s vicinity. Thus, زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ readily conveys removal from the Fire: the person is moved away from a close relationship with it.

Min can likewise indicate separation and the point from which distance is measured. In مُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ, the question is whether something can separate the person from punishment and move him away from it.

This difference does not establish that ʿan necessarily describes crossing a sphere of influence, or that min necessarily marks a physical starting point of punishment. The verses themselves do not state those details.

The defensible distinction is more limited:

In Āl ʿImrān, عن النار highlights the direction of movement away from a reference point.

In Al-Baqarah, من العذاب expresses separation and distancing from punishment.

In both, the core of zaḥzaḥa remains the same: a change of position relative to the reference point from which the person is moved away.

Does zaḥzaḥa inherently mean wrenching something from a fixed place?

Some explanations of the word refer to a position, location, or settled place. Such expressions can suggest that zaḥzaḥa necessarily concerns something long fixed in place, then wrenched free by overcoming resistance.

That conclusion goes beyond the evidence.

Saying that something was moved away from its position does not necessarily mean it possessed great stability, inertia, or resistance. The position is simply the location relative to which movement is measured.

The example tazaḥzaḥ illustrates this too. The person is told to move aside; there is no reason to imagine that he is stuck to the ground or must be pulled free by a special force.

Zaḥzaḥah can therefore be said to involve displacement from a previous position or relationship, while “overcoming stability” or “breaking free of an attraction” is not a fixed part of its definition.

Z Ḥ Z Ḥ in comparison with B ʿ D

Zaḥzaḥa and the family ب ع د can converge in their result, since zaḥzaḥah usually results in greater distance.

Their perspectives are not identical, however.

Buʿd can describe the state of distance itself. Something can be far away without the sentence saying how it came to be at that distance.

Zaḥzaḥa focuses on displacement: the position changes and distance opens up.

Put simply:

Buʿd can be a state of distance.

Zaḥzaḥah is moving something away, or moving away, from a position.

This is not an absolute distinction covering every derivative of both roots, but it is very useful for the two Qur’anic uses of zaḥzaḥa.

In Āl ʿImrān, the verse does not merely say that a person is far from the Fire; an event has occurred:

زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ

Zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nār.

He has been moved away from the Fire.

Zaḥzaḥah and izāḥah

Arabic izāḥah also comes close to moving aside and displacing. This may encourage a fixed distinction: that izāḥah specifically concerns removing an obstacle, whereas zaḥzaḥah specifically moves the person or thing itself from its fixed place.

Such an absolute division is not reliable.

Both can belong to the field of moving aside and displacement. The actual sentence determines what moves and what it moves away from.

More relevant to Qur’anic study is that these two verses use zaḥzaḥa, which focuses on a change of position relative to punishment or the Fire. Understanding this choice does not require artificially restricting the meaning of izāḥah.

Comparison with izāḥah is useful only insofar as it places zaḥzaḥah within the vocabulary of displacement and moving aside, without inventing an absolute, exceptionless boundary between the two.

Zaḥzaḥah is distinct from salvation and success

The two Qur’anic uses readily connect zaḥzaḥah with salvation. Both verses concern being moved away from punishment or the Fire.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the event’s result.

If someone is moved away from the Fire, that removal forms part of their salvation in that context; but salvation is not zaḥzaḥa’s direct lexical meaning.

Āl ʿImrān 3:185 makes this distinction particularly clear:

زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ

Zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nār.

is one event.

أُدْخِلَ الْجَنَّةَ

Udkhila l-jannah.

is another event.

فَقَدْ فَازَ

Fa-qad fāz.

is the stated result.

If zuḥziḥa is translated as “was saved” from the outset, part of the whole sentence’s result has been placed inside a single verb.

The same issue appears in Al-Baqarah 2:96. At the lexical level, the verse does not call a long life a non-savior; it says that it does not move the person away from punishment.

Translation of the root should preserve this relationship.

Why do both Qur’anic uses concern punishment?

It is notable that both Qur’anic occurrences of ز ح ز ح belong to a shared field.

In Al-Baqarah:

مِنَ الْعَذَابِ

Mina l-ʿadhāb.

And in Āl ʿImrān:

عَنِ النَّارِ

ʿAni n-nār.

These associations show that the Qur’an uses this rare verb in two situations concerning distance from an unwelcome outcome.

Their recurrence does not establish that zaḥzaḥa inherently meant “to remove from punishment” in Arabic.

The early linguistic example rules out such a restriction. One person can ask another to tazaḥzaḥ: simply to move aside.

Punishment and the Fire are therefore the Qur’anic instances of zaḥzaḥah, rather than part of the root’s lexical definition.

This distinction prevents a particular Qur’anic use from becoming the root’s general meaning.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Depending on the sentence, suitable English equivalents for zaḥzaḥa include “move away,” “move aside,” and “remove from a position.”

For the reflexive تَزَحْزَحَ, tazaḥzaḥa, natural renderings include:

“move aside”

Or:

“distance oneself.”

These express the subject’s own movement.

In Al-Baqarah 2:96:

وَمَا هُوَ بِمُزَحْزِحِهِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ

Wa-mā huwa bi-muzaḥziḥihi mina l-ʿadhābi an yuʿammar.

Suggested rendering:

“Yet being granted a long life will not move him away from punishment.”

This preserves the grammatical role of أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ without turning muzaḥziḥ into the broader concept of a savior.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:185:

فَمَنْ زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ وَأُدْخِلَ الْجَنَّةَ فَقَدْ فَازَ

Fa-man zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nāri wa-udkhila l-jannata fa-qad fāz.

Suggested rendering:

“Whoever is moved away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has indeed attained success.”

This rendering keeps the two events and the stated result distinct: removal, admission, and success.

Renderings such as “be moved away little by little,” “be pulled aside with difficulty,” “be freed from the Fire’s attraction,” or “be wrenched from a fixed place” should not enter the translation solely on the basis of the word’s quadriliteral form.

Conclusion

Z Ḥ Z Ḥ is a reduplicated quadriliteral root that the Qur’an uses only twice, in the forms زُحْزِحَ and مُزَحْزِح.

The linguistic evidence identifies its core as moving aside from a position, moving away, and creating distance. In the reflexive form, the person moves aside or distances themselves.

The root’s repeated formation may highlight movement through sound, but it does not by itself prove that the action must be gradual, repeated, intense, or accompanied by overcoming resistance. Nor should “position” in a lexical definition be converted into “great stability” or “inertia.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:96, the syntax is especially important: أَنْ يُعَمَّرَ, being granted a long life, is the proposed removing agent, and the verse denies that effect. Even a very long life cannot move the person away from punishment.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:185, being moved away from the Fire and being admitted to the Garden are expressed through two separate verbs, and the combined situation is called fawz, success. This distinction shows that zaḥzaḥa itself should not be equated with entering the Garden or attaining success. The wāw joining the two verbs does not, on its own, determine their temporal order.

The difference between ʿani n-nār and mina l-ʿadhāb also deserves attention within the sentence: the former highlights the direction of movement away from the Fire, and the latter separation from punishment. This difference should not become a theory about a physical sphere of influence around the Fire or the starting point of punishment.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

Z Ḥ Z Ḥ: moving someone or something away from a position or reference point and creating distance from it; in the reflexive form, moving aside and distancing oneself. The word emphasizes a change of position relative to a reference point, but its quadriliteral formation alone does not establish gradualness, intensity, repetition, or the overcoming of resistance.
