Remembering, mentioning, reminding, and keeping awareness alive: from remembrance of God and revelation to human renown and male as distinct from female

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The three-consonant family Dh K R has two prominent semantic branches in Arabic, which must be distinguished from the outset.

The first concerns remembering, keeping in mind, mentioning, and bringing something into thought or speech.

The second is dhakar, “male,” as opposed to unthā, “female.”

Both branches are recorded under the same consonants, but historical evidence is needed to establish that either developed from the other. One classical lexical analysis likewise recognizes two separate semantic foundations.

Both matter in Qur’anic study. Restricting the entire family to remembrance of God is as misleading as deriving remembrance and revelation from the designation of male sex.

Remembering and mentioning

Dhakara al-shayʾ means to remember something, keep it in mind, or mention it.

Dhakartuhu bi-lisānī means “I mentioned it with my tongue.”

Dhakartuhu bi-qalbī means “I remembered it inwardly.”

Classical lexicons connect dhikr both with retention in memory and with expressing a name or statement verbally.

These uses are related but not identical.

A person may remember a friend without saying the friend’s name. Conversely, someone may mention a person in conversation without having been thinking about them beforehand.

Dhikr therefore does not always mean recovering something after forgetting it. A subject already present in awareness can continue to be remembered or discussed.

Dhikr can denote both keeping something in mind and remembering or mentioning it.

Evidence from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry

The opening of the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

قِفَا نَبْكِ مِنْ ذِكْرَى حَبِيبٍ وَمَنْزِلِ
بِسِقْطِ اللِّوَى بَيْنَ الدَّخُولِ فَحَوْمَلِ

Qifā nabki min dhikrā ḥabībin wa-manzili / bi-siqṭi al-liwā bayna al-dakhūli fa-ḥawmali.

“You two, stop, so that we may weep at the memory of a beloved and a dwelling, at Siqṭ al-Liwā between al-Dakhūl and Ḥawmal.”

Here dhikrā denotes the memory of the beloved and her dwelling.

The poet recalls her at the traces of a place once associated with her. Sorrow and weeping accompany this recollection.

But sorrow and weeping are not the meaning of dhikrā itself; they are the poet’s response to the memory brought to mind.

Recalling an event may bring joy to one person and sorrow to another. What the experiences share is the subject’s presence in memory.

This opening is well established in the Arabic literary tradition. Nevertheless, pre-Islamic poetry survives through later transmission and compilation; attribution alone does not establish the composition date of every line or every detail of its wording.

With that qualification, the passage attests to dhikrā as memory or recollection of a known person or place in the early poetic tradition.

Before any connection to worship or revelation, this use describes an ordinary human experience.

Dhikr, reputation, and renown

Classical Arabic also uses dhikr for a person’s name and reputation.

Lahu dhikrun bayna al-nās means that people mention and speak about that person.

Such renown may be good or bad.

One person may be known for justice and generosity, another for oppression and misconduct. In either case, their name circulates in conversation.

Accordingly, dhikr can mean good reputation, honor, or commendable renown in some contexts; its positive value comes from the subject and context.

Mentioning someone does not in itself mean praising them.

In a disparaging context, dhakara fulānan can refer to mentioning someone’s faults or speaking ill of them.

The root should therefore not be defined as invariably positive in moral value.

Dhakar: male

Dhakar, with a short a after each of the first two consonants, designates male sex and contrasts with unthā.

It applies to humans and animals alike.

Dhakarun mina al-insān refers to a male human; dhakarun mina al-anʿām to a male livestock animal.

Its established plurals include dhukūr and dhukrān.

Lexicons record further forms, such as midhkār for a person or animal producing many male offspring.

Mudhakkar can describe something resembling a male or classified as grammatically masculine.

Classical Arabic also applies dhakar descriptively to hard iron, a strong blade, and some coarse plants. These uses matter historically but do not establish that “male” inherently means superior, braver, or more valuable than female.

They are descriptive and sometimes figurative uses. Human worth cannot be inferred from a sex designation.

Evidence does not establish a single semantic origin uniting this branch with remembrance. The two are therefore treated separately here.

2. Qur’anic forms and word formation

Dh K R is a widely represented Qur’anic root family.

The Qur’anic morphological index records 292 occurrences in 14 principal morphological and lexical categories.

Important forms include:

Dhakara, yadhkuru, udhkur, udhkurū: the basic verb, meaning to remember or mention.

Dhakkara, yudhakkiru, dhukkira: form II, to remind another or to be reminded.

Tadhakkara, yatadhakkaru: form V, to remember, take heed, or become attentive to a reminder.

Iddakara: form VIII, to remember or take heed.

Dhikr: remembrance, mention, reminding, and in some contexts a message that reminds.

Dhikrā: remembrance, recollection, or an admonition that brings a subject into awareness.

Tadhkirah: something serving as a reminder.

Tadhkīr: the act of reminding.

Dhākirīn and dhākirāt: male and female active-participle plurals for those who remember; the lexical singular is dhākir.

Mudhakkir: someone who reminds others.

Muddakir: someone who takes heed of a reminder.

Madhkūr: something remembered or mentioned.

Dhakar, dhukūr, dhukrān: male and its plural forms.

Some forms, such as dhikr, are frequent; tadhkīr, mudhakkir, and madhkūr each occur only once.

Frequency does not determine semantic importance in advance. A rare use may reveal the limits of an overly broad definition.

The distinctive form muddakir

Muddakir occurs in Sūrat al-Qamar.

It belongs to form VIII of Dh K R. Sound assimilation changes the underlying idhtakara to iddakara, whose active participle is muddakir.

Muddakir is therefore not derived from an independent D K R root.

Iddakara and tadhakkara are close in meaning but belong to different morphological patterns.

Both may be translated “remembered” or “took heed,” while their different patterns should remain clear in linguistic analysis.

Semantic core and its limits

In the remembrance branch, the central meaning can be expressed as follows:

Keeping, bringing, or returning a person, subject, or meaning to mind or speech; and, in the relevant derived forms, reminding another of it.

This includes both inward remembrance and verbal mention.

“Divine truth,” “guidance,” and “righteous action” should not be made fixed components of every form’s lexical meaning.

People may remember God, mention a person, recall an event, or repeat a false statement.

Divine reminding is important in the Qur’an, but the root’s general meaning is broader.

In the second branch, dhakar designates male sex and requires its own definition.

3. Remembering, mentioning, and recalling a memory

Remembering after forgetting

Yūsuf 12:45 states:

وَقَالَ الَّذِي نَجَا مِنْهُمَا وَادَّكَرَ بَعْدَ أُمَّةٍ أَنَا أُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِتَأْوِيلِهِ فَأَرْسِلُونِ

Wa-qāla alladhī najā minhumā wa-iddakara baʿda ummatin anā unabbiʾukum bi-taʾwīlihi fa-arsilūni.

“The one of the two who had escaped and remembered after a time said: ‘I will inform you of its interpretation, so send me.’”

Yūsuf 12:45

Here iddakara denotes recalling something after time has passed.

In Yūsuf 12:42, Joseph had asked the person he expected to be released to mention him to his master.

That mention does not take place, and Joseph remains imprisoned for a time.

When dream interpretation becomes relevant again, the survivor remembers Joseph’s case.

Here remembrance indeed concerns something that had been absent from the person’s attention.

But this is not the Qur’an’s only kind of dhikr: remembrance can occur without prior forgetting.

Reminding another

Al-Baqarah 2:282 uses tudhakkira in its discussion of documenting financial obligations and testimony.

If one of the two female witnesses errs in recalling or stating the matter, the other reminds her.

This shows that tadhkīr also applies to practical and legal matters.

The reminder concerns details important to accurate testimony.

The verb does not mean exclusively religious instruction, nor does it itself justify a universal claim about the memory of all women and men.

The verse addresses a particular setting of financial testimony whose conditions require contextual study.

Dhakkara here means helping another recall and bring a matter to mind.

Mentioning and recounting

The Qur’an sometimes uses dhakara for mentioning or speaking about someone.

Ṣād 38:17 commands remembrance of David; Ṣād 38:41 mentions Job, and Ṣād 38:45 Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

These uses connect with recounting their lives and presenting examples from them.

Al-Kahf 18:83 promises an account of Dhū al-Qarnayn in response to a question about him.

Al-Kahf 18:70 concerns refraining from asking about a matter until Moses’ companion himself speaks of it.

These passages show that dhikr can mean recounting, introducing, or reporting a subject, rather than necessarily restoring a memory the listener already possessed.

Al-Baqarah 2:235 also uses the family for remembering women in the context of intending marriage. The subject is determined by the verse’s family setting.

Not every dhakara should therefore be translated “remembered after forgetting.”

“Mentioned,” “spoke about,” or “remembered” may be more accurate.

Being remembered and gaining renown

Al-Sharḥ 94:4 states:

وَرَفَعْنَا لَكَ ذِكْرَكَ

Wa-rafaʿnā laka dhikraka.

“And We raised your renown for you.”

Al-Sharḥ 94:4

Dhikraka here concerns the Prophet’s name and remembrance.

Rafaʿnā expresses raising, and the combination conveys increased renown and an honored name.

But Dh K R does not always mean honor or greatness: a person may be remembered favorably or unfavorably.

Here the positive value comes from rafaʿnā and the address to the Prophet.

Al-Zukhruf 43:44 calls the Qur’an dhikr for the Prophet and his people and immediately mentions their accountability. Reminding, the message’s significance, and the responsibility of receiving it are connected in that context.

Madhkūr: something mentioned

Al-Insān 76:1 states:

هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى الْإِنْسَانِ حِينٌ مِنَ الدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُنْ شَيْئًا مَذْكُورًا

Hal atā ʿalā al-insāni ḥīnun mina al-dahri lam yakun shayʾan madhkūran.

“Has there come upon the human a period of time when he was not a thing mentioned?”

Al-Insān 76:1

Madhkūran is the passive participle of dhakara.

It describes something remembered or mentioned.

This expression alone does not establish absolute nonexistence in every possible sense during that period.

“Not being mentioned” and “being absolutely nonexistent” are different propositions.

Nor does the absence of a known name or identity by itself establish anything about God’s prior knowledge.

The word concerns being remembered or mentioned; broader conclusions require evidence from context.

4. Remembering God: inwardly, in speech, and in conduct

Remembering God is one of this root’s most important Qur’anic uses.

فَاذْكُرُونِي أَذْكُرْكُمْ وَاشْكُرُوا لِي وَلَا تَكْفُرُونِ

Fa-udhkurūnī adhkurkum wa-ushkurū lī wa-lā takfurūni.

“So remember Me; I will remember you. Give thanks to Me, and do not be ungrateful to Me.”

Al-Baqarah 2:152

The verb occurs twice in this verse.

First, people are commanded to remember God.

Then God says that He will remember them.

The shared verb does not imply that human and divine remembrance operate identically.

Humans can forget and remember again, but Maryam 19:64 states that the Lord is not forgetful.

Adhkurkum, when spoken by God, therefore does not mean overcoming forgetfulness or retrieving lost information.

It expresses divine attention and remembrance of His servants, whose nature should not be defined by the limitations of human memory.

Thankfulness and ingratitude follow. Their association connects remembrance with a person’s response to blessings and responsibility.

Dhikr, shukr, and kufr nevertheless remain distinct concepts and should not simply replace one another in translation.

Remembrance inwardly and verbally

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205 describes remembering the Lord inwardly, humbly and fearfully, without loud speech.

Remembrance is therefore not limited to loudly uttered words.

Al-Baqarah 2:114, meanwhile, concerns preventing God’s name from being mentioned in places of worship.

This also involves outward expression and mention of His name.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:41 calls believers to remember God abundantly; Al-Aḥzāb 33:42 follows with glorification morning and evening.

Qur’anic dhikr cannot therefore be exclusively mental, with no place for verbal expression.

Neither can it be restricted to repeating a few names or formulas.

Remembrance may be present in the heart, speech, and conduct. Which aspect is prominent depends on each verse’s structure and context.

Remembrance and returning from wrongdoing

Āl ʿImrān 3:135 describes people who remember God and seek forgiveness after committing an indecency or wronging themselves.

Remembrance follows misconduct in this passage.

A person may overlook truth and responsibility while doing wrong. Remembering God opens the possibility of reconsideration and return.

Remembering God does not itself mean seeking forgiveness, since the verse states the latter separately.

The sequence connects these actions without making them identical.

Remembrance in different circumstances

Āl ʿImrān 3:191 describes people remembering God standing, sitting, and on their sides, and reflecting on the creation of the heavens and earth.

It does not confine remembrance to a single bodily posture.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:103 likewise mentions remembrance in different postures after salat.

By contrast, Al-Nisāʾ 4:142 describes people standing for salat to be seen by others while remembering God only a little.

Outward movement and conscious remembrance are not necessarily identical.

This distinction does not make all bodily acts of worship worthless: the Qur’an itself commands salat and specific actions.

The outward form should not replace worship’s substance and purpose.

Remembrance and salat

Ṭā Hā 20:14 states:

إِنَّنِي أَنَا اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنَا فَاعْبُدْنِي وَأَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ لِذِكْرِي

Innanī anā Allāhu lā ilāha illā anā fa-uʿbudnī wa-aqimi al-ṣalāta li-dhikrī.

“Indeed, I am The God; there is no god except Me. So worship Me and establish salat for My remembrance.”

Ṭā Hā 20:14

Establishing salat is directly connected with remembering God.

Li-dhikrī clarifies the command’s purpose or orientation.

This construction identifies remembering God as a central purpose of salat.

But Dh K R does not name every component or manner of salat. The Qur’an sometimes mentions the two separately.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:9 also calls for proceeding to remembrance of God when the call for Friday salat is made.

After mentioning recitation of the Book and establishing salat, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45 declares the greatness of remembrance of God.

The possessive construction can linguistically concern people remembering God or remembrance attributed to God. Human remembrance fits the immediate discussion of conduct, but the other grammatical possibility should not be dismissed without evidence.

For a fuller account of remembrance and salat, their dedicated Forqan studies are relevant. The lexical point here is that the two are connected without being complete synonyms.

Women and men who remember

Alongside other qualities of faith and conduct, Al-Aḥzāb 33:35 separately names men and women who remember God abundantly.

Al-dhākirīn is a masculine plural active participle; al-dhākirāt is its feminine counterpart.

They belong to the remembrance branch, not to the noun dhakar meaning male.

Both groups share the promise of forgiveness and reward.

Remembering God is not exclusive to either sex. Women and men can both practice it, and their responsibility and conduct are assessed by the same standards of faith and ethics.

The Forqan principle Remembrance of God is relevant to the connection between remembrance, thought, and conduct.

Turning away from remembrance

Ṭā Hā 20:124 connects turning away from divine remembrance with a constricted livelihood and being raised blind on the Day of Resurrection. Dhikr here can include divine reminding and the message; the verse should not be confined to abandoning a verbal formula.

Nor should the constricted livelihood be reduced, without contextual study, exclusively to poverty or psychological distress. The verse describes consequences of turning away, not a test for judging the faith of every poor or distressed person.

Al-Zukhruf 43:36 connects overlooking the remembrance of the Merciful with a satanic companion. Al-Zukhruf 43:37 explains the companion’s obstruction of the way and the resulting presumption of being guided.

These verses concern responses to reminders and their consequences. Censure comes from turning away and its context, not from the lexical meaning of every Dh K R form.

Remembering blessings

Al-Baqarah 2:231 places remembrance of God’s blessing and the revealed Book and wisdom in a setting of family responsibility. Blessing is the object of remembrance, whose acknowledgment should accompany avoidance of harm; this does not make dhikr and shukr synonyms.

5. Revelation, reminding messages, and means of remembrance

The Qur’an as dhikr

إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

Innā naḥnu nazzalnā al-dhikra wa-innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūna.

“Indeed, We Ourselves sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are its guardians.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:9

Al-dhikr here refers to the message sent down.

The Qur’an is called dhikr in this context: reminding and conveying the divine message are fundamental functions of this text.

This does not make dhikr exclusively a proper name for the Qur’an in every verse.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:48 also calls what was given to Moses and Aaron dhikr.

Ghāfir 40:53 and Ghāfir 40:54 describe the Book inherited by the Children of Israel as guidance and a reminder for people of understanding.

Dhikr can therefore refer to earlier divine messages.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:50 calls the Qur’an a blessed reminder. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:10 mentions a Book containing the addressees’ dhikr.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:105 also relates al-dhikr to the Zabūr. Its precise referent requires consideration of context and earlier scriptures; the definite article alone does not make it invariably the Qur’an.

Why is a divine message called dhikr?

Reminding does not always mean recovering a personal memory.

A person may already know a truth but neglect it, or hear a new message that brings awareness of a truth and remains in memory thereafter.

Revelation can thus be dhikr even when some of its information is new to its first audience.

Qur’anic dhikr is not merely repetition of prior knowledge; it may be a message, warning, instruction, or statement of truth that people should know and remember.

Nevertheless, this reminding function does not make “divine guidance” the lexical meaning of dhikr in every use.

That quality comes from the message’s source, content, and function.

Who are the people of the reminder?

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا مِنْ قَبْلِكَ إِلَّا رِجَالًا نُوحِي إِلَيْهِمْ فَاسْأَلُوا أَهْلَ الذِّكْرِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

Wa-mā arsalnā min qablika illā rijālan nūḥī ilayhim fa-isʾalū ahla al-dhikri in kuntum lā taʿlamūna.

“We sent before you only men to whom We revealed. So ask the people of the reminder if you do not know.”

Al-Naḥl 16:43

This verse concerns earlier Messengers.

The question is whether the Messengers before Muhammad were likewise human beings receiving revelation.

Ahl al-dhikr here refers to those acquainted with the messages and information concerning earlier Messengers.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:7 makes the same point.

Context does not support turning this expression into the exclusive title of a particular religious class with final authority over every subject.

The verse does, however, show the importance of consulting those knowledgeable about the matter at issue.

Its command to ask is qualified by the addressees’ lack of knowledge.

This concerns inquiry and learning from informed people, not unconditional surrender of responsibility for thought or acceptance of every assertion without evidence.

Dhikr, dhikrā, tadhkirah, and tadhkīr

These four forms share a family, but their formation and uses are not identical.

Dhikr is broad: remembrance, mention, renown, an account, or a reminding message.

Dhikrā in many verses is a reminder or admonition intended to affect its recipient. Yet early poetry also uses it for memory of a beloved, so religious admonition is not inherent in every lexical use.

Tadhkirah names something serving as a means or occasion of remembrance.

Tadhkīr is the verbal noun of form II: the act of reminding.

In Yūnus 10:71, Noah speaks of reminding people of God’s signs, using tadhkīrī.

Ṭā Hā 20:3 presents the Qur’an as a reminder for someone who fears.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:48 and Al-Muddaththir 74:54 likewise call the divine message tadhkirah.

But tadhkirah is not restricted to a book or speech.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:73 states:

نَحْنُ جَعَلْنَاهَا تَذْكِرَةً وَمَتَاعًا لِلْمُقْوِينَ

Naḥnu jaʿalnāhā tadhkiratan wa-matāʿan li-l-muqwīna.

“We made it a reminder and a provision for travelers in the wilderness.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:73

The pronoun refers to the fire discussed in the preceding verses. Al-muqwīn is commonly understood as travelers in open wilderness; broader readings include those needing and benefiting from fire.

A tangible phenomenon such as fire can therefore serve as a tadhkirah.

Reminding is not exclusively verbal or textual. Observing a phenomenon, considering an event’s consequences, or attending to natural signs may bring a truth to mind.

Fire does not thereby become the lexical meaning of dhikr; it is made a means of remembrance in this context.

The Qur’an made easy for remembrance

وَلَقَدْ يَسَّرْنَا الْقُرْآنَ لِلذِّكْرِ فَهَلْ مِنْ مُدَّكِرٍ

Wa-laqad yassarnā al-qurʾāna li-l-dhikri fa-hal min muddakirin.

“We have certainly made the Qur’an easy for remembrance. Is there, then, anyone who will take heed?”

Al-Qamar 54:17

The same wording recurs in Al-Qamar 54:22, Al-Qamar 54:32, and Al-Qamar 54:40.

Li-l-dhikr gives the purpose or orientation of this facilitation.

Muddakir is someone who takes heed and benefits from a reminder.

Here remembrance concerns receiving the message and responding to it.

Muddakir should not be reduced to someone who has merely memorized the Qur’an’s words.

Memorization may aid remembrance, but taking heed here involves attention to the message and its warnings.

Making the Qur’an easy for remembrance does not mean every linguistic or legal issue, or every ambiguous verse, requires no study.

The stated purpose is accessible reminding, not dispensing with reflection, knowledge of language, or comparison of verses.

The Messenger as reminder

فَذَكِّرْ إِنَّمَا أَنْتَ مُذَكِّرٌ

Fa-dhakkir innamā anta mudhakkirun.

“So remind; you are only a reminder.”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:21

The following verse states:

لَسْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِمُصَيْطِرٍ

Lasta ʿalayhim bi-muṣayṭirin.

“You are not a controller over them.”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22

The commission to remind is accompanied by a denial of control over the audience.

Mudhakkir means someone who reminds.

The word itself grants no authority to compel acceptance.

Nor does tadhkīr lexically mean enforced obedience.

Here the Messenger reminds people of the message, while they remain responsible for their response.

Reminding, conveying, and clarifying a message are not equivalent to taking control of another person’s choice and faith.

6. Dhakar: male as distinct from female

The family’s second Qur’anic branch concerns male sex.

It occurs 18 times, including dhakar, dhakarayn, dhukūr, and dhukrān.

Here the direct meaning is male, not remembrance, renown, or receiving revelation.

Male and female in creation

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ ذَكَرٍ وَأُنْثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innā khalaqnākum min dhakarin wa-unthā wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾila li-taʿārafū inna akramakum ʿinda Allāhi atqākum inna Allāha ʿalīmun khabīrun.

“O people, We created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes so that you may know one another. Indeed, the most honored of you before The God is the most mindful of Him. Indeed, The God is Knowing, Aware.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

Dhakar contrasts with unthā here.

The two terms occur in describing humanity’s origin.

The verse then mentions diverse peoples and tribes and identifies taqwa as the criterion of honor before God.

Male moral or human superiority over female cannot be inferred from the word dhakar itself.

Sex is a description; the evaluative criterion comes from another part of the verse.

Al-Najm 53:45, Al-Qiyāmah 75:39, and Al-Layl 92:3 likewise mention male and female in connection with creation.

Al-Shūrā 42:49 and Al-Shūrā 42:50 concern the gift of daughters or sons, both, or no children.

Dhukūr and dhukrān likewise designate male sex in these uses.

Dhakar applied to animals

Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144 ask about two male and two female livestock animals.

In these verses, al-dhakarayn is the accusative dual of dhakar, functioning as the object of the verb of prohibition.

The referents are two male animals, not two men.

The same word translated “man” or “boy” in a human context means “male” when applied to animals.

Al-Anʿām 6:139 discusses claims about what is permitted or forbidden to men and women concerning the contents of livestock wombs.

Dhukūrinā refers there to the group’s men.

Translation of the sex-designating branch must therefore suit its referent.

Male sex and moral responsibility

Āl ʿImrān 3:195, Al-Nisāʾ 4:124, Al-Naḥl 16:97, and Ghāfir 40:40 place women and men together in relation to faith, righteous action, and reward.

For example, Al-Naḥl 16:97 states:

مَنْ عَمِلَ صَالِحًا مِنْ ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنْثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَلَنُحْيِيَنَّهُ حَيَاةً طَيِّبَةً وَلَنَجْزِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْرَهُمْ بِأَحْسَنِ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

Man ʿamila ṣāliḥan min dhakarin aw unthā wa-huwa muʾminun fa-la-nuḥyiyannahu ḥayātan ṭayyibatan wa-la-najziyannahum ajrahum bi-aḥsani mā kānū yaʿmalūna.

“Whoever does righteousness, male or female, while being a believer, We will surely give that person a good life, and We will surely reward them according to the best of what they used to do.”

Al-Naḥl 16:97

Dhakar identifies male sex here; faith and righteous action are the conditions of the divine promise.

Male sex should therefore not be equated with virtue, faith, or righteous action.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 use the same word in specific inheritance provisions.

Those provisions require their own legal and family context. A use in inheritance does not redefine male as “the one entitled to a larger share.”

Sex distinctions in contextual speech

In Āl ʿImrān 3:36, commentators differ on whether the statement comparing male and female continues Mary’s mother’s words or is a divine parenthetical statement.

Either reading must consider the vow, the mother’s expectation, and Mary’s particular story; this sentence should not be detached from its context and made a universal ranking of the sexes.

Al-Najm 53:21 challenges assigning male offspring to oneself and female offspring to God as part of its criticism of polytheistic claims.

In neither passage should dhakar be isolated from speaker and context and interpreted as inherent male superiority.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:165 uses al-dhukrān in discussing the conduct of Lot’s people. The plural designates males or men; condemnation comes from the sentence and its context, not from an intrinsically negative meaning of dhukrān.

For the contrasting branch, see the Qur’anic root ʾ N Th study.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Dh K R and N S Y: remembrance and forgetting

N S Y concerns forgetting or a subject’s departure from attention.

Dh K R can contrast with it when a forgotten subject is remembered again.

Yūsuf 12:45 illustrates remembrance after an interval.

But remembrance does not always follow forgetting.

Someone can already keep a subject in mind and continue remembering it.

Nor is Qur’anic forgetting always an involuntary memory lapse; it can concern neglecting a covenant or truth in conduct.

The contrast must therefore be assessed in each verse’s context.

For further detail, see the Qur’anic root N S Y study.

Dh K R and Gh F L: remembrance and heedlessness

Heedlessness involves inattention and a subject’s absence from active awareness.

People may possess information yet fail to attend to it in decisions and conduct.

Remembrance can renew that attention.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205 pairs remembering the Lord with a prohibition of heedlessness.

Heedlessness and forgetting are not complete synonyms either: someone may remember a fact but neglect it in practice.

For this distinction, see the Qur’anic root Gh F L study.

Dh K R and Ḥ F Ẓ: remembrance and preservation

Ḥifẓ concerns retaining or protecting something.

Dhikr concerns its presence in awareness, bringing it to mind, or mentioning it.

The two may overlap in memory, but an absolute distinction between storage and retrieval would be misleading.

Arabic uses of ḥifẓ and dhikr sometimes overlap.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:9, dhikr names the revealed message, while preservation concerns protecting it.

The verse thus gives the concepts different roles.

The Qur’anic root Ḥ F Ẓ study examines this distinction further.

Dh K R and T L W: remembrance and recitation

Qur’anic tilāwah concerns reading and delivering verses in sequence.

Dhikr can name a reminding message or the act of remembering.

The Qur’an is both recited and called dhikr.

Recitation and remembrance nevertheless are not identical designations.

Recitation can convey a reminder, while listening to and reading verses can bring it into awareness.

But not every dhikr means reciting a text: mentioning a name or recalling a memory are examples.

For recitation, see the Qur’anic root T L W study.

Dh K R and F K R: remembrance and thought

Fikr concerns mentally examining a subject.

Dhikr concerns keeping it in mind, recalling it, or bringing it into awareness and speech.

Someone may recall a subject and then think about it.

Recalling an event does not necessarily mean examining every aspect of it.

Conversely, reflection may disclose something new that was not already in memory.

These activities accompany one another in many verses, but neither should be made an obligatory fixed stage of the other.

See the Qur’anic root F K R study for this distinction.

Dh K R and ʿ L M: remembering and knowing

ʿIlm concerns knowledge and awareness.

Dhikr can concern bringing something into memory or speech.

A person may know a subject without recalling it at a particular moment.

Someone may also mention a thing without understanding it accurately.

Mentioning a subject alone does not establish knowledge of it.

Accurate reminding, however, can help people understand and use prior knowledge.

This distinction matters for asking the people of the reminder: their designation must be considered in relation to their knowledge of the subject in context.

Tadhkīr and mawʿiẓah: reminding and admonition

Qur’anic mawʿiẓah concerns counsel that makes a person attentive to conduct and its consequences.

Tadhkīr is the act of reminding.

They may accompany one another: some verses describe the divine message as both admonition and dhikr or dhikrā.

But not every reminder is necessarily moral counsel.

Mentioning a name, reminding someone of an appointment, or recounting history can belong to Dh K R without being admonition in that sense.

Conversely, admonition may recall known truths to encourage better conduct.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The range of uses makes a single fixed equivalent unsuitable throughout the Qur’an.

Forms concerning memory and speech

Dhakara: “remembered,” “recalled,” or “mentioned.”

When a person or event is being discussed, “mentioned” or “recalled” may fit.

When something returns to mind after forgetting, “remembered” makes that clearer.

Dhikr: “remembrance,” “mention,” “reminder,” “renown,” or, in revelation contexts, “the Reminder” or “a reminding message.”

Dhikrā: “remembrance,” “recollection,” or, where appropriate, “admonition.”

Dhakkara: “reminded” or “brought to another’s attention.”

Tadhakkara: “remembered,” “took heed,” or “learned a lesson.”

Iddakara: “remembered” or “took heed.”

Tadhkirah: “a reminder” or “a means of remembrance.”

Tadhkīr: “reminding.”

Mudhakkir: “one who reminds.”

Muddakir: “one who takes heed,” or in an appropriate context “one who learns a lesson.”

Madhkūr: “remembered” or “mentioned.”

Dhākirīn and dhākirāt: “men who remember” and “women who remember,” or “those who remember” where sex is already clear from the construction.

Forms concerning male sex

Dhakar: generally “male”; for humans, “man” or “boy” as context requires.

Dhakarayn: “two males” or “two male animals.”

Dhukūr: “males,” “men,” or “sons.”

Dhukrān: “males,” or “men” in a human context.

These forms must not be translated as “those who remember.”

Nor should “superior,” “brave,” “powerful,” or “entitled to more” be added to the noun unless the verse’s other words and specific subject express such a description.

Important Qur’anic combinations

Al-Baqarah 2:152 calls people to remember God and promises His remembrance of them.

Al-Ḥijr 15:9 uses al-dhikr for the revealed message. “The Reminder” preserves the designation, while commentary can explain its reminding function.

In Al-Naḥl 16:43, ahl al-dhikr may be rendered “the people of the reminder.” Their contextual knowledge of earlier messages belongs in the explanation, without restricting the phrase to a later religious office.

In Al-Qamar 54:17, li-l-dhikr means “for remembrance,” and muddakir denotes someone who takes heed.

In Al-Sharḥ 94:4, dhikraka concerns a person’s name and remembrance; combined with “We raised,” it conveys renown.

In Al-Insān 76:1, madhkūran means “mentioned”; the word itself does not mean physical existence or absolute nonexistence.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:35, al-dhākirīn and al-dhākirāt belong to remembrance of God and must not be confused with dhakar, male.

Sex-designating uses should be translated according to whether humans or animals are meant.

Conclusion

Dh K R has two main Arabic semantic branches, which should not be reduced to an artificial single meaning.

The first concerns remembering, keeping in mind, mentioning, and bringing a subject into thought or speech.

It includes inward remembrance, verbal mention, renown, reporting an event, warning, and a reminding message.

Remembering God is a prominent Qur’anic use. People are called to it, and it can affect their hearts, speech, salat, gratitude, repentance, and conduct.

Lexically, however, dhikr is neither exclusively verbal repetition nor exclusively inward attention. Both can be forms of remembrance, whose character depends on context.

The Qur’an also calls itself dhikr as a reminding message for humanity. Earlier scriptures and messages belong within this semantic field as well.

Dhikrā, tadhkirah, tadhkīr, mudhakkir, and muddakir each have their own form and function. Reminding can occur through speech, a book, observation, or consideration of human history.

Dhakar, by contrast, designates male sex in humans and animals and contrasts with unthā.

The Qur’an mentions male and female in creation and offspring, specific legal provisions, and human moral responsibility.

The sex designation itself establishes no moral value or human superiority. Faith, righteous action, taqwa, and justice must be understood from their relevant verses.

Historical evidence is also required before claiming that dhakar, male, derives from dhikr, remembrance. The branches remain analytically distinct unless a specific historical relationship is established.

Final definition for Forqan:

Dh K R is a word family with two prominent branches:

1. Dhakara / dhikr: remembering, retaining in memory, mentioning, or bringing a person, subject, or meaning into thought and speech; in causative forms, bringing it to another’s awareness. This branch includes reminding, renown, reminding messages, remembrance of God, revelation as reminder, and taking heed.

2. Dhakar: male as distinct from female, in humans and other living beings, without any particular moral quality, value, or legal status being part of the noun itself.

The Qur’an extends the first branch from personal memory and ordinary speech to remembrance of God and divine messages; the second designates male sex in creation, family life, and particular provisions.

Each form must be understood through its morphology, object, speaker, and verse context, without forcing one fixed meaning upon the entire family.
