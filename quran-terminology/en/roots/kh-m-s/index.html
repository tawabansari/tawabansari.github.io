From counting and division into five parts to camel-watering intervals, army organization, and a Qur’anic financial share

Arabic خ م س centers on five and things’ relationship to that number. Unlike roots beginning with a physical image such as cutting, covering, or moving, this family has a numerical field: counting five, the fifth position, or dividing a whole into five parts.

The family includes khamsah for five, khāmis for fifth, khums for one of five equal parts, and khamsūn for fifty.

Early Arabic extends beyond simple counting. Al-khamīs denotes an army explained as having five divisions; al-khims names a familiar camel-watering interval; makhmūs can describe something whose measure or structure is based on five.

Qur’anic uses concern the number of angels, reported counts of the companions of the cave, divine knowledge of private conversations, Noah’s stay among his people, the temporal measure of a day, the fifth declaration in a marital dispute, and one fifth of what has been gained as spoils.

The numerical concept remains clear throughout, but its role changes: counting people, ordering a declaration, measuring time, or specifying a financial proportion.

The family’s meaning should therefore begin with five, without making the legal, historical, or theological features of each application part of the root’s meaning.

1. Forms and Derivatives

خ م س is a sound triliteral root.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • خَمْس (khams): five, especially when counting grammatically feminine nouns.
  • خَمْسَة (khamsah): five, especially when counting grammatically masculine nouns.
  • خَامِس (khāmis): fifth, someone or something occupying the fifth position.
  • خَامِسَة (khāmisah): the feminine of fifth.
  • خَمْسُون (khamsūn): fifty.
  • خُمُس / خُمْس (khumus/khums): one fifth, one of five equal parts of a whole.
  • أَخْمَاس (akhmās): plural of khums, shares of one fifth.
  • خَمَسَ ـ يَخْمُسُ (khamasa–yakhmusu): in financial usage, to take a fifth of property.
  • خَمَسَ ـ يَخْمِسُ (khamasa–yakhmisu): to become a group’s fifth member or complete its number as five.
  • أَخْمَسَ (akhmasa): a group reached five members; in pastoral usage, to water camels at the interval called khims.
  • خَمَّسَ (khammasa): to make something fivefold or divide it into five parts.
  • تَخْمِيس (takhmīs): making fivefold; in particular uses, taking a fifth of property or making something with five components.
  • مُخَمَّس (mukhammas): something with five parts, sides, or components.
  • مَخْمُوس (makhmūs): something whose measure or structure rests on five, such as a rope made of five strands.
  • خُمَاس (khumās): five at a time, or in groups of five.
  • خِمْس (khims): a camel-watering interval belonging to a cycle associated with five days.
  • خَمِيس (khamīs): Thursday; in another application, an army.

These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic text contains four principal groups: khamsah, khamsīn, khāmisah, and khumus. They occur eight times in eight verses and seven surahs: three instances of five, two of fifty, two of the ordinal fifth, and one of the fraction one fifth.

Their semantic roles differ, but all relate to five.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eight occurrences in four groups

2. The Semantic Core: Five and Its Relationships

Ibn Fāris identifies one underlying principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالسِّينُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ فِي الْعَدَدِ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-sīnu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa fī al-ʿadadi

Translation:

“Khāʾ, mīm, and sīn have one underlying principle, belonging to number.”

This clearly states the root’s core.

Unlike some families with multiple meanings, no hypothetical physical image is needed to connect these derivatives.

Five is a count; fifth is a member’s position in an ordered group of five or more; one fifth is the proportion of a part to a whole divided into five equal parts.

A group of five people has a count of five. Someone following four others in a specified order is fifth. If property is divided into five equal portions, each portion is one fifth.

These related concepts are not identical.

Five as a count says how many things there are.

Fifth identifies a position in an order.

One fifth expresses a proportion of a whole.

The Qur’an preserves this distinction.

Khamsah in Āl ʿImrān 3:125 contributes to the number of angels; al-khāmisah in Al-Nūr 24:7 and Al-Nūr 24:9 specifies the declaration’s sequence; khumusahu in Al-Anfāl 8:41 expresses a financial proportion of one fifth.

Root study must therefore distinguish cardinal number, ordinal number, and fraction.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the root’s numerical core

Khams and khamsah

Arabic numbers three through ten have a special relationship to the grammatical gender of the counted noun.

Ordinarily, khamsah accompanies masculine nouns and khams accompanies feminine nouns.

For example:

خَمْسَةُ رِجَالٍ (khamsatu rijālin) means five men.

خَمْسُ نِسَاءٍ (khamsu nisāʾin) means five women.

This difference concerns Arabic grammatical gender, not a difference in the meaning of five.

The number’s ending can also vary with its grammatical role: khamsatu, khamsata, or khamsati.

Case differences should not be treated as separate derivatives or meanings.

3. Applications in Early Arab Life

Khims: a camel-watering interval

Al-khims, with kasrah on khāʾ, is familiar in early Arab pastoral life.

It denotes a watering interval involving several days of grazing between visits to water.

Access to water was central to pastoral life in dry regions. Camels went away from water to graze and returned after a period.

Arabic named such intervals in relation to the number of days.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah transmits Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad’s explanation of al-khims as drinking “on the fourth day from the day of departure,” with departure day counted. This counting expression should be compared with other lexicographers’ explanations rather than silently converted into a single fixed interval.

In Tahdhīb al-Lughah, Al-Azharī objects: the camels drink and depart on the same day, spend three further days grazing, and return to water on the fourth day after departure. Drinking day and departure day are not two separate days in this explanation.

Lisān al-ʿArab records both accounts.

The disagreement concerns counting the cycle, not the word’s existence. Under Al-Azharī’s explanation, if drinking and departure are day one, days two through four are grazing days and day five is the return. A five-day designation should therefore not become five complete intervening days or five days of grazing.

Simply stated, khims is the name of a familiar return-to-water interval associated with five in pastoral counting.

This does not make thirst the root’s meaning.

Thirst, grazing, and returning to water provide the setting, while the naming is numerical.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Al-Azharī on drinking, departure, and three grazing days

Khamīs: an army with five divisions

Early Arabic uses al-khamīs for an army.

One familiar explanation is its division into five parts:

Vanguard, center, right wing, left wing, and rearguard.

Under this explanation, the army is called khamīs for its fivefold organization.

Some sources offer another explanation connecting the name to taking a fifth of spoils.

The five-division explanation appears in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab. Ibn Fāris, however, lists khamīs as a large army among applications departing from the family’s primary regular pattern. This lexical explanation should therefore not be treated as a certain naming history for every army.

Khamīs need not mean an army of five soldiers. It may be a large force organized into five divisions.

Nor did every army necessarily have this organization at every period. The familiar historical and literary name cannot establish the structure of all Arab armies.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the army sense among departures from the primary pattern

Thursday and the fifth position

Yawm al-khamīs is Thursday.

In traditional reckoning beginning with Sunday, Thursday is the fifth day.

Khamīs in this use consequently relates to the fifth numerical position.

Thursday’s name does not occur in the Qur’an.

The name also cannot establish that the Qur’an prescribes a particular first day of the week through this word.

Khums and taking one fifth

Arabic khumus or khums means one fifth of something.

If property is divided into five equal portions, each is one khums.

Khamasa al-māla likewise denotes taking a fifth of property.

Before any particular legal or financial terminology, this application rests on the numerical ratio one to five.

The root alone does not identify the property, collector, or purpose of taking the fifth.

These depend on the property, agreement, rule, and context.

In Al-Anfāl 8:41, khumusahu specifies the proportion; the rest of the verse identifies the subject of the spoils and their uses.

Khamīs and makhmūs in measurement

Khamīs and makhmūs can describe a garment, spear, or other object measuring five cubits.

Ḥablun makhmūsun also describes a rope made of five strands.

Five thus appears in measurements of length and the structure of objects.

Makhmūs in such cases need not mean divided into five financial shares. It may mean five-stranded or measuring five units.

This prevents restricting the entire root family to the financial term khums.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ, and Asās al-Balāghah: fractions, financial verbs, and measurements

4. Early Arabic Poetic Witnesses

Makhmūs in verse attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ reads:

هَاتِيكَ تَحْمِلُنِي وَأَبْيَضَ صَارِمًا
وَمُذَرَّبًا فِي مَارِنٍ مَخْمُوسِ

hātīka taḥmilunī wa-abyaḍa ṣāriman / wa-mudharraban fī mārin makhmūsi

Translation:

“That mount carries me, a white cutting sword, and a sharpened spear with a forepart five cubits long.”

The poet describes his mount and weapons.

Makhmūs qualifies the spear’s mārin portion, lexically explained as five cubits long.

The family thus also appears in measuring weapons.

Makhmūs itself does not mean sharp or warlike; those features come from the other words and the subject of the verse.

Lexical quotations vary in mudharraban and related forms, but the principal sources explain makhmūs numerically through five.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ʿUbayd’s witness and the five-cubit measure

Mukhmis in verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ transmits a verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays:

يُثِيرُ وَيُبْدِي تُرْبَهَا وَيُهِيلُهُ
إِثَارَةَ نَبَّاثِ الْهَوَاجِرِ مُخْمِسِ

yuthīru wa-yubdī turbahā wa-yuhīluhu / ithārata nabbāthi al-hawājiri mukhmisi

A translation conveying the image:

“It stirs, exposes, and heaps up its soil, like the turning of earth in midday heat by someone whose camels drink at the khims interval.”

The lexical commentaries relate mukhmis here to someone whose camels are watered at the khims interval.

Early accounts describe a wild bull whose behavior is compared to that of a camel owner. Al-Aṣmaʿī’s explanation concerns digging to reach cooler soil beneath; the image should not simply become a search for water.

Mukhmis can thus belong to camel-watering and life in dry regions, rather than simple counting or dividing property alone.

The verse’s wording and some details of its explanation vary, so the image should not be described more confidently than the evidence allows. Tahdhīb al-Lughah and Jamharat al-Lughah also transmit yudhrī in place of yubdī; the quotation above retains Lisān al-ʿArab’s wording.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Al-Aṣmaʿī’s explanation, the wild bull, and cool soil

“Striking fifths for sixths”

Early Arabic records the proverb ضَرْبُ أَخْمَاسٍ لِأَسْدَاسٍ (ḍarbu akhmāsin li-asdāsin).

It describes pursuing one apparent aim while intending another, or approaching a purpose gradually and indirectly.

Early explanations connect its origin to camel-watering intervals.

In one lexical account, herders propose lengthening watering intervals while actually seeking to draw closer to their families’ homes.

Akhmās and asdās here name different intervals associated with five and six.

Deception or concealed purpose is not the direct meaning of five: it arises from the whole proverb and its social setting.

Khams itself should therefore not be defined as deception or secretive behavior.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: mukhmis and the proverb of fifths and sixths

5. Five in the Qur’an

Five thousand angels — Āl ʿImrān 3:125

بَلَىٰ إِنْ تَصْبِرُوا وَتَتَّقُوا وَيَأْتُوكُمْ مِنْ فَوْرِهِمْ هَٰذَا يُمْدِدْكُمْ رَبُّكُمْ بِخَمْسَةِ آلَافٍ مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ مُسَوِّمِينَ

balā in taṣbirū wa-tattaqū wa-yaʾtūkum min fawrihim hādhā yumdidkum rabbukum bi-khamsati ālāfin mina al-malāʾikati musawwimīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Yes, if you remain steadfast and mindful, and they rush upon you at once, your Lord will reinforce you with five thousand marked angels.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:125

Khamsati is a cardinal number, and ālāfin counts thousands.

Khamsati ālāfin consequently means five thousand.

The number occurs in a conditional construction: إِنْ تَصْبِرُوا وَتَتَّقُوا, “if you remain steadfast and mindful.”

The numerical meaning must be distinguished from the conditions of the promised event.

Five alone does not signify a particular degree of faith or spiritual rank of the angels. The verse specifies a count, while musawwimīn is a separate description of the angels.

Āl ʿImrān 3:124 mentions three thousand angels. The relationship between these figures requires the sequence and stated conditions, rather than a changed meaning of five.

Five and the cave companions’ number — Al-Kahf 18:22

سَيَقُولُونَ ثَلَاثَةٌ رَابِعُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ خَمْسَةٌ سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ رَجْمًا بِالْغَيْبِ وَيَقُولُونَ سَبْعَةٌ وَثَامِنُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ

sa-yaqūlūna thalāthatun rābiʿuhum kalbuhum wa-yaqūlūna khamsatun sādisuhum kalbuhum rajman bi-al-ghaybi wa-yaqūlūna sabʿatun wa-thāminuhum kalbuhum

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They will say: Three, their fourth being their dog; and they say: Five, their sixth being their dog, guessing about the unseen; and they say: Seven, their eighth being their dog.”

Al-Kahf 18:22

Khamsatun means five, while sādisuhum specifies the sixth position.

Numerically, there are five people and their dog as the sixth member of the group.

The verse, however, reports the number as something people say.

Its presence in the Qur’an does not conclusively endorse that count.

The rest of the verse attributes knowledge of their number to the Lord and discourages unnecessary dispute about it.

This shows that a number’s arithmetical validity differs from the truth of a report about reality.

Khamsah always means five; whether there were actually five people is separate from the number’s lexical meaning.

Five people in private conversation — Al-Mujādilah 58:7

مَا يَكُونُ مِنْ نَجْوَىٰ ثَلَاثَةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ رَابِعُهُمْ وَلَا خَمْسَةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ سَادِسُهُمْ

mā yakūnu min najwā thalāthatin illā huwa rābiʿuhum wa-lā khamsatin illā huwa sādisuhum

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“There is no private conversation of three without Him being their fourth, nor of five without Him being their sixth.”

Al-Mujādilah 58:7

Khamsatin refers to five people in a private conversation.

It appears alongside sādisuhum, “their sixth.”

The verse begins with divine knowledge of what is in the heavens and earth, and ends with people being informed of their deeds.

“He is their sixth” should therefore not be converted into bodily presence like one of the five participants.

The context concerns divine knowledge and encompassing awareness.

Lexically, khamsah specifies only the people’s number. Divine accompaniment and awareness are understood from the construction as a whole.

The verse continues:

وَلَا أَدْنَىٰ مِنْ ذَٰلِكَ وَلَا أَكْثَرَ

wa-lā adnā min dhālika wa-lā akthara

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “Nor fewer than that, nor more.”

Three and five therefore do not restrict divine knowledge to groups of those sizes; they illustrate its comprehensive scope.

Al-Ṭabarī: knowledge and awareness in the private-conversation context

6. Fifty and the Measurement of Time

Noah’s stay among his people — Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ فَلَبِثَ فِيهِمْ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ إِلَّا خَمْسِينَ عَامًا

wa-laqad arsalnā nūḥan ilā qawmihi fa-labitha fīhim alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“We indeed sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them a thousand years except fifty years.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14

Khamsīna means fifty.

It occurs in an exception:

أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ إِلَّا خَمْسِينَ عَامًا

alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman

A thousand years minus fifty gives the stated duration of nine hundred and fifty years.

Fifty here is part of a larger numerical construction expressed through an exception, rather than the principal subject by itself.

Another point is the distinction between sanah and ʿām.

The thousand accompanies sanah and fifty accompanies ʿām. They belong to different roots and their semantic ranges can be investigated separately.

خ م س itself does not establish that the fifty years were easy or difficult.

The verse says labitha fīhim, describing Noah’s stay among his people. It does not without further evidence restrict his entire lifespan to precisely that duration.

Fifty thousand years — Al-Maʿārij 70:4

تَعْرُجُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ إِلَيْهِ فِي يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُ خَمْسِينَ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

taʿruju al-malāʾikatu wa-al-rūḥu ilayhi fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu khamsīna alfa sanatin

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:4

Khamsīna again means fifty, but with alfa sanatin specifies fifty thousand years.

The construction concerns the measure of a day:

فِي يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُ... (fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu...), “in a day whose measure was…”

The number must therefore be distinguished from how time is measured.

Fifty thousand is clear, but the day’s nature and its relationship to human experience of time require the surah’s context and related verses.

Other temporal measures appear in Al-Ḥajj 22:47 and Al-Sajdah 32:5.

Different measures in different verses do not alone permit treating them all as one day or converting one into another without evidence.

Khamsīna in Al-Maʿārij has the same lexical meaning, fifty, as in Al-ʿAnkabūt. The different expression results from combining it with “thousand years” and its context.

7. The Fifth Declaration in Al-Nūr 24:7 and Al-Nūr 24:9

Al-Nūr uses five through the ordinal al-khāmisah.

The passages concern a man accusing his wife of adultery without witnesses other than himself.

Al-Nūr 24:6 describes four declarations from the man.

Al-Nūr 24:7 then reads:

وَالْخَامِسَةُ أَنَّ لَعْنَتَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ إِنْ كَانَ مِنَ الْكَاذِبِينَ

wa-al-khāmisatu anna laʿnata allāhi ʿalayhi in kāna mina al-kādhibīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And the fifth is that the curse of The God be upon him if he is among the liars.”

Al-Nūr 24:7

Al-khāmisah refers to the fifth declaration.

Al-Nūr 24:8 then describes four declarations by the woman rejecting the accusation.

Al-Nūr 24:9 reads:

وَالْخَامِسَةَ أَنَّ غَضَبَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا إِنْ كَانَ مِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

wa-al-khāmisata anna ghaḍaba allāhi ʿalayhā in kāna mina al-ṣādiqīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And the fifth is that the wrath of The God be upon her if the man is among the truthful.”

Al-Nūr 24:9

Both words have the same numerical meaning: the fifth item in a specified sequence.

The content of the fifth declaration differs between the parties.

The man’s declaration names the curse of The God; the woman’s names the wrath of The God.

That distinction comes from each declaration’s wording, not from fifth as a number.

In the common reading, wa-al-khāmisatu in verse seven is nominative, whereas wa-al-khāmisata in verse nine is accusative. This case difference concerns syntax and reading, without changing the ordinal’s underlying meaning.

Five acquires a procedural and legal role here as part of the declarations’ sequence.

That role comes from the ruling and the passages’ full construction.

Khāmisah itself does not mean oath, curse, wrath, or proof of guilt: it expresses the fifth position in an order.

“The fifth declaration” should consequently not be confused with “one fifth” or “five witnesses.”

Al-Ṭabarī: the fifth declaration in the sequence of mutual imprecation

8. Khumusahu in Al-Anfāl 8:41: One Fifth of the Spoils

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا غَنِمْتُمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ فَأَنَّ لِلَّهِ خُمُسَهُ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ

wa-iʿlamū annamā ghanimtum min shayʾin fa-anna li-allāhi khumusahu wa-li-al-rasūli wa-li-dhī al-qurbā wa-al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīni wa-ibni al-sabīli

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Know that, of whatever you gain as spoils, one fifth belongs to The God, the Messenger, close relatives, orphans, those in need, and the stranded traveler.”

Al-Anfāl 8:41

Khumusahu has two components:

Khumus means one fifth.

The attached pronoun -hu refers to what was gained as spoils.

Khumusahu therefore means one fifth of it.

It is one of five equal parts of the whole property: twenty percent.

This is neither a count of five nor a fifth position, but a specified financial proportion.

A fifth is not five separate shares

If property is divided into five equal parts, one part is its khums.

This does not mean that each named person or group necessarily receives a separate fifth of the whole.

The verse uses the singular khumusahu, then names a collection of claimants or uses.

The amount is clear, but its distribution among the named groups is a separate question.

خ م س alone does not determine the number or equality of subsidiary shares or the procedure for distribution.

These require the whole verse’s construction and related evidence.

What is the fifth taken from?

Before khumusahu comes:

أَنَّمَا غَنِمْتُمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ

annamā ghanimtum min shayʾin

The fifth thus applies to what ghanimtum identifies.

That verb belongs to غ ن م, not خ م س.

Ghanima and ghanīmah concern obtaining benefit or spoils. Al-Anfāl discusses battle, property, and the division of spoils.

Al-Anfāl 8:41 mentions yawm al-furqān and yawm iltaqā al-jamʿān: the day when the two groups met.

This provides a clear connection to battle.

The legislative scope of ghanimtum min shayʾ, including its limits or extension to other earnings, cannot be settled solely through khums.

Khumus determines the proportion; ghanimtum and the context identify the subject of the ruling.

A full investigation of financial khums therefore requires separate study of غ ن م, Al-Anfāl’s setting, and verses concerning property.

Does khums itself mean a permanent tax?

Lexically, no.

Khumus names a fraction, not the full conditions of a financial system.

Al-Anfāl 8:41 places it in a legislative construction, specifying a proportion of the property at issue.

The fraction alone does not establish that everyone at every time must pay twenty percent of all income, savings, or assets under identical conditions.

That ruling would require independent evidence about its subject, addressees, and application.

Conversely, the word’s mathematical meaning does not justify ignoring the verse’s explicit financial instruction.

The numerical meaning of khums must be distinguished from the financial ruling expressed through it.

The verse specifies a proportion, but implementation requires its wording and related evidence, rather than a legal definition imposed on the root in advance.

Al-Ṭabarī: the spoils context and differing allocations of the fifth

9. Comparison with Related Roots and Families

KH M S (khams) and R B ʿ (rubʿ)

Rubʿ means one quarter, whereas khums means one fifth.

Dividing a whole into four equal portions makes each a quarter; dividing it into five makes each a fifth.

This distinction is entirely numerical.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 contains inheritance fractions, including rubuʿ and thumun.

Their lexical meanings do not themselves identify who inherits in which circumstances.

The same principle applies to khums in Al-Anfāl 8:41.

Study of R B ʿ

KH M S (khamsah) and S D S (suds)

Suds belongs to the family of six and means one sixth.

Khums means one fifth.

Ordinally, khāmis means fifth and sādis sixth.

These numbers appear together in Al-Kahf 18:22 and Al-Mujādilah 58:7.

Count and order must nevertheless be distinguished: in خَمْسَةٌ سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ, the first number counts people and the second gives the dog’s place in the group.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:7, five likewise counts the private conversation’s participants, while sixth serves the verse’s expression of divine encompassing awareness.

KH M S (khums) and GH N M (ghanīmah)

Khums is a numerical proportion.

Ghanīmah denotes something gained, potentially subject to financial distribution in particular contexts.

Al-Anfāl 8:41 combines them, but their roles differ.

Ghanimtum identifies the property; khumusahu specifies the proportion.

Khums should therefore not mean spoils, nor should spoils be defined as one fifth.

KH M S (khams) and KH M Ṣ (khamṣ)

خ م ص is a separate root concerning hunger and a hollow belly.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 contains makhmaṣah.

Despite their similar sounds, khams and khamṣ have different letters and established meanings.

Khams means five, whereas makhmaṣah concerns hunger and need for food.

Resemblance should not connect five with hunger or necessity.

KH M S (khams) and ʿ SH R (ʿashr)

ʿAshr concerns ten; its fractional form ʿushr means one tenth.

Khums means one fifth.

Mathematically, one fifth equals two tenths, but that equivalence does not make financial terms associated with the fractions identical.

If a financial system prescribes ʿushr or khums for something, its subject and conditions require separate identification.

Their rulings cannot be extracted from the numerical relationship between the fractions.

Study of ʿ SH R

10. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Each derivative should be translated according to its numerical role.

Khamsah means “five.”

Khamsīna means “fifty.”

Khāmisah in Al-Nūr means “fifth.”

Khumus means “one fifth.”

Contextual features such as declarations, spoils, angels, or duration should not become part of the number’s own meaning.

Brief root meaning

Five: a count of five, the fifth position, and the proportion one fifth of a whole.

Meanings in Qur’anic applications

Khamsati — Āl ʿImrān 3:125: five, in “five thousand angels.”

Khamsatun — Al-Kahf 18:22: five people, one of the reported counts of the cave companions.

Khamsatin — Al-Mujādilah 58:7: five people in a private conversation.

Khamsīna — Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14: fifty years, excepted from a thousand.

Khamsīna — Al-Maʿārij 70:4: fifty, in fifty thousand years.

Al-khāmisatu — Al-Nūr 24:7: the fifth declaration in the man’s sequence.

Al-khāmisata — Al-Nūr 24:9: the fifth declaration in the woman’s sequence.

Khumusahu — Al-Anfāl 8:41: one fifth of what was gained as spoils.

Conclusion

Arabic خ م س centers on five. Unlike roots with widely separated semantic fields, this family’s principal derivatives all concern counting five, the fifth position, or the proportion one fifth.

Early Arabic gives the number specialized everyday applications: camel-watering intervals, fivefold army organization, measurements of implements and garments, and division of property into five shares.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period shows the family in descriptions of weapon measurements and pastoral conditions.

The Qur’an uses five in counting angels and people, expressing duration, ordering declarations, and specifying a financial proportion.

The numerical meaning remains throughout, but context identifies its subject and function.

Five in Al-Kahf 18:22 belongs to a reported claim; in Al-Mujādilah 58:7 it counts people; in Al-Nūr 24:7 and Al-Nūr 24:9 it specifies the fifth declaration; in Al-Anfāl 8:41, khumus is one fifth of the property at issue.

The root’s fundamental meaning is five and its numerical relationships. The root alone cannot verify a historical report, determine a ruling’s legal conditions, or extend a financial obligation’s scope.

This distinction preserves the word’s clear meaning while allowing theological, legal, and historical questions to be studied through each verse’s own construction and context. Brief root meaning: Five: a count of five, the fifth position, and the proportion one fifth of a whole.
