The Qur’anic Root A W H

Sighing and lament: from expressing sorrow and pain to compassion, reverent fear, and supplication

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَوَّاه
(awwāh)
Qur’anic form: pronounced or frequent sighing and its associated response

لَأَوَّاهٌ
(la-awwāhun)
Qur’anic form with emphatic lām; Al-Tawbah 9:114

أَوَّهَ / تَأَوَّهَ
(awwaha / taʾawwaha)
Lexical verb citation forms: uttering a sigh; not Qur’anic

تَأَوَّهُ
(taʾawwahu)
Imperfect in the poetic example, shortened from tataʾawwahu; not Qur’anic

آهِ / أَوْهِ
(āhi / awhi)
Arabic expressions of sighing; not Qur’anic

آهَة
(āhah)
Noun for a sigh in the poetic example; not Qur’anic

The Arabic root A W H relates to sighing, expressing sorrow, and uttering a sound prompted by pain, compassion, or concern. أَوَّهَ (awwaha) and تَأَوَّهَ (taʾawwaha) describe someone sighing and expressing grief or emotion. أَوَّاه (awwāh) characterizes a person in whom this response is frequent or pronounced.

The Qur’an uses awwāh only of Abraham: once in the account of his seeking forgiveness for his father, and again during his discussion concerning Lot’s people. In both cases it accompanies ḥalīm, “forbearing”; in Hūd, munīb, “turning toward,” is also added.

These contexts show that Abraham’s being awwāh should not be confined to outward moaning or weeping. He is not indifferent to others’ fate, acts with forbearance, and turns to The God in his concern. Supplication, forbearance, and turning toward The God nevertheless remain distinct concepts; they should not all be absorbed into awwāh’s lexical meaning.

1. Linguistic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The Qur’an uses only awwāh: لَأَوَّاهٌ (la-awwāhun), with an emphatic lām, in Al-Tawbah 9:114, and أَوَّاهٌ (awwāhun) in Hūd 11:75.

It has the faʿʿāl pattern, which here can express a feature’s frequency or prominence. Awwāh therefore denotes more than a single sigh: it describes someone in whom sighing and its associated inward response are pronounced.

Arabic also records awwaha, taʾawwaha, آهِ (āhi), أَوْهِ (awhi), and آهَة (āhah). These exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an, but help explain the family’s meaning.

A sigh: the sound of sorrow and emotion

In earlier Arabic, āhi and awhi are sounds uttered in pain, sorrow, regret, or emotion. Someone grieving a loved one’s loss may sigh, as may someone experiencing severe pain.

The sigh may express the person’s own suffering or sorrow for someone else. Taʾawwuh is therefore not confined to complaining of physical pain.

Taʾawwaha expresses this response and may denote sighing or voicing sorrow.

These tangible uses also support a broader human description: someone affected by another’s suffering, danger, or fate, whose response appears in conduct, speech, or supplication.

The direct meaning and extensions of usage must nevertheless remain distinct. Sighing signals an inward state; compassion and supplication may express that state, but not every sigh is necessarily a prayer or a sign of mercy.

An example from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Al-Muthaqqib Al-ʿAbdī states:

إِذَا مَا قُمْتُ أَرْحَلُهَا بِلَيْلٍ
تَأَوَّهُ آهَةَ الرَّجُلِ الْحَزِينِ

idhā mā qumtu arḥaluhā bi-laylin / taʾawwahu āhata al-rajuli al-ḥazīni

“When I rise at night to saddle that she-camel for travel, she sighs like a sorrowful man.”

The poet likens his she-camel’s sound while being prepared for travel to the sigh of a grieving person.

Taʾawwuh here is tangible and perceptible. He is not saying that the camel prays or repents of sin: its sound is compared to a sorrowful person’s sigh.

Lexical transmissions record variants, including ahhah in place of āhah and the wording tahawwahu hāhata. The supplied taʾawwahu āhata al-rajuli al-ḥazīni occurs in the poet’s attributed collection and attests earlier sighing usage. Taʾawwahu here is an imperfect form shortened from tataʾawwahu by dropping one tāʾ; its subject is the she-camel, rather than the poet. The verse is attributed to the pre-Islamic period, but the transmission itself does not establish its precise composition date.

From sighing to a human characteristic

Arabic dictionaries use awwāh of someone who sighs frequently, is sorrowful or tenderhearted, or turns to prayer and supplication.

Reported explanations include frequent supplication, compassion and tenderness of heart, humility, frequent remembrance of The God, and understanding of religion.

These explanations are not all on the same level. “Frequently sighing” is closer to the word’s structure and lexical family. “Frequently supplicating” or “humble” may instead describe the conduct of someone whose sighing expresses a relationship with The God.

One report even glosses awwāh as “believer” in an Ethiopic language. That report alone cannot establish an independent derivation from that language and should not replace clear Arabic evidence.

Lexical meaning and religious application must therefore be distinguished. Someone may sigh over personal sorrow, feel another’s pain, or supplicate out of reverent fear of The God. These conditions relate to the sighing family without being identical.

2. La-awwāhun ḥalīm: Abraham seeking forgiveness for his father

Surah Al-Tawbah states:

وَمَا كَانَ اسْتِغْفَارُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لِأَبِيهِ إِلَّا عَنْ مَوْعِدَةٍ وَعَدَهَا إِيَّاهُ فَلَمَّا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُ أَنَّهُ عَدُوٌّ لِلَّهِ تَبَرَّأَ مِنْهُ إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَأَوَّاهٌ حَلِيمٌ

wa-mā kāna istighfāru ibrāhīma li-abīhi illā ʿan mawʿidatin waʿadahā iyyāhu fa-lammā tabayyana lahu annahu ʿaduwwun li-llāhi tabarraʾa minhu inna ibrāhīma la-awwāhun ḥalīmun

“Abraham’s seeking forgiveness for his father was only because of a promise he had made to him. When it became clear to him that he was an enemy of The God, he dissociated himself from him. Abraham was indeed given to sighing and supplication, forbearing.” (Al-Tawbah 9:114)

The verse concerns Abraham’s relationship with his father. Abraham had sought forgiveness for him, but dissociated himself when his father’s enmity toward The God became clear.

The explanation ends by describing Abraham as awwāh and ḥalīm.

Both descriptions matter in understanding his conduct.

Concern for another’s fate

In Maryam 19:46, Abraham’s father speaks harshly and threatens him. In Maryam 19:47, Abraham responds with peace and promises to ask his Lord to forgive him.

His seeking forgiveness thus occurs within a difficult relationship marked by opposition. Despite his father’s harshness, Abraham at that stage chooses supplication and calm speech.

Here, awwāh may express his deep concern and compassion: his father’s fate matters to him, and he voices that concern by praying for forgiveness.

Awwāh itself does not mean “someone seeking forgiveness for his father.” Seeking forgiveness is the particular conduct already stated; awwāh describes a broader feature of Abraham’s character.

Nor does the verse state that he was necessarily crying loudly or continually moaning. The verse itself does not establish such a picture.

Compassion and its limits

After mentioning Abraham’s seeking forgiveness, the Qur’an also speaks of his dissociation.

Both actions occur in one verse. He initially seeks forgiveness for his father, then dissociates himself once the situation described becomes clear.

Being awwāh here therefore does not mean disregarding truth or every boundary without qualification. His compassion and forbearance accompany adherence to what has become clear to him.

Tenderness of heart does not itself imply endorsing the conduct of someone for whom one feels compassion.

Conversely, this verse does not authorize calling every opponent or relative with different beliefs an enemy of The God. It specifies fa-lammā tabayyana lahu, “when it became clear to him”; its scope must remain within that context.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:4 also singles out Abraham’s promise to seek forgiveness for his father while presenting his example. Compassion alone therefore cannot turn that particular action into a universal ruling independently of qualifications in other verses.

Ḥalīm alongside awwāh

Ḥalīm belongs to Ḥ L M and concerns forbearance, self-restraint, and avoiding a hasty reaction.

Alongside awwāh, it gives a clearer picture of Abraham’s conduct. His concern and compassion do not come with violence, haste, or an uncontrolled response.

Ḥalīm and awwāh are not synonyms. Someone may be forbearing yet little affected by another’s suffering; someone else may feel compassion yet fail to restrain anger and reaction.

The Qur’an places these qualities together in Abraham without erasing their distinct meanings.

3. Awwāhun munīb: Abraham and the fate of Lot’s people

Surah Hūd states:

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُنِيبٌ

inna ibrāhīma la-ḥalīmun awwāhun munībun

“Abraham was indeed forbearing, given to sighing and supplication, turning toward The God.” (Hūd 11:75)

This verse appears within the account of divine messengers visiting Abraham.

In Hūd 11:69, the messengers bring him good news. In Hūd 11:70, when their hands do not reach for the food, Abraham becomes uneasy, and they announce that they have been sent to Lot’s people.

Hūd 11:74 then says that when his fear had passed and the good news reached him, Abraham discussed and pleaded with The God concerning Lot’s people.

In this context the Qur’an describes him as ḥalīm, awwāh, and munīb.

Being affected by others’ fate

Awwāh’s placement amid Abraham’s discussion of Lot’s people and the descriptions of forbearance and turning toward The God helps explain its meaning.

He is discussing the fate of people whose punishment has been announced. This discussion, together with awwāh, accords with a deeply concerned, compassionate disposition.

His being awwāh is therefore not confined to his relationship with his father. The same feature appears in the account of Lot’s people, alongside forbearance and turning toward The God.

Verse 75 does not reproduce the entire discussion, however. Awwāh alone cannot reconstruct every motive, request, or precise emotion. What is clear is the description’s association with a forbearing person who turns toward The God and discusses Lot’s people’s fate.

Munīb alongside awwāh

Munīb belongs to N W B and describes someone returning or turning toward. In this verse, that turning is toward The God.

Awwāh concerns emotional response, sighing, and supplication; munīb concerns returning or turning toward. They may coexist in one person without meaning the same thing.

Someone may be deeply affected by another’s suffering, but that alone does not constitute inābah, turning back. Conversely, turning toward The God need not be accompanied by sighing or outward sorrow.

Together, these three descriptions portray Abraham as forbearing, deeply affected, and turning toward The God.

Forbearance and supplication before the divine command

In Hūd 11:76, Abraham is told to leave the discussion: his Lord’s command has come, and the people face a punishment that will not be turned back.

This continuation matters to awwāh’s role. Despite his concern and discussion concerning them, Abraham encounters a decisive divine command within that same context.

Being awwāh neither signals enmity toward The God nor gives authority to alter every divine command. It describes Abraham’s emotional and devotional character, while surrounding verses clarify the event and the discussion’s limits.

Understanding awwāh here as someone supplicating to The God out of compassion and concern fits the context. Its lexical meaning should nevertheless not become “arguing” or “requesting the cancellation of punishment.”

4. Distinguishing related roots

A W H (awwāh) compared with A W B (awwāb)

Study of A W B

Awwāh and awwāb resemble one another in spelling and morphological pattern, but come from different roots.

Awwāh belongs to the sighing family; awwāb belongs to A W B, returning.

The Qur’an uses awwāb of those who return frequently or repeatedly turn toward The God, such as David in Ṣād 38:17 and Job in Ṣād 38:44.

Awwāh may likewise accompany turning toward The God in a religious context, but its principal meaning is not returning. In Hūd 11:75, munīb itself expresses returning and turning toward alongside awwāh.

Resemblance in form or religious application should therefore not collapse the words into one.

A W H (awwāh) compared with Ḥ L M (ḥalīm)

Study of Ḥ L M

Ḥalīm concerns forbearance and restraint of reaction, whereas awwāh concerns sighing, emotional response, and, where context supports it, supplication and compassion.

Both Abraham verses place these descriptions together. Their association shows compatibility without establishing identical meanings.

A W H (awwāh) compared with N W B (anāba)

Study of N W B

Anāba concerns returning and turning toward; munīb describes someone doing so.

Hūd 11:75 contains both awwāh and munīb. Their association connects emotional response and supplication with turning toward The God, without making one the definition of the other.

A W H (awwāh) compared with D AYN W (daʿā)

Study of D AYN W (D ʿ W)

Qur’anic daʿā concerns calling, summoning, and requesting.

Awwāh may relate to supplication in a context such as Abraham’s seeking forgiveness. Yet not everyone who supplicates is necessarily awwāh, and not every sigh is a supplication.

In Al-Tawbah 9:114, seeking forgiveness is Abraham’s particular action, while awwāh describes his character. They are related but lexically distinct.

A W H (awwāh) compared with R Ḥ M (raḥima)

Study of R Ḥ M

Raḥmah concerns kindness and wishing good for another. In human use, compassion and tenderness of heart may accompany mercy.

Some lexical explanations likewise apply awwāh to a tenderhearted, compassionate person, but its family is more closely tied to sighing and expressing emotion.

Mercy need not involve sorrow or sighing: someone may help another out of kindness without showing outward grief or distress.

“Very kind” may therefore convey part of awwāh’s contextual sense without retaining its full linguistic character.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

“One who sighs frequently” is close to awwāh’s lexical family. In English, however, it may evoke only someone grieving or continually lamenting.

That restriction does not fit Abraham’s two occurrences. Al-Tawbah 9:114 concerns seeking forgiveness for his father and forbearance; Hūd 11:75 concerns discussion of Lot’s people, with awwāh alongside ḥalīm and munīb.

“Given to sighing and supplication” therefore preserves the link with the root while also bringing out some of the context’s devotional direction.

“Compassionate and supplicating” also explains the contextual sense, but moves further from directly sighing. “Tenderhearted” or “softhearted” alone does not bring out supplication.

“Frequently supplicating” is an established explanation, but choosing it alone blurs the distinction between awwāh and words such as daʿʿāʾ, “one who calls or prays frequently.”

For Al-Tawbah 9:114, the proposed rendering is:

“Abraham was indeed given to sighing and supplication, forbearing.”

For Hūd 11:75:

“Abraham was indeed forbearing, given to sighing and supplication, turning toward The God.”

In more fluent explanatory prose, “compassionate and supplicating” is suitable, alongside a clear explanation of its semantic connection with sighing and lament.

Conclusion

The Arabic root A W H concerns sighing and expressing sorrow, pain, and emotion. In people, that response may arise from personal suffering, compassion for another, concern, reverent fear, or supplication.

Awwāh describes someone in whom this response is pronounced and frequent. The Qur’an applies it only to Abraham, both times alongside ḥalīm.

In Al-Tawbah 9:114, his being awwāh accompanies seeking forgiveness for his father and forbearance within a difficult relationship. In Hūd 11:75, it occurs in his discussion concerning Lot’s people, alongside munīb.

Abraham’s being awwāh therefore cannot be confined to weeping, moaning, or repenting of sin. Nor should supplication, mercy, and turning back be treated as direct synonyms without regard to their differences.

A W H’s semantic core is sighing and expressing emotion prompted by pain, sorrow, or concern. Applied to Abraham, awwāh describes someone whose tenderness of heart and deep concern accompany prayer and supplication: a forbearing person who is not indifferent to others’ fate and turns toward The God.
