Core meaning: S D S is a numerical family used in the Qur’an for the sixth position in a group and a one-sixth share of a whole. Sādis denotes the sixth member; sudus denotes one of six equal parts. The family is historically and morphologically linked with S T T, the number six, but its forms have different structures and uses.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

Arabic S D S concerns the number six and forms derived from it.

Sādis means sixth: someone or something occupying the sixth place in a count or order.

Sudus means one-sixth: one of six equal parts of something.

Verbal usage also records sadasa al-qawma for becoming a group’s sixth member, or, with a different conjugation, taking one-sixth of its wealth.

The family’s primary use expresses a numerical relationship, unlike roots concerning movement, physical action or natural conditions.

Six, sixth and one-sixth nevertheless differ.

Six gives a quantity.

Sixth gives a member’s position in a count or order.

One-sixth expresses the ratio of one part to a whole divided into six equal parts.

Arabic recognizes this distinction, which is also clear in the Qur’an.

An early Arabic witness: sixth in a group

Early lexical and grammatical sources transmit this verse:

إِذَا مَا عُدَّ أَرْبَعَةٌ فِسَالٌ
فَزَوْجُكِ خَامِسٌ وَأَبُوكِ سَادِي

idhā mā ʿudda arbaʿatun fisālun / fa-zawjuki khāmisun wa-abūki sādī

Translation:

“If four base men are counted, your husband is their fifth and your father their sixth.”

Fisāl is the plural of fasl, meaning base or dishonorable people.

Khāmis denotes the fifth person.

Sādī here denotes the sixth.

The usual form is sādis; the poetic sādī substitutes yāʾ for the final sīn.

The verse illustrates two features of Arabic.

First, being sixth means occupying the sixth position in a count.

Second, some early uses attest sādī alongside sādis.

Early sources attribute the verse to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī, while some collections also attribute it to Imruʾ al-Qays.

Al-Nābighah lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The verse’s attribution is disputed as well.

It cannot therefore be treated as a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

Its transmission in lexical and grammatical works nevertheless documents sādis and its variant sādī in the early Arabic tradition.

Numerical uses in Arab life

Lexicons record forms of S D S in various areas of life.

Sādis al-qawm is the sixth member of a group.

Sudus al-māl is one-sixth of the property.

Asdasa al-qawmu means that the group reached six members.

Al-sids also names a camel-watering interval associated with returning on the sixth day. Sources differ in counting the intervening days and whether watering days are included, so this should not be reduced to an unqualified “five full days without water.”

Sadīs is also recorded for a stage of growth and dentition in certain livestock.

Six thus appears beyond directly counting people: it also concerns dividing wealth, scheduling turns and describing animal growth stages.

Recording these usages in later lexicons does not establish that all circulated in the same form before Islam.

What can be stated securely is that sādis and sudus are established Classical Arabic forms for sixth and one-sixth; Qur’anic usage demonstrates those meanings.

A historical definition of the family

In numerical usage, S D S expresses a relationship to six: sādis is the sixth member in a count or group, and sudus is one of six equal parts.

The family is related to Arabic sitt and sittah.

Their historical relationship should be examined separately from their direct meanings.

2. The historical relationship with S T T

Arabic has two familiar structures for six and associated forms:

Sitt and sittah.

Sādis and sudus.

The first group has a doubled tāʾ.

The second retains dāl and sīn.

This difference matters for the history of the number’s structure.

Lexical analysis of the earlier form

Early Arabic lexical analysis proposes earlier forms sids and sidsah for sitt and sittah.

On that account, sound changes transformed dāl and sīn, eventually producing the doubled tāʾ.

One supporting observation is the diminutive of sittah.

Arabic records sudaysah as that diminutive.

The reappearance of dāl has been taken as evidence connecting the families.

Sādis and sudus likewise preserve the dāl-and-sīn structure in derivatives of six.

These forms clarify the morphological relationship.

Morphological analysis must nevertheless be distinguished from a definitive historical reconstruction.

Early lexicographers reconstructed an earlier form from existing structures. Their analysis matters morphologically but does not by itself date every stage of sound change.

Why examine the families separately?

In the Qur’anic morphological index used here, sittah and sittīn are placed under S T T.

Sādis and sudus are placed under S D S.

This is a useful indexing convention for distinguishing the attested forms, not proof that every dictionary must classify them separately.

Nor does separate indexing establish complete historical independence.

Sittah denotes six.

Sādis identifies the sixth member.

Sudus is one-sixth of a whole.

This is a real difference of use, not a disagreement about the value of six.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Sādis

It means sixth.

In ordinary usage, it identifies someone or something occupying the sixth position in a count or sequence.

If five people are present and another joins them, the newcomer becomes the sixth member.

Being sixth does not always mean arriving later than the other five.

All six may be present from the beginning, with one counted sixth.

Numerical position and chronological order should therefore remain distinct.

Sādisuhum occurs twice in the Qur’an.

Sudus and suds

Both are recorded Arabic forms for one-sixth.

Sudus is one of six equal parts of a whole.

Its plural is asdās.

Al-sudusu occurs three times in the Qur’an.

All three are in Al-Nisāʾ’s inheritance passages.

Sadasa, yasdusu

One verbal usage concerns taking one-sixth of a group’s wealth.

سَدَسَ الْقَوْمَ

sadasa al-qawma

“He took one-sixth of the group’s wealth.”

What is taken is a property share, not necessarily one person out of six.

Sadasa, yasdisu

Another usage concerns becoming a group’s sixth member.

سَدَسَ الْقَوْمَ

sadasa al-qawma

“He became the group’s sixth member.”

Lexicons record the different imperfect vowels in these uses.

Verb structure and subject matter therefore help determine meaning.

Asdasa

This is the Form IV verb.

In counting, asdasa al-qawmu means that the group reached six members.

The form also occurs in camel-watering terminology and certain animal growth stages.

Sadīs

Some uses give it the meaning one-sixth.

In livestock terminology, it also denotes a growth or dentition stage in camels or sheep.

It does not necessarily mean every animal so described is six years old: dentition stages occur at different ages in different animals.

An age or tooth-stage term should not be converted into years of life without examination.

Sudāsī and musaddas

Sudāsī describes something associated with six units or parts.

Geometrically, musaddas denotes a six-sided figure.

In poetic meter, it can denote a structure of six components.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Forms actually used in the Qur’an

S D S occurs five times in two forms:

  • Al-sudusu twice in Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and once in Al-Nisāʾ 4:12.
  • Sādisuhum in Al-Kahf 18:22 and Al-Mujādilah 58:7.

All Qur’anic uses therefore concern either the sixth position in a group or a one-sixth property share.

4. Sādisuhum in two different contexts

Sādisuhum occurs twice in the Qur’an.

Its form is identical in both.

Its referent and the verses’ subjects differ.

Al-Kahf 18:22: counting the Companions of the Cave and their dog

Al-Kahf 18:22 says:

وَيَقُولُونَ خَمْسَةٌ سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ رَجْمًا بِالْغَيْبِ

wa-yaqūlūna khamsatun sādisuhum kalbuhum rajman bi-al-ghayb

Al-Kahf 18:22

Translation:

“They say: five, their dog being their sixth—guessing at the unseen.”

Khamsatun gives the number five.

Sādisuhum denotes the sixth member.

Kalbuhum identifies that member as their dog.

The reported statement therefore counts five humans and a dog.

The number’s meaning must be distinguished from the report’s truth.

Does the Qur’an confirm there were five people?

Al-Kahf 18:22 reports several claims about the Companions’ number.

Some say three, with their dog fourth.

Others say five, with their dog sixth.

Still others propose seven, with their dog eighth.

The verse includes rajman bi-al-ghayb, guessing at the unseen.

Muḥammad is then commanded to say that his Lord knows their number best.

Merely mentioning sādisuhum does not definitively confirm that the Companions numbered five.

The word appears inside a reported claim.

Its linguistic meaning is clear: the sixth member.

Whether that count is correct is a separate question.

This distinction matters because the Qur’an can report human statements or conjectures without endorsing them.

Here sixth is part of a reported count, not a conclusive finding about the Companions’ number.

Is the dog counted as human?

The sentence counts the dog as the sixth member of the group.

That does not make it human.

Sādisuhum expresses a numerical relationship.

Kalbuhum identifies the kind of being occupying that position.

Sixth is a numerical description and does not itself determine a being’s nature or species.

Al-Mujādilah 58:7: sixth in a context of divine knowledge

Al-Mujādilah 58:7 says:

مَا يَكُونُ مِنْ نَجْوَىٰ ثَلَاثَةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ رَابِعُهُمْ وَلَا خَمْسَةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ سَادِسُهُمْ

mā yakūnu min najwā thalāthatin illā huwa rābiʿuhum wa-lā khamsatin illā huwa sādisuhum

Al-Mujādilah 58:7

Translation:

“There is no private conversation among three but He is their fourth, nor among five but He is their sixth.”

Sādisuhum here refers to The God.

Its context differs fundamentally from Al-Kahf 18:22.

The verse begins with His knowledge of everything in the heavens and earth.

It continues that whether fewer or more converse privately, He is with them wherever they are.

It then says He will inform them of their deeds on the Day of Resurrection.

The subject is His comprehensive knowledge: human speech and actions remain unhidden from Him.

Is The God a sixth person sitting in a gathering like a human?

Sādisuhum is meaningful in the verse, but the kind of accompaniment must be understood from context.

The verse begins and ends with divine knowledge.

It does not limit that accompaniment to a particular place:

أَيْنَ مَا كَانُوا

ayna mā kānū

Al-Mujādilah 58:7

“Wherever they are.”

Sādisuhum does not establish that The God sits bodily beside five people or occupies a point in their gathering’s physical space.

The expression concerns divine knowledge and encompassing awareness.

Conversely, the difference between The God and created beings does not render sādisuhum meaningless.

The Qur’an speaks of His being with them and connects this with knowledge of their private conversations.

The numerical wording remains, but the nature of that presence cannot be derived from counting human bodies.

Comparing the two uses

In Al-Kahf 18:22, a reported claim identifies the Companions’ dog as sixth.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:7, sixth is predicated of The God in the context of His comprehensive knowledge.

In both, sādis expresses a numerical relation to five.

The first counts beings in a historical report.

The second concerns divine accompaniment and knowledge.

The numerical wording of sādis remains in both, while the relationship expressed must be understood through each verse’s subject and context.

5. Al-sudusu: one-sixth in inheritance passages

The other three uses occur in Al-Nisāʾ.

Al-sudusu concerns one-sixth of property.

It does not identify a sixth person, but a specified share in inheritance.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11: each parent’s share

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 says:

وَلِأَبَوَيْهِ لِكُلِّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا السُّدُسُ مِمَّا تَرَكَ إِنْ كَانَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ

wa-li-abawayhi li-kulli wāḥidin minhumā al-sudusu mimmā taraka in kāna lahu walad

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11

Translation:

“For his parents, for each of them, one-sixth of what he leaves, if he has a child.”

Al-sudusu gives the one-sixth share.

The sentence’s structure matters beyond the number.

Li-kulli wāḥidin minhumā specifies the share for each parent separately.

It does not assign the parents a combined one-sixth.

Each is assigned one-sixth.

In kāna lahu walad states the condition.

The share should not be generalized to all inheritance situations without considering offspring.

The fraction is clear; its recipient and conditions come from the sentence’s other components.

The mother’s share when there are siblings

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 then presents another situation.

If the deceased has no child and the parents inherit, the mother is assigned one-third.

It then says:

فَإِنْ كَانَ لَهُ إِخْوَةٌ فَلِأُمِّهِ السُّدُسُ

fa-in kāna lahu ikhwatun fa-li-ummihi al-sudus

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11

Translation:

“If he has siblings, his mother receives one-sixth.”

Al-sudusu still means one-sixth.

Its recipient and condition differ from the preceding use.

The first passage assigns each parent one-sixth when there is a child.

This passage relates the mother’s share to the presence of siblings.

Repeating sudus does not repeat an identical legal situation.

The fraction remains the same while its conditions differ.

That difference comes from the complete sentence, not a new meaning of sudus.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12: a brother or sister in the kalālah situation

After spouses’ shares, Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 addresses inheritance in a kalālah situation.

Part of the verse says:

وَلَهُ أَخٌ أَوْ أُخْتٌ فَلِكُلِّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا السُّدُسُ

wa-lahu akhun aw ukhtun fa-li-kulli wāḥidin minhumā al-sudus

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12

Translation:

“And he has a brother or sister, then for each of the two, one-sixth.”

The fraction belongs to a brother or sister in the stated situation.

Akhun aw ukhtun presents the two possibilities.

Li-kulli wāḥidin minhumā assigns one-sixth to each of those descriptions in the case under discussion.

This provision occurs within the kalālah context. Determining the relevant sibling relationship and the meaning of kalālah requires separate study alongside Al-Nisāʾ 4:176; sudus alone resolves neither question.

The provision should not be detached from its condition and generalized to every sibling-inheritance case.

The continuation states that if they are more, they share one-third.

The verse distinguishes one beneficiary’s share from multiple beneficiaries.

An individual sixth and a shared portion

The first case assigns one-sixth to a single brother or sister in that situation.

The continuation gives a shared third when their number increases.

The individual fraction should therefore not be repeated indiscriminately for any number of heirs.

Generalizing the single-beneficiary provision could contradict the shared portion specified by the verse.

Al-sudus translates straightforwardly as one-sixth, but the full inheritance arrangement cannot be derived from that word alone.

Bequests and debts

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 also connect inheritance provisions with bequests and debts.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 includes ghayra muḍārr.

This qualification addresses not causing harm in connection with bequests and debts.

One-sixth thus functions within provisions that also address heirs’ rights, debts, bequests and prevention of harm.

Sudus itself does not mean a bequest, debt or justice.

Those are distinct concepts conveyed by other words.

Is one-sixth a fixed share in every inheritance case?

Mathematically, one-sixth is constant.

Who receives it is not constant in every situation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 discusses parents’ shares when there is offspring.

The same verse assigns the mother one-sixth in another situation as well.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 uses one-sixth for a brother or sister in the kalālah context.

The number is constant; the legal conditions differ.

Sudus consistently means one-sixth. What property is divided, who receives the share and under what conditions are determined by the full provision.

6. Numerical and semantic distinctions

S D S and S T T

The families are historically related, but their Qur’anic forms serve different roles.

Sittah gives the number six.

Sādis identifies the sixth member.

Sudus denotes one-sixth of a whole.

For example, six people may form a group.

The sixth person is one member of that group.

One-sixth need not be a person: it is a proportion of a whole and can concern wealth, quantity, area or anything else divisible.

This distinction follows from the forms’ numerical structures.

S D S and R B ʿ

Rābiʿ means fourth.

Al-Kahf 18:22 uses rābiʿuhum and sādisuhum in reported human claims.

Al-Mujādilah 58:7 uses both for divine accompaniment of people conversing privately.

Their ordinal structures are similar.

Fourth and sixth nevertheless differ numerically.

Each sentence establishes the description’s subject and referent.

S D S and TH L TH

Thuluth denotes one-third.

Sudus is one-sixth.

Both occur in Al-Nisāʾ’s inheritance passages.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 assigns the mother a third or a sixth in different situations.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 places an individual sixth beside a shared third.

An heir’s designation alone does not determine the share: the provision’s conditions matter.

The fractions themselves have clear numerical meanings without invented additional senses.

S D S and KH M S

Khams means five.

Sādis identifies the sixth member.

They occur together in Al-Kahf 18:22 and Al-Mujādilah 58:7.

Five people or members form the basis of these expressions, and sādis introduces the sixth.

This numerical relation does not establish identity of kind, nature or standing between the sixth and the five.

In Al-Kahf, the reported count includes the dog as sixth.

In Al-Mujādilah, sixth is predicated of The God in the context of His comprehensive knowledge.

Counting alone does not determine beings’ nature or their relationship.

S D S and S D D

The roots resemble each other in writing but differ in their final letter.

S D S ends in sīn.

S D D ends in dāl.

Sudus concerns the number six.

The S D D family concerns blocking passages in material usage and correctness and soundness in its speech-related branch.

Visual resemblance does not justify conflating the families’ meanings.

7. Conclusion

Arabic S D S concerns six and its associated forms.

Numerically, sādis denotes the sixth member and sudus one-sixth of a whole.

Early sources also record sadasa al-qawma, asdasa al-qawmu and sadīs.

These occur in counting people, dividing wealth, watering schedules and livestock terminology.

Early poetry attests sādī for sixth, although the cited verse’s attribution and precise date remain uncertain.

S D S and S T T have a recognized morphological relationship.

Early lexicographers proposed forms with dāl and sīn behind sitt and sittah.

Sādis and sudus clarify that relationship without independently establishing every historical stage of sound change.

S D S occurs five times in the Qur’an.

Al-Kahf 18:22 uses sādisuhum of the Companions’ dog in a reported human claim.

The ordinal relation does not establish the reported count’s truth.

Al-Mujādilah 58:7 uses sādisuhum in a context of divine knowledge of private conversations.

This concerns divine accompaniment and awareness; it should not be turned into bodily attendance like a human in a gathering.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 contain three inheritance uses of al-sudus.

One-sixth remains its meaning throughout.

The conditions for receiving the share differ.

It is assigned to each parent in one case, to the mother in another, and to a brother or sister in the kalālah context.

These distinctions come from conditions, pronouns and complete sentences, not from the number itself.

Qur’anic S D S expresses the sixth position in a group and a one-sixth share of a whole. Both depend on six, but their grammatical roles and uses differ.

Translation is not the principal difficulty here: sādis and sudus have clear equivalents.

The study’s value lies in distinguishing sixth from six, one-sixth from sixth position, and numerical meaning from historical, legal or theological conclusions.

A number specifies quantity or position; context establishes the relationship, conditions and subject matter.
