Sin and attributable wrongdoing; the tail or rear of something; a share or portion

DH N B forms an Arabic family with several branches. ذَنْب denotes sin, fault, or wrongdoing attributed to a person; ذَنَب names an animal’s tail and, by extension, the rear or trailing part of something; ذَنُوب elsewhere denotes a water bucket or a share received from something.

The branches are established in Arabic, but should not be collapsed into an artificial single definition. Some lexical explanations connect a tail with consequences following an act. That does not make dhanb directly mean consequence, appendage, or punishment in every verse.

The Qur’an uses two principal nominal forms: ذَنْب, whose plural is ذُنُوب, and ذَنُوب, meaning a share. The former concerns wrongdoing, responsibility, forgiveness, and punishment. The latter has a different meaning in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59 and should not be translated “sins.”

The Qur’anic uses illuminate several issues. A person may commit a dhanb and then acknowledge it, seek The God’s forgiveness, or persist in wrongdoing. Sometimes it concerns responsibility toward others, sometimes communities punished for their conduct, and sometimes a question about what sin a defenseless person was killed for exposes the injustice of others.

The Qur’an also uses dhanb concerning Moses and in direct address to Muḥammad. These uses do not permit restricting it beforehand to deliberate grave crime, or discarding its established meaning without evidence wherever a prophet is mentioned.

The root occurs 39 times in 37 verses: 37 instances of ذَنْب and its plural or suffixed forms, and two instances of ذَنُوب in one verse. Verbs such as أَذْنَبَ and the active participle مُذْنِب exist in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic branches and early Arabic usage

Dhanb: sin and wrongdoing

ذَنْب denotes sin, an offense, or wrongful conduct attributed to a person.

One lexical definition reads:

الذَّنْبُ: الْإِثْمُ وَالْجُرْمُ وَالْمَعْصِيَةُ

al-dhanbu al-ithmu wa-al-jurmu wa-al-maʿṣiyah

Meaning: Dhanb is sin, offense, and disobedience.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

أَذْنَبَ الرَّجُلُ: the man committed a sin or fault.

اعْتَرَفَ بِذَنْبِهِ: he acknowledged his sin or offense.

اسْتَغْفَرَ مِنْ ذَنْبِهِ: he sought forgiveness for his sin.

الْمُذْنِبُ: someone who has committed a dhanb.

الذُّنُوبُ: the plural of dhanb, sins.

These uses place dhanb within human conduct and responsibility. The word alone does not determine the act, degree of harm, intention, or extent of responsibility.

For example, deliberately killing someone and unintentionally harming someone can differ greatly in law, ethics, and responsibility. Using a word connected with fault or offense for both does not make their rulings identical.

The act concerned and its circumstances must likewise be examined in the Qur’anic uses.

Dhanab: a tail or rear part

ذَنَب, with a fatḥah on the nūn, names an animal’s tail.

It is used for the tails of horses, camels, foxes, and other animals.

Extended uses apply it to the end or rear of something.

For example:

ذَنَبُ الشَّيْءِ: its rear or trailing part.

أَذْنَابُ الْقَوْمِ: those behind others or following them.

ذِنَابَةُ الْوَادِي: the end or continuation of a watercourse.

مَذَانِبُ التِّلَاعِ is also used for channels carrying water through sloping land and watercourses.

The sense of a trailing or rear part is clear. The bodily term is extended to something’s position at the end of a whole or its following behind.

Dhanab, “tail,” nevertheless differs in vocalization and usage from dhanb, “sin.” Lexical study should preserve the distinction while examining them within one root family.

Was “sin” derived from “tail”?

One lexical analysis records three separate semantic principles for DH N B:

أَحَدُهَا الْجُرْمُ، وَالْآخَرُ مُؤَخَّرُ الشَّيْءِ، وَالثَّالِثُ كَالْحَظِّ وَالنَّصِيبِ

aḥaduhā al-jurmu wa-al-ākharu muʾakhkharu al-shayʾi wa-al-thālithu ka-al-ḥaẓẓi wa-al-naṣīb

Meaning: One is offense, another the rear of something, and the third a share or portion.

This distinction matters because it does not derive every use from one image.

Another analysis connects dhanb with something’s trailing part and applies it to an act whose consequences are unwelcome.

As a tail follows a body, consequences may follow a human act in this analysis.

The image is understandable: much wrongdoing has consequences for the person or others.

The resemblance does not establish that dhanb means “the act’s consequence,” rather than the wrongdoing itself, in every Qur’anic use.

When Al-Tawbah 9:102 says اعْتَرَفُوا بِذُنُوبِهِمْ, the subject is acknowledging their sins and conduct. Likewise, forgiveness of sins should not be converted without evidence solely into removal of natural consequences by deleting the meaning of sin.

The tail-and-consequence connection may explain relationships between some meanings, but is not the direct, fixed definition of dhanb in the Qur’an.

A poetic witness: dhanb as a fault

A poem attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād, a pre-Islamic poet, reads:

ذَنْبِي لِعَبْلَةَ ذَنْبٌ غَيْرُ مُغْتَفَرِ
لَمَّا تَبَلَّجَ صُبْحُ الشَّيْبِ فِي شَعَرِي

dhanbī li-ʿablata dhanbun ghayru mughtafari / lammā taballaja ṣubḥu al-shaybi fī shaʿarī

Translation: My offense in ʿAblah’s eyes is unforgivable, since the dawn of gray hair appeared among my hair.

The poet describes his appearance changing with age, portraying his gray hair as an unforgivable offense in his beloved’s eyes.

The witness matters lexically because dhanb means neither a tail nor the end of something. It concerns something for which a person may be reproached.

Gray hair is not actually a moral sin. The poet uses the word figuratively and ironically to express his beloved’s displeasure.

It illustrates dhanb in a setting of censure without requiring a religious offense or deliberate wrongdoing in every use.

The poem appears in collected versions of ʿAntarah’s poetry. As with much poetry attributed to pre-Islamic authors, assigning every collected verse to the poet requires historical caution. It is an attributed witness, rather than conclusive proof of each verse’s composition date.

Dhanūb: a bucket and a share

The third branch appears in ذَنُوب.

Dhanūb denotes a bucket used to draw water from a well. Many lexical explanations emphasize its containing water.

Explanations differ on the amount: a full bucket, a nearly full one, or a bucket containing some water.

An exact amount, such as half or the whole capacity, should therefore not be made an unquestionable, unchanging part of the meaning.

Where people drew water from wells, the bucket could serve in distributing it. Several users of one well might each receive a turn or share.

Dhanūb is also known in the sense of a share or portion through this use.

ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl’s witness: dhanūb as a portion

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah, known as ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl, requests generosity and support:

وَفِي كُلِّ حَيٍّ قَدْ خَبَطْتَ بِنِعْمَةٍ
فَحَقَّ لِشَأْسٍ مِنْ نَدَاكَ ذَنُوبُ

wa-fī kulli ḥayyin qad khabaṭta bi-niʿmatin / fa-ḥaqqa li-shaʾsin min nadāka dhanūb

Translation: You have spread your bounty through every tribe; so Shaʾs too deserves a share of your generosity.

Dhanūb means a share or portion here.

The poet invokes his addressee’s generosity and asks that someone named Shaʾs benefit from it too.

Some transmissions have قَوْمٍ instead of حَيٍّ. This does not change the meaning of dhanūb in the verse.

The witness appears in early transmissions and lexical or exegetical explanations of Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59.

Its significance is the presence of dhanūb, “share,” in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, rather than its being constructed solely through later interpretation of the Qur’anic verse.

Another early expression reads:

لَنَا ذَنُوبٌ وَلَكُمْ ذَنُوبُ
فَإِنْ أَبَيْتُمْ فَلَنَا الْقَلِيبُ

lanā dhanūbun wa-lakum dhanūbu / fa-in abaytum fa-lanā al-qalīb

Translation: We have a share of water, and you have a share; if you refuse, the well will be ours.

The rajaz illustrates the relationship between a water bucket and sharing water, although its poet and date are not securely established in the transmissions examined.

The three branches should therefore be distinguished: sin, something’s trailing part, and a share connected with buckets in water distribution.

2. Dhanb in the Qur’an: wrongdoing and responsibility

Sin and attributable conduct

Āl ʿImrān 3:135 reads:

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا فَعَلُوا فَاحِشَةً أَوْ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ ذَكَرُوا اللَّهَ فَاسْتَغْفَرُوا لِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَمَنْ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-alladhīna idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan aw ẓalamū anfusahum dhakarū allāha fa-istaghfarū li-dhunūbihim wa-man yaghfiru al-dhunūba illā allāhu wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū wa-hum yaʿlamūn

Translation: Those who, when they commit an indecency or wrong themselves, remember The God and seek forgiveness for their sins—and who forgives sins except The God?—and do not knowingly persist in what they have done.

Dhunūb occurs twice here.

The verse first mentions committing an indecency or wronging oneself, then seeking forgiveness for sins.

Dhanb therefore concerns wrongful conduct committed by the person in this context.

The Qur’an also describes the response afterward, rather than reporting the sin alone.

Remembering The God, seeking forgiveness, and not knowingly persisting in wrongdoing characterize those described.

وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ distinguishes committing a fault from knowingly persisting in it.

It does not establish that everyone committing a dhanb must remain in that state forever or has no way back.

Admitting dhanb and the possibility of reform

Al-Tawbah 9:102 reads:

وَآخَرُونَ اعْتَرَفُوا بِذُنُوبِهِمْ خَلَطُوا عَمَلًا صَالِحًا وَآخَرَ سَيِّئًا عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَتُوبَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-ākharūna iʿtarafū bi-dhunūbihim khalaṭū ʿamalan ṣāliḥan wa-ākhara sayyiʾan ʿasā allāhu an yatūba ʿalayhim inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīm

Translation: Others acknowledged their sins, mixing a righteous deed with another that was bad. It may be that The God will turn toward them; indeed, The God is abundantly forgiving, especially merciful.

Dhanb is connected with acknowledgment and human conduct.

خَلَطُوا عَمَلًا صَالِحًا وَآخَرَ سَيِّئًا shows that someone can have righteous conduct and also have committed wrongdoing.

Having a dhanb alone does not therefore make all a person’s good deeds worthless.

Acknowledgment is part of the group’s condition, but is not identical with completed reform and return. A person may admit a fault and then decide how to act in the future.

The Qur’an expresses hope here for renewed mercy and divine acceptance.

Ghāfir 40:11 and Al-Mulk 67:11 also mention admitting sins, but in afterlife situations involving awareness of their consequences.

Acknowledgment can therefore occur at different times and under different conditions. An admission during life with opportunities for reform differs in setting and outcome from one after punishment becomes evident.

Dhanb in human relationships

Yūsuf 12:29 includes an address to the Egyptian official’s wife:

يُوسُفُ أَعْرِضْ عَنْ هَٰذَا وَاسْتَغْفِرِي لِذَنْبِكِ إِنَّكِ كُنْتِ مِنَ الْخَاطِئِينَ

yūsufu aʿriḍ ʿan hādhā wa-istaghfirī li-dhanbiki innaki kunti mina al-khāṭiʾīn

Translation: Joseph, turn away from this matter; and you, seek forgiveness for your sin, for you have been among those at fault.

ذَنْبِكِ concerns conduct already described in the story.

Dhanb is thus also used in human speech to attribute a fault or wrongdoing to someone.

Dhanb and khāṭiʾīn occur together, showing their semantic proximity. This alone does not make every dhanb identical to every derivative of KH Ṭ ʾ.

In Yūsuf 12:97, Joseph’s brothers say to their father:

يَا أَبَانَا اسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا خَاطِئِينَ

yā abānā istaghfir lanā dhunūbanā innā kunnā khāṭiʾīn

Translation: Our father, seek forgiveness for our sins; we have been at fault.

The request accompanies an acknowledgment of wrongdoing.

It is connected with the brothers’ earlier behavior: planning to separate Joseph from his father and then giving a false account.

Dhanb concerns their actual responsibility, rather than a simple numerical mistake or an error without consequences.

In both examples, the story and conduct establish the nature and degree of responsibility; the word dhanb alone does not specify intention, gravity, or punishment.

3. Dhanb in Moses’ story and in address to Muḥammad

“They hold an offense against me”: responsibility and an allegation

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:14, Moses says:

وَلَهُمْ عَلَيَّ ذَنْبٌ فَأَخَافُ أَنْ يَقْتُلُونِ

wa-lahum ʿalayya dhanbun fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn

Translation: They hold an offense against me, so I fear they will kill me.

This important occurrence places dhanb within Moses’ relationship with Pharaoh’s people.

لَهُمْ عَلَيَّ ذَنْبٌ indicates a matter held against Moses that may lead to pursuit and punishment.

The Qur’an supplies further explanation of that matter.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15 describes Moses encountering two men fighting in a city. One asked for his help; Moses struck the other, and the blow led to his death.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:16, Moses says:

رَبِّ إِنِّي ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِي فَاغْفِرْ لِي فَغَفَرَ لَهُ

rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī fa-ighfir lī fa-ghafara lah

Translation: My Lord, I have wronged myself, so forgive me. Then He forgave him.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:33, Moses states more explicitly:

رَبِّ إِنِّي قَتَلْتُ مِنْهُمْ نَفْسًا فَأَخَافُ أَنْ يَقْتُلُونِ

rabbi innī qataltu minhum nafsan fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn

Translation: My Lord, I have killed one of them, so I fear they will kill me.

This illuminates the matter called dhanb in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:14.

It therefore cannot be presented as a completely groundless accusation alone. The Qur’an reports the man’s death and quotes Moses concerning it.

Nor does the word alone establish that Moses acted with a premeditated intention to kill him. The narrative describes a blow and the resulting death; intention, responsibility, and the circumstances require examination within that account.

Dhanb here concerns an actual act and a responsibility others might hold against Moses.

The act occurring, the intention behind it, an unintended consequence, and the justice or injustice of possible punishment should be distinguished.

“Seek forgiveness for your dhanb”: a command to Muḥammad

Muḥammad 47:19 reads:

فَاعْلَمْ أَنَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لِذَنْبِكَ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مُتَقَلَّبَكُمْ وَمَثْوَاكُمْ

fa-iʿlam annahu lā ilāha illā allāhu wa-istaghfir li-dhanbika wa-li-al-muʾminīna wa-al-muʾmināti wa-allāhu yaʿlamu mutaqallabakum wa-mathwākum

Translation: Know that there is no deity except The God, and seek forgiveness for your sin and for believing men and women. The God knows your movements and your resting place.

وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لِذَنْبِكَ directly addresses Muḥammad.

Ghāfir 40:55 contains a similar expression:

فَاصْبِرْ إِنَّ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ حَقٌّ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لِذَنْبِكَ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ بِالْعَشِيِّ وَالْإِبْكَارِ

fa-iṣbir inna waʿda allāhi ḥaqqun wa-istaghfir li-dhanbika wa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika bi-al-ʿashiyyi wa-al-ibkār

Translation: Be patient; The God’s promise is true. Seek forgiveness for your sin, and glorify your Lord with praise in the evening and morning.

The two commands should not be disregarded because of a theological theory concerning the Prophet’s standing.

The Qur’an expressly uses ذَنْبِكَ and commands seeking forgiveness.

Neither verse names a particular offense, establishes its intention, or specifies a grave sin.

Two extreme conclusions are therefore unjustified.

The first turns dhanb, when addressing the Prophet, into something wholly unrelated to sin or fault without evidence.

The second treats the word alone as proof of any particular grave crime or behavior not stated in these verses.

The established meaning and the limits of the Qur’an’s statement should both be respected.

Al-Fatḥ 48:2: what precedes and follows of dhanb

Al-Fatḥ 48:2 reads:

لِيَغْفِرَ لَكَ اللَّهُ مَا تَقَدَّمَ مِنْ ذَنْبِكَ وَمَا تَأَخَّرَ وَيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكَ وَيَهْدِيَكَ صِرَاطًا مُسْتَقِيمًا

li-yaghfira laka allāhu mā taqaddama min dhanbika wa-mā taʾakhkhara wa-yutimma niʿmatahu ʿalayka wa-yahdiyaka ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā

Translation: So that The God may forgive you what preceded of your sin and what followed, complete His favor upon you, and guide you to a straight path.

Dhanb appears with forgiveness and two temporal expressions: مَا تَقَدَّمَ and مَا تَأَخَّرَ.

The construction expresses a range of before and after, but dhanb itself does not identify the conduct concerned.

Interpretations differ: some understand forgiveness of past and future sins; others discuss possible faults or responsibilities connected with the prophetic mission differently.

None should become the root’s lexical definition without independent examination.

Dhanb is the same word used elsewhere for sin and wrongdoing. The exact scope of forgiveness requires examining the temporal construction and the chapter’s context.

The verse alone does not authorize concluding that every future human act has already become right or exempt from moral assessment, irrespective of its reality and responsibility.

The explicit promise of forgiveness should not be diminished or removed either.

These three addresses to Muḥammad and the passages concerning Moses show that root study should avoid imposing prior theological theories on a word, while attributing nothing to prophets beyond the text’s evidence.

4. Dhanb, forgiveness, repentance, and consequences

Seeking forgiveness

Dhanb frequently accompanies GH F R in the Qur’an.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:16, a group of the God-conscious say:

رَبَّنَا إِنَّنَا آمَنَّا فَاغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا وَقِنَا عَذَابَ النَّارِ

rabbanā innanā āmannā fa-ighfir lanā dhunūbanā wa-qinā ʿadhāba al-nār

Translation: Our Lord, we have believed; forgive our sins and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.

Āl ʿImrān 3:147 combines seeking forgiveness for sins with seeking forgiveness for excesses in one’s affairs.

Āl ʿImrān 3:193 combines forgiveness of sins with removal of bad deeds.

These combinations show that the Qur’an does not describe wrongdoing and consequences with one word alone.

Dhanb, sayyiʾah, and isrāf have related fields without being identical in every use.

Āl ʿImrān 3:31, Al-Aḥzāb 33:71, and Al-Ṣaff 61:12 likewise connect forgiveness with faith, obedience, integrity, and righteous behavior.

Ibrāhīm 14:10, Al-Aḥqāf 46:31, and Nūḥ 71:4 include مِنْ ذُنُوبِكُمْ in promises of forgiveness.

Min in this construction is grammatically debated: partitivity, explanation, and an additional particle giving emphasis have been proposed. Some reject these analyses in affirmative sentences. The scope of forgiveness should therefore not be determined from the particle’s presence alone, without context and attention to the grammatical disagreement.

The complete sentence and related verses establish the promise’s nature and conditions.

Forgiving all sins and forbidding despair

Al-Zumar 39:53 reads:

قُلْ يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

qul yā ʿibādiya alladhīna asrafū ʿalā anfusihim lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati allāhi inna allāha yaghfiru al-dhunūba jamīʿan innahu huwa al-ghafūru al-raḥīm

Translation: Say: My servants who have exceeded bounds against themselves, do not despair of The God’s mercy. The God forgives all sins; He is the abundantly forgiving, the especially merciful.

الذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا means all sins.

It reveals the breadth of the possibility of forgiveness and rejects despair of The God’s mercy.

The following verse, Al-Zumar 39:54, calls people to return to their Lord and submit to Him.

The promise should not become permission to persist knowingly in injustice and sin.

A sin repented of, with efforts toward reform, also differs in the person’s condition from a sin knowingly persisted in.

Ghāfir 40:3 describes The God as غَافِرِ الذَّنْبِ وَقَابِلِ التَّوْبِ: forgiver of sin and acceptor of repentance.

The combination connects dhanb and the way back within the Qur’anic network of meanings.

For a fuller discussion, consult Forqan’s independent GH F R study and its discussion of forgiveness, repentance, covering faults, and protection from punishment.

Study of GH F R

Dhanb and the punishment of communities

Elsewhere, sins are presented as a cause or ground for punishing human communities.

Al-Anʿām 6:6 includes:

فَأَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَأَنْشَأْنَا مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ قَرْنًا آخَرِينَ

fa-ahlaknāhum bi-dhunūbihim wa-anshaʾnā min baʿdihim qarnan ākharīn

Translation: We destroyed them for their sins and brought forth another generation after them.

Related expressions appear in Āl ʿImrān 3:11, Al-Aʿrāf 7:100, Al-Anfāl 8:52, Al-Anfāl 8:54, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40, Ghāfir 40:21, and Al-Shams 91:14.

Dhanb concerns wrongdoing involved in judging and punishing these communities.

Two propositions should be distinguished.

First, the Qur’an reports particular groups punished for their sins.

Second, every misfortune anywhere and at any time is necessarily direct divine punishment for the inhabitants’ sins.

The second does not follow automatically from the first. Establishing a natural or social event’s cause requires evidence concerning it; without a clear text, it should not be declared divine punishment of particular people.

Al-Māʾidah 5:49 uses بِبَعْضِ ذُنُوبِهِمْ, “because of some of their sins.”

Baʿḍ shows that the Qur’an can restrict the sins concerned when stating a cause or ground of punishment.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:17 and Al-Furqān 25:58 mention The God’s knowledge of His servants’ sins. Dhanb is an object of divine knowledge and judgment, rather than itself meaning punishment.

Sin, forgiveness, and punishment are related but distinct. Committing a dhanb may create responsibility; forgiveness concerns pardon and protection from punishment; punishment is a consequence that may follow wrongdoing under particular conditions.

The three should not be collapsed into one lexical meaning.

5. Dhanb in judgment and questions of responsibility

“For what sin was she killed?”: whose fault?

Al-Takwīr 81:8 and Al-Takwīr 81:9 read:

وَإِذَا الْمَوْءُودَةُ سُئِلَتْ

wa-idhā al-mawʾūdatu suʾilat

Translation: When the girl buried alive is asked,

بِأَيِّ ذَنْبٍ قُتِلَتْ

bi-ayyi dhanbin qutilat

Translation: For what sin was she killed?

These are important witnesses for dhanb’s relationship to responsibility.

The question بِأَيِّ ذَنْبٍ concerns a child who was killed. Its interrogative construction exposes the injustice.

It does not mean that the child actually committed a sin whose type must now be identified.

Rather, it indicates that no sin by the victim could justify such killing.

The word’s presence in a sentence alone does not establish the person’s guilt. Questions, negation, conditions, and pronoun references matter to interpretation.

Dhanb can therefore appear within a discussion of responsibility and the justification or injustice of another’s behavior.

The child is a victim of injustice here, rather than someone whose sin caused her killing.

“Not asked about his sin”: denying questioning in a scene

Al-Raḥmān 55:39 reads:

فَيَوْمَئِذٍ لَا يُسْأَلُ عَنْ ذَنْبِهِ إِنْسٌ وَلَا جَانٌّ

fa-yawmaʾidhin lā yusʾalu ʿan dhanbihi insun wa-lā jānn

Translation: On that Day, no human or jinn will be questioned about his sin.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:78 also includes:

وَلَا يُسْأَلُ عَنْ ذُنُوبِهِمُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ

wa-lā yusʾalu ʿan dhunūbihimu al-mujrimūn

Translation: The guilty are not questioned about their sins.

At first sight, these may raise a question: how does accountability take place if sins are not questioned?

Other passages, such as Al-Ḥijr 15:92 and Al-Ḥijr 15:93, mention questioning about deeds. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24 commands that people be detained because they will be questioned.

The kind of question and its setting must therefore be distinguished.

Some early explanations connect the denial with a stage when conduct is evident and recorded. Another denies questioning to acquire information, since The God knows human conduct.

Al-Raḥmān 55:41 also includes:

يُعْرَفُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ بِسِيمَاهُمْ

yuʿrafu al-mujrimūna bi-sīmāhum

Translation: The guilty are recognized by their signs.

This connection suggests that their condition may be evident, removing the need to question them for identification.

The definite sequence of all afterlife scenes cannot nevertheless be established from these phrases alone.

The lexical point remains clear: dhanb means sin here. Affirming or denying questioning concerns the sentence and setting of judgment, rather than changing the word’s meaning.

6. Dhanūb in Al-Dhāriyāt: a share, rather than sins

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59 reads:

فَإِنَّ لِلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا ذَنُوبًا مِثْلَ ذَنُوبِ أَصْحَابِهِمْ فَلَا يَسْتَعْجِلُونِ

fa-inna li-alladhīna ẓalamū dhanūban mithla dhanūbi aṣḥābihim fa-lā yastaʿjilūn

Translation: Those who have wronged have a share like their earlier counterparts’ share; let them not ask Me to hasten its arrival.

Dhanūb occurs twice here.

Its form and meaning differ from the plural dhunūb, “sins.”

ذُنُوب, with a ḍammah on the dhāl, is the plural of ذَنْب. ذَنُوب, with a fatḥah, is a singular noun for a bucket and, by extension, a share or portion.

Vocalized Arabic makes the distinction clear, although both appear as ذنوب without vowel marks.

From a water bucket to a share

Drawing water from wells with a bucket was familiar in Arab life. Each person drawing from a shared well could have a portion or turn.

Dhanūb is consequently also used for a share.

As ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl’s verse illustrates, it can denote a share of generosity: something a person is entitled to, or hopes to receive.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59 uses the same sense of a share, but in a different context from generosity.

Earlier passages relate the outcomes of communities such as Noah’s people, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Lot’s people.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59 then states that the wrongdoers addressed have a share like their predecessors’. فَلَا يَسْتَعْجِلُونِ means they should not ask Me to hasten it. The first-person object is part of the construction, although its final yāʾ is not written in this form.

Dhanūb concerns a portion of the outcome or punishment under discussion.

The word alone does not mean punishment. “Share” is lexical; its being a share of punishment is understood from context.

Why should this not be translated “sins”?

Rendering ذَنُوبًا مِثْلَ ذَنُوبِ أَصْحَابِهِمْ as “sins like their companions’ sins” obscures the established sense and the connection with the following warning.

The verse concerns something awaiting the wrongdoers whose arrival they should not seek to hasten.

“A share like their predecessors’ share” therefore better fits the construction and context.

Early explanations also gloss dhanūb as a share and illustrate the Arabic use through ʿAlqamah’s verse and the rajaz about sharing well water.

Although dhanb and dhanūb belong to one family, their meanings here differ.

Elsewhere, people may be punished because of their dhunūb. In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59, dhanūb is itself the share mentioned in the warning.

This is one of the study’s most important findings.

7. Distinctions from related words

DH N B and ʾ TH M: sin and sinful conduct

Study of ʾ TH M

Ithm denotes sinful conduct or a sinful condition in the Qur’an, sometimes connected with harm, transgression, and moral responsibility.

Dhanb likewise concerns wrongdoing attributable to a person.

The families can concern the same act. Without examining all passages, absolute distinctions such as “dhanb is only a minor fault” and “ithm is only a grave sin” should not be constructed.

An act having unwelcome consequences is not alone a complete definition of either root.

DH N B and KH Ṭ ʾ: sin and error

Study of KH Ṭ ʾ

KH Ṭ ʾ concerns making a mistake or departing from what is right. Its derivatives also distinguish unintended error from culpable wrongdoing.

In Yūsuf 12:97, Joseph’s brothers use ذُنُوبَنَا and خَاطِئِينَ together.

Their combination shows that fault and dhanb can apply to the same wrongdoing.

It does not establish that every dhanb is unintended and every khaṭīʾah deliberate. The verb, morphology, intention, and awareness require separate examination.

DH N B and J R M: sin and offense

Study of J R M

Jurm is also used for wrongdoing and offenses in Arabic; Qur’anic derivatives include mujrim and mujrimūn.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:78 place dhanb in judgments concerning guilty groups.

Jurm and dhanb can therefore be related without expressing exactly one concept.

Mujrim describes a person, whereas dhanb can denote the wrongful act attributed to them.

All legal and ethical features of the designation mujrim should not be imported into dhanb’s definition without examination.

DH N B and S W ʾ: sin and a bad deed

Study of S W ʾ

Sayyiʾah has a broad Qur’anic field, including a bad deed, unwelcome condition, or harmful consequence.

Dhanb’s principal Qur’anic uses concern attributable sin or fault.

Āl ʿImrān 3:193 places ذُنُوبَنَا and سَيِّئَاتِنَا together.

This shows proximity without making every sayyiʾah, including an unwelcome event or condition, mean dhanb.

DH N B, T B ʿ, and ʿ Q B: a trailing part and following

Study of T B ʿ

Physical dhanab means a tail or rear part. Arabic also calls people following others adhnāb, “tails.”

T B ʿ and ʿ Q B are independent families with their own fields.

Study of ʿ Q B

The figurative connection between trailing parts and consequences does not authorize turning dhanb in sin-related verses into the verb “to follow.”

Translation should preserve the word’s actual form and function.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Most Qur’anic uses of dhanb and dhunūb can be rendered “sin” and “sins.”

These are natural, clear equivalents in Āl ʿImrān 3:135, Al-Tawbah 9:102, Yūsuf 12:97, Al-Zumar 39:53, and other passages on forgiveness and responsibility.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:14, “offense” or “an allegation of an offense” can better express the legal and social aspect of Moses’ relationship with Pharaoh’s people. The Qur’an nevertheless reports the man’s death; “allegation” should not deny that event.

In Al-Takwīr 81:9, بِأَيِّ ذَنْبٍ قُتِلَتْ is appropriately rendered “For what sin was she killed?” The question and the victim’s situation disclose her innocence in context.

In Muḥammad 47:19, Ghāfir 40:55, and Al-Fatḥ 48:2, the basic meaning should be retained while the nature and scope are left to independent contextual interpretation.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59, however, dhanūb must be rendered “share” or “portion,” rather than “sin.”

The phrase:

فَإِنَّ لِلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا ذَنُوبًا مِثْلَ ذَنُوبِ أَصْحَابِهِمْ

fa-inna li-alladhīna ẓalamū dhanūban mithla dhanūbi aṣḥābihim

can be rendered:

“Those who have wronged have a share like their earlier counterparts’ share.”

Its connection with outcome and punishment should be explained separately.

The distinction should also be preserved in Forqan’s word boxes and translation: dhunūb, the plural of sin, and dhanūb, “share,” differ in form and use despite their unvocalized resemblance.

9. Conclusion

DH N B has three important Arabic branches: attributable sin or wrongdoing, a tail or rear part, and a water bucket or share.

Early language uses dhanb for conduct or a matter inviting censure, dhanab for a tail or trailing part, and dhanūb for a bucket or water share in communal water distribution.

Analyses of the branches’ relationship differ: one separates several principles, while another connects a trailing part with consequences. These explanations should not replace examining each derivative’s actual usage.

Qur’anic dhanb denotes sin, fault, or wrongdoing that can be acknowledged, forgiven, attributed as responsibility, or punished.

The word alone determines neither intention, gravity, punishment, nor the impossibility of return. The Qur’an distinguishes committing a fault, acknowledging it, seeking forgiveness, reform, and knowingly persisting.

Its use concerning Moses and in address to Muḥammad also counsels against changing the meaning to defend a predetermined theological theory. Equally, the word alone does not establish a grave crime or specific behavior without evidence.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59 uses the different form dhanūb, “share.” It is not the plural of sin and should not be translated identically.

Final definition: DH N B is a family with several branches. Dhanb denotes sin, offense, or fault attributed to a person; dhanab names a tail or rear part; and dhanūb in another branch denotes a water bucket or a share.

Sin and moral responsibility dominate the Qur’anic uses, but Al-Dhāriyāt 51:59 demonstrates the necessity of examining exact form and context.

Understanding dhanb therefore requires distinguishing conduct, its attribution, awareness and intention, consequences, and the possibility of forgiveness. None should be substituted for another without evidence.
