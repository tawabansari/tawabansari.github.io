Hardship and adverse outcomes: from ʿĀd’s days of punishment to smoke and copper in the nuḥās family

1. Introduction and semantic core

Arabic N Ḥ S has several semantic branches. The best known concerns hardship, harm, adverse conditions, and an unfortunate outcome. Here naḥs contrasts with saʿd and describes a situation involving suffering, loss, or an unwelcome event.

A naḥs day can be one on which a severe, harmful event occurred. A person or undertaking can likewise be described through an unfortunate outcome.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished: a time proving adverse for people caught in an event, and believing that the time itself possesses a hidden power to cause misfortune.

The lexical meaning alone establishes no such independent power. It can describe an adverse condition or consequence without specifying its cause.

Naḥs is also recorded for suffering and harm. Drought, intense cold, or a damaging event can produce a naḥs situation. This concerns experienced hardship, rather than necessarily an unknown force within time or place.

Nuḥās also appears under this root in Arabic. It denotes smoke without flame and the metal copper. Niḥās and nuḥās can additionally concern a person’s origin, nature, or disposition.

These meanings should not be forcibly derived from misfortune. Shared consonants and a dictionary entry do not establish that every word historically arose from one original meaning.

Qur’anic forms

The Qur’anic morphological index records three occurrences in three forms:

  • Naḥisātin: adverse or ill-fated days; Fuṣṣilat 41:16.
  • Naḥsin: hardship, misfortune, or an ominous condition; Al-Qamar 54:19.
  • Nuḥāsun: smoke or copper, with an established contextual disagreement; Al-Raḥmān 55:35.

The first two concern ʿĀd’s punishment and belong to the hardship and misfortune branch.

The third concerns something sent upon jinn and humankind alongside a flame of fire, belonging to the nuḥās branch.

Naḥisātin is the feminine plural of descriptive naḥisah. Another recitation has naḥsātin, with an unvowelled ḥāʾ.

Naḥs is a noun or descriptive form concerning adverse conditions and misfortune.

Nuḥās is a noun and should not be treated as another form of the adjective naḥis.

Other Arabic derivatives include nāḥis, naḥīs, manḥūs, nuḥūsah, anḥas, naḥḥasa, tanaḥḥasa, and istanḥasa. They do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic branches

Naḥs: hardship, harm, and the opposite of good fortune

Two important lexical explanations are al-jahdu wa-al-ḍurru, hardship and harm, and khilāfu al-saʿdi, the opposite of good fortune or a favourable outcome.

The explanations are close without being identical.

Hardship and harm describe conditions actually experienced. Good and bad fortune can additionally involve beliefs about events’ causes or future consequences.

A year of drought and famine might be called ʿāmun naḥīsun: a hard, adverse year.

This does not necessarily make the year itself an independent cause of famine, apart from natural factors and human behaviour.

Naḥasahu al-jadbu likewise identifies drought as the cause of harm to someone.

Naḥs then concerns an actual damaging consequence.

In some Arabic cultural uses, however, saʿd and naḥs were also attributed to stars and associated times. People could infer future fortune from stellar observations or positions.

That historical usage establishes familiarity with auspiciousness and ill omen, rather than proving those beliefs true or endorsed by the Qur’an.

A witness attributed to al-Shanfarā: a cold, difficult night

The famous Lāmiyyah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Shanfarā says:

وَلَيْلَةِ نَحْسٍ يَصْطَلِي الْقَوْسَ رَبُّهَا
وَأَقْطَعَهُ اللَّائِي بِهَا يَتَنَبَّلُ

wa-laylati naḥsin yaṣṭalī al-qawsa rabbuhā / wa-aqṭaʿahu allāʾī bihā yatanabbalu

Translation:

“And what a cold, difficult night, when the bow’s owner burns his bow and arrows to keep warm.”

Naḥs describes a difficult night here.

The cold is pictured as severe enough to drive someone to burn his hunting and defensive equipment for warmth.

The word can therefore concern the night’s harsh, damaging conditions without requiring an inherent power to cause misfortune.

Some transmitted versions have ṣirr instead of naḥs. Beyond textual variation, the attribution of the whole Lāmiyyah to al-Shanfarā has been debated.

It is a recognized poetic witness, but neither its wording nor attribution should be treated as unqualified certainty.

The cold and hardship senses are also independently recorded in Arabic, rather than depending solely on this poem.

Naḥs: dust, cold wind, and adverse weather

Some Arabic uses denote dust, or a dust-laden wind.

Hāja al-naḥsu is recorded for dust rising.

Naḥs is also recorded for a harsh, cold wind.

Ibn Aḥmar, a poet near the beginning of the Islamic period, says:

كَأَنَّ مُدَامَةً عُرِضَتْ لِنَحْسٍ
يُحِيلُ شَفِيفُهَا الْمَاءَ الزُّلَالَا

ka-anna mudāmatan ʿuriḍat li-naḥsin / yuḥīlu shafīfuhā al-māʾa al-zulālā

Approximate sense:

“As though wine had been exposed to a harsh, cold wind, its chill making clear water flow down the throat.”

The lexical explanation takes naḥs as a cold wind here.

This connects with difficult, unpleasant cold. It does not establish wind or dust as the original meaning from which misfortune necessarily developed.

The historical priority of these branches is uncertain.

Nor does the cold-wind use require translating every Qur’anic naḥs as wind.

In the ʿĀd passages, rīḥ independently names the wind, while naḥs describes the time of the event.

Nuḥās: smoke and copper

Nuḥās is another important branch.

Early Arabic records it for smoke apart from flame: smoke rising from fire without being the flame itself.

A verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī says:

يُضِيءُ كَضَوْءِ سِرَاجِ السَّلِيطِ
لَمْ يَجْعَلِ اللَّهُ فِيهِ نُحَاسَا

yuḍīʾu ka-ḍawʾi sirāji al-salīṭi / lam yajʿali allāhu fīhi nuḥāsā

Translation:

“It shines like an oil lamp’s light, in which God has placed no smoke.”

Nuḥās means smoke here, rather than copper.

Lexical transmission attributes the verse to al-Jaʿdī, although some interpretive transmissions attribute it to al-Dhubyānī. Attribution to al-Jaʿdī has clearer support in a number of lexical and poetic transmissions.

Al-Jaʿdī lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse’s exact date is uncertain, so it should not be presented as an unqualified pre-Qur’anic witness.

The smoke meaning also fits early lexical explanations.

Nuḥās nevertheless also names copper, the reddish metal used for tools and vessels.

Another verse, attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, says:

كَأَنَّ شُوَاظَهُنَّ بِجَانِبَيْهِ
نُحَاسُ الصُّفْرِ تَضْرِبُهُ الْقُيُونُ

ka-anna shuwāẓahunna bi-jānibayhi / nuḥāsu al-ṣufri taḍribuhu al-quyūnu

Approximate sense:

“As though their flames and sparks on its two sides were sparks of metal struck by smiths.”

The lexical explanation identifies nuḥās here as sparks or particles separating when metal is hammered. The verse should therefore not be reduced to naming the metal itself.

The verse is attributed to al-Dhubyānī. Its wording, the explanation involving metal sparks, and claims about a precise date or attribution details must be distinguished.

The lamp witness illustrates smoke; the smiths’ witness concerns metal sparks. Nuḥās as the name of copper is separately established in Arabic.

Niḥās or nuḥās can also mean origin, nature, or disposition, as in karīmu al-niḥāsi for someone considered noble in character or ancestry.

A definitive historical derivation connecting these meanings requires evidence.

Different derivational explanations connect the name of copper with misfortune or with something’s origin and foundation. Such lexical proposals must be distinguished from established naming history.

Copper therefore should not be said certainly to have acquired its name because it was less valuable than gold and silver. That is a lexical hypothesis, rather than proven naming history.

Other uses

Naḥḥasa al-akhbāra and tanaḥḥasa al-akhbāra are recorded for following up or seeking news.

Other uses concern abstaining from certain foods or changes in someone’s condition.

They do not establish a single common core linking misfortune with smoke and copper.

They illustrate the breadth of the Arabic family, but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic study should distinguish the two principal branches actually represented: hardship and misfortune, and nuḥās as smoke or copper.

3. Qur’anic usage in ʿĀd’s story

The first two occurrences concern ʿĀd’s punishment.

The Qur’an reports a particular historical event, rather than a general calendar of auspicious and inauspicious days.

A. Fuṣṣilat 41:16: ill-fated days

فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا فِي أَيَّامٍ نَحِسَاتٍ لِنُذِيقَهُمْ عَذَابَ الْخِزْيِ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَلَعَذَابُ الْآخِرَةِ أَخْزَىٰ وَهُمْ لَا يُنْصَرُونَ

fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran fī ayyāmin naḥisātin li-nudhīqahum ʿadhāba al-khizyi fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-la-ʿadhābu al-ākhirati akhzā wa-hum lā yunṣarūna

Translation:

“Then We sent upon them a fierce, tremendous wind during ill-fated days, to make them taste humiliating punishment in worldly life. The punishment of the Hereafter is more humiliating, and they will not be helped.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:16

Naḥisātin describes ayyāmin, days.

The verse reports a tremendous wind sent upon ʿĀd during specified days, then identifies its purpose and consequence: humiliating punishment in worldly life.

The adverse character of those days is thus explained by the context.

ʿĀd encountered a devastating event: those were hard, damaging days for them.

The verse does not say those dates already possessed an independent power to cause misfortune before the event.

Nor does it state that a similar event will recur every year on the same calendar dates.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding naḥs.

Naḥisātin and recitational variation

Ḥafṣ reads naḥisātin, with i on ḥāʾ.

Other recognized recitations, including Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, and Abū ʿAmr, have naḥsātin, with an unvowelled ḥāʾ.

The first is analysed as the plural of descriptive naḥisah.

The second can be explained from naḥs as a noun or verbal noun used descriptively.

Both concern days associated with hardship and misfortune.

The difference in the ḥāʾ’s vowel therefore does not produce wholly unrelated meanings.

Nor does morphology establish that one recitation proves days inherently unlucky while the other denies it.

Why describe the days as naḥs?

The sentence itself explains:

لِنُذِيقَهُمْ عَذَابَ الْخِزْيِ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا

li-nudhīqahum ʿadhāba al-khizyi fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā

Meaning:

“To make them taste humiliating punishment in worldly life.”

Naḥisāt directly relates to the harmful event and ʿĀd’s outcome.

The previous verse also describes their conduct:

فَأَمَّا عَادٌ فَاسْتَكْبَرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ

fa-ammā ʿādun fa-istakbarū fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi

“As for ʿĀd, they acted arrogantly on earth without right.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:15, excerpt

Punishment therefore occurs in a context describing the people’s conduct and disobedience.

This differs clearly from believing in days’ independent ill omen.

The adverse outcome relates to their conduct and the reported punishment, rather than an unknown property of weekday numbers or names.

B. Al-Qamar 54:19: an ill-fated day and continuation

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا فِي يَوْمِ نَحْسٍ مُسْتَمِرٍّ

innā arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran fī yawmi naḥsin mustamirrin

Translation:

“We sent upon them a fierce, tremendous wind on a day of continuing misfortune.”

Al-Qamar 54:19

This is the second occurrence in the hardship and misfortune branch.

The construction is yawmi naḥsin.

The recitation used here has a construct phrase: a day of naḥs or misfortune. A descriptive reading, yawmin naḥsin, is also mentioned in linguistic transmission; its construction and case marking should not be confused with the construct phrase.

Mustamirrin follows as a description.

From M R R, it can concern continuation, persistence, or firmness and intensity in this context.

Continuation is nevertheless not part of naḥs itself; it comes from independent mustamirr.

What does mustamirr describe?

Several interpretations exist.

One takes the misfortune and punishment to have continued rather than remaining a short, passing event.

Another emphasizes intensity and persistence: a severe event from which they could not escape.

Some lexical explanations also relate persistence to bitterness and hardship.

Parallel passages about ʿĀd support the continuing punishment and persistent wind reading.

None of these possibilities makes that day permanently unlucky in every year.

The phrase belongs first to an event in ʿĀd’s history.

Does yawm contradict the plural days in Fuṣṣilat?

Fuṣṣilat 41:16 has ayyāmin naḥisātin: several days.

Al-Qamar 54:19 has yawmi naḥsin mustamirrin. “Day” may denote the event’s occasion or its beginning, but identifying the first day is an explanation reconciling passages, rather than something established by yawm alone.

Another passage helps clarify the issue.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 says:

وَأَمَّا عَادٌ فَأُهْلِكُوا بِرِيحٍ صَرْصَرٍ عَاتِيَةٍ

wa-ammā ʿādun fa-uhlikū bi-rīḥin ṣarṣarin ʿātiyatin

“As for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a fierce, overpowering wind.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6

And Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7:

سَخَّرَهَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَانِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

sakhkharahā ʿalayhim sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyatin

“He subjected it to them for seven nights and eight successive days. You see the people lying fallen within them, as though hollow palm trunks.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

The duration is explicit: seven nights and eight days.

In light of Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, the first day is one possible reconciliation; yawm’s broader use for an occasion or period can also be considered. Neither should be presented as a separate, explicit chronology supplied by the phrase itself.

Arabic yawm can concern an occasion or period of an event, rather than invariably a precise twenty-four-hour interval.

A day introducing or describing an event need not contradict its continuation for several days.

No additional duration or detailed schedule should be invented. The explicit Qur’anic number is seven nights and eight days.

C. ʿĀd’s outcome and the wind’s action

Al-Qamar 54:20 says:

تَنْزِعُ النَّاسَ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ مُنْقَعِرٍ

tanziʿu al-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿirin

Translation:

“It uproots people, as though they were uprooted palm trunks.”

This describes the fierce wind’s action.

N Z ʿ expresses uprooting people, while munqaʿir completes the uprooting image.

The destruction and displacement come from those words and accompanying verbs.

N Ḥ S itself does not mean uprooting, storm, or killing.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:24 also describes ʿĀd seeing something approaching their valleys and assuming it was a rain-bearing cloud:

قَالُوا هَٰذَا عَارِضٌ مُمْطِرُنَا

qālū hādhā ʿāriḍun mumṭirunā

“They said: This is a cloud bringing us rain.”

The response was:

بَلْ هُوَ مَا اسْتَعْجَلْتُمْ بِهِ رِيحٌ فِيهَا عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

bal huwa mā istaʿjaltum bihi rīḥun fīhā ʿadhābun alīmun

“Rather, it is what you sought to hasten: a wind containing painful punishment.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:24, excerpts

What they initially expected to bring benefit and rain became a devastating event for them.

An event’s benefit or harm therefore cannot be judged only by its initial appearance.

This should not become a universal rule making every present-day storm or natural disaster divine punishment upon a particular group.

The Qur’an itself identifies this event’s cause and outcome. The same judgment cannot be made certain about other events without such evidence.

4. Does the Qur’an endorse inherently unlucky days?

A major question concerns the root’s connection with beliefs about unlucky days or hours and auspicious times.

Some cultures regard particular weekdays or dates as suitable or unsuitable for travel, marriage, starting work, or important decisions.

These beliefs are sometimes attributed to the positions of stars, the moon, or planets.

Three matters must be distinguished: lexical naḥs, a Qur’anic event report, and a claim that time possesses a fixed hidden property.

Describing a day through an event that occurred in it

Someone might ordinarily say, “That was a very bad day for us.”

They may mean that an adverse event occurred that day.

This need not make the date unlucky for everybody in every year.

Fuṣṣilat and Al-Qamar likewise describe days when ʿĀd encountered severe punishment.

Their adverse character contextually relates to that event and those people’s outcome.

The passages alone do not establish permanent ill omen for a particular weekday or date.

Is Wednesday unlucky?

Some interpretive reports identify the beginning of ʿĀd’s punishment as Wednesday or the month’s final Wednesday.

The Qur’an gives no weekday name for the event.

Fuṣṣilat 41:16 mentions ill-fated days; Al-Qamar 54:19 a day of continuing misfortune; Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 seven nights and eight days of wind.

None says Wednesday or another weekday is inherently unlucky in every year.

Even independently establishing that an event occurred on Wednesday would not prove all Wednesdays share that quality.

An event’s date differs from its cause.

Using ʿĀd’s story to assert permanent ill omen for a day therefore exceeds the Qur’anic wording.

Do blessed and ill-fated times contradict one another?

Al-Dukhān 44:3 speaks of laylatin mubārakatin, a blessed night:

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَاهُ فِي لَيْلَةٍ مُبَارَكَةٍ إِنَّا كُنَّا مُنْذِرِينَ

innā anzalnāhu fī laylatin mubārakatin innā kunnā mundhirīna

Translation:

“We sent it down on a blessed night; indeed, We have been warning.”

The night’s blessing is connected with the event of revelation.

ʿĀd’s ill-fated days, by comparison, involve severe punishment.

This does not divide all times into fixed classes independently and invariably producing good or bad fortune.

Blessing and naḥs have different semantic fields; each use’s cause and scope must be examined in its verse.

A blessed night also does not establish an unlimited list of inherently ominous days.

Can one time prove adverse for some and favourable for others?

ʿĀd’s punishment days were severe and ill-fated for them.

The account does not judge every person worldwide during those days.

Linguistically, a time can involve a harmful event for one group while others have a different experience.

This fits the relative character of adverse outcomes in some uses.

It does not make every Arabic naḥs merely subjective feeling: hardship, harm, and destruction can be objective realities.

ʿĀd’s story concerns a devastating event, rather than simply feeling bad or psychological pessimism.

Distinguish a harmful event’s reality from attributing independent power to the day on which it occurred.

This preserves the passage’s actual meaning and avoids constructing beliefs the Qur’an does not state.

5. Nuḥās in Al-Raḥmān 55:35: smoke or copper?

The third occurrence differs substantially from the first two.

يُرْسَلُ عَلَيْكُمَا شُوَاظٌ مِنْ نَارٍ وَنُحَاسٌ فَلَا تَنْتَصِرَانِ

yursalu ʿalaykumā shuwāẓun min nārin wa-nuḥāsun fa-lā tantaṣirāni

Translation following the smoke reading:

“A flame of fire and smoke will be sent against you two groups, and you will be unable to defend yourselves.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:35

Nuḥāsun is a noun, joined with shuwāẓun min nārin.

Shuwāẓ concerns a flame or tongue of fire.

Nuḥās has two established contextual interpretations: smoke and copper.

First reading: smoke without flame

Nuḥās then denotes smoke rising from fire without being flame itself.

The meaning is recorded in Arabic and supported by the poetic witness attributed to al-Jaʿdī.

The verse describes two fire-related phenomena being sent: flame and smoke.

Their juxtaposition is natural: both can arise from fire while remaining distinct phenomena.

Nuḥās here names smoke, rather than misfortune or ill omen.

Second reading: copper or molten metal

The alternative takes nuḥās as copper.

Some interpretations identify molten copper poured upon the addressees in the scene of punishment.

Copper is an established Arabic meaning and cannot be dismissed merely because the verse mentions fire.

Nuḥās alone nevertheless does not mean molten copper. Its molten state and manner of being sent come from the punishment context and interpretive possibilities.

Choosing copper therefore requires distinguishing the word’s meaning from added details about the metal’s state.

Recitation and grammatical role

Ḥafṣ has nominative wa-nuḥāsun, which can be coordinated with shuwāẓun.

Some recognized recitations have genitive wa-nuḥāsin, which can be coordinated with nārin. Other analyses address this construction’s relation to flame and smoke, including possible omitted wording.

This affects sentence structure without independently deciding between smoke and copper.

Both lexical senses require contextual examination.

This study prefers smoke because of its juxtaposition with flame and the Arabic witnesses. That preference does not make copper linguistically impossible.

Copper has genuine lexical support and should not be dismissed as baseless.

Why nuḥās cannot simply be translated as misfortune

The ʿĀd verses use naḥs for hardship and an adverse outcome.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:35, nuḥās is something sent with a flame of fire.

The construction fits a substance or phenomenon’s name.

Rendering it ill omen, bad luck, or misfortune without sufficient linguistic evidence would ignore the recorded smoke and copper senses.

Proving smoke or copper necessarily derived from misfortune is also unnecessary.

Actual Arabic uses and context suffice for examining the word.

6. Related roots and translation proposals

N Ḥ S (نَحْس) and S ʿ D (سَعْد): adverse and favourable outcomes

A familiar Arabic contrast is naḥs versus saʿd.

Saʿd can concern happiness, good fortune, or favourable circumstances.

Naḥs concerns hardship, harm, and an adverse outcome.

The contrast should not become a theory of stellar control over human destiny.

An event can be described through either without its cause being attributed to time or stars.

The Qur’an also uses derivatives of S ʿ D, including suʿidū and saʿīd, concerning happiness and people’s final outcomes.

Their contexts likewise matter; every saʿd or naḥs should not be automatically associated with astrology or divination.

N Ḥ S (نَحْس) and Sh ʾ M (شُؤْم): misfortune and ill omen

Arabic shuʾm concerns ill omen, adverse conditions, and an unwelcome outcome.

It also appears in lexical explanations of naḥs.

The words are close in some uses without being interchangeable in every construction.

Shuʾm can concern beliefs about ill omen or attributing adversity to a person or thing.

Naḥs can likewise occur there, but its Qur’anic days concern an actual severe event.

“Ominous days” is intelligible Persian, while “ill-fated days” in this context reduces the likelihood of a superstitious inference.

The choice does not deny the established shuʾm meaning; it aims to convey the verse more precisely in Persian.

N Ḥ S (نَحْس) and Ḍ R R (ضَرَر): harm and misfortune

Ḍ R R concerns harm and injury.

Harm appears among naḥs’s close lexical explanations.

Ḍarar can directly name damage suffered, while temporal naḥs describes an overall adverse condition or outcome.

A harmful event’s day can be called naḥs, while the injury itself can be called ḍarar.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute boundary.

Study of Ḍ R R

N Ḥ S (نَحْس) and Ḥ S M (حَسْم): misfortune and termination

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 applies ḥusūman to ʿĀd’s seven nights and eight days of punishment.

The word belongs to Ḥ S M, rather than N Ḥ S.

Ḥasm concerns cutting, terminating, and in some uses persisting in an action until its outcome.

The verse can concern successive days and punishment that brought the people to their end.

The adverse outcome does not make ḥusūman an N Ḥ S derivative.

In Fuṣṣilat, naḥisāt describes ill-fated days; in Al-Ḥāqqah, ḥusūman describes continuation or the punishment’s terminating effect.

They concern the same event but belong to different roots.

Study of Ḥ S M

N Ḥ S (نَحْس) and N J S (نَجَس): misfortune and impurity

The roots resemble one another in spelling and sound but differ semantically.

Arabic N J S concerns impurity and contamination.

N Ḥ S in ʿĀd’s story concerns hardship, adversity, and an unfortunate outcome.

A day’s being naḥs therefore does not make it physically or ritually impure.

Impurity is not naḥs’s independent meaning.

Translation and Qur’anic explanation should preserve the distinction.

Study of N J S

Translation proposals for Forqan

General naḥs may be rendered misfortune, ill omen, hardship, or an adverse condition, according to context.

Naḥīs applied to a difficult year or situation can be hard, harmful, or ill-fated.

Persian manḥūs can be unfortunate or afflicted with misfortune, without establishing an inherent independent power within the person.

Material nuḥās requires choosing smoke or copper contextually.

Suggested renderings of the three Qur’anic occurrences follow:

In Fuṣṣilat, ill-fated days identifies times associated with a harmful event.

In Al-Qamar, mustamirr must also be preserved as an independent part of the sentence.

This study proposes smoke for Al-Raḥmān 55:35. Forqan’s current translation has molten copper, following the other reading; copper’s meaning and the inference of a molten state require separate explanation of the verse.

Inherently ominous days, days cursed forever, or days unlucky for everybody add restrictions absent from the Arabic wording.

Nor should nuḥās in Al-Raḥmān become ill omen or misfortune.

7. Conclusion

Arabic N Ḥ S has several branches that cannot all be securely traced to one historical origin.

The first concerns hardship, harm, adverse conditions, and misfortune. Naḥs contrasts with saʿd and can describe a day, year, person, or event.

Other uses concern cold wind, dust, and harsh natural conditions.

Nuḥās names smoke without flame and copper; uses involving origin and disposition also occur in the lexical family.

The Qur’an contains three occurrences.

Fuṣṣilat 41:16 and Al-Qamar 54:19 concern ʿĀd’s punishment days, whose adversity relates to the severe, devastating event they experienced.

Parallel passages, especially Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, describe the tremendous wind and its seven nights and eight days.

These reports do not alone establish an inherently unlucky date or weekday for everybody in every age.

Al-Raḥmān 55:35 joins nuḥās with flame. Both smoke and copper are established Arabic senses and interpretive possibilities. This study prefers smoke for the basic rendering while recognizing copper’s lexical basis.

Smoke and copper should not be forcibly derived from misfortune, nor should shared roots make every meaning identical.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

In the saʿd/naḥs branch, N Ḥ S concerns hardship, harm, adverse conditions, and an unfortunate outcome. A time can be described through a harmful event affecting a person or group without establishing independent permanent power within that time.

In another branch, nuḥās denotes smoke and copper. Its historical relationship with misfortune is uncertain; shared consonants should not impose one definition.

Qur’anic naḥs and naḥisāt concern hardship and the adverse outcome of ʿĀd’s punishment days. This study’s preferred nuḥās reading in Al-Raḥmān 55:35 is smoke, while copper remains an established Arabic and interpretive alternative.

Hardship and adverse outcomes therefore define the first branch, while the wider family requires distinct semantic branches. The Qur’anic occurrences do not justify turning days of disaster into inherently ominous dates recurring in a calendar.
