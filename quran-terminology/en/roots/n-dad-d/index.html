Arranging parts closely together: from layers of stone and household goods to date clusters and laden trees

1. The root and its semantic core

In Arabic, N DAD D concerns gathering and placing parts beside or upon one another in a close, orderly arrangement.

“نَضَدَ الشَّيْءَ” describes gathering and arranging something’s parts in a particular disposition, as with garments stacked upon one another, stones laid in rows or layers, or goods arranged in a particular place.

Two features matter: parts being brought together, and their close or orderly arrangement.

This need not mean that every part sits above another. Objects can be arranged horizontally, vertically or in a compact group.

“نَضِيد” also describes something whose parts fit closely together or are piled upon one another. Such descriptions concern date clusters, abundant foliage and fruit, dense clouds and closely laid stones.

The same semantic field includes recorded words for arranged household goods, cushions and a support upon which objects are placed.

In some social uses, “أَنْضَاد” denotes relatives or others who gather around a person and provide support.

This has an intelligible connection with gathering and close association, without restricting the root’s primary meaning to kinship or social support.

A defensible semantic core is arranging and gathering parts in a close, orderly disposition.

Abundance, density, beauty, firmness and readiness may accompany particular instances, but none alone defines every derivative.

A small group of goods can, for example, be carefully arranged and described as mandūd, while a large number of scattered, disordered objects may not be.

This matters when identifying the Qur’anic uses.

The Qur’anic forms

The root occurs three times, in three verses across three surahs.

VerseQur’anic formMorphology
Hūd 11:82مَنْضُودٍPassive participle
Qāf 50:10نَضِيدٌDescription on the faʿīl pattern
Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29مَنْضُودٍPassive participle

“مَنْضُود” is the passive participle of the Form I verb “نَضَدَ”.

“نَضِيد” follows the pattern “فَعِيل” and here describes parts laid beside or upon one another. Lexicographers often explain it as equivalent to mandūd.

No verb from this root, such as “نَضَدَ”, “يَنْضِدُ”, “نَضَّدَ” or “تَنَضَّدَ”, occurs in the Qur’an.

Arabic also records “نَضْد”, “تَنْضِيد”, “نَضِيدَة”, “نَضَائِد”, “مَنْضَدَة” and “أَنْضَاد”, but these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

All the Qur’anic occurrences therefore describe the state of parts being closely arranged, rather than directly expressing the action of arranging them.

2. Early Arabic uses

A. Arranging goods and garments

A clear early use concerns household goods.

One lexical explanation states:

نَضَدْتُ الْمَتَاعَ ... جَعَلْتُ بَعْضَهُ عَلَى بَعْضٍ

naḍadtu al-matāʿa … jaʿaltu baʿḍahu ʿalā baʿḍin

Meaning:

“I arranged the goods, placing some upon others.”

Another explanation describes bringing the parts together.

Naḍd is therefore not restricted to positioning objects in one direction. Its broader sense is gathering and arranging them closely and in order.

“نَضَدَ الثِّيَابَ” can describe stacking garments, and “نَضَدَ الْمَتَاعَ” arranging goods.

In these uses, the action relates to the resulting disposition of the group.

The word does not establish that the goods were necessarily valuable or expensive.

Some sources also use naḍad for choice household goods, but value is not the primary meaning of the verb.

B. A poetic witness from al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says in his famous ode:

خَلَّتْ سَبِيلَ أَتِيٍّ كَانَ يَحْبِسُهُ
وَرَفَّعَتْهُ إِلَى السِّجْفَيْنِ فَالنَّضَدِ

khallat sabīla atiyyin kāna yaḥbisuhu / wa-raffaʿathu ilā al-sijfayni fa-al-naḍadi

An approximate rendering:

“She opened the way for flowing water previously held back, and brought it to the two curtains at the front of the dwelling and the arranged household goods.”

Here naḍad denotes household goods or garments stacked together.

“السِّجْفَيْن” refers to the curtains hanging at the front of the dwelling.

Early commentaries explain the verse within a picture of an abandoned dwelling, decay and moving water.

For this root study, the relevant point is naḍad naming a collection of arranged household goods.

The precise interpretation of the noun is disputed, however.

Some take it to denote the arranged goods themselves.

Another explanation identifies the support or couch upon which the goods are placed.

A criticism of the latter explanation prefers the meaning of the arranged goods themselves.

The verse supports arrangement and arranged goods, but a disputed explanation of their support should not become the word’s sole certain meaning.

It is especially relevant because it is recorded in the poet’s collected verse and early commentaries, representing the tradition of pre-Islamic poetry.

C. Naḍd and tanḍīd: arranging and packing closely

Arabic records both forms:

نَضَدَ الشَّيْءَ

naḍada al-shayʾa

And:

نَضَّدَ الشَّيْءَ

naḍḍada al-shayʾa

Both can mean placing parts beside or upon one another.

Some lexical explanations give Form II a greater emphasis on density and close placement.

One explanation treats tanḍīd as equivalent to naḍd, with consonant doubling intensifying the compact arrangement.

This is intelligible morphologically.

It does not establish that every Form I instance is loose or sparse, while every Form II instance necessarily expresses the greatest possible density.

Actual usage can overlap.

Neither verb occurs in the Qur’an; the Qur’anic forms describe something already arranged.

D. Stones and mountain layers

“أَنْضَادُ الْجِبَالِ” denotes stones or rocky mountain masses placed upon or beside one another.

The root is therefore not restricted to goods arranged by people.

Stones, too, can be described as closely set or layered.

This does not make naḍd a technical geological term for a particular sedimentary rock.

The early usage describes the apparent arrangement and disposition of the stones.

It is relevant to mandūd in Hūd 11:82, where the root occurs in the setting of stones described as being from sijjīl.

E. Clouds

“نَضَدٌ مِنَ السَّحَابِ” is recorded for dense, closely gathered clouds.

The clouds are viewed as parts piled upon one another or gathered side by side.

A poetic witness transmitted in lexical sources states:

أَلَا تَسْأَلُ الْأَطْلَالَ بِالْجَرَعِ الْعُفْرِ
سَقَاهُنَّ رَبِّي صَوْبَ ذِي نَضَدٍ صُمْرِ

alā tasʾalu al-aṭlāla bi-al-jaraʿi al-ʿufri / saqāhunna rabbī ṣawba dhī naḍadin ṣumri

An approximate rendering:

“Will you not ask the dwelling traces in that sandy ground? May my Lord water them with rain from a closely gathered cloud.”

Naḍad here describes the state of the cloud.

The poet’s identity is unclear in the transmission examined. The final word is transmitted as both “صُمْرِ” and “ضُمْرِ”, so that part of the witness requires caution.

It records the root’s use for clouds but should not be attributed without evidence to a particular pre-Islamic poet.

Naḍad itself is not the name of the rain here.

The clouds are dense and rain falls from them, but density and rainfall are distinct matters.

F. Leaves and fruit

“شَجَرٌ نَضِيدٌ” describes trees with leaves or fruit closely packed from lower to upper parts.

This shares a semantic basis with arranged household goods: parts sit closely together in an ordered disposition.

In a tree, however, the state results from natural growth, rather than necessarily from human arrangement.

Naḍd does not itself require a person’s deliberate action.

Nor does every tree described as naḍīd necessarily have ripe fruit ready to eat.

The emerging parts of a cluster can also sit closely together and be called naḍīd.

This matters particularly in Qāf 50:10.

G. Naḍīdah, naḍāʾid and manḍadah

Early Arabic uses naḍīdah for a cushion or an item placed among arranged furnishings.

Its plural is naḍāʾid.

Early usage records naḍad for a support or couch upon which goods are arranged. Manḍadah should not be assigned to that period without evidence.

Modern Arabic uses both minḍadah and manḍadah for a table; both formations have been accepted morphologically.

The modern table meaning should not be projected onto every earlier use of a support for arranging objects.

A support upon which objects are placed can differ in shape and function from a modern table.

The semantic connection is clear: a support on which objects are arranged, or an item among arranged goods.

H. Anḍād: relatives and social support

“أَنْضَادُ الرَّجُلِ” is recorded for paternal and maternal uncles, and those who gather around someone to support them.

Some uses also connect naḍad with social standing and honor.

This can be related to people gathering and associating closely.

Honor and social standing are not the root’s primary meaning, however.

Relatives may form a close group while differing in conduct or moral standing.

The pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says:

وَقَوْمُكَ إِنْ يَضْمَنُوا جَارَةً
يَكُونُوا بِمَوْضِعِ أَنْضَادِهَا

wa-qawmuka in yaḍmanū jāratan / yakūnū bi-mawḍiʿi anḍādihā

An approximate rendering:

“If your people undertake a woman’s protection, they will stand in the place of her supporters and protectors.”

Anḍādihā here concerns a position of support and protection.

This witness is transmitted in Arabic literary tradition.

Accounts differ over the precise social position intended by the poet, however.

The sense of support and gathered relatives should remain clear without asserting social details the text does not explicitly establish.

3. Hūd 11:82: “سِجِّيلٍ مَنْضُودٍ”

فَلَمَّا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا جَعَلْنَا عَالِيَهَا سَافِلَهَا وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهَا حِجَارَةً مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ مَنْضُودٍ

fa-lammā jāʾa amrunā jaʿalnā ʿāliyahā sāfilahā wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhā ḥijāratan min sijjīlin manḍūdin

A rendering close to the wording:

“When Our command came, We made its upper part its lower part and rained upon it stones of closely layered sijjīl.”

Hūd 11:82

This is the root’s first occurrence in Qur’anic textual order.

The verse belongs to the account of Lot’s people and their punishment.

Unlike the plant and fruit uses, mandūd here concerns stones and a material named sijjīl.

A. The lexical meaning of mandūd

Mandūd is the passive participle of naḍada.

Its primary meaning is something whose parts have been arranged closely, in order or in layers.

Applied to stones or material, explanations include “laid upon one another”, “layered” or “closely packed and arranged”.

Mandūd itself does not mean stone, clay, brick or fire.

The verse contains several independent words:

“حِجَارَةً”: stones.

“سِجِّيلٍ”: the material from which the stones are described as coming.

“مَنْضُودٍ”: a description of arrangement and close placement.

The whole phrase’s meaning should therefore not be compressed into one word.

B. Which word does mandūd describe?

The important syntactic point is that mandūd is genitive.

Sijjīl is also genitive.

Ḥijārah, however, is accusative.

In the sentence’s direct construction, mandūd therefore qualifies sijjīl rather than directly qualifying ḥijārah.

One syntactic explanation likewise highlights this difference.

On that explanation, a direct adjective of “حِجَارَةً” would be expected to take the feminine singular accusative form “مَنْضُودَةً”, agreeing with this nonhuman plural.

Hūd 11:83 uses the accusative “مُسَوَّمَةً”, which relates to the stones.

The difference between the descriptions should remain clear.

A translation such as “stones that rained down successively in order” may convey one interpretation, but does not show mandūd’s direct grammatical relation to sijjīl.

C. Does sijjīl mean clay or baked stone?

Lexical and interpretive explanations of sijjīl differ.

Some connect it with clay or stones made from clay.

Some reports propose a Persian origin, including compounds connected with stone and clay.

That requires an independent study of the word’s origin and history.

Another verse states:

لِنُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهِمْ حِجَارَةً مِنْ طِينٍ

li-nursila ʿalayhim ḥijāratan min ṭīnin

“So that we may send upon them stones of clay.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33

It relates to Hūd 11:82 through the stones sent upon Lot’s people.

Even accepting a connection between sijjīl and clay does not make mandūd itself mean baked clay or hard stone.

Mandūd concerns parts being closely set or placed upon one another.

The material and the manner in which it hardens must be examined separately.

D. Does mandūd mean stones descending successively?

Interpretive tradition offers several explanations here.

One describes the stones descending in order or succession.

Another describes the sijjīl material as closely layered.

Others understand stones arranged and prepared for the punishment.

These readings share a field of arrangement, close placement and gathering.

The reading concerning the sijjīl itself, however, has more direct grammatical and lexical support.

One interpretive preference adopts that reading because mandūd qualifies sijjīl.

This does not establish that the stones necessarily descended without order or succession.

The verse independently describes stones raining down, and the event could involve succession. Deriving that specifically from mandūd without considering its grammatical relation is less precise.

E. Does mandūd describe the stones’ geological formation?

The description can suggest layered material or parts laid upon one another.

The verse says nothing, however, about minerals, pressure, temperature, deposition time or the precise process of becoming stone.

“سِجِّيلٍ مَنْضُودٍ” should therefore not be restricted without independent evidence to a specific sedimentary rock or geological structure.

That would replace an ordinary Arabic description with a technical term and theory.

F. Mandūd and musawwamah

Hūd 11:83 states:

مُسَوَّمَةً عِنْدَ رَبِّكَ وَمَا هِيَ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ بِبَعِيدٍ

musawwamatan ʿinda rabbika wa-mā hiya mina al-ẓālimīna bi-baʿīdin

Translation:

“Marked in the keeping of your Lord; and it is not far from the wrongdoers.”

Musawwamah belongs to S W M and concerns being marked.

Mandūd belongs to N DAD D and concerns ordered, close placement of parts.

Marking and arranging should therefore remain distinct.

The parallel passages Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:34 also describe stones of clay as marked.

This connection helps identify the passages’ shared subject without replacing separate study of each root.

4. Qāf 50:10: “طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ”

وَالنَّخْلَ بَاسِقَاتٍ لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ

wa-al-nakhla bāsiqātin lahā ṭalʿun naḍīdun

Translation:

“And tall date palms with closely packed emerging clusters.”

Qāf 50:10

This is the root’s second Qur’anic occurrence in textual order.

Unlike Hūd 11:82, it concerns date-palm growth and produce rather than punishment and stones.

A. Al-nakhl and bāsiqāt

Al-nakhl names date palms.

Bāsiqāt belongs to B S Q and describes their height.

The trees’ height therefore comes from bāsiqāt, rather than naḍīd.

Study of B S Q

Naḍīd should not be rendered as “very tall”.

It describes the state of the ṭalʿ, not the palm trunk’s height.

B. Ṭalʿ: the emerging date-palm cluster

Applied to palms, ṭalʿ denotes the emerging inflorescence or cluster at an early stage of growth.

Lexical accounts connect it with its covering, described by kufurrā and akmām.

One interpretation likewise identifies ṭalʿ here as the emerging, enclosed part of the cluster.

Al-Raḥmān 55:11 states:

فِيهَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَالنَّخْلُ ذَاتُ الْأَكْمَامِ

fīhā fākihatun wa-al-nakhlu dhātu al-akmāmi

“In it are fruit and date palms with enclosing sheaths.”

Al-Anʿām 6:99 also mentions ṭalʿ and qinwān.

These verses use different words for the tree, the emerging blossom or cluster, its covering and the fruit clusters.

Ṭalʿ in Qāf 50:10 should therefore not immediately become “fully ripe dates”.

Study of N KH L; Study of Ṭ L ʿ

C. The meaning of naḍīd

Naḍīd follows the pattern faʿīl.

In this family its meaning is close to mandūd: parts set closely together or upon one another.

Early explanations describe parts placed upon other parts.

Lexical explanations likewise identify ṭalʿ naḍīd as a cluster whose parts sit one upon another.

A more specific explanation considers the cluster while still enclosed in its covering.

On that account, it is called naḍīd while enclosed because its parts remain closely joined.

Naḍīd here concerns the close, compact arrangement of the cluster’s parts.

It does not establish that all the dates are already ripe and ready to eat.

Nor does it itself mean sweetness, large size or a guaranteed abundant crop, although a full, dense cluster can reinforce such an image.

D. Does naḍīd mean abundant?

Some translations render “طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ” as “abundant clusters” or “dense clusters”.

These are intelligible as a general image.

Abundance and arrangement should nevertheless remain distinct.

Naḍīd directly concerns parts placed beside or upon one another.

Density may suggest abundance, but abundance alone is not the whole meaning.

Many fruits may, for example, be scattered across separate locations; that abundance is not necessarily naḍd.

“Closely packed”, “dense and arranged” or “layered together” better explain the word’s particular sense.

E. The surrounding verses

Qāf 50:9 states:

وَنَزَّلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً مُبَارَكًا فَأَنْبَتْنَا بِهِ جَنَّاتٍ وَحَبَّ الْحَصِيدِ

wa-nazzalnā mina al-samāʾi māʾan mubārakan fa-anbatnā bihi jannātin wa-ḥabba al-ḥaṣīdi

Translation:

“We sent down blessed water from the sky and through it caused gardens and harvestable grain to grow.”

Qāf 50:10 then describes tall palms and closely packed emerging clusters.

Qāf 50:11 states:

رِزْقًا لِلْعِبَادِ وَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِ بَلْدَةً مَيْتًا كَذَٰلِكَ الْخُرُوجُ

rizqan li-al-ʿibādi wa-aḥyaynā bihi baldatan maytan kadhālika al-khurūju

Translation:

“As provision for the servants; through it We brought a dead land to life. Such will be the emergence.”

Together these connect plant growth with human provision and the possibility of life after death.

Naḍīd itself does not mean resurrection, however.

It describes the cluster’s state; the resurrection connection comes from the passage as a whole.

Lexical study should preserve that distinction.

5. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29: “طَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ”

وَطَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ

wa-ṭalḥin manḍūdin

A rendering close to the wording:

“And talh with closely packed, densely arranged parts.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29

This is the root’s third and final Qur’anic occurrence in textual order.

Mandūd here describes ṭalḥ.

Understanding the image requires examining what ṭalḥ denotes in Arabic.

A. The verse’s context

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:27 states:

وَأَصْحَابُ الْيَمِينِ مَا أَصْحَابُ الْيَمِينِ

wa-aṣḥābu al-yamīni mā aṣḥābu al-yamīni

“And the companions of the right: what companions of the right!”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:30, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:32 then state:

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūdin

“Among sidr described as makhḍūd.”

وَطَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ

wa-ṭalḥin manḍūdin

“And talh described as mandūd.”

وَظِلٍّ مَمْدُودٍ

wa-ẓillin mamdūdin

“And extended shade.”

وَمَاءٍ مَسْكُوبٍ

wa-māʾin maskūbin

“And poured, flowing water.”

وَفَاكِهَةٍ كَثِيرَةٍ

wa-fākihatin kathīratin

“And abundant fruit.”

These describe gifts for the Garden’s inhabitants.

They mention trees, shade, water and fruit.

Ṭalḥ mandūd therefore belongs to a picture of vegetation and enjoyment of gifts.

That context does not make mandūd itself mean shade, water or fruit.

B. Ṭalḥ: a thorn-bearing tree or banana?

A familiar early Arabic use names a large, thorn-bearing tree.

Early explanations of the verse record that meaning, referring to trees known in the Arabs’ natural surroundings.

Many interpretive reports instead identify ṭalḥ as banana.

One report also states that people in Yemen called banana ṭalḥ.

This suggests that the word need not have denoted only one kind of tree across all Arabic varieties.

Neither meaning should therefore be asserted without explanation as the sole certain meaning of every historical use.

That dispute concerns Ṭ L Ḥ, however, rather than N DAD D.

Mandūd retains the meaning of closely and densely arranged parts in either reading.

If ṭalḥ denotes banana, fruit arranged in close rows and bunches can be understood.

If it denotes the familiar thorn-bearing tree, mandūd can describe dense, abundant leaves, branches or produce.

Mandūd itself does not decide which tree ṭalḥ identifies.

C. Does mandūd mean banana?

No.

Mandūd belongs to N DAD D and describes parts closely arranged or densely placed together.

Banana is one explanation of ṭalḥ.

Rendering the whole phrase as “abundant bananas” combines one reading of ṭalḥ with one possible implication of mandūd.

That can be useful in an interpretive explanation, but does not clearly preserve each word’s independent meaning.

For a root study, the meanings should remain separate.

D. The reported reading “طَلْعٍ مَنْضُودٍ”

An interpretive report attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib proposes “وَطَلْعٍ مَنْضُودٍ” in place of “وَطَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ”. Quoting this report does not itself establish the attribution’s historical reliability.

The report also refers to Qāf 50:10:

لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ

lahā ṭalʿun naḍīdun

The lexical similarity is relevant: ṭalʿ naḍīd and ṭalʿ mandūd have close semantic fields.

The status of the reading must nevertheless be stated accurately.

“وَطَلْعٍ مَنْضُودٍ” is not among the ten accepted Qur’anic readings; it is transmitted as an irregular reading outside them.

The wording accepted in the ten readings is:

وَطَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ

wa-ṭalḥin manḍūdin

That is the established wording.

The report can be examined historically and lexically, but should not replace the received text.

Nor does the similarity of ṭalḥ and ṭalʿ make them derivatives of one root.

Ṭalḥ belongs to Ṭ L Ḥ.

Ṭalʿ belongs to Ṭ L ʿ.

Mandūd belongs to N DAD D.

All three require independent identification.

E. Does mandūd mean a thornless tree?

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 states:

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūdin

“Among sidr described as makhḍūd.”

Makhḍūd belongs to KH DAD D; early explanations connect it with thornless sidr or trees with branches prepared and laden with fruit.

Mandūd belongs to N DAD D and has a different meaning.

The words’ proximity in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 does not make mandūd mean thornless too.

If the Garden’s ṭalḥ is said to be free of harmful thorns, that requires other evidence rather than the meaning of mandūd itself.

F. Does mandūd mean fruit from roots to branches?

Some interpretations describe the Garden’s trees as covered with fruit from bottom to top, without empty spaces between their parts.

That image relates to the Arabic expression shajar naḍīd.

The verse itself does not specify fruit covering every part of every trunk and branch without gaps.

“Densely and closely arranged” is therefore a more secure meaning.

Fruit covering every part of the trees can remain an interpretive explanation without being imposed on the basic translation.

6. Naḍīd and mandūd

The two Qur’anic forms differ morphologically.

Naḍīd follows faʿīl.

Mandūd follows mafʿūl.

In many Arabic uses, faʿīl can have a sense close to a passive participle.

Lexicographers therefore explain naḍīd through mandūd.

Is one denser than the other?

The patterns alone do not establish that naḍīd is always denser than mandūd, or the reverse.

Such a ranking would require clear usage evidence.

In Qāf 50:10, naḍīd describes ṭalʿ.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29, mandūd describes ṭalḥ.

The verses’ different images also reflect their subjects, rather than only the adjectives’ patterns.

Both are best understood within the field of close, compact arrangement, with the sentence determining the particular referent.

Does naḍīd always result from human work?

No.

Qāf 50:10 describes the emerging date-palm cluster as naḍīd.

Its disposition can result from the cluster’s natural growth.

Arranged household goods, by contrast, usually involve human action.

Naḍīd or mandūd alone does not identify the arranger.

Hūd 11:82 describes sijjīl as mandūd without specifying precisely how the arranged state came about.

This distinction should remain clear.

7. Comparison with related roots

N DAD D and R SAD SAD: arrangement and close joining

R SAD SAD concerns parts joined and firmly placed beside one another.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4 states:

كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

“As though they were a closely joined, firm structure.”

This relates to naḍd through close placement of parts.

Marṣūṣ emphasizes the structure’s joining and firmness, while naḍd more strongly concerns close, ordered placement.

Something can be mandūd without being bonded together like a firm building.

The roots are close but not complete synonyms.

N DAD D and SAD F F: arranging and placing in rows

SAD F F concerns forming or placing in rows.

The Qur’an uses “صَفًّا” and “صَافَّاتٍ” in different contexts.

Naḍd can likewise concern lining up or placing side by side.

Ṣaff highlights a row or linear arrangement, whereas naḍd can also concern stacking and piling.

Not everything described as mandūd necessarily forms one straight row.

Maṣfūf is one early explanation of mandūd in Hūd 11:82, but conveys only part of its semantic field.

Study of Ṣ F F

N DAD D and R K M: arrangement and accumulation

R K M concerns piling up and becoming dense.

Al-Nūr 24:43 states:

ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا

thumma yajʿaluhu rukāman

“Then He makes it a piled-up mass.”

This concerns clouds.

Arabic also uses naḍd for dense clouds.

A tendency is that naḍd can highlight orderly, close disposition of parts, while rakm emphasizes accumulation and density.

This is not an absolute boundary; their uses can overlap.

N DAD D and N Z M: arranging and ordering

In Arabic, N Ẓ M concerns stringing things together and putting them in order.

Naḍd likewise concerns arranging parts.

It highlights their placement upon or beside one another.

Naẓm can instead foreground joining parts into a particular sequence, like beads on a string.

These are close concepts, but not every use of naẓm means stacking, nor every use of naḍd making a string.

N DAD D and N TH R: arranging and scattering

N TH R concerns scattering parts.

Naḍd, by contrast, concerns gathering and placing them together.

In many situations the two can be conceived as opposites.

Scattered pearls, for example, can be contrasted with closely arranged objects.

The opposition is not absolute, however.

Scattered parts can later be arranged, and an arranged group can be scattered in an accident.

Nor is nathr necessarily negative in the Qur’an: “لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًا” in Al-Insān 76:19 belongs to a favorable image.

Arrangement and scattering should therefore not automatically become moral goodness and badness.

Study of N TH R

N DAD D and KH DAD D: mandūd and makhḍūd

These forms occur beside one another in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29:

سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

sidrin makhḍūdin

And:

طَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ

ṭalḥin manḍūdin

They belong to different roots, however.

Makhḍūd belongs to KH DAD D.

Mandūd belongs to N DAD D.

Explanations of makhḍūd concern broken or removed thorns and branches made ready.

Mandūd concerns arrangement and density of parts.

Their similar spelling does not justify translating them identically.

Study of KH Ḍ D

N DAD D and H DAD M: ṭalʿ naḍīd and ṭalʿ haḍīm

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148 states:

وَزُرُوعٍ وَنَخْلٍ طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ

wa-zurūʿin wa-nakhlin ṭalʿuhā haḍīmun

“And crops and date palms whose emerging clusters are haḍīm.”

Haḍīm belongs to H DAD M.

Lexical explanations connect it with softness, compression or the state of the emerging cluster’s parts.

Qāf 50:10 likewise uses “طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ”.

The images relate through date clusters, without making the roots complete synonyms.

Naḍīd emphasizes close disposition and arrangement of parts.

Haḍīm requires the independent evidence of its own root.

Comparison can help identify the stages and features of ṭalʿ, but does not replace a separate study of H DAD M.

8. Translation questions and proposals for Forqan

The main challenge is preserving a shared meaning of close arrangement across three different settings.

Always rendering naḍd as “abundance” would erase the arrangement and proximity of parts.

Always rendering it as “vertical layers” would unduly restrict dense foliage and clusters.

Rendering it as “firmness” could introduce a feature not required by the root itself.

Mandūd in Hūd 11:82 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 has the same form, but the qualified nouns and subjects differ.

Natural Persian translation should account for that difference.

Proposed Qur’anic renderings

VerseQur’anic expressionProposed rendering
Hūd 11:82مِنْ سِجِّيلٍ مَنْضُودٍFrom closely arranged and layered sijjīl
Qāf 50:10لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌWith densely and closely packed emerging clusters
Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29وَطَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍAnd densely, closely arranged talh

In Hūd, mandūd’s grammatical relation to sijjīl should remain visible.

In Qāf, naḍīd should describe the emerging cluster rather than palm height or fully ripe dates.

In Al-Wāqiʿah, mandūd describes ṭalḥ; neither banana nor a thorn-bearing tree should become the adjective’s own meaning.

Is “abundant” or “dense” sufficient?

The Persian anbūh can convey density and abundance.

It does not necessarily make the arrangement of parts explicit.

“Closely arranged” is therefore more precise as a lexical explanation.

“Dense and closely packed” can be more natural in some sentences.

Two close equivalents need not be added to every translation, however.

Is “orderly” sufficient?

“Orderly” conveys part of the sense.

It can nevertheless suggest only a tidy appearance without proximity and density.

A few objects can, for example, be neatly arranged with large gaps, whereas many early uses of naḍd involve parts placed close together.

“Closely arranged” or “closely packed” conveys a fuller image.

Does mandūd mean prepared and ready?

Some explanations of Hūd 11:82 describe stones prepared for the punishment.

That can be a contextual interpretation.

N DAD D itself does not mean preparing for punishment.

Readiness can result from gathering and arranging something, but is not the word’s direct meaning.

“Prepared for punishment” is therefore not an adequate replacement for mandūd in a basic translation.

Proposals for Forqan’s word boxes

Hūd 11:82

Word: مَنْضُودٍ

Root: N DAD D

Brief root meaning: Arranging parts closely beside or upon one another in order.

Meaning in this verse: Closely layered; describing sijjīl and the disposition of its parts.

Qāf 50:10

Word: نَضِيدٌ

Root: N DAD D

Brief root meaning: Closely arranged and packed together.

Meaning in this verse: Closely packed and dense; describing the emerging date-palm cluster’s parts.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29

Word: مَنْضُودٍ

Root: N DAD D

Brief root meaning: Placed closely beside or upon one another.

Meaning in this verse: Closely and densely arranged; describing the parts of ṭalḥ. Identifying the tree requires an independent examination of that word.

This preserves the root’s shared sense while explaining the different subjects of the three verses.

9. Conclusion

N DAD D concerns gathering and arranging parts closely and in order.

Early uses concern household goods, garments, stones, clouds, leaves and tree clusters.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī’s verse attests its use for arranged household goods.

Another recorded branch uses anḍād for relatives and social supporters, an intelligible connection with gathering and association.

Honor, support and social value should not become invariant features of the root, however.

One early lexical analysis identifies close gathering and orderly disposition as the family’s core.

That agrees with lexical evidence and the three Qur’anic uses.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an in two forms:

“مَنْضُود” and “نَضِيد”.

In Hūd 11:82, mandūd describes sijjīl and its layered or closely arranged parts.

Grammatically it qualifies sijjīl directly, rather than ḥijārah.

In Qāf 50:10, naḍīd describes the emerging date-palm cluster and its closely packed parts.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29, mandūd describes ṭalḥ, emphasizing dense arrangement.

The referent of ṭalḥ remains lexically and interpretively disputed: some reports identify banana, while another familiar Arabic use names a large, thorn-bearing tree.

That dispute should not be transferred into the meaning of mandūd.

The reported ṭalʿ mandūd reading in Al-Wāqiʿah is irregular and outside the ten readings; it should not replace the established ṭalḥ mandūd wording.

The uses show that naḍd is inherently neither positive nor negative.

In Hūd, it occurs in the setting of punishment.

In Qāf, it forms part of a picture of growth and provision.

In Al-Wāqiʿah, it describes the Garden’s gifts.

Each preserves close arrangement, while the subject and context determine the verse’s significance.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N DAD D means arranging and placing something’s parts closely beside or upon one another in order.

The arrangement can be horizontal, vertical or compact; natural instances need not involve direct human intervention.

Mandūd describes something whose parts have been arranged closely together or laid upon one another.

Naḍīd in its Qur’anic use is close to mandūd, describing a compact, orderly disposition of parts.

The Qur’anic uses concern sijjīl, emerging date clusters and ṭalḥ.

Thus, closely arranged and set together is the precise common meaning of the Qur’anic uses of N DAD D.

Abundance, firmness, beauty, readiness and value may accompany particular contexts, but should not be added to the root’s direct meaning without evidence.
