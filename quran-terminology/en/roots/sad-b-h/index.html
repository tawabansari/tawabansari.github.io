The appearance of daylight, morning and entry into a new condition: from dawn and changing circumstances to lamps and illumination

Arabic Ṣ B Ḥ concerns morning, emerging daylight and the beginning of a new period. Its family includes ṣubḥ, ṣabāḥ, iṣbāḥ and miṣbāḥ. Aṣbaḥa, originally used for entering morning, also acquired the broader senses of becoming and being in a condition.

This extension matters in the Qur’an: many aṣbaḥa occurrences describe changes in people, societies or nature without necessarily identifying an hour of the day.

The root occurs 45 times in 43 verses, in seven morphological or lexical categories: ṣabbaḥa once; aṣbaḥa and its inflections 28 times; ṣabāḥ once; ṣubḥ five times; miṣbāḥ and its plural four times; iṣbāḥ once; and muṣbiḥīn five times.

Three main fields appear: morning and its light, becoming or entering a condition, and lamps with their illumination.

1. The semantic core and Arabic usage

Ṣubḥ: the beginning of daylight

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

الصُّبْحُ: أَوَّلُ النَّهَارِ

al-ṣubḥu: awwalu al-nahāri

That is, morning is the beginning of the day.

Ṣubḥ is also explained through fajr, while ṣabāḥ is contrasted with masāʾ, evening.

Temporal ṣubḥ therefore concerns night’s darkness ending and daylight beginning.

The first appearance of light differs from the whole morning period. When ṣubḥ contrasts with night, the boundary of darkness and light is prominent; ṣabāḥ can sometimes cover a broader early-day period.

Lexicons including Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr report divisions of morning and evening that sometimes extend morning to noon. Such divisions show that these words are not invariably restricted to one astronomical instant.

Ṣubḥ may thus identify emerging light and ṣabāḥ the first part of the day, without an absolute distinction in every use.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: morning and temporal extent

Was redness the root’s earliest meaning?

Ibn Fāris’s Maqāyīs al-Lughah relates the root to color, identified as redness in one lexical account.

He connects the names of morning and the lamp with redness, and also discusses wajhun ṣabīḥun, a bright or handsome face.

The analysis is intelligible: the dawn sky can be red or rosy, and older lamps’ flames may appear reddish or yellowish.

The resemblance does not prove that redness historically originated every derivative.

Lexicons also define ṣubḥ through early daylight; miṣbāḥ ordinarily names a lamp. A lamp can illuminate without redness being a defining requirement.

Non-Qur’anic aṣbaḥ can also describe certain reddish or light-mixed colors. This color adjective should be distinguished from the verb aṣbaḥa, entering morning or becoming.

Light, morning and certain color qualities are related in the family, without sufficient grounds for a definite historical progression from redness to morning to every derivative.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: proposed connections with color and redness

Ṣabāḥ and the first part of the day

Ṣabāḥ denotes morning or the beginning of the day.

Ṣabīḥah likewise identifies a particular day’s morning.

Ordinary Arabic combinations such as ṣabāḥa masāʾa express recurrence morning and evening.

Ṣabbaḥtuhu can mean greeting someone in the morning.

These uses share morning time without identical grammatical functions: a time noun, a morning-period expression and an action performed in the morning.

2. Early Arabic: morning, travel, drink and raids

Morning and raiding

Morning mattered for beginning journeys, group movement and, in some circumstances, attacking an enemy in tribal Arab life.

Ṣabaḥa al-qawma meant coming to people in the morning and could express a morning raid in warfare contexts.

Lisān al-ʿArab records ṣabaḥathumu al-khaylu and ṣabbaḥathumu al-khaylu for horses coming upon people in the morning.

Literary yawmu al-ṣabāḥi can likewise denote a day of raiding.

A verse by the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā reads:

بِهِ تَرْعُفُ الْأَلْفَ إِذْ أُرْسِلَتْ
غَدَاةَ الصَّبَاحِ إِذَا النَّقْعُ ثَارَا

bihi tarʿufu al-alfa idh ursilat / ghadāta al-ṣabāḥi idhā al-naqʿu thārā

Translation: “With this horse you outstrip a thousand [horses and riders], when they are released on the morning of the raid and dust has risen.” Lexical commentary explains tarʿufu as outstripping. Transmissions differ slightly; the Maqāyīs reading is retained here.

Ghadāta al-ṣabāḥi identifies the time of movement and raiding.

Morning could thus appear in early expressions concerning battle and groups arriving suddenly, beyond merely naming a time.

Ṣabāḥ itself does not mean war or assault. The setting and accompanying expressions supply that sense.

This matters for ṣabbaḥahum in Al-Qamar 54:38 and al-mughīrāti ṣubḥan in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Maqāyīs and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Al-Aʿshā’s witness

Ṣabūḥ: a morning drink

Arabic ṣabūḥ denotes a morning drink, contrasted with evening ghabūq.

Lexical ṣabūḥ may refer to milk or another drink.

Iṣṭabaḥa is also recorded for drinking in the morning.

The word identifies the drink’s time of consumption, rather than one particular liquid.

It should not invariably be restricted to one drink or to intoxicants.

Morning sleep

Ṣubḥah and taṣabbuḥ are recorded for sleeping early in the day.

The connection concerns an action’s time. As morning drinking can be iṣṭibāḥ, sleeping then can be expressed through related forms.

These senses of the family do not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣabāḥah: facial brightness and beauty

Ṣabīḥ and ṣabīḥah can describe a beautiful, bright face.

Ṣabāḥah denotes beauty or good looks.

The face’s brightness and appearance provide a semantic connection with other branches.

Facial beauty is nevertheless not part of temporal ṣubḥ. Ṣabīḥ in such descriptions characterizes a person, rather than naming a time.

Miṣbāḥ: a lamp

Arabic miṣbāḥ means a lamp; its plural is maṣābīḥ.

Its illuminating function relates to the Ṣ B Ḥ family, while actual usage names an implement providing light.

Older lamps ordinarily burned fuel with a wick.

Miṣbāḥ should not, however, be confined to an oil container or a red-flamed device. Its familiar function is being a lamp that provides illumination.

Lexicons also record other uses, including a camel remaining at its resting place until morning. Maṣābīḥ can also denote vessels for morning drinking, and miṣbāḥ a large drinking cup.

These uses deserve separate recognition; the Qur’anic lamp and illuminating senses are clear where miṣbāḥ and maṣābīḥ occur.

Early lexicons: drink, sleep, beauty and lamps

3. Morning, dawn and light in the Qur’an

Al-Anʿām 6:96: fāliqu al-iṣbāḥi

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ وَجَعَلَ اللَّيْلَ سَكَنًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ حُسْبَانًا ذَٰلِكَ تَقْدِيرُ الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ

fāliqu al-iṣbāḥi wa-jaʿala al-layla sakanan wa-al-shamsa wa-al-qamara ḥusbānan dhālika taqdīru al-ʿazīzi al-ʿalīmi

Translation: The splitter of dawn; He made the night for rest, and the sun and moon for reckoning. This is the determination of the Mighty, the Knowing.

Al-iṣbāḥ is a verbal noun of aṣbaḥa, describing morning’s appearance or the beginning of daylight.

Fāliq belongs to F L Q and expresses splitting.

The splitting image thus comes from fāliq; al-iṣbāḥ identifies its object.

Morning contrasts with night, which is described as sakanan, rest. Sun and moon appear within reckoning and measured ordering.

The verse concerns the order of light and darkness and its relation to life and time.

Early lexical exegesis explains fāliqu al-iṣbāḥi as bringing morning light out of night’s darkness.

Splitting should not become an independent Ṣ B Ḥ meaning. Iṣbāḥ concerns dawn’s emergence; another root supplies the splitting image.

The verse does not itself detail light’s physical emergence, atmospheric refraction or sky colors. These may be scientific subjects without constituting the lexical definition of iṣbāḥ.

Al-Ṭabarī: dawn light emerging from night

Hūd 11:81: al-ṣubḥu

قَالُوا يَا لُوطُ إِنَّا رُسُلُ رَبِّكَ لَنْ يَصِلُوا إِلَيْكَ فَأَسْرِ بِأَهْلِكَ بِقِطْعٍ مِنَ اللَّيْلِ وَلَا يَلْتَفِتْ مِنْكُمْ أَحَدٌ إِلَّا امْرَأَتَكَ إِنَّهُ مُصِيبُهَا مَا أَصَابَهُمْ إِنَّ مَوْعِدَهُمُ الصُّبْحُ أَلَيْسَ الصُّبْحُ بِقَرِيبٍ

qālū yā lūṭu innā rusulu rabbika lan yaṣilū ilayka fa-asri bi-ahlika bi-qiṭʿin mina al-layli wa-lā yaltafit minkum aḥadun illā imraʾataka innahu muṣībuhā mā aṣābahum inna mawʿidahumu al-ṣubḥu a-laysa al-ṣubḥu bi-qarībin

Translation: They said, “Lot, we are your Lord’s messengers. They will never reach you. Take your family away during a part of the night, and let none of you turn back, except your wife: what befalls them will befall her. Their appointed time is morning. Is morning not near?”

Al-ṣubḥ occurs twice here.

First it identifies the appointed time: inna mawʿidahumu al-ṣubḥu.

Then it emphasizes that time’s nearness: a-laysa al-ṣubḥu bi-qarībin.

The temporal sense is clear: Lot’s family leaves during part of the night, and the appointed time arrives in the morning.

Ṣubḥ cannot simply become deliverance, victory or truth becoming apparent here. Night and the appointed time establish the temporal use.

Reflection may associate morning with one condition ending or a new phase beginning, without changing the direct meaning.

Al-Muddaththir 74:34: wa-al-ṣubḥi idhā asfara

وَالصُّبْحِ إِذَا أَسْفَرَ

wa-al-ṣubḥi idhā asfara

Translation: And by morning, when it becomes bright and clear.

Asfara describes morning in this verse.

It belongs to S F R, here concerning becoming apparent and bright.

Ṣubḥ names early daylight or its period; asfara foregrounds its emergence.

Morning can thus have a stage when its light becomes more evident.

Asfara’s meaning should not be transferred to Ṣ B Ḥ so that every ṣubḥ necessarily identifies fully evident daylight.

Al-Takwīr 81:18: wa-al-ṣubḥi idhā tanaffasa

وَالصُّبْحِ إِذَا تَنَفَّسَ

wa-al-ṣubḥi idhā tanaffasa

Translation: And by morning, when it breathes.

Tanaffasa, breathing, is applied to morning.

Gradually emerging light is pictured as breathing.

The image may evoke night’s constriction and darkness ending as daylight opens out.

Breathing is not the lexical meaning of ṣubḥ; tanaffasa supplies that image.

Nor should this become an unsupported physical theory about the sky breathing, air circulation or oxygen.

The verse uses a vivid image to describe morning’s appearance.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3: fa-al-mughīrāti ṣubḥan

فَالْمُغِيرَاتِ صُبْحًا

fa-al-mughīrāti ṣubḥan

Translation: Then by those raiding at morning.

Ṣubḥan is an adverbial of time, identifying when the raid occurs.

Al-mughīrāti derives from aghāra, to raid. The Quranic Arabic Corpus indexes it under GH Y R; several early lexicons discuss aghāra and ghārah under GH W R. This indexing difference must not merge raiding into ṣubḥan’s own meaning.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: al-mughīrāt indexed under GH Y R

Assault does not belong to ṣubḥan itself. Morning identifies the event’s time.

Lisān al-ʿArab: aghāra and ghārah under GH W R

This agrees with Arabic poetic witnesses of morning raids.

Identifying the raiders requires the whole surah. Ṣubḥan alone does not determine whether they must be horses, riders or another group.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177: ṣabāḥu al-mundharīna

فَإِذَا نَزَلَ بِسَاحَتِهِمْ فَسَاءَ صَبَاحُ الْمُنْذَرِينَ

fa-idhā nazala bi-sāḥatihim fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīna

Translation: When it descends upon their courtyard, how evil will be the morning of those warned.

Ṣabāḥ appears in ṣabāḥu al-mundharīna.

A morning could bring an army or sudden event in Arabic usage. “How evil a morning” in a threatening punishment context evokes a day beginning with disaster.

Ṣabāḥ does not itself mean punishment. Fa-sāʾa and the threat establish the morning’s badness.

A time word may acquire different emotional or evaluative associations in different settings without changing its basic meaning.

4. Aṣbaḥa: from entering morning to entering a new condition

Aṣbaḥa is among the root’s most extensive Qur’anic branches.

Its primary Arabic use can mean entering morning: aṣbaḥnā, we reached morning.

It also functions as a deficient or copular verb, expressing becoming or being in a condition.

Like ṣāra, it can take an ism and a khabar, a subject noun and predicate, expressing its subject’s new condition.

Many of its 28 Qur’anic occurrences concern becoming, a condition arising or an event’s result becoming apparent.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103: from enmity to brotherhood

The verse reads:

فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ بِنِعْمَتِهِ إِخْوَانًا

fa-aṣbaḥtum bi-niʿmatihi ikhwānan

Translation: “So by His favor you became brothers.”

Aṣbaḥtum expresses entry into a new condition here.

The preceding wording describes enmity and their hearts being joined.

It concerns a transformed human relationship, without necessarily saying they became brothers at sunrise.

The earlier temporal meaning has extended to becoming.

Al-Māʾidah 5:30 and Al-Māʾidah 5:31: action and consequence

Al-Māʾidah 5:30 reads:

فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-khāsirīna

Translation: “So he became one of the losers.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:31 reads:

فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَ

fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-nādimīna

Translation: “So he became one of the regretful.”

Both uses describe a condition resulting from preceding events.

The first presents loss as the killing’s result. The second describes regret after learning how to cover his brother’s body.

Aṣbaḥa alone does not determine the regret’s precise time.

Losing and regretting are not independent root meanings; min al-khāsirīna and min al-nādimīna supply those conditions.

Aṣbaḥa expresses being in the new condition in both constructions.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:78 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:91: peoples’ fate

Both verses contain:

فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīna

Translation: “So they lay motionless on the ground in their dwelling place.”

Aṣbaḥū follows a destructive event.

Contextually it may describe the condition left by punishment; an association with morning can also be examined in some narratives.

The verb alone nevertheless does not establish the punishment’s precise time.

The pattern recurs in Hūd 11:67, Hūd 11:94 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:37.

Jāthimīna describes their final state, with aṣbaḥū presenting them in it.

Fa-aṣbaḥū lā yurā illā masākinuhum in Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 likewise describes what remains after destruction.

Aṣbaḥa can express an event’s resulting condition without each occurrence necessarily reporting its hour.

Al-Kahf 18:40, Al-Kahf 18:41 and Al-Kahf 18:45: nature’s changing conditions

Al-Kahf 18:40 reads:

فَتُصْبِحَ صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا

fa-tuṣbiḥa ṣaʿīdan zalaqan

Translation: “Then it becomes bare, slippery ground.”

Al-Kahf 18:41 reads:

أَوْ يُصْبِحَ مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا

aw yuṣbiḥa māʾuhā ghawran

Translation: “Or its water sinks away into the depths.”

Al-Kahf 18:45 reads:

فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ

fa-aṣbaḥa hashīman tadhrūhu al-riyāḥu

Translation: “Then it became debris scattered by the winds.”

All three focus on entering another condition.

A garden may become barren ground, its water inaccessible, and vegetation dry debris.

None of these results is contained in aṣbaḥa itself. Ṣaʿīdan zalaqan, ghawran and hashīman identify the respective conditions.

Al-Kahf 18:42 has fa-aṣbaḥa yuqallibu kaffayhi: after his produce was destroyed, he began turning his hands in regret and grief.

Aṣbaḥa here accompanies a following imperfect verb, presenting the beginning or continuation of the resulting condition.

Becoming in Arabic can thus concern a state, action or result following an event.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82: human conditions and time

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:10 reads:

وَأَصْبَحَ فُؤَادُ أُمِّ مُوسَىٰ فَارِغًا

wa-aṣbaḥa fuʾādu ummi mūsā fārighan

Translation: “And the heart of Moses’ mother became empty.”

This describes her inward condition.

Fārighan supplies the condition; aṣbaḥa expresses the heart’s being in it.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 reads:

فَأَصْبَحَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ خَائِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ

fa-aṣbaḥa fī al-madīnati khāʾifan yataraqqabu

Translation: “So he was in the city, fearful and watchful.”

Morning also fits this narrative context. Moses is fearful in the city after the preceding event, and the account continues.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82 reads:

وَأَصْبَحَ الَّذِينَ تَمَنَّوْا مَكَانَهُ بِالْأَمْسِ يَقُولُونَ

wa-aṣbaḥa alladhīna tamannaw makānahu bi-al-amsi yaqūlūna

Those who had wished for Korah’s position yesterday were now speaking differently.

Bi-al-amsi clearly contrasts yesterday with their later condition. Aṣbaḥa may therefore indicate morning or the following day alongside a changed attitude.

Entering morning and becoming should not always be separated as incompatible uses. Both may be present in one context.

Al-Ṭabarī: changed attitudes toward Korah’s position

Al-Rūm 30:17: ḥīna tuṣbiḥūna

فَسُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ حِينَ تُمْسُونَ وَحِينَ تُصْبِحُونَ

fa-subḥāna allāhi ḥīna tumsūna wa-ḥīna tuṣbiḥūna

Translation: So The God is exalted, when you enter evening and when you enter morning.

Tuṣbiḥūna clearly concerns time because it contrasts with tumsūna.

The verbs express entering evening and morning.

Simply rendering tuṣbiḥūna as “you become,” omitting morning, would lose the meaning.

Subḥāna at the verse’s beginning belongs to the separate root S B Ḥ. Similarity to tuṣbiḥūna does not make them one root.

Other Qur’anic uses of aṣbaḥa

Al-Māʾidah 5:52, Al-Māʾidah 5:53 and Al-Māʾidah 5:102 concern regret, loss or rejection following conduct.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:40 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157 describe regret after actions or their outcome.

The earth becomes green after water descends in Al-Ḥajj 22:63; Al-Mulk 67:30 considers water sinking away beyond reach.

Aṣbaḥtum in Fuṣṣilat 41:23 concerns becoming losers; Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 warns of regret after rash action; aṣbaḥū ẓāhirīna in Al-Ṣaff 61:14 describes believers becoming dominant.

Fa-aṣbaḥat ka-al-ṣarīmi in Al-Qalam 68:20 describes the garden after the night’s event.

These show the verb’s broad use for resulting conditions and change.

This extension should not eliminate time. Each verse may concern actual morning, another condition, or a context containing both.

5. Muṣbiḥīn, ṣabbaḥa and morning arrival

Muṣbiḥīn in narratives and events

Muṣbiḥīn is the masculine plural active participle of aṣbaḥa.

It occurs five times in the Qur’an.

Al-Ḥijr 15:66 identifies morning when announcing a people’s fate.

Al-Ḥijr 15:83 reads:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّيْحَةُ مُصْبِحِينَ

fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣayḥatu muṣbiḥīna

Translation: “So the blast seized them as they entered morning.”

Muṣbiḥīna identifies the event’s morning time or condition.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:137 reads:

وَإِنَّكُمْ لَتَمُرُّونَ عَلَيْهِمْ مُصْبِحِينَ

wa-innakum la-tamurrūna ʿalayhim muṣbiḥīna

Translation: “And you pass by them in the morning.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:138 then mentions passing at night. The contrast clearly establishes the temporal sense.

In Al-Qalam 68:17, the garden’s owners plan to harvest in the morning; Al-Qalam 68:21 describes them calling one another at morning.

Muṣbiḥīn identifies people at morning or entering it in these uses.

It should not simply become “those transformed.” Unlike many aṣbaḥa uses, time is prominent in these five instances.

Al-Qamar 54:38: ṣabbaḥahum bukratan

وَلَقَدْ صَبَّحَهُمْ بُكْرَةً عَذَابٌ مُسْتَقِرٌّ

wa-laqad ṣabbaḥahum bukratan ʿadhābun mustaqirrun

Translation: An abiding punishment indeed came upon them at daybreak.

Ṣabbaḥahum is a Form II past verb.

Bukratan also identifies early morning.

ʿAdhābun mustaqirrun, an abiding punishment, is the subject: the punishment came upon them at morning.

Arabic ṣabbaḥa al-qawma concerns coming to people in the morning, and in certain contexts a morning raid.

Ṣabbaḥa does not intrinsically mean punishing. Punishment is the subject here, establishing the punitive setting.

Bukratan emphasizes the morning time without requiring two mornings or separate punishment stages.

6. Miṣbāḥ and maṣābīḥ: lamps and illumination

Al-Nūr 24:35: a lamp in a niche

The Light Verse reads:

مَثَلُ نُورِهِ كَمِشْكَاةٍ فِيهَا مِصْبَاحٌ

mathalu nūrihi ka-mishkātin fīhā miṣbāḥun

Translation: “The likeness of His light is a niche containing a lamp.”

It continues:

الْمِصْبَاحُ فِي زُجَاجَةٍ

al-miṣbāḥu fī zujājatin

Translation: “The lamp is in glass.”

Miṣbāḥ occurs twice in the verse.

It follows the mifʿāl pattern and names an illuminating implement here.

The verse combines lamp, glass, oil and light within a parable.

Miṣbāḥ directly means the lamp, rather than light generally, the glass or the oil.

These elements have separate names and functions.

Does miṣbāḥ mean light itself?

A lamp provides light, so figurative uses may connect the two.

In Al-Nūr 24:35, however, mishkāh, zujājah and zayt establish the lamp in the immediate image.

The parable requires analysis of its entire structure. Ṣ B Ḥ alone cannot yield every meaning concerning guidance, divine light or faith’s reality.

This accords with Forqan’s lexical method: identify the direct sense first, then separately examine the verse’s semantic relationships.

Fuṣṣilat 41:12: bi-maṣābīḥa

The verse reads:

وَزَيَّنَّا السَّمَاءَ الدُّنْيَا بِمَصَابِيحَ وَحِفْظًا

wa-zayyannā al-samāʾa al-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa-ḥifẓan

Translation: “And We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps, and for protection.”

Maṣābīḥ is the plural of miṣbāḥ.

These are not manufactured lamps: the verse describes heavenly bodies or lights through a lamp image.

Usage extends from earthly lighting implements to heavenly lights.

This does not transfer every oil lamp’s wick, fuel or glass vessel to heavenly bodies.

Their illumination and lamp-like appearance matter to the image.

Al-Mulk 67:5: the heaven’s lamps

وَلَقَدْ زَيَّنَّا السَّمَاءَ الدُّنْيَا بِمَصَابِيحَ وَجَعَلْنَاهَا رُجُومًا لِلشَّيَاطِينِ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لَهُمْ عَذَابَ السَّعِيرِ

wa-laqad zayyannā al-samāʾa al-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa-jaʿalnāhā rujūman li-al-shayāṭīni wa-aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāba al-saʿīri

Translation: We have indeed adorned the nearest heaven with lamps, made them a means of driving away devils, and prepared for them the punishment of the blazing Fire.

Maṣābīḥ again concerns heavenly lamps.

Context identifies lights in the nearest heaven.

The word alone does not determine their physical type, composition or relation to subsequent events in the verse.

Examining wa-jaʿalnāhā rujūman li-al-shayāṭīni requires separate attention to the pronoun’s referent, rujūman and the whole construction.

Ṣ B Ḥ research alone does not answer all these questions.

Lamp and light distinguished

Miṣbāḥ names a lamp or illuminator; nūr denotes light itself.

Their Qur’anic uses are related without complete synonymy.

The lamp forms one element of Al-Nūr 24:35’s light image.

Fuṣṣilat 41:12 and Al-Mulk 67:5 apply lamps to luminous heavenly phenomena.

Miṣbāḥ can therefore name an actual lighting implement or figuratively describe something shining like a lamp.

7. Nearby roots and words distinguished

Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ) and F J R (fajr)

Fajr belongs to F J R, associated with splitting darkness and emerging daylight in its dawn use.

Ṣubḥ likewise concerns the beginning of daylight.

The words approach one another in many contexts while their imagery differs.

Splitting and opening characterize the fajr family; ṣubḥ names early daylight and its period.

Fajr cannot, however, invariably be declared an instant and ṣubḥ a long period.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 uses fajr for dawn’s white becoming distinct from night’s black. Ṣubḥ in Hūd 11:81 identifies the appointed time after night.

They overlap in the day’s beginning without identical lexical imagery.

Study of F J R

Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ) and B K R (bukrah)

Bukrah denotes early day or morning.

Ṣabbaḥahum bukratan in Al-Qamar 54:38 places both families together.

Ṣabbaḥahum expresses an event’s morning arrival; bukratan specifies early morning.

Bukrah can imply earliness in some uses, without that quality transferring to every Ṣ B Ḥ form.

Study of B K R

Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ) and GH D W (ghuduww)

GH D W concerns movement or events at the day’s beginning.

Qur’anic ghuduww frequently contrasts with ʿashiyy or āṣāl, evening periods.

Ṣubḥ chiefly names early daylight; verbal ghuduww uses can concern setting out or moving early.

This is not an absolute boundary. Both families concern morning and early-day activities.

Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ) and N W R (nūr)

Nūr names light in daytime or nighttime, from earthly or heavenly sources.

Temporal ṣubḥ concerns the beginning of daylight.

Every morning involves light, but not every light is morning.

Miṣbāḥ also relates to nūr as a lighting implement, without making a lamp and its light identical concepts.

Study of N W R

Ṣ B Ḥ (ṣubḥ) and S B Ḥ (sabḥ)

These remain separate roots despite similar writing and sounds.

Ṣ B Ḥ concerns morning, light and lamps.

Qur’anic S B Ḥ concerns swimming, movement and glorification.

Both appear in Al-Rūm 30:17: fa-subḥāna belongs to S B Ḥ and tuṣbiḥūna to Ṣ B Ḥ.

Similar pronunciation does not prove historical or semantic kinship.

Study of S B Ḥ

8. Understanding and translation proposals

Ṣubḥ and aṣbaḥa cannot receive one identical rendering in every verse.

Al-ṣubḥ in Hūd 11:81 names a time and should be translated morning.

Al-iṣbāḥ in Al-Anʿām 6:96 concerns emerging morning light; dawn or morning’s appearance can convey it.

Tuṣbiḥūna in Al-Rūm 30:17 means entering morning because it contrasts with tumsūna.

Fa-aṣbaḥtum in Āl ʿImrān 3:103, however, means you became. “You reached morning” would distort the conceptual relationship.

Fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-nādimīna in Al-Māʾidah 5:31 means he became one of the regretful, without necessarily dating the regret to a particular morning.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82’s contrast with bi-al-amsi allows consideration of the following day alongside changed attitudes.

Fa-tuṣbiḥu al-arḍu mukhḍarratan in Al-Ḥajj 22:63 describes the earth becoming green. “The following morning” would impose a particular time without sufficient evidence.

Muṣbiḥīna in Al-Ḥijr 15:83 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:137 means at morning or upon entering morning.

Ṣabbaḥahum in Al-Qamar 54:38 describes an event coming upon people at daybreak.

Miṣbāḥ in Al-Nūr 24:35 should be lamp, rather than morning, redness or merely light.

Maṣābīḥ in Fuṣṣilat 41:12 and Al-Mulk 67:5 can be lamps, with their application to heavenly lights explained separately.

One fixed rendering for every derivative is therefore unsuitable for Forqan. Each form needs its own structure and context.

9. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ B Ḥ concerns morning, emerging daylight and dawn. Besides time words, the family includes verbs, brightness and beauty descriptions, and the lamp noun.

Lexicons propose color and redness to explain certain connections, without proving redness as every form’s historical origin.

Three principal Qur’anic fields appear: morning and early daylight; entering morning or a new condition; and lamps or lamp-like lights.

Aṣbaḥa frequently means becoming or being in a condition and should not always be restricted to literal morning. Clear temporal contrasts, such as morning and evening or night and dawn, require preserving morning.

Qur’anic miṣbāḥ denotes a lamp; plural maṣābīḥ can describe heavenly lights without transferring every earthly lamp’s property to them.

Short definition: Ṣ B Ḥ primarily concerns early daylight and morning. Aṣbaḥa can mean entering morning or a new condition; miṣbāḥ and maṣābīḥ name lamps or illuminators. Brief root meaning: Morning and the beginning of daylight; aṣbaḥa: entering morning or becoming; miṣbāḥ: a lamp.

Historical and semantic connections do not make uses identical. Accurate Qur’anic translation distinguishes morning as time, becoming as change, and a lamp as an illuminator, assessing each context through the verse’s construction.
