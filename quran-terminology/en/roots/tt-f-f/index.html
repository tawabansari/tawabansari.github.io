Giving short measure or weight: from small transactional shortages to depriving others of their due

The Arabic root ṭ f f concerns small quantities, proximity to a limit or edge, and, in commerce, giving short measure or weight. طَفِيف means slight or small; طِفَاف concerns a vessel's or measure's rim or upper limit; تَطْفِيف means withholding part of an amount that should be delivered in full.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as الْمُطَفِّفِينَ in the opening verse of Al-Muṭaffifīn. The following two verses explain their conduct: they demand their full due when receiving from people, but reduce the amount when giving others their due.

This is more than a measuring error. It illustrates a double standard concerning people's rights: completeness is demanded for oneself but disregarded when fulfilling others' claims.

Taṭfīf does not concern the insignificance of another person's entitlement, but wrongfully reducing what should be paid or delivered. Even a slight shortage remains a violation when deliberately created for one's own benefit at another's expense.

Not every shortage, inadvertent error, or reduction is taṭfīf. The conduct condemned in the Quran involves unfair measurement and fulfillment of what is due.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic usage

طَفِيف: a slight or small quantity

Classical Arabic uses طَفِيف for something small or slight in quantity.

شَيْءٌ طَفِيفٌ means something small.

It can describe an amount of wealth, a difference between measurements, or something very small relative to the original quantity.

The word does not itself mean stolen property or a violated right. Smallness describes a numerical or relative quantity; wrongful taking or giving depends on the relationship and conduct involved.

A person may willingly give away a small amount of property. The amount may be طفيف, but the gift is not shortchanging.

By contrast, someone who undertakes to deliver a specified quantity and deliberately retains part of it diminishes another's due, even if that part is small relative to the whole transaction.

This distinction matters: smallness describes quantity; commercial taṭfīf describes incomplete fulfillment of another's due.

طِفَاف: the rim and limit of a measure

Older Arabic records طَفّ, طِفَاف, and طُفَافَة for the edge, rim, and upper limit of a vessel or measure.

Some usages concern an amount reaching the rim; others concern a measure approaching fullness without being completely full.

Imagine a vessel that should be filled to a designated mark or level.

If its contents remain slightly below that level, it may appear almost full while the amount delivered remains incomplete.

This image helps explain the semantic connection between approaching the limit of a measure and giving less than its full amount.

Not every use of طفاف, however, describes an incomplete measure: the word also denotes a vessel's rim or upper limit, and sometimes reaching fullness.

A more precise historical account connects this family with a limit, edge, proximity to a given amount, and small quantity. In commercial usage, taṭfīf denotes giving less than should be delivered in full.

Drawing near and becoming accessible

طَفَّ الشَّيْءُ and أَطَفَّ are also recorded for drawing near, coming into view, or becoming accessible.

خُذْ مَا طَفَّ لَكَ means taking what has come near or within reach.

This branch concerns proximity and something entering the range of accessibility.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl

A verse attributed to ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, states:

يَظَلُّ فِي الْحَنْظَلِ الْخُطْبَانِ يَنْقُفُهُ
وَمَا اسْتَطَفَّ مِنَ التَّنُّومِ مَخْذُومُ

yaẓallu fī al-ḥanẓali al-khuṭbāni yanqufuhu / wa-mā istaṭaffa mina al-tannūmi makhdhūm

“He remains among the striped colocynths, pecking them open; whatever of the tannūm plant comes within reach is cut off.”

The passage describes a male ostrich feeding on desert plants.

اسْتَطَفَّ here concerns what comes near or within its reach.

The witness supports the branch of proximity. It describes an accessible plant that the bird can cut, rather than a shortage in a transaction.

The line is recorded in poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah and in collections of early verse. His pre-Islamic classification should still be distinguished from the precise date and textual reading of each individual line.

It supports an early usage for approaching and becoming accessible, but does not alone establish that commercial taṭfīf had exactly the same semantic scope in that period.

The historical meaning of taṭfīf

Arabic dictionaries explain طَفَّفَ الْكَيْلَ as giving short measure.

طَفَّفَ عَلَيْهِ can likewise mean giving someone less than their entitlement.

One lexical explanation connects the name with taking only a small amount from a measure or weight. Concealment of the shortage is a possible analysis of the conduct, rather than a necessary component of the word's meaning.

A semantic relationship has therefore been identified between taṭfīf and طفيف, meaning slight or small.

This should not become an absolute rule that taṭfīf occurs only when the amount withheld is extremely small.

Withholding a little from a measure can constitute taṭfīf. Wrongfully withholding more does not remove responsibility or condemnation.

Broader terms such as بخس, نقص, or خسارت may also apply in such circumstances.

Among the evidence examined here, the poem supplies a witness for proximity and accessibility; it does not independently establish pre-Quranic commercial use of taṭfīf. The Quranic occurrence and its explanation provide direct evidence for short measure in the language of revelation.

This family should therefore not be reduced to one image. Small quantity, proximity to a rim or limit, and deficient measurement are related recorded branches, but their precise historical sequence remains uncertain.

2. الْمُطَفِّفِينَ: the sole Quranic derivative

Al-Muṭaffifīn states:

وَيْلٌ لِلْمُطَفِّفِينَ

waylun li-l-muṭaffifīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:1

“Woe to those who give short measure.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:1

الْمُطَفِّفِينَ is the sound masculine plural of the active participle of Form II طَفَّفَ.

Its singular is مُطَفِّف; its plural is مُطَفِّفُونَ in the nominative and مُطَفِّفِينَ in the accusative or genitive.

The preposition لِـ is attached in the verse, producing لِلْمُطَفِّفِينَ.

It designates people who give short measure: those who diminish others' due when measuring or delivering an amount.

The masculine plural does not restrict the condemnation to men. Such Arabic plurals can designate human groups including women and men who engage in the same conduct.

وَيْلٌ expresses severe warning and condemnation, presenting shortchanging as conduct subject to accountability.

Some later explanations identify woe with a particular place in Hell. That geographical identification does not follow from the word and sentence alone; the clear function here is a severe warning.

Why Form II?

طَفَّفَ belongs to Form II.

This pattern can convey causation, repetition, intensity, or producing a state, but none of these is automatically established in every occurrence by the pattern alone.

The established meaning here is giving a deficient amount or measure.

Form II does not by itself prove repeated cheating within every transaction or that the shortage is always very small and gradual.

The following verses nevertheless describe a recurring pattern: full receipt for oneself and deficient delivery for others.

Repetition and an unfair habitual method are therefore inferred from context, not merely morphology.

What conduct receives the warning?

The Quran immediately explains the conduct after naming الْمُطَفِّفِينَ.

That explanation indicates more than someone making an error in measurement.

A defective scale, human error, or different measuring methods may cause an unintended surplus or shortage. This cannot simply be equated with deliberately withholding another's due for profit.

Conversely, someone knowingly continuing to use a defective method at others' expense cannot justify the conduct merely by calling it a technical error.

The decisive considerations are the relationship between delivery and actual entitlement, and the person's responsibility for it.

3. Two standards in one transaction: receiving fully and giving less

The second verse states:

الَّذِينَ إِذَا اكْتَالُوا عَلَى النَّاسِ يَسْتَوْفُونَ

alladhīna idhā iktālū ʿalā al-nāsi yastawfūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:2

“Those who, when taking a measure from people, take it in full.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:2

اكْتَالُوا belongs to ك ي ل and Form VIII.

Here it concerns receiving measured goods for oneself.

يَسْتَوْفُونَ belongs to و ف ي and means receiving in full or obtaining one's entire due.

Their first characteristic is therefore demanding completeness when receiving.

That demand is not itself blameworthy. Everyone may receive in full what another rightly owes them.

The condemnation concerns the difference between their standards when receiving and giving.

The third verse states:

وَإِذَا كَالُوهُمْ أَوْ وَزَنُوهُمْ يُخْسِرُونَ

wa-idhā kālūhum aw wazanūhum yukhsirūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3

“But when they measure or weigh for them, they give less.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3

كَالُوهُمْ and وَزَنُوهُمْ concern measuring or weighing for others.

The pronoun هُمْ refers to those for whom goods are measured or weighed, not to weighing the people themselves.

يُخْسِرُونَ belongs to خ س ر and here concerns reducing the quantity and causing loss in measurement.

Together the verses describe an unfair relationship:

They demand their entire due when receiving, but deliver an incomplete amount when they owe another person.

The shortchanger demands for personal entitlement the very standard violated when fulfilling another's entitlement.

A small shortage does not remove the wrong

Suppose a seller undertakes to provide a kilogram of goods but deliberately delivers less while charging for a kilogram.

The difference may not be immediately apparent; even after discovering it, the buyer may consider it very small.

The issue, however, is more than the financial value of that small amount.

The seller accepted a specific obligation in return for a specified payment. Knowingly delivering less retains part of the buyer's due.

Repeated across many transactions, small shortages can produce substantial profit for the seller and widespread losses for buyers.

Even if the total wrongful gain is not large, the conduct remains wrong.

Shortchanging should therefore not be evaluated only by the money gained. It involves violating an entitlement and dishonest fulfillment of an obligation.

Fairness applies when giving as well as receiving

People often rightly protest when their rights are violated, yet may show less concern when they themselves owe someone else.

An employer may demand exact adherence to working hours but omit some actual hours when calculating wages.

An employee may demand full wages while knowingly recording hours not worked.

In both cases, someone demands full receipt while failing to fulfill the corresponding obligation.

These examples differ from measures and scales in form, but illustrate the double standard condemned in the shortchangers' conduct.

Not every employment dispute or performance shortfall is taṭfīf. Actual obligations, agreements, abilities, and responsibilities must be clear. The relevant pattern is receiving one's full due while knowingly and wrongfully giving another less than theirs.

4. Taṭfīf and responsibility before the Lord

After describing the shortchangers, the Quran asks:

أَلَا يَظُنُّ أُولَٰئِكَ أَنَّهُمْ مَبْعُوثُونَ

a-lā yaẓunnu ulāʾika annahum mabʿūthūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:4

“Do they not think that they will be raised?”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:4

It continues:

لِيَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ

li-yawmin ʿaẓīm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:5

“For a tremendous day.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:5

Then:

يَوْمَ يَقُومُ النَّاسُ لِرَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

yawma yaqūmu al-nāsu li-rabbi al-ʿālamīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:6

“The day when people will stand before the Lord of all worlds.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:6

These verses connect a commercial wrong with human accountability before The God.

Someone withholding others' due may suppose that concealing the conduct from people also removes accountability.

The buyer may not notice, may be unable to prove the shortage, or may face recovery costs greater than the loss.

People's inability to detect or pursue a violation does not make it right.

The Quran recalls that accountability extends beyond visible transactions and limited human judgment.

Accountability and fairness in exchange

The question أَلَا يَظُنُّ أُولَٰئِكَ أَنَّهُمْ مَبْعُوثُونَ indicates that awareness of resurrection should affect worldly conduct.

Someone who believes in accountability cannot respect others' rights only while being watched.

This connection between belief in the hereafter and transactional fairness matters.

It does not follow that everyone who shortchanges inwardly denies The God or the hereafter.

Inner awareness and belief require separate evidence. Someone may believe in accountability yet act against that belief through greed, heedlessness, or moral weakness.

The Quran explicitly shows the incompatibility of shortchanging with serious attention to the day of accounting.

Its condemnation should not become a categorical judgment about the inner intentions and beliefs of everyone who has erred in a transaction.

A day when double standards bring no advantage

In worldly life, shortchangers apply a standard favoring themselves when receiving and disadvantaging others when giving.

The day of accounting is not an occasion for securing privileges through that double standard or dismissing others' rights as insignificant.

Economic power, the ability to deceive a buyer, or a weaker party's inability to pursue a claim does not obstruct divine judgment.

This relates to Quranic justice: people's rights should not depend on their ability to protest, financial power, or social status.

5. Taṭfīf and other Quranic expressions for diminishing what is due

The root occurs once, but related vocabulary addresses its subject elsewhere.

The account of Shuʿayb repeatedly calls for full measures and weights and against diminishing people's entitlements.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:181 states:

أَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ وَلَا تَكُونُوا مِنَ الْمُخْسِرِينَ

awfū al-kayla wa-lā takūnū mina al-mukhsirīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:181

“Give full measure, and do not be among those who give less.”

The following verse states:

وَزِنُوا بِالْقِسْطَاسِ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ

wa-zinū bi-l-qisṭāsi al-mustaqīm

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:182

“And weigh with a straight balance.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:182

Then:

وَلَا تَبْخَسُوا النَّاسَ أَشْيَاءَهُمْ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا فِي الْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ

wa-lā tabkhasū al-nāsa ashyāʾahum wa-lā taʿthaw fī al-arḍi mufsidīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:183

“Do not diminish people's things, and do not spread corruption in the land.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:183

These verses connect full measure, accurate weighing, preserving people's entitlements, and avoiding corruption.

Taṭfīf names a particular deficiency in measurement and exchange; بخس can have a wider scope of diminishing entitlement or value.

ط ف ف and ب خ س: shortchanging and diminishing entitlement

ب خ س concerns diminishing, withholding, and, in contexts of entitlement, depriving others of their due.

Hūd 11:85 states:

وَيَا قَوْمِ أَوْفُوا الْمِكْيَالَ وَالْمِيزَانَ بِالْقِسْطِ وَلَا تَبْخَسُوا النَّاسَ أَشْيَاءَهُمْ

wa-yā qawmi awfū al-mikyāla wa-l-mīzāna bi-l-qisṭi wa-lā tabkhasū al-nāsa ashyāʾahum

Hūd 11:85

“My people, fulfill the measure and the balance equitably, and do not diminish people's things.”

Here بخس is not confined to a commodity's small quantity; it concerns people's things and entitlements.

Al-Muṭaffifīn explains taṭfīf specifically through measuring and weighing.

Shortchanging can therefore be a form of diminishing entitlement, without reducing every usage of بخس to tampering with scales.

A low price or small quantity is not invariably بخس or taṭfīf. Knowingly and willingly giving property away or selling it cheaply is not equivalent to violating someone else's rights.

For a fuller examination, Forqan's study of ب خ س discusses diminishing entitlement in exchange, debts, and recompense for deeds.

ط ف ف and خ س ر: giving less and causing loss

خ س ر concerns deficiency, loss, and forfeiture.

يُخْسِرُونَ in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3 explains the shortchangers' conduct and its result.

They give an incomplete amount when measuring or weighing for someone, causing that person loss.

The family extends beyond trade: it can describe financial loss, deficient measurement, and loss in worldly life or the hereafter.

Taṭfīf names deficient delivery in its Quranic context; إخسار can express diminishing and causing loss more broadly.

The distinction is not complete separation: taṭfīf itself can cause إخسار.

ط ف ف and و ف ي: withholding and fulfilling

و ف ي concerns completion, full delivery, and fulfillment of commitments.

يَسْتَوْفُونَ in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:2 shows that shortchangers demand the full amount when receiving.

The passages about Shuʿayb, by contrast, command أَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ: give full measure.

These forms clarify the two directions of exchange.

استيفاء here means receiving one's own due in full.

إيفاء in Shuʿayb's command means fully delivering another's due.

A just person should seek more than full receipt for themselves: they should also fulfill what others are owed.

This is a central semantic connection between Al-Muṭaffifīn and other passages on fair exchange.

ط ف ف and ك ي ل: shortchanging and measuring

ك ي ل concerns measuring the quantity of goods by capacity.

Measuring is not inherently good or bad: it may be done accurately or with a deliberate shortage.

Taṭfīf in Al-Muṭaffifīn concerns the wrongful form of this activity.

Measuring cannot therefore be defined as shortchanging or presumed unfair in advance.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:35 states:

وَأَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ إِذَا كِلْتُمْ وَزِنُوا بِالْقِسْطَاسِ الْمُسْتَقِيمِ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَحْسَنُ تَأْوِيلًا

wa-awfū al-kayla idhā kiltum wa-zinū bi-l-qisṭāsi al-mustaqīmi dhālika khayrun wa-aḥsanu taʾwīlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:35

“Give full measure when you measure, and weigh with a straight balance. That is better and finer in outcome.”

The verse connects correct measurement and weighing with a good outcome.

It states the positive principle contradicted by the shortchangers' conduct.

ط ف ف and و ز ن: deficient delivery and weighing

و ز ن concerns weighing and assessment by weight.

وَزَنُوهُمْ appears alongside كَالُوهُمْ in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3.

Their pairing indicates that shortchanging can involve goods measured by capacity or weight.

Al-Raḥmān 55:9 states:

وَأَقِيمُوا الْوَزْنَ بِالْقِسْطِ وَلَا تُخْسِرُوا الْمِيزَانَ

wa-aqīmū al-wazna bi-l-qisṭi wa-lā tukhsirū al-mīzān

Al-Raḥmān 55:9

“Establish weight equitably, and do not diminish the balance.”

This situates accurate measurement within the broader relationship between equity and balance.

It does not make ط ف ف the lexical name for every injustice. Shortchanging is one particular violation that can illustrate a wider principle of justice.

ط ف ف and ط ف ق: two distinct roots

The two roots look similar but belong to different lexical families.

طَفِقَ concerns beginning or setting about an action, as in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121.

That meaning differs from taṭfīf as giving short measure.

Sharing the first two radicals does not establish a definite semantic or historical relationship.

6. From measures and scales to respecting rights today

The direct Quranic application concerns deficient measures and weights. The following description also illustrates an ethical standard that can be examined in other relationships.

Not all transactions today use traditional measures and scales.

Many commitments concern time, quality, number, quantity, contracts, or an agreed amount of work.

A company might promise a specified quantity and quality of materials or services, receive full payment, then knowingly deliver an incomplete product.

A contractor might insist on every contractual provision favoring payment, yet reduce the promised materials, working hours, or services during performance.

These differ from measuring grain or weighing goods but can exhibit the same unfair pattern of full receipt and incomplete delivery.

If both parties knowingly and genuinely consent to changing quantity or terms, however, a reduction from the original amount should not automatically be called shortchanging.

Someone who causes a genuine mistake and corrects it upon discovery differs from someone deliberately giving less for greater profit.

These examples illustrate fairness and responsibility in different circumstances; they do not arbitrarily expand lexical meaning.

Responsibility among powerful actors and large institutions

The wider an economic or social activity, the more people deliberate underdelivery may affect.

A seller withholding a small part of one customer's due violates that person's entitlement.

The same method repeated across a large company, distribution system, public contract, or extensive services can affect many more people and much greater total entitlements.

Trustworthiness and fairness therefore matter beyond the behavior of small traders.

Those responsible for measurement, pricing, contracts, distribution, or public services should apply the standard they expect for their own rights.

An institution's size does not itself prove shortchanging or corruption. Responsibility must be assessed through actual obligations, conduct, and examinable evidence.

Small deficiencies and large consequences

Shortchangers may keep a shortage small enough to escape notice or make pursuing it seem unprofitable.

Repeated over many transactions, such small deficiencies can have large consequences.

Beyond financial loss, they undermine trust.

When people cannot trust weights, measures, contracts, or commitments, they must spend more on checking, supervision, and proving their entitlement.

Shortchanging can therefore damage cooperation and social trust beyond the undelivered quantity itself.

This consequence follows from analyzing the conduct, not from the root's direct lexical meaning.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish small quantity, proximity to a limit, and withholding what is due.

طَفِيف can be rendered “slight,” “small,” or “of little quantity.”

طَفّ and طِفَاف in vessel-related usage concern a rim, upper limit, or amount approaching fullness.

طَفَّ and أَطَفَّ can mean drawing near, coming within reach, or reaching a limit.

Commercial طَفَّفَ means giving less, measuring deficiently, or shortchanging.

تَطْفِيف means giving short measure or weight, and more broadly in transactions, diminishing the amount owed.

مُطَفِّف means someone who gives short measure or weight.

The Quran uses only لِلْمُطَفِّفِينَ.

An appropriate rendering of Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:1 is:

“Woe to those who give short measure.”

This is concise, natural, and consistent with the following two verses.

“Woe to those who give less” is understandable, but the Persian equivalent meaning shortchangers more clearly identifies unfair commercial conduct.

Reflection can explain that the condemned conduct's ethical principle may be examined in comparable commitments and entitlements beyond traditional instruments.

That extension should not be imposed on the direct translation of الْمُطَفِّفِينَ.

Nor should مُطَفِّف always become “thief,” “deceiver,” “oppressor,” or “corrupter.” Such descriptions may arise in ethical assessment, but the specific contextual meaning is giving short measure or weight.

8. Summary

ط ف ف relates to small quantity, proximity to a limit or edge, and giving short measure or weight.

Historical usages include طفيف for a small quantity and طفاف for a measure's rim or limit. Another branch concerns proximity and accessibility, illustrated by the poem attributed to ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl.

The evidence illuminates the semantic field without establishing the precise origin or sequence of every branch.

The sole Quranic form is الْمُطَفِّفِينَ. The next two verses explain it: demanding full receipt while giving others deficient measures or weights.

Taṭfīf therefore means more than a small quantity: it concerns wrongfully withholding part of what should be delivered in full.

The Quran strongly condemns this conduct and connects it with accountability on the day of resurrection.

Taṭfīf relates to بخس, إخسار, and نقص without being completely synonymous. بخس can cover a wider range of diminishing entitlement or value; إخسار concerns deficiency and loss; Quranic taṭfīf emphasizes deficient measure or weight.

The wrong should not be confined to small losses. A slight deliberate deprivation remains wrongful despite its size.

Conversely, inadvertent measuring errors, legitimate differences in quantity, or agreed changes in obligations should not automatically be called taṭfīf.

In transactional usage, ط ف ف means giving less than the amount or measure owed in full, especially by measure or weight, against a lexical background involving small quantity and a limit or rim.

This preserves the particular meaning of الْمُطَفِّفِينَ without equating it with every deficiency or injustice.

The conduct nevertheless illustrates a broader principle: people should not demand completeness for themselves while abandoning that standard when giving others their due.

Integrity in exchange and commitments requires respecting another's entitlement when giving as well as receiving. Justice cannot be established with one measure for oneself and another for everyone else.
