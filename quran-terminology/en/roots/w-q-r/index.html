Heaviness, burden, and dignity: from heaviness in the ears and carried loads to composure and honor

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root W Q R concerns heaviness and steadiness. The heaviness may be physical, like a load on an animal’s back; concern hearing, making sound difficult to receive; or describe a person’s dignity, composure, and measured conduct.

The family includes waqr, wiqr, waqār, waqūr, and tawqīr. Although related by root, these forms do not have identical meanings in use.

W Q R occurs nine times in the Qur’an: six waqr occurrences concerning hearing, one wiqr for a load, one waqār for grandeur and honor, and one tuwaqqirūhu for honoring and showing respect.

The principal Qur’anic forms are:

  • وَقْرًا / وَقْرٌ (waqran / waqrun): heaviness in the ears or an obstacle to hearing; Al-Anʿām 6:25, Al-Isrāʾ 17:46, Al-Kahf 18:57, Luqmān 31:7, Fuṣṣilat 41:5, and Fuṣṣilat 41:44.
  • وِقْرًا (wiqran): a load or carried weight; Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2.
  • وَقَارًا (waqāran): grandeur, dignity, and honor; Nūḥ 71:13.
  • وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ (wa-tuwaqqirūhu): and honor him and show him respect; Al-Fatḥ 48:9.

Early Arabic also records waqūr, waqqara, awqara, mūqar, mūqir, and waqrah, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Heaviness: the principal connection

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris traces W Q R to heaviness, explaining heaviness in hearing, carried loads, and human dignity through it.

Heaviness here is not restricted to physical weight. Something may be heavy with a large load; an ear may fail to receive sound; and a person of measured, steady conduct may be called waqūr.

Context nevertheless prevents these uses from being exchanged indiscriminately. Waqr in an ear is not wiqr on an animal’s back, and human waqār does not mean bodily weight.

Wiqr: a carried load

Wiqr, with kasrah on the wāw, denotes a load carried by a person or animal, especially a substantial, heavy one.

Lexicographers record wiqr al-baghl and wiqr al-ḥimār for a mule’s or donkey’s load. Awqār is a plural in this sense.

Awqara may refer to loading an animal. Awqara al-dābbata means putting a load, or a heavy load, on it.

The family also describes trees bearing abundant fruit. Nakhlah mūqirah denotes a palm with a plentiful crop, its branches heavy with fruit.

The family therefore expresses more than a load prepared by people for transport: weight from a natural crop is included.

Early lexicons: loads, heaviness, and laden trees

An early witness concerning a laden tree

In a line transmitted by lexicons, Labīd ibn Rabīʿah describes date palms:

عُصُبٌ كَوَارِعُ فِي خَلِيجِ مُحَلِّمٍ
حَمَلَتْ فَمِنْهَا مُوقَرٌ مَكْمُومُ

ʿuṣubun kawāriʿu fī khalīji muḥallimin / ḥamalat fa-minhā mūqarun makmūmu

Translation:

“Groups of palms drawing water from Muḥallim’s channel have borne fruit; among them are laden palms whose clusters remain enclosed.”

Mūqar describes a tree bearing abundant fruit.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram, living through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The line attests early usage, but should not be declared certainly pre-Islamic without dating this particular composition.

Lisān al-ʿArab, K M M: Labīd’s witness concerning laden palms

Waqr: heaviness in hearing

Waqr, with fatḥah on wāw and sukūn on qāf, is familiar for heaviness in hearing.

Lexical sources sometimes explain it as reduced hearing and sometimes as deafness.

These conditions differ: difficulty hearing does not always mean a complete loss of hearing.

Heaviness in the ears therefore preserves the principal meaning, whereas complete deafness may add more than the word alone conveys in some contexts.

Arabic can use the expression for an actual hearing condition. When the Qur’an places it alongside turning away from the message and rejecting signs, however, its role extends beyond describing a bodily condition.

Waqār: composure and steady conduct

Waqār concerns calmness, dignity, measured behavior, and gravitas.

A waqūr person avoids frivolity, misplaced haste, and agitation, responding to circumstances with measured steadiness.

This differs from physical heaviness. A dignified person need not be heavy or slow-moving: their conduct and character have weight and standing.

Waqār may accompany ḥilm and razānah. The former concerns forbearance and controlling reactions, the latter dignity and steady behavior.

It should nevertheless not be reduced to speaking quietly, moving little, remaining silent, or looking formal. Outward calm may coexist with poor judgment; someone may act plainly and swiftly when necessary without losing dignity.

Lexically, waqār concerns dignity and gravitas in conduct; its moral value becomes clear in action and circumstances.

Study of Ḥ L M

Tawqīr: honoring and showing respect

One established use of the Form II verb waqqara is honoring or showing respect to someone.

A person treats another without belittling their standing and observes the respect due to them.

This connects tawqīr with waqār: waqār describes composure and gravitas, while tawqīr can express acknowledging and honoring another’s standing.

Tawqīr does not, however, mean worship. Respecting a person or honoring their position does not inherently attribute divinity to them.

The proper limits of respect follow from the person’s position, the relationship, and ethical and Qur’anic principles.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: hearing, dignity, and honoring

Less common branches

Arabic sources also record waqrah for a crack, hollow, or injury in stone, bone, or an animal’s hoof.

This differs from loads and dignity and must not be reduced to them merely to preserve one uniform definition.

A line attributed to Al-Aʿshā reads:

يَا دَهْرُ قَدْ أَكْثَرْتَ فَجْعَتَنَا
بِسَرَاتِنَا وَوَقَرْتَ فِي الْعَظْمِ

yā dahru qad aktharta fajʿatanā / bi-sarātinā wa-waqarta fī al-ʿaẓmi

Translation:

“O Time, how often you have bereaved us of our leaders and opened a crack in the bone.”

Waqarta fī al-ʿaẓmi pictures deep injury and a crack in bone.

The line appears in lexicons and supplements to Al-Aʿshā’s collected poetry. Attribution should be reported at that level: inclusion in a collection attributed to a pre-Islamic poet does not alone establish the particular line’s date or certain authorship.

This branch matters for Arabic’s range, but cracking bone is not directly present in the root’s Qur’anic uses.

3. Waqr: heaviness in the ears and an obstacle to receiving the message

Six of the nine Qur’anic occurrences concern waqr in the ears. All connect hearing with receiving the message.

Al-Anʿām 6:25: listening without understanding

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَسْتَمِعُ إِلَيْكَ وَجَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا

wa-minhum man yastamiʿu ilayka wa-jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran

Translation:

“Among them is one who listens to you; and We have placed coverings over their hearts so that they do not understand it, and heaviness in their ears.”

Someone apparently listens, yet encounters an obstacle to receiving and understanding the message.

Two expressions appear together: akinnah over hearts and waqr in ears.

The heart coverings concern failure to understand, while the ears’ heaviness creates the image of difficulty hearing.

Hearing a sound and understanding its message are therefore not one identical stage. Speech may reach the ears without being properly received.

The verse continues with people who do not believe even when seeing signs and who dispute the message. Waqr here therefore indicates an obstacle to acceptance and understanding, rather than a definitive diagnosis of a medical disorder.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46: heaviness in hearing and Qur’anic recitation

وَجَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا

wa-jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran

Translation:

“And We have placed coverings over their hearts so that they do not understand it, and heaviness in their ears.”

The construction clearly resembles Al-Anʿām 6:25.

The preceding verse mentions reciting the Qur’an and a barrier between Muhammad and those disbelieving in the hereafter. The continuation describes their reaction when the one Lord is mentioned.

Waqr again appears in the context of hearing the message and responding to it.

Its lexical meaning remains heaviness in hearing, but the context concerns more than the physical ability to receive sound.

Al-Kahf 18:57: turning away after a reminder

إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا

innā jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran

Translation:

“Indeed, We have placed coverings over their hearts so that they do not understand it, and heaviness in their ears.”

The verse begins with someone reminded of the Lord’s signs who turns away and forgets what he has sent ahead.

Heaviness in the ears thus accompanies the person’s response to the reminder.

The verse also attributes placing the heart coverings to God. That explicit attribution must remain, while waqr itself does not explain the entire relationship between human action, divine guidance, and this condition’s emergence.

That relationship requires the wider network of verses concerning choice, guidance, turning away, and human responsibility.

Luqmān 31:7: an explicit comparison with heaviness in hearing

وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ آيَاتُنَا وَلَّىٰ مُسْتَكْبِرًا كَأَنْ لَمْ يَسْمَعْهَا كَأَنَّ فِي أُذُنَيْهِ وَقْرًا

wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā wallā mustakbiran ka-an lam yasmaʿhā ka-anna fī udhunayhi waqran

Translation:

“When Our signs are recited to him, he turns away arrogantly, as though he had not heard them, as though there were heaviness in his two ears.”

This is a particularly clear witness to waqr’s figurative function.

Ka-anna occurs twice: as though he had not heard and as though heaviness were in his ears.

The verse therefore does not necessarily say he is actually deaf. His behavior is compared with someone whom sound does not reach.

Wallā mustakbiran, turning away arrogantly, is prominent.

The subject is therefore not someone prevented from receiving the message by a real hearing disability, but someone whose arrogant response is compared with not hearing.

Fuṣṣilat 41:5: the opponents’ claim about themselves

وَقَالُوا قُلُوبُنَا فِي أَكِنَّةٍ مِمَّا تَدْعُونَا إِلَيْهِ وَفِي آذَانِنَا وَقْرٌ

wa-qālū qulūbunā fī akinnatin mimmā tadʿūnā ilayhi wa-fī ādhāninā waqrun

Translation:

“They said: Our hearts are within coverings against what you call us to, and there is heaviness in our ears.”

The verse quotes the opponents themselves.

They claim their hearts are covered and their ears have heaviness, then speak of a barrier between themselves and the Messenger.

Together, these expressions picture distance and nonacceptance.

Because wa-fī ādhāninā waqrun is their own speech, it should not be treated as a medical diagnosis or an independent Qur’anic description of their physical condition without examining the context.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44: heaviness in hearing and blindness toward guidance

وَالَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ فِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرٌ وَهُوَ عَلَيْهِمْ عَمًى

wa-alladhīna lā yuʾminūna fī ādhānihim waqrun wa-huwa ʿalayhim ʿaman

Translation:

“Those who do not believe have heaviness in their ears, and it is blindness for them.”

Earlier, the verse calls the Qur’an guidance and healing for believers. It contrasts this with heaviness in the ears and blindness for those who do not believe.

One message can thus have different effects in relation to its recipients’ condition.

Waqr itself, however, means neither unbelief nor blindness. Heaviness in hearing is an independent image placed alongside ʿaman.

Different words should therefore not be reduced to one vague shared meaning such as misguidance.

What the six uses establish

In all six verses, waqr concerns heaviness in hearing. Their principal contextual concern, however, is human understanding, reception, and response to the message.

Luqmān 31:7 particularly shows through ka-anna that heaviness in the ears can be a comparison without actual deafness being intended.

Heaviness in the ears is therefore precise and natural in the base translation. Its figurative meaning and relation to turning away, arrogance, or rejection of truth should be explained from each verse’s context.

4. Wiqr: a heavy load in Al-Dhāriyāt

Al-Dhāriyāt opens with:

فَالْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا

fa-al-ḥāmilāti wiqran

Translation:

“Then by those carrying a load.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2

Wiqr, with kasrah on wāw, denotes a carried load.

Its pairing with al-ḥāmilāt makes that clear: the first word identifies the carriers and the second what they carry.

The preceding verse says:

وَالذَّارِيَاتِ ذَرْوًا

wa-al-dhāriyāti dharwan

“By those scattering, in scattering.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1

The sequence then mentions fa-al-jāriyāti yusran and fa-al-muqassimāti amran.

Early commentaries commonly identify the bearers of a load as clouds carrying water or rain. This fits the natural image of moving winds and laden clouds.

Al-ḥāmilāti wiqran itself, however, explicitly names neither clouds nor rain.

Lexical meaning must therefore be distinguished from a proposed referent.

Wiqr means a load; here, one the carriers bear or transport.

Exactly what those carriers are requires study of the opening oaths together. That inquiry must not replace the root’s meaning.

Nor can a particular quantity or weight be calculated from the verse. Wiqr denotes a load and heaviness, rather than a fixed scientific unit of weight.

5. Waqār and tawqīr: from dignity to honoring

Waqār in Nūḥ

Nūḥ says:

مَا لَكُمْ لَا تَرْجُونَ لِلَّهِ وَقَارًا

mā lakum lā tarjūna li-allāhi waqāran

A rendering close to the sense:

“What is the matter with you that you give no regard to The God’s grandeur and honor?”

Nūḥ 71:13

Waqār here concerns God.

Arabic waqār can describe composure and personal gravitas, but when applied to God it must not be restricted to bodily stillness or human conduct.

The context concerns greatness, grandeur, and honor befitting God.

Tarjūna also matters. Rajāʾ commonly means hope or expectation, but exegetical sources explain this construction through reckoning with, attending to, and recognizing God’s grandeur. Some explain it through fear of divine greatness.

Why do you not fear God therefore highlights only one interpretive direction without expressing waqār itself.

The following verse also matters:

وَقَدْ خَلَقَكُمْ أَطْوَارًا

wa-qad khalaqakum aṭwāran

“Although He has created you in successive stages.”

Nūḥ 71:14

The passage then discusses the heavens, moon, and sun.

Noah’s question about God’s waqār thus continues into reminders of creation’s greatness. People are invited to recognize their Lord’s standing and grandeur through signs in themselves and the world.

The verse establishes neither physical weight in God nor dignity as a human’s calm behavior. The word concerns standing, grandeur, and honor that should be recognized as His.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings of tarjūna and divine grandeur

Tawqīr in Al-Fatḥ

Al-Fatḥ says:

لِتُؤْمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَتُعَزِّرُوهُ وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ وَتُسَبِّحُوهُ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

li-tuʾminū bi-allāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-tuʿazzirūhu wa-tuwaqqirūhu wa-tusabbiḥūhu bukratan wa-aṣīlan

A rendering retaining the pronouns for contextual examination:

“So that you may believe in The God and His Messenger, support and honor him, and glorify him morning and evening.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:9

Tuwaqqirūhu is a Form II subjunctive imperfect. It expresses honoring and showing respect to someone.

It occurs beside tuʿazzirūhu.

Taʿzīr here concerns assistance, support, and strengthening, while tawqīr concerns honoring and respect.

They may accompany one another in practical conduct without having completely identical meanings.

The pronoun’s referent in tuwaqqirūhu

The referent of hu is a question separate from tawqīr’s lexical meaning.

The preceding verse introduces Muhammad as witness, bringer of good news, and warner. Verse nine also mentions God and His Messenger together.

Some commentators refer the pronouns in tuʿazzirūhu and tuwaqqirūhu to the Messenger and that in tusabbiḥūhu to God, since glorification in this construction is worship directed toward God.

Others refer all the pronouns to God, explaining supporting God as supporting His religion and Messenger.

Lexically, tawqīr can apply both to respecting people and honoring God.

Its basic meaning, honoring and showing respect, is clear; the pronoun’s referent requires grammatical and contextual study.

W Q R itself establishes neither that tawqīr always means worship nor that respecting a person assigns them divine standing.

Conversely, the legitimacy of respect for a prophet grants neither unlimited authority, absolute knowledge, nor divinity. Those questions require independent Qur’anic evidence.

Al-Qurṭubī: both pronoun analyses in Al-Fatḥ 48:9

Relating waqār to tawqīr

Waqār commonly describes dignity, gravitas, and grandeur; tawqīr describes acknowledging another’s grandeur and honor.

Someone may, for example, behave with dignity while others respect their standing or qualities.

This does not mean every dignified person deserves unquestioning obedience or everyone honored is immune from error.

Waqār is a linguistic and behavioral quality. Correct judgment and legitimate obedience are separate matters.

6. Comparing related roots

W Q R (waqara) and TH Q L (thaqula)

TH Q L is a more general root for heaviness, applied to physical weight, difficulty, and the weight of responsibility or action.

Wiqr likewise concerns a heavy load, specifically something carried in its Qur’anic use.

Waqr with fatḥah expresses heaviness in hearing in six verses.

Although close in the field of heaviness, TH Q L and W Q R must not be equated in every use.

Study of TH Q L

W Q R (waqara) and Ḥ M L (ḥamala)

Ḥamala concerns lifting, carrying, or undertaking something; wiqr denotes the carried load.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2 combines the families:

فَالْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا (fa-al-ḥāmilāti wiqran)

Ḥāmilāt identifies carrying agents and wiqran the load carried.

The act of carrying and the load itself, although related, thus follow from separate words.

Study of Ḥ M L

W Q R (waqara) and Ṣ M M (ṣamma)

Ṣ M M concerns deafness or not hearing.

Waqr in the ear passages may denote severe hearing difficulty or even deafness, but its lexical image is heaviness in the ears.

Luqmān 31:7 compares the arrogant person with someone having heaviness in the ears. Elsewhere, the Qur’an also uses ṣumm and bukm for people who do not receive the message.

The expressions differ in imagery. One fixed rendering, deaf or does not hear, should not erase all their distinctions.

Study of Ṣ M M

W Q R (waqara) and ʿ Z R (ʿazara)

Al-Fatḥ 48:9 connects these roots.

Taʿzīr here concerns assistance and support; tawqīr concerns honoring and respect.

Practical support for someone or a mission may accompany respect, but respect without practical support and support without respect are also linguistically conceivable.

Their conjunction therefore does not justify erasing their semantic difference.

W Q R (waqara) and Q R R (qarra)

Qarār and Q R R concern settling, coming to rest, and remaining steady.

Human waqār also connects with composure and steady conduct.

The semantic fields are close, but the roots differ.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:33 contains wa-qarna fī buyūtikunna, debated in morphology and readings. The familiar analysis relates it to Q R R and staying settled; some lexicographers connect certain readings with W Q R.

It should therefore not be added to the definite W Q R count without examining the reading and morphology. Similarity between steadiness and dignity also cannot alone establish one historical origin for both roots.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Each form’s equivalent should follow its structure and context.

Waqr: heaviness in the ears; in the message-reception passages, heaviness obstructing hearing and understanding.

Wiqr: a load; a heavy load.

Waqār: dignity, grandeur, honor, or greatness, according to subject and context.

Tuwaqqirūhu: honor him; show him respect.

Waqūr: dignified, composed, and measured in conduct; an established Arabic form, but not a form present in the Qur’an.

Awqara: loaded or made heavy; in non-Qur’anic usage.

Mūqar: laden or weighed down with a load, according to construction and use.

Qur’anic examples:

وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا (Wa-fī ādhānihim waqran) — and heaviness in their ears.

كَأَنَّ فِي أُذُنَيْهِ وَقْرًا (Ka-anna fī udhunayhi waqran) — as though there were heaviness in his two ears.

فَالْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا (Fa-al-ḥāmilāti wiqran) — then by those carrying a load.

مَا لَكُمْ لَا تَرْجُونَ لِلَّهِ وَقَارًا (Mā lakum lā tarjūna li-allāhi waqāran) — what is the matter with you that you give no regard to The God’s grandeur and honor?

وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ (Wa-tuwaqqirūhu) — and honor him and show him respect.

In the hearing passages, heaviness in the ears is more cautious than complete deafness and closer to the word’s principal image.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2, a load naturally renders wiqr without adding clouds, rain, or another proposed referent to the translation itself.

In passages concerning waqār and tawqīr, honor, honoring, and grandeur should follow the construction and addressee, rather than preserving a physical image through God’s weight or making the Messenger heavy.

8. Conclusion

W Q R has a family of Arabic meanings concerning heaviness, loads, dignity, and honoring.

Physically, wiqr denotes a carried load. The same field includes a fruit-laden tree and a loaded animal.

Waqr denotes heaviness in hearing. The Qur’an uses it six times in contexts of receiving or rejecting the message, where it may picture an obstacle to hearing and understanding without always meaning bodily deafness.

In the branch concerning behavior and standing, waqār describes personal composure and gravitas, or divine grandeur and honor. Tawqīr concerns honoring and observing respect.

Arabic also records cracks and hollows within the family. These uses must remain acknowledged, but their presence in a dictionary does not make them the meaning of every verse.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Heaviness and a load; in hearing, heaviness in the ears; in conduct and standing, dignity, grandeur, and honor.

Each derivative’s precise meaning follows its form and context. Physical heaviness, an obstacle to hearing, and honoring are related uses, but must not be translated in place of one another.
