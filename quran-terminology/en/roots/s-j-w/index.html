Core meaning: settling, subsiding movement and disturbance, and becoming still. Of night, this can accompany darkness spreading and settling; of the sea, it describes waves becoming calm. Covering is another recorded Arabic use. The Qur’an contains this root once, in wa-al-layli idhā sajā.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic S J W concerns stillness, settling, and subsiding movement.

Sajā al-laylu describes the night settling into stillness.

Sajā al-baḥru means the sea became calm and its waves subsided.

Ṭarfun sājin describes a quiet, untroubled gaze.

Laylatun sājiyah is a night in which stillness prevails.

The shared meaning is not simply becoming dark: the sea and a person's gaze can be described this way without darkness being central.

Nor should the family be confined to cessation of bodily movement. Calm waves, subsiding nightly activity, and a quiet gaze each express stillness, with different subjects and qualities.

Early poetry: a calm sea contrasted with a turbulent sea

Al-Aʿshā, an Arab poet near the beginning of Islam, says in a poem addressing ʿAlqamah ibn ʿUlāthah:

أَتُوعِدُنِي أَنْ جَاشَ بَحْرُ ابْنِ عَمِّكُمْ
وَبَحْرُكَ سَاجٍ لَا يُوَارِي الدَّعَامِصَا

atuʿidunī an jāsha baḥru ibni ʿammikum / wa-baḥruka sājin lā yuwārī al-daʿāmiṣā

Translation:

“Do you threaten me because your cousin's sea has surged, while your sea lies still and does not even cover the little creatures of the pools?”

The poet constructs two contrasting images.

Jāsha expresses surging and turbulence.

Sājin contrasts with it, expressing stillness.

Al-daʿāmiṣ, plural of duʿmūṣ, denotes small creatures inhabiting pools and shallow waters.

The sea represents people's power, standing, and generosity.

The first sea surges; the second is still and so meagre that it cannot cover tiny pool creatures.

The image is satirical and disparaging.

Sājin must nevertheless be distinguished from the resulting satire.

Sājin means calm or still.

The sea's meagreness comes from the rest of the verse and the comparison.

S J W itself does not mean insufficient water, incapacity, or worthlessness.

In another setting, a calm sea can be desirable.

The verse matters because it contrasts sājin with turbulence, independently illustrating its meaning beyond the Qur’anic passage.

Lexical quotations also begin the verse with fa-mā dhanbunā. Atuʿidunī is transmitted in the poet's collected verse. The variation does not change the sense of sājin.

The poem is attributed to al-Aʿshā, who lived in the pre-Islamic and early Islamic period. Its exact date is not independently secure; it is early poetic evidence, rather than an indisputably dated pre-Qur’anic document.

Another example: a quiet night

An Arabic rajaz verse is transmitted as follows:

يَا حَبَّذَا الْقَمْرَاءُ وَاللَّيْلُ السَّاجْ
وَطُرُقٌ مِثْلُ مُلَاءِ النَّسَّاجْ

yā ḥabbadhā al-qamrāʾu wa-al-laylu al-sāj / wa-ṭuruqun mithlu mulāʾi al-nassāj

Translation:

“How lovely the moonlit night, the still night, and roads spread like a weaver's cloths.”

Al-laylu al-sāj refers to the quiet night.

The same image includes moonlight.

Sāj therefore need not mean complete darkness.

A moonlit night can also be calm and still.

The verse is attributed to a poet identified as al-Ḥārithī, but the precise identity and composition date are not sufficiently clear to make it certain pre-Islamic evidence.

It illustrates the established use of sāj to describe night.

What the examples show

Al-Aʿshā uses sājin for a calm sea.

The rajaz verse uses the description for night.

Both show stillness and settling as established meanings of S J W.

Stillness need not mean absolutely no movement.

A calm sea may still have currents.

A quiet night does not mean all creatures cease activity.

The description concerns subsiding activity and relative calm in the particular setting.

A historical definition

Early poetry and recorded lexical uses support this definition:

In its principal branch, S J W describes settling into stillness, especially when noticeable movement, disturbance, or change subsides and a relatively stable calm prevails.

Of night, this can accompany darkness spreading and settling.

Of the sea, subsiding waves are prominent.

Covering is also recorded in this family. It should be distinguished from stillness; the historical sequence of these branches cannot be inferred merely from related imagery.

2. Important forms and derivatives

سَجَا يَسْجُو

The family's Form I verb.

It means to become calm, settle, or be still.

سَجَا الْبَحْرُ

sajā al-baḥru

Meaning: “The sea became calm.”

سَجَا اللَّيْلُ

sajā al-laylu

Meaning: “The night settled.”

Of night, the verb can accompany darkening and the settling of darkness.

Darkness is not essential to all its uses.

سَجْو and سُجُوّ

Verbal nouns of Form I.

They concern stillness, settling, and subsiding activity.

Sujūw al-baḥr means the sea becoming calm.

Sujūw al-layl concerns the night's stillness and settling.

سَاجٍ

The active participle of Form I.

Something calm or still.

بَحْرٌ سَاجٍ

baḥrun sājin

Meaning: “A calm sea.”

لَيْلٌ سَاجٍ

laylun sājin

Meaning: “A quiet night.”

This is the form encountered in the poetic examples.

سَاجِيَة

The feminine of sājin.

لَيْلَةٌ سَاجِيَةٌ

laylatun sājiyah

Meaning: “A quiet night.”

Lexicons even apply this description to a night with calm conditions of cold, wind, and cloud, not necessarily a dark night.

Sājiyah itself does not mean a very dark night.

سَجْوَاء

A lexical description of calmness.

Nāqatun sajwāʾ describes a she-camel that remains still while being milked.

Some uses also concern an animal's coat lying smoothly.

Imraʾatun sajwāʾ al-ṭarf describes a woman with a quiet gaze.

The quality of stillness varies with the subject.

A camel standing quietly for milking and a person's quiet gaze are not identical bodily conditions, but both belong to the field of stillness.

سَجَّى يُسَجِّي

A Form II verb.

In the covering branch, sajjā al-shayʾa means covering something.

For example:

سَجَّى الْمَيِّتَ بِالثَّوْبِ

sajjā al-mayyita bi-al-thawbi

Meaning: “He covered the deceased with a cloth.”

This concerns a material covering rather than the sea or night becoming calm.

تَسْجِيَة

The verbal noun of sajjā.

Covering something.

Of the human body, it can describe drawing a cloth over it.

Tasjiyah itself does not mean death.

Death is the person's condition; covering is a separate action performed on the body.

مُسَجًّى

The passive participle of sajjā.

Something that has been covered.

It can describe a body covered with cloth.

أَسْجَى

Recorded uses include covering, and becoming calm in the expression asjā al-baḥru, the sea became calm. An intransitive example should not automatically be rendered transitively as calming something else.

The construction and subject determine the precise sense.

سَجِيَّة

Sajiyyah means a person's disposition, nature, or relatively enduring characteristic.

Lexicons also discuss it under the S J W family.

A semantic relationship can be envisaged between an enduring personal characteristic and stability or settledness.

That explanation alone does not establish historical derivation from the quieting of night or sea.

In established usage, sajiyyah means disposition, not material stillness.

The form actually found in the Qur’an

S J W occurs only once:

Sajā in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2.

It is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb of Form I.

Qur’anic sajā is written with alif maqṣūrah, but its morphological family has final wāw, as shown by yasjū.

Other forms such as sājin, sājiyah, tasjiyah, and sajiyyah do not occur in the Qur’an.

3. The settling night in Al-Ḍuḥā

وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا سَجَىٰ

wa-al-layli idhā sajā

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2

Translation:

“And by the night when it grows still.”

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2

Sajā is a perfect verb.

Its subject is an implicit pronoun referring to al-layl; al-layl itself is genitive after the oath particle wāw.

Idhā identifies the time or circumstance in which this condition occurs.

The verse concerns the night in its state of sujūw.

It accompanies an oath by the morning brightness.

What is the night's stillness?

As night arrives and settles, many daytime activities subside.

Movement and sounds may diminish.

Some creatures rest.

Spreading darkness also changes the appearance of the surroundings.

This can be described as the night growing still.

It does not mean every person, animal, or other creature becomes motionless.

Some creatures are nocturnal, and people may work, travel, or worship at night.

Sajā describes the night's general condition in this image, not an absolute end to all activity.

Does sajā mean becoming dark?

Arabic sources also record darkness spreading or settling as a sense of sajā al-laylu.

Lexical explanations include both darkening and the night's becoming still.

The spreading of darkness is another recorded explanation of this nocturnal use.

These explanations clearly relate to night.

As darkness spreads and settles over the surroundings, night becomes established.

Stillness and darkness can accompany each other here.

They remain distinct.

Stillness concerns calm.

Darkness concerns light conditions.

Since sajā al-baḥru and ṭarfun sājin describe a calm sea and gaze, darkness cannot be the shared meaning of every form.

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2, the night's becoming still is well attested; spreading and settling darkness are also recorded meanings of this nocturnal use.

Does sajā mean night arriving or departing?

Some older explanations describe night arriving, others its departure.

These have different temporal directions.

An arrival reading concerns night entering the condition described.

A departure reading concerns its ending.

The clear uses of sājin for calm sea and quiet night primarily support stillness and settledness.

That sense alone specifies neither arrival nor departure exclusively.

“When the night grows still” therefore retains an attested lexical sense without restricting the expression to arrival or departure.

Does the verse imply absolute darkness?

Sajā alone does not establish that.

Just as al-laylu al-sāj can describe a moonlit night in poetry, a quiet night need not lack moonlight, stars, or every source of light.

The verse also does not specify midnight or maximum darkness.

Adding “absolute darkness,” “midnight,” or “the complete stillness of the universe” to the base translation has no direct basis in the word itself.

4. Its relationship to the oath by morning brightness

Al-Ḍuḥā opens with an oath by morning brightness.

An oath by the night when it grows still follows.

The response to the oath in the third verse is:

مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَمَا قَلَىٰ

mā waddaʿaka rabbuka wa-mā qalā

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3

Translation:

“Your Lord has not forsaken you, nor has He detested you.”

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3

This places brightness and the night's stillness in a context of reassurance to Muhammad.

Brightness and stillness: different conditions

Morning brightness concerns light appearing and spreading.

The quiet night is associated with subsiding activity and settled darkness.

They differ in human experience.

Neither condition by itself indicates that God has abandoned the world.

Night is part of creation, as daylight is.

The two oaths together can thus help illuminate the reassurance that follows.

This contextual inference should not become the lexical meaning of sajā.

Sajā does not lexically mean God's love, the presence of revelation, or the end of sorrow.

Does night symbolize severed contact with God?

The verse takes the night in its stillness as the subject of an oath.

It does not call darkness sin, error, or distance from God.

The response assures Muhammad that his Lord has neither forsaken nor detested him.

The contrast cannot establish an absolute rule equating all light with divine approval and all darkness with anger or absence.

Night, like day, is a sign of creation in the Qur’an.

People can rest, reflect, worship, or pursue other activities at night.

The difference between light and darkness is not itself a moral judgment.

The settled night and the surah's message

Daylight is not perpetual, nor is night a condition lasting at all times.

Both have a place in a recognizable order.

Their alternation can inform reflection on the surah: changing circumstances and experiences of darkness or stillness do not alone signify abandonment by one's Lord.

This reflection should not become a definitive account of why sajā was chosen.

The verse itself describes the night growing still.

The response explicitly denies Muhammad's abandonment and his Lord's hostility toward him.

Their rhetorical relationship emerges from the surah, not from an independent theological meaning within S J W.

5. Stillness, covering, and settledness

Alongside becoming calm, Arabic S J W has covering-related uses.

Sajjā al-mayyita, for example, means covering the body with cloth.

Sujūw al-layl has also been explained through the night covering daylight and surrounding things with darkness.

These uses invite examination of the relationship between stillness and covering.

Are stillness and covering the same?

They can coincide, but are not identical.

A sea can be calm without covering anything.

A cloth can cover something without its becoming calm being the subject.

Night can become quiet and hide many things from sight through darkness.

Both features can thus appear in one nocturnal image.

That does not make stillness and covering synonymous throughout Arabic.

Nor does it establish without independent historical evidence which sense came first.

Does sajā mean remaining forever?

Lexicons sometimes explain sajā with dāma, continuing or remaining in a state.

Such continuity does not mean eternity.

A calm sea may later become turbulent.

A quiet night ends when day comes.

Settledness can therefore form part of the image in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2 without implying endless night.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots

S J W: sujūw, and S K N: sukūn

S K N concerns calm, stillness, and settlement.

S J W likewise concerns becoming calm in its principal branch.

They overlap in describing night and sea.

Al-Anʿām 6:96 describes night as sakanan.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2 uses sajā.

The uses are connected through nocturnal rest or stillness.

S K N has a broad Qur’anic field, including residence, tranquillity, settlement, and human relationships.

S J W has only one Qur’anic occurrence; all meanings of S K N should not be transferred to it.

S J W: sujūw, and GH SH Y: ghashy

Ghashy concerns enveloping and covering.

Al-Layl 92:1 says wa-al-layli idhā yaghshā.

It concerns night as it envelops and covers.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2 says wa-al-layli idhā sajā.

The nocturnal images are close.

Yaghshā emphasizes enveloping and covering.

Sajā has a clear association with stillness and settledness.

Night can be both enveloping and quiet; that does not make the verbs interchangeable everywhere.

S J W: sujūw, and GH Ṭ SH: ghaṭash

Qur’anic GH Ṭ SH concerns making dark.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:29 speaks of darkening the night.

That concerns its darkness.

Sajā can accompany settled darkness at night, but its stillness sense also appears outside nocturnal settings.

Darkening and becoming calm are related but distinct features.

S J W: sujūw, and R K D: rukūd

Rukūd concerns subsiding movement and remaining still.

It is used to explain sajā al-baḥru.

The two families overlap here.

A dictionary equivalent does not make every derivative and usage identical.

S J W also has covering-related and nocturnal uses.

Rukūd can clarify stillness without replacing the family's entire semantic field.

S J W: sujūw, and D J W: dujūw

Dajā and dujūw concern darkness and deepening nocturnal darkness.

They resemble sujūw in sound and nocturnal usage.

D J W belongs more particularly to the field of darkness.

S J W also clearly concerns stillness and calm.

Similar sound and shared nocturnal settings do not establish identical roots or derivational histories.

S J W: sajā, and S J D: sujūd

These roots share only their first two letters.

S J W concerns calm and stillness.

Qur’anic S J D concerns prostration and lowering oneself.

A body may remain still in prostration, but that resemblance does not establish derivation from sujūw or make calm the lexical meaning of prostration.

The roots require independent examination.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Principal meanings

Sajā: became calm or still; of night, its darkness settled.

Yasjū: becomes calm or still.

Sajw and sujūw: stillness, calm, and subsiding activity.

Sājin: calm or still.

Sājiyah: calm or still; of night, one in which stillness prevails.

Sajwāʾ: calm, describing a gaze or certain animals.

Sajjā: covered.

Tasjiyah: covering something.

Musajjā: covered.

Sajiyyah: a relatively enduring disposition or nature.

Proposed rendering of the Qur’anic form

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2:

وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا سَجَىٰ

wa-al-layli idhā sajā

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2

“And by the night when it grows still.”

This rendering preserves the attested stillness sense. It is a proposal for the root study and does not replace the installed verse translation.

An explanation can add that the night becomes quiet and its darkness settles.

That addition is not necessary in the base translation.

Renderings such as “when night becomes completely dark” emphasize one nocturnal aspect without expressing stillness.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For the brief root box:

“Becoming calm and still; of night, associated with settled darkness.”

For Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2:

“The night growing still as its activity subsides and darkness settles.”

Preserve the Arabic word's installed spelling in the box; this article quotes it as sajā. These proposals do not authorize rewriting existing authored explanations.

The form is third-person masculine singular perfect, from S J W; its implicit subject pronoun refers to the night mentioned in the verse.

The general root meaning and verse-specific meaning should remain distinct.

8. Conclusion

Arabic S J W concerns stillness, calm, and subsiding activity.

Al-Aʿshā's attributed verse uses sājin for a calm sea contrasted with a turbulent one.

It shows stillness as an established early sense.

The rajaz verse uses al-laylu al-sāj for a quiet night.

This can even be moonlit; sujūw should not be confined to complete darkness.

Lexicons record calm seas, quiet nights and gazes, still animals, and covering something within this family.

Night provides an image linking stillness and covering, but that association does not establish the historical origin of every derivative.

S J W occurs once in the Qur’an.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2 applies sajā to night.

A clear sense is the night's growing still and settling.

Spreading darkness is also attested in nocturnal usage, but is not shared by every branch.

The verse accompanies an oath by morning brightness; Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3 answers that Muhammad's Lord has neither forsaken nor detested him.

Contextually, brightness and quiet night are different conditions in creation, neither by itself indicating abandonment by God.

The surah's reassurance should not become the lexical meaning of sajā.

The principal sense of S J W is becoming calm and still. Of night, this can accompany darkness spreading and settling. Covering is also recorded, without a securely established common historical origin for every branch.

“And by the night when it grows still” is a natural, defensible rendering of Al-Ḍuḥā 93:2.

It preserves the verb's clear sense while leaving other nocturnal features, including settled darkness and the relationship to the opening oath, for contextual explanation.
