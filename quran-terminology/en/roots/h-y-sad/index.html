Turning aside, seeking escape, and a way out of difficulty

The Arabic root Ḥ Y Ṣ concerns turning aside from something, changing course to avoid it, and attempting to escape. When people or animals encounter danger and veer away to keep clear of it, the verb ḥāṣa–yaḥīṣu can be used. In some uses, the same verb also means avoiding something or withdrawing from an encounter.

In early Arabic, this root described animal movement, withdrawing in battle, and attempts to get out of trouble. The expression ḥayṣa bayṣa was also known for a confused predicament from which someone could find no way out.

In the Qur’an, this root appears only in maḥīṣ: a word denoting escape, a way of escape, or the possibility of release. All its Qur’anic uses concern the absence of such a way.

These uses involve a situation in which people can no longer escape the consequences confronting them by changing position, relying on power, appealing to leaders, or seeking another route.

To understand this precisely, the act of escaping, a way of escape, and the absence of any possibility of escape must be distinguished. The root Ḥ Y Ṣ does not itself mean permanent entrapment or punishment. Those meanings come from the negative construction and the verses’ contexts.

1. Forms of the root and its semantic core

The root Ḥ Y Ṣ occurs five times in the Qur’an, in only one lexical form: maḥīṣ.

This appears as maḥīṣan in Al-Nisāʾ 4:121, and as maḥīṣin in Ibrāhīm 14:21, Fuṣṣilat 41:48, Al-Shūrā 42:35, and Qāf 50:36.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences of maḥīṣ

The differing final vowels reflect the word’s grammatical role, rather than a different root or independent meaning.

The Arabic lexicons record other forms in the same family:

  • حَاصَ ـ يَحِيصُ (ḥāṣa–yaḥīṣu): to turn aside, move away from something, change direction, or flee.
  • حَيْص، حُيُوص، حَيَصَان and حَيْصُوصَة (ḥayṣ, ḥuyūṣ, ḥayaṣān, and ḥayṣūṣah): verbal nouns for turning aside, changing course, or escaping.
  • حَيْصَة (ḥayṣah): a single act of turning aside or moving to escape.
  • مَحِيص (maḥīṣ): escape, a way of escape, or the possibility of release.
  • مَحَاص (maḥāṣ): turning aside, or a place or way of escape.
  • اِنْحَاصَ (inḥāṣa): to turn aside or deviate from something.
  • اِنْحِيَاص (inḥiyāṣ): turning aside and moving away.
  • حَايَصَ (ḥāyaṣa): to avoid an encounter through changes of direction or movement, or seek a means of avoiding something.
  • مُحَايَصَة (muḥāyaṣah): an attempt to escape or avoid something, sometimes involving a kind of contest or struggle.
  • تَحَايَصَ (taḥāyaṣa): to turn aside or avoid something.
  • حَيُوص (ḥayūṣ): describing an animal that veers from the intended course or resists the movement its owner wants.

These forms show the root’s recognized Arabic family. None appears in the Qur’an except maḥīṣ.

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the central meaning as follows:

الْمَيْلُ فِي جَوْرٍ وَتَلَدُّدٍ

al-maylu fī jawrin wa-taladdudin

That is, inclining or turning off course, with a kind of veering and movement back and forth. (Al-maylu fī jawrin wa-taladdudin.)

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: semantic core and poetic witness

His example ḥāṣa ʿani al-ḥaqqi describes someone who has deviated from truth.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains al-ḥayṣ through al-ḥaydu ʿani al-shayʾ: turning aside from something.

These explanations point to a closely related semantic field. People move away from their previous position or course and change direction in order to distance themselves from something.

Yet a change of direction should be distinguished from its cause.

People may turn away from danger and preserve their lives. They may withdraw before an enemy. They may turn from a truth they do not want to accept. They may also resort to different means to escape the consequences of their conduct.

All these situations involve turning aside or attempting escape, but they do not have the same moral value.

The root’s semantic core can therefore be understood as turning aside or changing direction to distance oneself from something, with extensions to escaping and seeking a way out.

Here, escape is not confined to rapid bodily movement. People may turn to another means of getting out of a situation or try to avoid the consequences of an action.

The root itself, however, does not indicate whether the attempt succeeds or fails. Success, or the absence of a way out, is conveyed by the sentence’s other elements.

2. Usage in early Arabic

An animal’s movement and turning off course

The lexicons record ḥāṣa al-farasu for a horse veering away from its direction of travel.

Dābbatun ḥayūṣun likewise describes an animal that turns from its owner’s intended course and is not readily guided in that direction.

Sideways movement is prominent here. The animal may avoid an obstacle, resist the intended direction, or take another path.

This shows that, at the level of lexical inquiry, the root is not necessarily about fleeing punishment or opposing truth. An animal’s movement can also be expressed through this family.

Early explanations record several verbal nouns for ḥāṣa, including ḥayṣan, ḥuyūṣan, and maḥīṣan.

These verbal nouns show that Qur’anic maḥīṣ belongs to a recognized Arabic verbal family. It was not a word coined only to describe the hereafter.

Turning aside before an enemy

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī transmits from Al-Farrāʾ a distinction in usage between two expressions:

Of friends or allied forces, one says ḥāṣū ʿani al-ʿaduwwi: they turned aside from the enemy.

Of enemies, however, the same movement may be described by inhazamū: they were defeated or retreated.

This distinction shows how the same outward movement can receive different descriptions depending on the speaker’s standpoint.

Withdrawing before an enemy may be a change of position or the result of defeat. Backward movement alone does not establish its cause or value.

The meaning of ḥāṣa should therefore not be restricted to cowardly flight.

In ḥāṣa ʿani al-sharri, someone turns away from harm or danger and may thereby preserve themselves from injury.

Turning aside may thus prevent harm, just as in another context it may be an attempt to escape truth or responsibility.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: animal movement and withdrawing from an enemy

Ḥayṣa bayṣa: difficulty and confusion

One recognized expression in early Arabic is ḥayṣa bayṣa.

The lexicons record the following expression for people caught in a confused, difficult situation from which they cannot find a way out:

«وَقَعُوا فِي حَيْصَ بَيْصَ» (Waqaʿū fī ḥayṣa bayṣa.)

It means that they have fallen into a confused and difficult predicament.

Difficulty and the lack of a clear way out are prominent in this expression.

People may move in several directions and try different routes, yet still fail to get out of their predicament.

Yet ḥayṣa bayṣa should not be treated as a simple verb or another form of maḥīṣ. It is an idiomatic compound with its own established use in Arabic.

Lexicons also record different explanations of its second element, bayṣa. Some connect it with bawṣ and explain that its form was changed to match ḥayṣ.

Even if accepted, this historical analysis does not make both elements derivatives of Ḥ Y Ṣ.

A witness from early poetry

In a verse transmitted by Al-Aṣmaʿī and other lexicographers, Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī says:

قَدْ كُنْتُ خَرَّاجًا وَلُوجًا صَيْرَفًا
لَمْ تَلْتَحِصْنِي حَيْصَ بَيْصَ لَحَاصِ

qad kuntu kharrājan walūjan ṣayrafan / lam taltaḥiṣnī ḥayṣa bayṣa laḥāṣi

A translation close to the sense:

“I have been resourceful and skilled at finding ways into affairs and out again; no severe, confused predicament has trapped me in a corner.”

The poet presents himself as someone skilled at entering undertakings and finding a way out of difficulties, undaunted by complicated predicaments.

Here, ḥayṣa bayṣa expresses a confused, difficult situation from which escape is not easy.

The expression is connected with the idea of a way out, but ḥayṣ itself should not be defined as difficulty in all its uses. Difficulty is the meaning of the complete idiom.

Lisān al-ʿArab, L Ḥ Ṣ: poetic attribution and explanation of taltaḥiṣnī and laḥāṣ

This verse is also recorded in Dīwān al-Hudhaliyyīn. Umayyah lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods and into the Umayyad era. Nevertheless, the date of this particular verse has not been established, so it should not confidently be presented as a witness predating the Qur’an.

Al-Sīrāfī, Sharḥ Kitāb Sībawayh: Umayyah’s witness and reference to Dīwān al-Hudhaliyyīn

The verse is useful evidence for the recognized early Arabic use of ḥayṣa bayṣa.

Avoiding death

The lexicons also record nuḥāyiṣu al-mawta: we try to evade death or ward it off by keeping clear of danger.

Explaining this expression, Abū ʿUbayd observes that death is not a real participant in a reciprocal contest as two people would be. Rather, someone makes such an effort to avoid it that it is as though an opponent were being faced.

This matters linguistically: the form muḥāyaṣah does not necessarily imply two parties with equal will and power.

People may try to avoid an event even if it is ultimately unavoidable.

This example is a recorded expression and its lexical explanation. It should not be presented without evidence as an independent pre-Qur’anic witness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū ʿUbayd on evading death

3. Maḥīṣ in the Qur’an: escape and the absence of a way out

In all five Qur’anic uses, maḥīṣ occurs in a negative or interrogative construction expressing the absence of a way of escape.

This feature matters, but it must be understood correctly: the lexical meaning of maḥīṣ is escape or a way of escape. Its absence comes from words such as lā and mā, or the interrogative construction hal min.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:121: they find no escape from Hell

أُولَٰئِكَ مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ عَنْهَا مَحِيصًا

ulāʾika maʾwāhum jahannamu wa-lā yajidūna ʿanhā maḥīṣan

Proposed translation for this study:

“Those will have Hell as their abode, and they will find no way of escape from it.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:121

Here, maḥīṣan is the object of yajidūna. They do not find anything that could provide a way of escape from Hell.

The preposition ʿan in ʿanhā specifies movement away, and its pronoun refers to Hell.

Thus, wa-lā yajidūna ʿanhā maḥīṣan expresses the absence of a way of escape from Hell.

This also connects with the preceding context. Al-Nisāʾ 4:119 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:120 describe the promises and wishes Satan instills in his followers, while his promise is nothing but deception.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:121 then describes an outcome in which deceptive promises no longer provide any means of release.

Maḥīṣ itself, however, does not mean deception, following Satan, or Hell. Those concepts are known from the accompanying verses.

Nor does maḥīṣ alone specify the form, place, or possible means of escape. The verse states that no way out exists, rather than describing its spatial details.

Ibrāhīm 14:21: leaders and followers confronting shared consequences

وَبَرَزُوا لِلَّهِ جَمِيعًا فَقَالَ الضُّعَفَاءُ لِلَّذِينَ اسْتَكْبَرُوا إِنَّا كُنَّا لَكُمْ تَبَعًا فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ مُغْنُونَ عَنَّا مِنْ عَذَابِ اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ قَالُوا لَوْ هَدَانَا اللَّهُ لَهَدَيْنَاكُمْ سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْنَا أَجَزِعْنَا أَمْ صَبَرْنَا مَا لَنَا مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-barazū li-llāhi jamīʿan fa-qāla al-ḍuʿafāʾu li-lladhīna istakbarū innā kunnā lakum tabaʿan fa-hal antum mughnūna ʿannā min ʿadhābi allāhi min shayʾin qālū law hadānā allāhu la-hadaynākum sawāʾun ʿalaynā a-jaziʿnā am ṣabarnā mā lanā min maḥīṣin

Proposed translation for this study:

“And they all appeared before The God. Then those who were weak said to those who had acted arrogantly: We were your followers; can you now avert any of The God’s punishment from us? They said: Had The God guided us, we would have guided you. Whether we become distraught or endure, it is all the same for us: we have no way of escape.”

Ibrāhīm 14:21

This verse provides one of the clearest uses of maḥīṣ in relation to human relationships and the responsibility involved in following others.

In worldly life, some people followed others. Now, before The God, the followers ask their leaders whether they can avert any of the punishment from them.

The response of those who had acted arrogantly ends with this sentence:

«مَا لَنَا مِنْ مَحِيصٍ» (Mā lanā min maḥīṣin.)

It means: we have no way of escape.

Here, lanā refers to the speakers: those who say in the same sentence that becoming distraught or enduring makes no difference to their release from punishment.

This does not make maḥīṣ mean crying for help or appealing to a leader. Those are elements of the verse’s dialogue, while maḥīṣ concerns a way of escape.

The verse also shows that following those with power does not by itself remove human responsibility. In this scene, followers expect help from those they followed, but the leaders themselves find no way out.

Another point is the presence of a-jaziʿnā and ṣabarnā: whether we become distraught or endure.

This statement concerns those particular speakers after confronting punishment. It does not imply that patience is useless in every circumstance, or that the Qur’an denies the value of perseverance in truth.

Here, the point is that their different reactions do not provide a way of escape from the consequences confronting them.

The absence of maḥīṣ concerns the inability to get out of that situation, rather than the permanent worthlessness of patience or human effort.

Fuṣṣilat 41:48: former supports disappear

وَضَلَّ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَدْعُونَ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَظَنُّوا مَا لَهُمْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yadʿūna min qablu wa-ẓannū mā lahum min maḥīṣin

Proposed translation for this study:

“And what they used to invoke vanished from them, and they thought that they had no way of escape.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:48

Here, maḥīṣ appears alongside the loss or disappearance of supports that people had previously invoked.

The preceding verse mentions the day when they are questioned about The God’s alleged partners (Fuṣṣilat 41:47).

What they once turned to is now unavailable and cannot answer their need.

Mā lahum min maḥīṣin states that there is no way of escape for them.

Unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:121, however, this sentence does not specify the object of escape through ʿan and a pronoun referring to Hell.

What consequences they are seeking to escape is therefore understood from the context of judgment and reckoning.

Another difference is the verb wa-ẓannū.

In Arabic, this verb can express a person’s supposition or belief, and in some contexts it approaches certainty. Exegetical explanations of this verse also propose the meaning of certainty.

That discussion concerns the degree of the speakers’ knowledge, rather than the meaning of Ḥ Y Ṣ.

A direct translation may retain “they thought,” while the contextual explanation clarifies the force of their confrontation with the absence of a way out.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpreting ẓann as certainty in this context

This verse shows that maḥīṣ is not necessarily a bodily route for running or moving away. People may place their hopes of release in certain supports, then discover when truth is disclosed that these provide no means of deliverance.

That conclusion follows from the verse as a whole. Maḥīṣ itself continues to denote escape or a way out.

Al-Shūrā 42:35: no escape for those disputing the signs

وَيَعْلَمَ الَّذِينَ يُجَادِلُونَ فِي آيَاتِنَا مَا لَهُمْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-yaʿlama alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyātinā mā lahum min maḥīṣin

Proposed translation for this study:

“And so that those who dispute Our signs may know that they have no way of escape.”

Al-Shūrā 42:35

This verse follows a description of ships appearing like mountains upon the sea.

Al-Shūrā 42:33 describes the possibility of the wind falling still and the ships remaining motionless. Al-Shūrā 42:34 mentions the possibility of their destruction and The God’s pardoning much of what people have done.

Al-Shūrā 42:35 thus connects with The God’s power over conditions that people do not always control.

Here, maḥīṣ concerns those who dispute The God’s signs.

The root does not itself mean disputation. That is expressed by yujādilūna; maḥīṣ participates in the statement that no way of escape exists.

Nor does the verse establish that every question or critical examination of a religious interpretation is the censured disputation. Identifying the kind of dispute requires examining J D L itself and the contexts of the relevant verses.

Study of J D L

In this verse, disputing divine signs is discussed against the background of The God’s power and people’s inability to escape its consequences.

Yet mā lahum min maḥīṣin does not itself determine those consequences’ precise time or form. Unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:121, this sentence does not name Hell.

Thus, maḥīṣ means escape or a way of escape, whose absence this construction expresses. The particular outcome and situation must be identified from the context.

Qāf 50:36: do power and exploration provide an escape?

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُمْ بَطْشًا فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi hal min maḥīṣin

Proposed translation for this study:

“And how many generations We destroyed before them who were mightier in force than they! They ranged and searched through the lands. Was there any way of escape?”

Qāf 50:36

This verse concerns earlier generations and societies that possessed greater power yet were not protected from destruction.

Hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan emphasizes their greater force.

The next phrase is fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi: they ranged and searched through the lands.

The verse closes with the question:

«هَلْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ» (Hal min maḥīṣin.)

Is there any way of escape?

In context, the question has a negative rhetorical force: power and extensive movement did not provide those societies with escape from the outcome they faced.

Yet the explicit wording must be distinguished from an interpretive conclusion.

Fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi does not say that all their travels were solely attempts to flee punishment. It may concern their exploration, penetration, and extensive movement through the lands.

Connecting these activities with the final question emphasizes their inability to find escape. It does not justify restricting the purpose of all their actions to seeking a hiding place.

The verse also concerns the destruction of earlier generations in history. The Qur’anic use of maḥīṣ is therefore not confined to a scene in Hell.

People or societies may possess great worldly power and resources, yet power alone does not guarantee escape from every consequence.

This conclusion comes from the context, while maḥīṣ continues to denote escape or a way out.

Al-Ṭabarī: ranging through the lands and the question of escape

4. The morphology and syntax of maḥīṣ

Maḥīṣ derives from the verb ḥāṣa–yaḥīṣu.

This verb belongs to a hollow root with yāʾ as its middle radical.

The lexicons include maḥīṣ among the verb’s verbal nouns and also use it for a way or place of escape.

Structurally, it may be related to a mīm-prefixed verbal noun or forms denoting the place or possibility of the action. Yet no single analysis should be declared its only certain meaning in every verse without examining the context.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: maḥīṣ as a verbal noun and way of escape

For example, mā lanā min maḥīṣin may denote the absence of escape, or the absence of any route or possibility of escape.

“We have no way of escape” conveys this naturally, without requiring a rigid distinction in every instance between escape as an action and a place of escape as a geographical point.

The role of min in a negative construction

In four Qur’anic uses, maḥīṣ appears in min maḥīṣin.

Here, min follows a negation or question and emphasizes comprehensive negation or the absence of any instance.

For example, mā lanā min maḥīṣin means that no way of escape exists for us at all.

This min should not be rendered as a starting point of movement: its role differs from ʿanhā in Al-Nisāʾ 4:121.

In wa-lā yajidūna ʿanhā maḥīṣan, ʿan expresses moving away from Hell.

In mā lanā min maḥīṣin, however, min occurs in a negative construction and contributes to its comprehensiveness.

Prepositions and accompanying constructions thus help determine the sentence’s meaning and should not all be reduced to a single fixed sense.

The question hal min maḥīṣin

The construction in Qāf 50:36 is interrogative.

In the context of recalling the destruction of powerful generations, however, the question is not a request for new information. It emphasizes the absence of any escape.

“Was there any way of escape?” is therefore a natural rendering.

Its negative rhetorical sense nevertheless comes from the question and context, rather than maḥīṣ itself meaning “there is no escape.”

This distinction matters in root study: incorporating negation into the word itself would also distort possible affirmative uses in Arabic.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ Y Ṣ (ḥayṣ) and Ḥ Y D (ḥayd): escaping and turning aside

These two roots are semantically very close.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains ḥayṣ itself through ḥayd. Both can denote turning aside from something.

Yet in recognized uses of Ḥ Y Ṣ, movement to escape, resourcefulness, and avoiding an encounter are prominent.

In Ḥ Y D, turning aside and changing direction have a wider range and are not necessarily accompanied by escape from danger.

In Qāf 50:19, for example, taḥīdu concerns evading death. In Qāf 50:36, maḥīṣ occurs in a question about a way out of destruction.

The two verses belong to one surah and are conceptually close, but they use different forms from different roots.

The first concerns the act of evading; the second, the absence of a way of escape.

This contrast does not mean that ḥayd is never used for flight or that ḥayṣ is never used for a simple change of direction. Lexicons record overlapping uses for both.

Their distinction is therefore a tendency, rather than an absolute boundary.

Study of Ḥ Y D

Ḥ Y Ṣ (ḥayṣ) and N W Ṣ (nawṣ): a way of escape and the time for escape

Ṣād 38:3 contains wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣin.

Manāṣ belongs to N W Ṣ, associated in Arabic with movement, withdrawing, and escaping.

It is close in meaning to maḥīṣ but does not share its root.

In the verse in Ṣād, ḥīna manāṣin foregrounds time: it is no longer the time to escape.

By contrast, hal min maḥīṣin in Qāf 50:36 asks whether a way of escape exists.

This distinction comes from the complete sentences, rather than manāṣ always denoting only time and maḥīṣ always denoting only place.

Both families may concern fleeing and moving away.

Ḥ Y Ṣ (ḥayṣ) and F R R (firār): fleeing and seeking a means of avoidance

Arabic firār concerns fleeing from someone or something.

Many uses of ḥayṣ belong to the same semantic field, but some witnesses give greater prominence to changing direction, veering, and trying to find a way to keep clear.

For example, a horse may veer from the intended course without necessarily fleeing a dangerous scene.

By contrast, farra minhu generally expresses flight from someone or something more directly.

This does not establish that every ḥayṣ is a complicated movement and every firār a straight one. No such spatial or kinetic rule follows from the lexical evidence.

Rendering maḥīṣ as a way of escape conveys these concepts to the extent needed.

Ḥ Y Ṣ (ḥayṣ) and L J ʾ (lajaʾ): escaping and taking refuge

L J ʾ concerns seeking refuge and turning to a place or person for protection.

Maḥīṣ concerns escape or a way out.

People may reach a shelter while escaping danger, but escaping and taking refuge are not a single act.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 describes seeking shelters and ways of entering them. It shows that a refuge can be a particular place.

In mā lahum min maḥīṣin, however, a physical shelter is not necessarily intended. The entire possibility of avoiding a consequence may be denied.

“Refuge” can therefore be an explanatory rendering of maḥīṣ in some settings, but “a way of escape” preserves its semantic range more precisely.

Ḥ Y Ṣ (ḥayṣ) and Ḥ Y R (ḥayrah): escaping and bewilderment

Ḥ Y R concerns bewilderment and the inability to find a clear direction or decision.

Ḥ Y Ṣ concerns turning aside and seeking escape.

People trying to escape danger may try different routes and become bewildered. This connection does not make the roots synonymous.

Someone may be bewildered on a journey without fleeing anything. Conversely, a person may deliberately avoid danger with a clear knowledge of the route.

Bewilderment is the condition of lacking a clear direction, whereas ḥayṣ may be an attempt to move away from a situation.

Early Arabic ḥayṣa bayṣa concerns confusion and difficulty and thus approaches bewilderment. Yet this is the sense of an idiomatic compound and should not be generalized to every use of the root.

Study of Ḥ Y R

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The most suitable general equivalent for Qur’anic maḥīṣ is “a way of escape.”

This preserves both moving away and the possibility of release without necessarily introducing a physical route or geographical shelter into the verse.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:121, wa-lā yajidūna ʿanhā maḥīṣan may be rendered: “And they find no way of escape from it.”

In Ibrāhīm 14:21, “We have no way of escape” naturally renders mā lanā min maḥīṣin.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:48 and Al-Shūrā 42:35, “They have no way of escape” likewise fits the construction.

In Qāf 50:36, hal min maḥīṣin can be rendered “Is there any way of escape?” or, in light of the past context, “Was there any way of escape?”

In all these instances, “a way of escape” is a clear equivalent with little ambiguity.

“An escape place” can also convey the meaning, but it may suggest a specific location more strongly. Qur’anic maḥīṣ is not necessarily limited to a physical place.

“Refuge” is understandable in some situations, but can replace the sense of escaping with taking shelter.

“A way of deliverance” also approaches the meaning in terms of its result, but deliverance emphasizes successful release, whereas maḥīṣ concerns the possibility of escape.

“A way of escape” is therefore preferred for Forqan’s basic rendering.

A lexical explanation may add that Arabic maḥīṣ is associated with turning aside, changing direction, and escaping from something.

7. Conclusion

In Arabic, Ḥ Y Ṣ concerns turning aside, changing direction, and trying to escape from something. Its lexical witnesses describe animal movement, withdrawing before an enemy, and seeking a way out of difficulty.

In ḥayṣa bayṣa, this semantic field is connected with confusion and being caught in a difficult situation. Yet the idiom’s sense should not define all the root’s derivatives.

The Qur’an uses this root five times, only in maḥīṣ. All instances concern the absence of a way of escape: sometimes before Hell and reckoning in the hereafter, and sometimes in recalling the outcome of earlier societies and powers that were destroyed.

These uses show that maḥīṣ does not necessarily mean a particular physical path. People may rely on power, leaders, resources, or different routes to avoid a consequence; in these verses, however, none provides escape.

The root itself nevertheless does not mean certain failure or punishment. That sense comes from negation, the negative rhetorical question, and the verses’ contexts.

Final definition: Ḥ Y Ṣ denotes turning aside and changing direction to move away from something; extended uses concern escaping and seeking a way out. Qur’anic maḥīṣ means escape or a way of escape, whose absence is expressed in all five occurrences. Brief root meaning: Turning aside and changing direction to move away from something; escaping and seeking a way out. Maḥīṣ: escape or a way of escape.
