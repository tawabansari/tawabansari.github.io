Central field: advancing, passing, and going beyond a boundary or position; in expressions about life, the departure of the soul and the end of life; concerning truth and falsehood, disappearance, collapse, and perishing.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root z h q ranges from advancing and overtaking to passing beyond a limit, departing, disappearing, and perishing.

Zahaqa al-sahmu describes an arrow overshooting its target without hitting it. Zahaqa al-farasu can describe a horse overtaking others.

Zahaqat nafsuhu is an expression for someone's soul departing and life ending.

Zahaqa al-shayʾu can describe something disappearing, perishing, or losing its former presence or effect.

An early lexical analysis describes the underlying field as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَقَدُّمٍ وَمُضِيٍّ وَتَجَاوُزٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā taqaddumin wa-muḍiyyin wa-tajāwuz

“One fundamental sense indicating advancing, passing, and going beyond.”

This does not restrict the root to death or destruction.

A horse can move ahead.

An arrow can overshoot its target.

Departure of the soul is a linguistic expression for the end of life; by itself it does not explain how the soul relates to the body.

Something that had presence or standing can disappear.

These connections do not prove that every usage historically arose from one original image. Each requires its own linguistic evidence.

Poetic witness: zāhiq attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

الْقَائِدُ الْخَيْلَ مَنْكُوبًا دَوَابِرُهَا
مِنْهَا الشَّنُونُ وَمِنْهَا الزَّاهِقُ الزَّهِمُ

al-qāʾidu al-khayla mankūban dawābiruhā / minhā al-shanūnu wa-minhā al-zāhiqu al-zahimu

Its general sense is:

“He leads horses with battered rear edges of their hooves; among them are lean ones and others full-fleshed and fat.” This follows the interpretation contrasting lean and well-fed animals.

Al-zāhiq describes a horse here.

One established lexical explanation concerns an animal with abundant flesh and fat and full marrow.

Al-zahim likewise concerns fatness in this description.

On this reading, zāhiq does not mean a dead or destroyed horse.

The root therefore cannot be restricted to destruction in every application.

An early analysis connects an animal's fatness with exceeding a moderate measure.

Other explanations differ: some associate zāhiq with full marrow and fatness, others with emaciation or scant marrow.

The verse also has textual variants, and its opening hemistich occurs with a different continuation.

Its presence in early lexical transmission should be distinguished from certainty about every detail of wording and interpretation.

At minimum, it witnesses a physical description of a horse, distinct from the Quranic disappearance of falsehood.

Movement and passing beyond

Several other expressions are recorded:

زَهَقَ الْفَرَسُ أَمَامَ الْخَيْلِ

zahaqa al-farasu amāma al-khayl

“The horse moved ahead of the others.”

زَهَقَ السَّهْمُ

zahaqa al-sahm

“The arrow overshot its target without hitting it.”

زَهَقَ فُلَانٌ بَيْنَ أَيْدِينَا

zahaqa fulānun bayna aydīnā

“Someone went ahead of us.”

These concern movement beyond a position or limit.

Lexical registration alone does not establish when each expression originated. Secure pre-Islamic dating requires independently datable evidence.

Historical meaning and the limits of the witnesses

Early Arabic evidence supports movement, passing beyond a limit, and departure from an earlier position or condition.

The verse attributed to Zuhayr illustrates animal description, subject to the uncertainties of wording and interpretation.

Overtaking, overshooting, the soul's departure, and disappearance are all registered in early lexicons, but do not all have equally secure independent pre-Islamic witnesses.

A defensible account is therefore:

The family includes advancing, passing beyond, and departure. In expressions about life and continued existence, it concerns ending and perishing. Their relationship can be described without asserting a certain historical sequence.

This preserves the range without imposing destruction on every form or automatically assigning later lexical records to pre-Islamic speech.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Zahaqa, yazhaqu

This basic verb has several recognized applications.

In movement, it can mean advancing, passing, or going beyond a limit.

Concerning continued existence:

زَهَقَ الشَّيْءُ

zahaqa al-shayʾ

“The thing disappeared or perished.”

Concerning life:

زَهَقَتْ نَفْسُهُ

zahaqat nafsuhu

“His soul departed; his life ended.”

Concerning falsehood:

زَهَقَ الْبَاطِلُ

zahaqa al-bāṭil

“Falsehood vanished.”

The last two applications occur in the Quran: the departure of souls and the disappearance of falsehood.

Zahiqa, yazhaqu

Another vocalization with i on the middle consonant is recorded, especially in zahiqat nafsuhu.

It likewise concerns the soul's departure.

The Quranic forms used here, in the reading of Ḥafṣ, have a on the hāʾ.

The reported vowel difference does not by itself create two separate accounts of death.

Zahq and zuhūq

These are verbal nouns of the root.

In movement, they concern advancing or passing.

Zuhūq al-nafs concerns the soul's departure and the end of life.

In the branch of perishing, zuhūq concerns disappearance.

The referent of the verb or verbal noun helps determine its meaning.

Zāhiq

The active participle of zahaqa.

In the branch of perishing, it describes something vanishing or no longer enduring.

أَمْرٌ زَاهِقٌ

amrun zāhiq

“A matter destined to pass away.”

In movement, it can describe something advancing or passing beyond a limit.

Animal descriptions also include physical condition and marrow.

Zāhiq is therefore not always synonymous with dead or destroyed.

The Quran applies this participle to falsehood.

Zahūq

On the pattern faʿūl, its Quranic use describes something liable to disappear and unable to endure.

The form foregrounds the quality of passing away.

The pattern alone specifies neither an exact time nor the speed or conditions of disappearance.

Biʾr zahūq is also recorded for a very deep well.

That application concerns depth and distance to the bottom.

Zahūq therefore does not always mean perishing; the subject matters.

Azhaqa, yuzhiqu

This is a Form IV verb.

Applied to life, it means causing the soul to depart or killing.

أَزْهَقَ نَفْسًا

azhaqa nafsan

“He took a life.”

Another application:

أَزْهَقَ الْبَاطِلَ

azhaqa al-bāṭila

“He caused falsehood to vanish.”

Some lexical records also concern carrying something beyond a limit or filling a vessel, although the wording and standing of particular examples are disputed.

Those uses should not be made the origin of every derivative without firm evidence.

Izhāq

The verbal noun of azhaqa.

In the branch of life, it concerns causing life to end.

Concerning falsehood, it means causing it to disappear or perish.

Izhāq al-arwāḥ is a familiar expression for taking lives.

It should not replace every Quranic use of zahaqa: in Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85, the souls themselves are the grammatical subject.

Zāhaqa, yuzāhiqu

This is a Form III verb.

Its relevant lexical application describes truth confronting and overcoming falsehood.

زَاهَقَ الْحَقُّ الْبَاطِلَ

zāhaqa al-ḥaqqu al-bāṭila

“Truth confronted and overcame falsehood.” The expression is not merely a neutral encounter between two parties.

Form III alone does not imply equal power or standing on both sides.

Inzahaqa

This is recorded for advancing, overtaking others, or moving beyond a previous position.

اِنْزَهَقَ الْفَرَسُ

inzahaqa al-faras

“The horse moved ahead of the others.”

This belongs to the movement branch.

Muzhaq and mazhūq

Muzhaq is the passive participle of azhaqa.

In the life-related application, it describes a life that has been taken or something caused to perish.

A distinct form, mazhūq, is recorded for a person subjected to pressure or constraint.

The two forms must be distinguished: mazhūq in the sense of constrained does not necessarily mean killed or dying.

Zahq

In another use, this denotes a hollow or depression in the ground.

Its descriptions mention a place into which something may fall.

A connection with moving below a surface is understandable, but the historical derivation is not certain.

Biʾr zāhiqah and biʾr zahūq

These describe a very deep well.

The salient feature is the distance to its bottom.

This should not be equated with death or destruction.

Faras zāhiq

Some accounts describe a fleshy horse with full marrow.

Other records associate the description with leanness or scant marrow.

Its meaning therefore requires the particular witness and context, rather than deduction from the Quranic sense.

Azāhīq

In the expression faras dhātu azāhīq, it describes a horse's rapid running; it is not simply a stand-alone adjective equivalent to “fast horse.”

This belongs to the field of movement and advancing.

Other associated forms for rapid movement and overtaking are also recorded.

The Quran uses the past verb zahaqa, the imperfect tazhaqa, the active participle zāhiq, and the description zahūq.

3. Quranic usage: the end of life

The root occurs five times: three verbs, one zāhiq, and one zahūq.

Two verbal occurrences concern human lives; the other three occurrences concern falsehood.

Al-Tawbah 9:55

فَلَا تُعْجِبْكَ أَمْوَالُهُمْ وَلَا أَوْلَادُهُمْ إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيُعَذِّبَهُم بِهَا فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَتَزْهَقَ أَنفُسُهُمْ وَهُمْ كَافِرُونَ

fa-lā tuʿjibka amwāluhum wa-lā awlāduhum innamā yurīdu Allāhu li-yuʿadhdhibahum bihā fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-tazhaqa anfusuhum wa-hum kāfirūn

Al-Tawbah 9:55

Translation:

“Let neither their wealth nor their children impress you. The God only intends to punish them through these in worldly life, and that their souls depart while they are disbelievers.”

Tazhaqa is an imperfect verb of z h q.

Anfusuhum, “their souls,” is its subject.

The construction connects the departure with the divine intention and punishment mentioned.

Wa-hum kāfirūn describes their condition when life ends.

Three components should therefore be distinguished:

Tazhaqa expresses departure and the end of life.

Anfusuhum identifies whose souls are meant.

Wa-hum kāfirūn describes their condition at death.

The root itself does not mean disbelief or dying in disbelief.

Arabic can use the verb for someone's death in faith or another condition as well.

Wealth, children, and punishment

The opening concerns the wealth and children of people whose conduct the Quran condemns.

Those benefits are then described as means of punishment in worldly life.

Al-Tawbah 9:54 and Al-Tawbah 9:56 supply context: people profess affiliation with believers while their conduct and inward commitments diverge.

Wealth and children are not, as such, lexically or ethically equivalent to punishment.

They can be blessings, responsibilities, or tests in the Quran.

Al-Anfāl 8:28 calls wealth and children a test.

Al-Kahf 18:46 calls wealth and children worldly adornment.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 also describes worldly bloom and testing through its benefits.

Al-Tawbah 9:55 should not become a universal judgment that wealth and children themselves establish divine punishment.

A particular group's conduct and condition are being assessed.

At the same time, the explicit association with divine intention must not be removed or reduced merely to a psychological feeling. The clause states the relation without detailing every mechanism or instance.

Al-Tawbah 9:85

وَلَا تُعْجِبْكَ أَمْوَالُهُمْ وَأَوْلَادُهُمْ إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَن يُعَذِّبَهُم بِهَا فِي الدُّنْيَا وَتَزْهَقَ أَنفُسُهُمْ وَهُمْ كَافِرُونَ

wa-lā tuʿjibka amwāluhum wa-awlāduhum innamā yurīdu Allāhu an yuʿadhdhibahum bihā fī al-dunyā wa-tazhaqa anfusuhum wa-hum kāfirūn

Al-Tawbah 9:85

Translation:

“Let neither their wealth nor their children impress you. The God only intends to punish them through these in this world, and that their souls depart while they are disbelievers.”

This closely resembles Al-Tawbah 9:55 in subject and construction.

Both mention wealth, children, worldly punishment, and life ending in disbelief.

Al-Tawbah 9:85 has an yuʿadhdhibahum, whereas Al-Tawbah 9:55 has li-yuʿadhdhibahum.

The grammatical difference does not change the meaning of tazhaqa.

In both, tazhaqa anfusuhum concerns the end of life and departure of souls.

Al-Tawbah 9:84 prohibits performing ṣalāh over a deceased member of this group or standing at his grave.

Al-Tawbah 9:85 then recalls their wealth, children, and final condition.

The verse belongs to a specific account of this group's conduct and outcome.

Does tazhaqa imply the annihilation of the whole person?

Both verses assign the verb to anfusuhum.

Zahaqat nafsuhu is an established Arabic expression for the soul's departure and the end of life.

It does not by itself establish the permanent annihilation of the whole person, consciousness, or every possibility of life after death.

Other Quranic passages discuss death, taking souls, resurrection, and return.

Al-Zumar 39:42 describes taking souls at death and during sleep.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:15 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:16 distinguish death from subsequent resurrection.

Tazhaqa anfusuhum in Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85 thus describes the end of worldly life, not a denial of later life and reckoning.

The verb alone also supplies no physical mechanism for the soul's departure or its relationship to the body.

4. Falsehood passing away before truth

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81

وَقُلْ جَاءَ الْحَقُّ وَزَهَقَ الْبَاطِلُ إِنَّ الْبَاطِلَ كَانَ زَهُوقًا

wa-qul jāʾa al-ḥaqqu wa-zahaqa al-bāṭilu inna al-bāṭila kāna zahūqan

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81

Translation:

“Say: Truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Falsehood is indeed bound to vanish.”

The root occurs twice here.

First:

وَزَهَقَ الْبَاطِلُ

wa-zahaqa al-bāṭil

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81

“Falsehood has vanished.”

Then:

إِنَّ الْبَاطِلَ كَانَ زَهُوقًا

inna al-bāṭila kāna zahūqan

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81

“Falsehood is indeed bound to vanish.”

In the first clause, zahaqa is a past verb reporting falsehood's disappearance.

In the second, zahūqan characterizes falsehood as liable to vanish.

The distinction matters.

The verse first states its disappearance.

It then connects this with falsehood's nature: it lacks the endurance and stability of truth.

Why is falsehood described as passing away?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 contrasts truth with falsehood.

Truth's arrival displaces falsehood's standing.

Falsehood may appear influential for a time, but prominence or public acceptance does not make it true.

An unfounded claim, for example, may be promoted by powerful people and believed widely for years.

Once clear evidence exposes it, neither popularity nor longevity alters its truth value.

In that example, falsehood loses its claim to validity before truth.

This illustrates an application of the verse's theme; it does not define zahaqa as merely exposing a false statement or exhaust the verse's meaning.

Does falsehood always disappear immediately?

The verse presents truth's arrival and falsehood's disappearance concisely and decisively.

The root alone specifies no duration for every social manifestation of falsehood.

People may persist in a refuted claim through self-interest, prejudice, or ignorance.

Persistence does not make it true.

Structures of power and interest may also sustain a wrongful practice for a time.

Falsehood's invalidity before truth must therefore be distinguished from the timing of the disappearance of all its social effects.

The verse concerns falsehood's standing and outcome before truth; the verb alone provides no timetable for every collapse.

Zahūq and the endurance of truth

Zahūqan follows the pattern faʿūl.

This can emphasize the quality of being bound to vanish.

The pattern alone does not imply immediate destruction in every situation without any process.

Meaning arises from truth's arrival, falsehood's disappearance, and its description as zahūq together.

Truth and falsehood are not presented as equal in standing.

Truth exposes falsehood's invalidity, and falsehood lacks firmness before it.

This accords with the Quran's distinction between established truth and baseless claims.

5. Zāhiq in Al-Anbiyāʾ: falsehood struck down by truth

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18

بَلْ نَقْذِفُ بِالْحَقِّ عَلَى الْبَاطِلِ فَيَدْمَغُهُ فَإِذَا هُوَ زَاهِقٌ وَلَكُمُ الْوَيْلُ مِمَّا تَصِفُونَ

bal naqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqqi ʿalā al-bāṭili fa-yadmaghuhu fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun wa-lakumu al-waylu mimmā taṣifūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18

Translation:

“Rather, We cast truth against falsehood, and it crushes it; then behold, it vanishes. Woe to you for what you describe.”

Zāhiqun is the active participle of z h q.

Fa-idhā huwa zāhiq expresses the result of truth's encounter with falsehood.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:16 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:17 distinguish the creation of the heavens and earth from purposeless play.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 then presents truth overcoming falsehood.

The closing warning challenges the addressees' descriptions.

The context concerns creation, false attributions, and knowledge of The God.

The role of fa-yadmaghuhu

Fa-yadmaghuhu precedes zāhiq.

It belongs to d m gh and evokes a blow to the head or brain.

The image is decisive: truth is cast against falsehood and crushes it.

Falsehood then vanishes.

Zāhiq itself does not mean striking a head.

The crushing blow comes from fa-yadmaghuhu.

Zāhiq describes falsehood's resulting condition.

The two parts of the image should remain distinct:

Fa-yadmaghuhu expresses the crushing action.

Zāhiq describes disappearance and perishing.

This prevents substituting one root's meaning for another's.

The role of fa-idhā

The construction foregrounds a sudden, manifest result.

After truth is cast upon it, falsehood is pictured as unable to withstand it.

This is more forceful than a plain report of disappearance.

The sudden presentation comes from the construction, including fa-idhā, not from an invariant meaning of instantaneous destruction in zāhiq.

Zāhiq and zahūq

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 uses zahūqan for falsehood.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 uses zāhiqun.

Both concern disappearance in these contexts.

Zāhiq is an active participle describing what is vanishing.

Zahūq, on the pattern faʿūl, emphasizes the quality of being liable to vanish.

In Al-Isrāʾ, the description follows the report of falsehood's disappearance.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ, the participle follows the crushing encounter with truth.

This suits the respective constructions, but creates no universal rule that zāhiq denotes only a brief event and zahūq only a permanent quality.

Each form is understood through morphology, sentence structure, and context.

6. The end of life and the end of falsehood

The Quran applies one root to two different subjects.

Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85 concern human lives ending.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 concern falsehood disappearing before truth.

They share an ending or disappearance of something previously present or effective.

The subjects nevertheless differ.

With the human soul, tazhaqa describes life's end.

With falsehood, the verb describes its disappearance before truth.

Falsehood is not a living organism with a body, respiration, and biological life.

The application therefore denotes disappearance, not bodily death in its ordinary sense.

Conversely, a person's death does not establish that their existence was falsehood.

Worldly life ending differs from the truth or falsity of a person's beliefs and conduct.

Shared vocabulary does not make falsehood's disappearance physically identical to a body's death.

A shared meaning of passing away does not make the two phenomena identical in nature.

7. Related roots and distinctions

Z H Q and Z W L: passing away

Zawāl concerns departure, removal, or a condition ending.

Zahaqa overlaps with it in the passages about falsehood.

Zahaqa al-bāṭilu in Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 can be rendered “falsehood vanished.”

Zawāl can also concern a change of position or condition without complete destruction.

Zahaqa in the life and falsehood passages conveys a stronger ending or disappearance.

This distinction is not absolute across Arabic usage.

Z H Q and H L K: perishing

Halāk concerns destruction, perishing, or ending.

Zahaqa can convey a related meaning.

Yet it also includes overtaking horses, overshooting arrows, and deep wells.

The roots overlap in perishing without having identical semantic ranges.

Zahaqat nafsuhu describes life's end without establishing annihilation of the person in the Hereafter.

Z H Q and M W T: death

Mawt concerns the end of life and contrasts with life in different Quranic applications.

Zahaqat nafsuhu is also used for life's end.

Mawt names death, while the expression with zahaqa presents the soul's departure.

That linguistic distinction should not become a definitive biological or metaphysical theory of stages of death.

The Quran's vocabulary of death, taking souls, and resurrection requires examination across passages.

Z H Q and F N Y: passing out of existence

Fanāʾ concerns passing away and impermanence.

Zahaqa likewise describes falsehood's disappearance in Al-Isrāʾ 17:81.

Fanāʾ can foreground perishability, whereas verbal zahaqa reports departure or disappearance.

This is a contextual tendency, not an absolute division.

Z H Q and B Ṭ L: falsehood

These occur together in Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 but have distinct roles.

Bāṭil describes something false, baseless, or lacking the validity of truth.

Zahaqa describes its disappearance.

Bāṭil identifies the thing opposed to truth; zahaqa expresses its outcome or vanishing condition.

A claim can be false before everyone recognizes its falsity.

Being false and falsehood disappearing are related but separate matters.

Z H Q and Q DH F: casting

Qadhf fundamentally concerns casting or throwing.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 describes casting truth against falsehood.

Zāhiq describes the outcome.

Naqdhifu and zāhiq are not synonymous merely because they form one image.

Nor does the casting image specify a physical mechanism or material tool for destroying falsehood.

Z H Q and Z H R: brightness

The roots resemble each other in letters but have different semantic fields.

Zahr concerns beauty, brightness, and blooming.

Quranic z h q concerns departure and disappearance.

A blossom's beauty may be temporary, but that does not prove a common derivation.

Ṭā Hā 20:131 uses zahrat al-ḥayāt al-dunyā for worldly bloom and splendour.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 uses zahaqa al-bāṭilu for falsehood's disappearance.

Phonetic resemblance should not conflate them.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following renderings distinguish important forms. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

Zahaqa: advanced or passed; in existence-related contexts, vanished or perished.

Zahaqa al-shayʾu: the thing disappeared.

Zahaqa al-bāṭilu: falsehood vanished or perished.

Zahaqat nafsuhu: his soul departed; his life ended.

Zahq: advancing or passing; separately, a depression in the ground.

Zuhūq: passing or disappearing; with the soul, the end of life.

Zāhiq: vanishing, perishing, or vanished, depending on context.

Zahūq: bound to vanish; unable to endure.

Azhaqa: caused to vanish; concerning life, caused death.

Izhāq: causing disappearance or, concerning life, death.

Zāhaqa: confronted and overcame, in the expression about truth and falsehood.

Inzahaqa: advanced or overtook others.

Muzhaq: caused to perish; mazhūq: subjected to pressure or constraint, in its recorded use.

Biʾr zahūq: a very deep well.

Faras zāhiq: a horse described in terms of fatness or marrow, with differing lexical accounts.

Azāhīq: rapid running, as in faras dhātu azāhīq.

For Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85:

“Their lives ending and their souls departing.”

For zahaqa in Al-Isrāʾ 17:81:

“Falsehood disappearing before truth.”

For zahūqan in that verse:

“Falsehood is bound to vanish and cannot endure.”

For Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18:

“Falsehood vanishing after its encounter with truth.”

These distinctions preserve the shared field without reducing bodily death and falsehood's disappearance to an identical phenomenon.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic family concerns advancing, passing, and going beyond a previous position or limit.

Physical applications include horses advancing and arrows overshooting targets.

Zahaqat nafsuhu concerns the soul's departure and life's end.

Applied to falsehood, zahaqa concerns its disappearance and perishing.

Early Arabic also uses the family for animal condition and well depth. The witness attributed to Zuhayr describes horses, with uncertainties concerning its wording and interpretation.

The entire family cannot be restricted to death or destruction.

Its five Quranic occurrences are distributed across four verses.

In Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85, tazhaqa anfusuhum concerns people dying in disbelief.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 uses zahaqa and zahūq for falsehood's disappearance and inability to endure before truth.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 uses zāhiq for falsehood's condition after being crushed by truth.

These belong to a shared semantic field, while their subjects remain different.

The end of human worldly life and falsehood's disappearance are not identical in nature; falsehood is not undergoing the bodily death of a living organism.

The family includes passing and departing, while its Quranic applications concern life's end and falsehood's disappearance. This semantic connection does not establish a certain historical order of derivation.

The word conveys departure or disappearance; causes, ethical significance, and manner come from the rest of the verse and wider Quranic context.

Zahaqa therefore does not itself mean disbelief or punishment, nor does every use of zāhiq mean death.

Each form must be read through its actual usage, referent, and verse context.
