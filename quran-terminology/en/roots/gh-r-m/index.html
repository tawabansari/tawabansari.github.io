Obligation, debt, financial loss, and persistent affliction

Arabic gh r m concerns obligation, indebtedness, bearing loss, and becoming caught in a condition from which release is difficult. Ghurm names a debt or payment owed; ghārim is someone bearing a debt or financial obligation; maghram denotes a financial burden, debt, or loss to be borne; and early gharām also describes severe, persistent suffering and affliction.

Becoming binding is important in this family. A debt remains owed until discharged or otherwise removed; a creditor may pursue payment; and severe affliction can cling to someone so closely that escape is difficult.

The derivatives nevertheless differ. Indebtedness, loss, suffering punishment, and intense attachment are related within the family but must each be understood in their particular uses.

The root occurs six times in the Quran in four main forms: ghārimīn, maghram, gharām, and mughramūn. The uses range from debtors and financial expenses to agricultural loss and the punishment of Hell.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early ghurm described a financial obligation or payment for which someone was held responsible. It could arise from debt, blood compensation, a guarantee, or compensation for damage.

Gharima concerns incurring such a debt or payment; gharāmah names the amount someone is required to pay.

In pre-Islamic Arab society this was not confined to trade or personal borrowing. An individual or group might undertake payments or blood compensation to end tribal bloodshed.

People who had not caused the conflict could thus assume financial responsibility to establish peace.

Gharāmah does not necessarily punish a guilty person. Someone may undertake payment through an accepted responsibility, guarantee, or contribution to compensation.

A witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period: compensation in Zuhayr’s poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes:

يُنَجِّمُهَا قَوْمٌ لِقَوْمٍ غَرَامَةً

yunajjimuhā qawmun li-qawmin gharāmatan

وَلَمْ يُهَرِيقُوا بَيْنَهُمْ مِلْءَ مِحْجَمِ

wa-lam yuharīqū baynahum milʾa miḥjami

Translation:

“One group pays it to another in instalments as compensation, though they have not shed even a cupping vessel’s fill of blood among them.”

The subject is paying blood compensation to remedy bloodshed and end tribal disputes.

The poet describes those who undertake payment despite not having caused the bloodshed themselves.

Gharāmatan names money whose payment has become their responsibility. It may be paid in instalments, with the obligation remaining until fulfilled.

The witness makes three points clear:

First, gharāmah is a real financial obligation.

Second, it can arise from compensation or social responsibility, not only purchasing or borrowing.

Third, the payer is not necessarily the person originally responsible for the damage.

This is a familiar verse attributed to the pre-Islamic period, transmitted in established versions of Zuhayr’s Muʿallaqah. Although some wording varies, gharāmah clearly concerns blood compensation and financial redress.

Another witness: gharām in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, includes:

إِنْ يُعَاقِبْ يَكُنْ غَرَامًا وَإِنْ يُعْـ

in yuʿāqib yakun gharāman wa-in yuʿ-

ـطِ جَزِيلًا فَإِنَّهُ لَا يُبَالِي

-ṭi jazīlan fa-innahu lā yubālī

Translation:

“If he punishes, his punishment is severe and unrelenting; if he gives, he gives generously and does not hold back.”

Gharām does not name a payment or debt here: the poet describes the punishment’s severity and persistent grip.

The difference from Zuhayr’s verse matters.

Zuhayr’s gharāmah is a financial obligation undertaken by people.

Al-Aʿshā’s gharām is an affliction or punishment that does not release its subject.

These uses show financial debt and payment, as well as persistent suffering, within the early lexical family.

They do not establish the precise historical development of either meaning from the other. They support the presence of both branches in early linguistic tradition and their semantic connection with obligation and persistence.

2. The semantic centre and its branches

A. Ghurm: debt and financial obligation

Financial ghurm concerns a debt, loss, or payment that falls upon someone.

Someone might borrow money to establish a workshop and become responsible for repaying it.

Even if the money produces profit, the original obligation remains until discharged according to the agreement.

Ghurm here concerns financial responsibility and required payment, rather than necessarily poverty or economic loss.

An indebted person may possess enough assets to pay. Conversely, someone can suffer financial loss in an accident without owing anyone a debt.

Indebtedness and financial loss are related but not identical.

B. Gharāmah: a required payment

Gharāmah names a payment required through an undertaking, responsibility, guarantee, or compensation for damage.

Compensation owed for destroying another person’s property, for example, can fall within this semantic field.

A person guaranteeing another’s debt may also become responsible for payment if the original debtor cannot pay.

The lexical meaning should not be confined to a modern governmental fine or monetary punishment. A payment can be compensatory, guaranteed, or contractual without being punitive.

C. Gharīm: creditor and debtor

A notable feature is the use of gharīm for both parties to a debt relationship.

Arabic lexicons apply it both to someone owing a debt and to someone demanding payment of a claim.

This use is explained through the relationship of persistence.

The debtor remains bound to payment, while the creditor continues pursuing their right until receiving it.

If someone lends another a thousand units, one owes repayment and the other has a claim.

Both belong to the financial relationship but occupy different positions.

Both can be called gharīm; ghārim, however, emphasizes the debtor or person upon whom payment falls.

This distinction matters for Quranic al-ghārimīn.

D. Gharām: persistent affliction and suffering

Early Arabic uses gharām for severe punishment, suffering, or affliction that does not release a person.

Payment is no longer necessarily involved. Persistence and difficulty of escape remain central.

A calamity might affect someone’s life for a long time, with consequences that are difficult to escape.

This illustrates gharām, but does not mean that every lasting calamity must be called gharām lexically.

The Quran uses this particular form of Hell’s punishment.

E. Gharām and intense attachment

Classical Arabic also records gharām and mughram for intense attachment and infatuation.

Mughramun bi-shayʾ describes someone deeply attached to or fascinated by something, finding separation difficult.

Here persistence concerns attachment rather than an external obligation or affliction. This semantic comparison does not establish the historical direction in which the meanings developed.

Someone might be so absorbed in something that they constantly think about it and find it hard to leave aside.

Persian also knows gharām in the sense of love or passionate attachment.

That romantic meaning does not occur in the Quran and should not be imported into gharāman in Al-Furqān.

Its presence in classical sources does not itself prove that the same meaning was equally widespread before Islam.

3. Morphological forms and Quranic derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • Gharima / yaghramu: to become indebted, incur a payment, or suffer financial loss.
  • Ghurm: debt, an obligation to pay, or financial loss.
  • Gharāmah: compensation or a required payment.
  • Ghārim: a debtor or someone owing a payment.
  • Gharīm: debtor or creditor, according to the relationship and context.
  • Ghuramāʾ: plural of gharīm.
  • Maghram: debt, a financial burden, or a loss borne.
  • Gharām: severe, persistent affliction; elsewhere, intense attachment.
  • Aghrama: to cause someone to incur a payment or obligation.
  • Gharrama: to require someone to pay.
  • Taghrīm: requiring payment or imposing compensation.
  • Mughram: someone burdened financially or caught in a condition; elsewhere, someone deeply attached or infatuated.

Four derivatives from these forms occur in the Quran.

Ghārimīn is the plural of the active participle ghārim.

Maghram is a noun concerning debt, loss, or an imposed burden.

Gharām is a noun concerning persistent affliction and suffering.

Mughramūn is the sound masculine plural of mughram, a passive participle of Form IV.

Maghram and mughram must be distinguished: the former names the burden or loss; the latter describes those who incur or bear it.

4. Al-ghārimīn: debtors among recipients of charitable funds

Al-Tawbah 9:60

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا وَالْمُؤَلَّفَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَالْغَارِمِينَ وَفِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۖ فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

innamā al-ṣadaqātu li-l-fuqarāʾi wa-l-masākīni wa-l-ʿāmilīna ʿalayhā wa-l-muʾallafati qulūbuhum wa-fī al-riqābi wa-l-ghārimīna wa-fī sabīli allāhi wa-bni al-sabīli farīḍatan mina allāhi wa-llāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

Al-Tawbah 9:60

“Charitable funds are only for the poor, the needy, those administering them, those whose hearts are brought together, for freeing people from bondage, for debtors, in the way of The God, and for the stranded traveller: an obligation from The God. The God is All-Knowing, Wise.”

Al-ghārimīn appears among the groups to whom charitable funds are allocated.

Ghārim is the active participle of gharima and names someone bearing a debt or financial obligation.

Al-ghārimīn therefore means debtors.

Why are debtors named separately from the poor?

Al-fuqarāʾ, al-masākīn, and al-ghārimīn are listed separately.

Indebtedness should not therefore be reduced uncritically to poverty or general need.

Someone may have an income, yet heavy debt absorbs much of it and reduces their ability to meet living needs.

For example, borrowing for a relative’s treatment leaves a repayment obligation after the treatment ends, potentially placing the family in hardship.

The problem is not simply insufficient income: a specific debt is also owed.

Someone might likewise guarantee a payment to prevent conflict or compensate others, then struggle to fulfil it.

Zuhayr’s early witness shows such responsibilities were familiar in Arab society.

Ghārim alone does not establish that every debtor, regardless of assets, debt type, or repayment capacity, equally needs charitable support.

The lexical meaning is indebtedness. Assessing the actual situation and appropriate assistance concerns justice, need, and proper distribution of resources.

Does ghārim specifically mean someone who took an interest-bearing loan?

No. Gh r m does not itself specify the financial contract.

Debt can arise from borrowing, deferred payment, a guarantee, damage, or another undertaking.

Debtors cannot therefore be restricted to borrowers paying interest. Nor does al-ghārimīn establish the permissibility or prohibition of a particular contract.

Usury, contractual fairness, creditors’ rights, and debtors’ circumstances require examination of the relevant verses.

Allowing time for a debtor in difficulty

Al-Baqarah 2:280 instructs that a debtor in hardship be allowed time until circumstances improve, and presents remitting the debt as better.

This connects with Al-Tawbah 9:60.

One verse lists debtors among charitable recipients; the other addresses consideration for a debtor in hardship.

Debt is thus not presented solely as a private relationship: social responsibility and assistance in relieving financial pressure also arise where there is need.

This connection clarifies the social significance of al-ghārimīn, but the entire set of rulings is not part of the root’s lexical definition.

5. Maghram: a financial burden, loss, or expense

Al-Tawbah 9:98: giving regarded as a loss

Al-Tawbah 9:98

وَمِنَ الْأَعْرَابِ مَن يَتَّخِذُ مَا يُنْفِقُ مَغْرَمًا وَيَتَرَبَّصُ بِكُمُ الدَّوَائِرَ ۚ عَلَيْهِمْ دَائِرَةُ السَّوْءِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-mina al-aʿrābi man yattakhidhu mā yunfiqu maghraman wa-yatarabbaṣu bikumu al-dawāʾira ʿalayhim dāʾiratu al-sawʾi wa-llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm

Al-Tawbah 9:98

“Among the desert Arabs are those who regard what they spend as a loss and await turns of misfortune for you. May the evil turn fall upon them; The God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”

Maghraman describes money someone gives.

Yattakhidhu mā yunfiqu maghraman shows that the person regards the contribution as a loss or financial burden.

This does not establish that giving is actually a loss.

The verse concerns the person’s attitude to giving.

Someone might contribute to a social assistance fund while disbelieving in its purpose, regarding the payment merely as an imposed expense reducing their assets.

Another might give the same amount as a way of meeting others’ needs and fulfilling social responsibility.

The amounts can be identical while the attitudes differ.

The following verse makes the difference clearer.

Al-Tawbah 9:99 describes other desert Arabs who regard their contributions as a means of drawing near to The God and obtaining the Messenger’s prayers.

Maghraman and qurubāt in successive verses thus present different understandings of giving.

One group sees a burden and loss; the other, a means of drawing near.

The contrast comes from context. Gh r m itself does not mean hypocrisy, disbelief, or hostility: those qualities emerge from conduct and speech.

Al-Tawbah 9:98 also says wa-mina al-aʿrābi man, referring to some desert Arabs. The behaviour is not attributed to them all.

Al-Ṭūr 52:40: is payment demanded for conveying the message?

Al-Ṭūr 52:40

أَمْ تَسْأَلُهُمْ أَجْرًا فَهُمْ مِنْ مَغْرَمٍ مُثْقَلُونَ

am tasʾaluhum ajran fa-hum min maghramin muthqalūn

Al-Ṭūr 52:40

“Or do you ask them for payment, so that they are weighed down by a financial burden?”

Maghramin occurs alongside muthqalūn.

Maghram names the financial burden or payment; muthqalūn emphasizes its weight upon those involved.

Heaviness should not be derived from gh r m alone, since muthqalūn contributes to the sentence.

The verse asks whether Muhammad has demanded payment for conveying the message, leaving his audience burdened by it.

Within the surah’s sequence of questions and arguments, it indicates that his call does not depend on obtaining payment from them.

Maghram need not be a specific debt previously borrowed from a creditor. It can be a financial burden resulting from a demanded payment.

Al-Qalam 68:46: the same question repeated

Al-Qalam 68:46

أَمْ تَسْأَلُهُمْ أَجْرًا فَهُمْ مِنْ مَغْرَمٍ مُثْقَلُونَ

am tasʾaluhum ajran fa-hum min maghramin muthqalūn

Al-Qalam 68:46

“Or do you ask them for payment, so that they are weighed down by a financial burden?”

The wording is identical to Al-Ṭūr 52:40.

Both ask about demanded remuneration and its financial weight upon the audience.

Maghram can therefore name a payment incurred or demanded without necessarily involving a loan agreement.

Ajran and maghramin must also be distinguished.

Ajr is the payment that might be requested; maghram is the financial burden that such a demand would impose on the payer.

They view the financial relationship from different angles and are not synonyms.

6. Innā la-mughramūn: loss through crop destruction

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66

إِنَّا لَمُغْرَمُونَ

innā la-mughramūn

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66

“We have indeed suffered a grave loss.”

This short statement occurs in a passage about sowing and growing crops.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:63 asks about what people sow.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:64 asks whether they make it grow or The God does.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65 describes the possibility of reducing it to dry fragments, leaving people astonished and distressed over their crop’s fate.

Their words follow in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66: innā la-mughramūn.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:67, they say: bal naḥnu maḥrūmūn.

Does mughramūn mean debtors here?

Mughramūn is morphologically the plural passive participle of mughram and can describe people upon whom a financial burden or loss has fallen.

A debt owed to a particular creditor is not the passage’s main subject.

The scene concerns farmers who sow and work for a crop, only to see it destroyed.

Imagine a farmer preparing land, buying seed, supplying water and labour, and waiting months for the harvest.

If the crop is destroyed before harvest, expected profit is not the only thing lost: the expenditure, seed, work, and resources invested may also be lost.

The farmer can then regard themselves as suffering a grave loss.

This image accords with mughramūn.

“We have become debtors” may restrict the meaning too far, since the verse identifies neither a creditor nor a specific repayment obligation.

“We have suffered a grave loss” or “the burden of loss has fallen upon us” fits the agricultural context better.

Mughramūn and maḥrūmūn

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66 concerns incurring loss.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:67 concerns deprivation.

These are close but distinct concepts.

Mughramūn can emphasize the burden of loss and damage borne.

Maḥrūmūn concerns failing to obtain a benefit or something one would enjoy.

The farmer may lose expenditure and capital while also being deprived of the expected crop and income.

This explains the connection without deriving a definite theory of the farmers’ emotional sequence from bal alone.

The speech also belongs to a hypothetical scene: The God could destroy the crop if He willed. It is not a definite report of a particular crop destroyed in the past, but an image of human dependence on created conditions and limited productive power.

7. Gharāman: persistent, unrelenting punishment

Al-Furqān 25:65

وَالَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا اصْرِفْ عَنَّا عَذَابَ جَهَنَّمَ إِنَّ عَذَابَهَا كَانَ غَرَامًا

wa-lladhīna yaqūlūna rabbanā iṣrif ʿannā ʿadhāba jahannama inna ʿadhābahā kāna gharāman

Al-Furqān 25:65

“And those who say: ‘Our Lord, turn Hell’s punishment away from us; its punishment is indeed unrelenting.’”

Gharāman describes Hell’s punishment.

Here it concerns severe affliction, persistence, and difficulty of escape. The word alone does not settle every question about the punishment’s duration.

Financially, a debt remains owed until paid or removed.

In the punishment use, the affliction clings to the person and release is not easy.

This semantic connection accords with early witnesses, but Hell’s punishment should not be converted into a financial debt or economic compensation.

Gharām here does not directly mean love, infatuation, or indebtedness: it describes a punishment that clings to its subject.

Does gharām alone mean eternal punishment?

Persistence and continuing attachment are present, but the root alone does not determine every question about the duration of punishment in the hereafter.

A debt may remain for a long time yet end with payment. Likewise, an affliction can be persistent without the word specifying unlimited duration in every use.

Al-Furqān 25:65 concerns Hell’s punishment, whose severity and unrelenting character emerge from context.

Al-Furqān 25:66 also describes Hell as an evil settlement and residence.

Duration and the nature of punishment require separate consideration of the relevant verses on abiding, punishment, divine will, and human outcomes.

Gharām should neither be rendered “love” because of its romantic use nor made the sole basis for every theological conclusion about eternity.

Gharām and the prayer of the Entirely Merciful’s servants

Al-Furqān 25:63 begins describing the servants of the Entirely Merciful.

Al-Furqān 25:64 mentions their prostration and standing at night.

Al-Furqān 25:65 then describes their request that The God turn Hell’s punishment away from them.

The prayer thus belongs to people living humbly and attentively before their Lord.

Their fear need not imply despair of divine mercy. They request deliverance rather than claiming guaranteed immunity.

Gharām emphasizes the seriousness and weight of a punishment from which they seek protection.

8. Distinctions from related roots

Gh r m and d y n: financial obligation and debt

Financial dayn names a debt or claim whose payment is owed.

Ghurm can also concern debt and obligation, but extends beyond borrowing to compensation, bearing damage, and financial burdens.

Someone can become indebted by borrowing, or be required to pay through a guarantee or damage without borrowing.

Both can involve ghurm, although the obligation’s origin differs.

Gh r m and kh s r: financial burden and loss

Kh s r concerns loss, diminished capital, or lost benefit.

Gh r m can likewise express financial loss, but obligation or an incurred burden is important in its main uses.

A product’s market value may fall and its owner lose money without owing a debt or compensation to another person.

Conversely, someone may owe a sum while retaining enough assets to meet the obligation.

The fields converge in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66, where crop destruction causes loss and financial difficulty.

Gh r m and l z m: obligation and persistence

L z m concerns accompanying, adhering, and not separating from something.

This is close to the shared centre of many gh r m uses.

L z m is broader and applies to many matters; financial gh r m concerns debt, damage, and required payment, while gharām concerns severe persistent affliction.

Every debt may impose an obligation, but not everything binding or lasting is a debt or compensation.

Gh r m and ʾ j r: remuneration and the burden of payment

Ajran and maghramin occur together in Al-Ṭūr 52:40 and Al-Qalam 68:46.

Ajr can be remuneration requested or paid for work.

Maghram there is the financial burden such payment would place on the audience.

The same payment can be remuneration to its recipient and an expense or obligation to its payer.

This difference does not make every wage unjust. Its moral and legal assessment depends on agreement, work, and fairness, not merely the word maghram.

Gh r m and gh n m: loss and gain

Arabic ghunm concerns benefit, profit, and what one gains.

Ghurm, by contrast, concerns bearing payment, damage, or a financial burden.

The two sometimes express the paired aspects of benefit and responsibility.

Someone benefiting from ownership or an activity may also bear its expenses and losses.

Their proximity and use in legal expressions do not alone create an absolute rule covering every kind of ownership, profit, and obligation.

Each economic relationship requires assessment according to its agreement, rights, responsibilities, and circumstances.

9. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does ghurm always mean a loan?

No. Borrowing is one source of indebtedness; ghurm can also concern compensation, guarantees, damage, or other payments.

Al-ghārimīn in Al-Tawbah 9:60 means debtors.

Maghram in Al-Tawbah 9:98 concerns expenditure regarded as a loss.

Mughramūn in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66 occurs in an agricultural-loss context.

A narrow equivalent such as “loan” cannot cover every derivative.

Is every ghārim poor?

No. Debt and poverty are distinguishable conditions.

An indebted person may be able to repay, while someone without debt may need financial assistance.

Al-Tawbah 9:60 names debtors separately from the poor and needy.

This requires attention to actual circumstances but does not independently specify every condition for distributing charitable funds.

Is gharāmah always punishment?

No. It can compensate for damage or fulfil an undertaking.

Zuhayr’s witness shows people who had not caused bloodshed undertaking blood compensation to end a dispute.

Gharāmah therefore does not establish the payer’s guilt in every sentence.

Does maghram always name an actual loss?

In Al-Tawbah 9:98, someone regards their contribution as maghram.

The word can thus occur in a report of someone’s attitude.

The Quran is showing their understanding of giving, rather than merely noting an expense.

An actual loss must be distinguished from treating a payment as a loss.

Does mughramūn in Al-Wāqiʿah mean incurring a loan?

Indebtedness is possible within the lexical family, but loss through crop destruction is clearer in context.

The verse mentions no creditor, loan agreement, or repayment deadline.

Translation should not introduce details absent from the text.

Does Quranic gharām mean love?

No. Persian and some Arabic uses concern intense attachment, but Al-Furqān 25:65 describes Hell’s punishment.

Severe, persistent, unrelenting affliction fits that context.

Romantic attachment is another use of the family, not a meaning to impose on every derivative.

10. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The following renderings are suggested for the Quranic forms:

Al-ghārimīn — Al-Tawbah 9:60: “debtors,” explained as people bearing debt or financial obligations.

Maghraman — Al-Tawbah 9:98: “loss” or “financial burden”; in the sentence, “regards what he spends as a loss.”

Maghramin — Al-Ṭūr 52:40: “financial burden” or “required payment.” With muthqalūn: “so that they are weighed down by a financial burden?”

Maghramin — Al-Qalam 68:46: the same sense, given the identical wording and similar question about remuneration.

La-mughramūn — Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66: “we have indeed suffered a grave loss” or “the burden of loss has fallen upon us.”

Gharāman — Al-Furqān 25:65: “unrelenting” or “severe and persistent affliction.”

Outside the Quran:

Ghurm can be rendered “debt,” “payment obligation,” or “financial loss.”

Gharāmah means “compensation” or “a required payment.”

Gharīm can mean “debtor” or “creditor,” according to context.

Taghrīm means requiring someone to pay.

Financial mughram can mean burdened by debt or loss; in the attachment use, infatuated or deeply attached.

These equivalents must distinguish lexical meaning from contextual additions. The debt’s weight, fairness of payment, intention, and duration emerge from the complete sentence and other evidence.

Conclusion

Gh r m concerns obligation, persistence, and being caught in an undertaking or condition from which release is difficult.

Financially, this includes debt, compensation, damage, and required payment. Gharām concerns severe persistent suffering. Other Arabic uses concern intense attachment.

Poetic witnesses attributed to the pre-Islamic period support both financial obligation and persistent affliction. Zuhayr’s gharāmah is payment for compensation and peace; al-Aʿshā’s gharām describes severe, unrelenting punishment.

The root occurs six times in the Quran.

Al-ghārimīn in Al-Tawbah 9:60 are debtors listed among charitable recipients.

Maghram in Al-Tawbah 9:98 is expenditure regarded as a loss. In Al-Ṭūr 52:40 and Al-Qalam 68:46, it is a financial burden that demanded remuneration would impose on the audience.

Mughramūn in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:66 concerns crop destruction and loss, and should not be restricted without evidence to contractual borrowing.

Gharām in Al-Furqān 25:65 describes severe persistent punishment from which people seek protection.

The root cannot be reduced to “loan,” “loss,” “fine,” or “love.” Each belongs to a particular branch or use.

A defensible semantic centre is the binding and persistent character of a burden, obligation, or affliction: something incurred by or clinging to a person, whether financial debt and loss or severe suffering.

This centre clarifies the main connections but does not replace individual examination of every derivative and its Quranic context.
