The Qur’anic Root Q Ḍ B

Cutting and separating; qaḍb as a name for green vegetation

Qur’anic forms

قَضْبًا

Qaḍban

Green vegetation cut for food or fodder; sole occurrence, 80:28

The Arabic root Q Ḍ B denotes cutting and separating; the verb qaḍaba expresses the act of cutting. Besides its use as a verbal noun, qaḍb names certain kinds of vegetation. In ʿAbasa, qaḍban names plant produce, not the act of cutting. Its association with harvesting helps explain the word, but does not make every cuttable object an instance of qaḍb.

The following portion of a verse is attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period:

وَآزِلَةٍ قَضَبْتَ عِقَالَهَا

And a tethered she-camel whose hobble you cut.

Qaḍabta here refers to cutting a hobble. Ibn Barrī preferred the second-person reading, with a fatḥah on the final tāʾ, and explained the image as the camel gaining spacious pasture under the person praised. Another transmission uses the first person. Release for grazing is therefore a transmitted interpretation of the poetic image, rather than a securely established historical event.

The agricultural use also appears in poetry by Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods:

إِذَا أَرْوَوْا بِهَا زَرْعًا وَقَضْبًا / أَمَالُوهَا عَلَى خُورٍ طِوَالِ

When they had watered crops and fresh cut vegetation with those buckets, they turned them toward tall date palms.

In this passage, Labīd compares abundant tears to water from irrigation buckets. Qaḍb alongside crops and palms shows its use as a recognized name for a category of plant produce.

Labīd’s verse is transmitted in his collected poetry, and al-Aʿshā’s witness in lexical works. This study has not independently established the precise date of either composition. Attribution to Labīd does not by itself establish a pre-Qur’anic date. Later dictionaries record and explain these usages; they do not establish when each originated.

In another witness attributed to Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī, a pre-Islamic poet, camels worn down by travel are described as follows:

رَذَايَا كَالْبَلَايَا أَوْ / كَعِيدَانٍ مِنَ الْقَضْبِ

Worn-out camels, like camels abandoned at graves, or like slender sticks of qaḍb.

Radhāyā denotes camels worn down by travel; balāyā is explained lexically as camels tethered at graves. The comparison with sticks conveys emaciation. The witness shows qaḍb naming a plant that yields slender sticks; it does not establish that the plant in the poem and the Qur’anic referent are the same species. This study has not established the verse’s precise date.

Forms examined: قَضَبَ (qaḍaba), يَقْضِبُ (yaqḍibu), قَضْب (qaḍb), قَضْبَة (qaḍbah), قَضِيب (qaḍīb), قُضْبَان (quḍbān), قَاضِب (qāḍib), قَضَّبَ (qaḍḍaba), تَقْضِيب (taqḍīb), اِقْتَضَبَ (iqtaḍaba), اِقْتِضَاب (iqtiḍāb), اِنْقَضَبَ (inqaḍaba), and مَقْضَبَة (maqḍabah).

Qur’anic form: قَضْب (qaḍb).

1. The semantic core and its physical image

In Arabic, قَضَبَ الْغُصْنَ (qaḍaba al-ghuṣna) means to cut a branch, and قَضَّبَ الْكَرْمَ (qaḍḍaba al-karma) means to cut or prune vine branches.

قَضِيب (qaḍīb) denotes a branch, twig, or slender piece of wood taken from a tree or plant. One plural is قُضْبَان (quḍbān).

قَاضِب (qāḍib) describes something capable of cutting. A sharp sword can accordingly be described as سَيْفٌ قَاضِبٌ (sayfun qāḍibun).

اِقْتَضَبَ (iqtaḍaba) denotes cutting off or taking a portion, while اِنْقَضَبَ (inqaḍaba) denotes being cut or separated.

In agriculture, قَضْب (qaḍb) and قَضْبَة (qaḍbah) refer to vegetation whose fresh parts are cut for use. Fresh alfalfa is a recognized example in Arabic lexical usage.

مَقْضَبَة (maqḍabah) is also a name for ground where such vegetation grows.

When harvesting a crop such as alfalfa, a farmer cuts the stems and leaves while the roots remain in the ground and may regrow. This provides a clear illustration of the agricultural use of qaḍb.

Regrowth after cutting is nevertheless a feature of some referents, not an essential component of every derivative’s meaning.

2. Qaḍb in the Qur’an: fresh vegetation cut for use

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Sūrat ʿAbasa.

The Qur’an first invites the human being to consider food:

فَليَنظُرِ الإِنسٰنُ إِلىٰ طَعامِهِ

Fa-l-yanẓuri l-insānu ilā ṭaʿāmih.

So let the human being look at his food.

'Abasa 80:24

It then speaks of water pouring down, the earth splitting open, and produce being brought forth:

فَأَنبَتنا فيها حَبًّا

Fa-anbatnā fīhā ḥabbā.

Then We caused grain to grow in it,

'Abasa 80:27

وَعِنَبًا وَقَضبًا

Wa-ʿinaban wa-qaḍbā.

And grapes and green vegetation cut for food or fodder,

'Abasa 80:28

قَضْبًا (qaḍban) is an indefinite accusative noun coordinated with عِنَبًا (ʿinaban). Both name products of أَنْبَتْنَا (anbatnā, “We caused to grow”) in the preceding verse. The syntax and its position alongside grapes clarify the plant-noun usage; accusative case alone does not make a word a verbal noun.

Accordingly, “cutting” is not an appropriate translation here. “Green vegetation cut for food or fodder” names the produce. Cutting and harvesting explain its association with the root family; the verse does not state a human act of cutting.

Arabic lexical sources record two scopes for the agricultural usage:

A narrower sense, alfalfa and fresh fodder; and a broader sense, plants and greens cut and consumed while fresh.

The broader sense can include leeks, edible greens, and certain fodder plants.

Fresh dates have also been proposed in exegetical explanations. The separate mention of palms in the next verse does not by itself disprove that proposal: a fruit and its tree may be named separately. This study’s choice of green vegetation cut for use rests on agricultural usage and lexical explanations concerning fodder and greens.

The verse’s context alone does not determine a single botanical species with certainty. This study adopts the broader rendering “green vegetation cut for food or fodder.” Alfalfa and fresh fodder are recognized lexical examples, but this choice is not presented as the only possible interpretation.

Food for people and livestock

After naming various kinds of produce, the Qur’an says:

مَتٰعًا لَكُم وَلِأَنعٰمِكُم

Matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum.

As provision for you and your livestock.

'Abasa 80:32

This verse describes people and livestock benefiting from the preceding produce as a whole; it does not separately allocate each crop.

This ending does not establish that qaḍb must feed both people and livestock. Its range of referents must be assessed through the word’s own usage, rather than solely through the beneficiaries named in the final verse.

Across the produce as a whole, some plants feed people, some feed livestock, and some can serve both.

Feeding livestock also forms part of the provision of human livelihoods. Animals supplying milk, meat, wool, and other benefits themselves depend on plants and the earth’s produce.

3. Iqtiḍāb and the limits of non-Qur’anic evidence

Cutting and separating in this root are not confined to physical objects.

Classical Arabic dictionaries also record اِقْتَضَبَ الْكَلَامَ (iqtaḍaba al-kalāma) for speaking without prior preparation, or speaking extemporaneously.

Recording this meaning in later dictionaries does not by itself establish that it was current before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Just as a branch can be cut away from a tree, part of a discourse can be taken from a larger whole.

This image helps explain a nonphysical usage, but speaking extemporaneously is not the meaning of qaḍb in ʿAbasa.

Lexical witnesses also use قَضِيب (qaḍīb) for a branch or slender piece of wood. The physical explanation concerns that branch sense; modern industrial uses do not provide the basis for interpreting the Qur’anic word.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q Ḍ B (qaḍb) and A B B (abb)

These two roots occur close together in Sūrat ʿAbasa.

وَفٰكِهَةً وَأَبًّا

Wa-fākihatan wa-abbā.

And fruit and pasture vegetation,

'Abasa 80:31

In its plant sense, qaḍb is associated with cutting and harvesting fresh plant parts. Abb chiefly denotes pasture vegetation and fodder for livestock.

A comparison between vegetation available for grazing and fodder cut and carried to animals can help illustrate the distinction. This teaching example does not establish an absolute boundary between all the referents of the two words.

The distinction does not mean that qaḍb is always cultivated and abb always wild, or that qaḍb feeds only people while abb feeds only animals.

Cutting and harvesting are prominent in explaining qaḍb, and pasture and animal food in explaining abb. Their semantic fields nevertheless overlap.

Q Ḍ B (qaḍb) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭʿ)

Qaṭʿ has a broader semantic field: cutting, separating, and severing a connection.

Qaḍb belongs within this field, but cutting branches and stalks and harvesting plant parts are particularly prominent uses.

Cutting a branch is an instance of qaṭʿ. Breaking a covenant, severing family ties, or cutting off a route, however, is not necessarily expressed through qaḍb.

Q Ḍ B (qaḍb) and Q Ṭ F (qaṭf)

Qaṭf concerns picking and harvesting plant produce, especially fruit and clusters.

In al-Ḥāqqah 69:23 and al-Insān 76:14, the Qur’an uses قُطُوف (quṭūf) for the fruit available to be picked in the Garden.

Qaḍb is more closely associated with cutting and separating plant parts such as stems and branches; qaṭf emphasizes picking and taking the produce.

The two ideas can coincide in the practical work of harvesting crops.

Q Ḍ B (qaḍb) and N B T (nabāt)

Nabāt concerns growing and the emergence of plants; qaḍb concerns cutting and, as a noun, vegetation harvested for consumption.

In ʿAbasa 80:27 and ʿAbasa 80:28, N B T expresses causing growth, while qaḍb names one of the resulting products.

Nabāt covers the general field of vegetation, whereas agricultural qaḍb refers to a more specific category within it.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

In general Arabic, قَضَبَ (qaḍaba) can be rendered “cut” or “cut off,” and قَضِيب (qaḍīb) as “branch,” “twig,” or “rod.”

For قَضْبًا (qaḍban) in ʿAbasa 80:28, “green vegetation cut for food or fodder” is proposed. “Edible greens” emphasizes human food, while “fresh fodder” emphasizes livestock feed. The selected rendering preserves the broader scope without treating every cuttable plant as a certain referent.

The complete verse translation is:

“And grapes and green vegetation cut for food or fodder.” — ʿAbasa 80:28

Brief root meaning: Cutting and separating, particularly branches and stalks. Qur’anic sense of qaḍb: Green vegetation cut for food or fodder, according to the interpretation adopted in this study.

Conclusion

Q Ḍ B denotes cutting and separating. This verbal meaning must be distinguished from the use of qaḍb as a name for vegetation; a plant noun is not translated directly as the act of cutting.

The Qur’an uses this root only in ʿAbasa 80:28, among the products brought forth from the earth.

Qaḍb and abb can both include livestock food, but qaḍb highlights cutting and harvesting, while abb highlights pasture and animal nourishment.

“Green vegetation cut for food or fodder” is this study’s chosen rendering of qaḍb in the verse. Fresh alfalfa is a recognized example. A single species, or shared consumption by people and livestock, cannot be established from the passage’s closing statement alone.
