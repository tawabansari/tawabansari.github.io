Appearance, purity, and explicit speech: from a conspicuous building to an open courtyard in the Qur’an

Arabic Ṣ R Ḥ relates to appearing, being manifest, and purity. Within its family, ṣarīḥ and taṣrīḥ concern purity or explicit expression; architectural ṣarḥ denotes a conspicuous building or an open, level space.

The root occurs four times in three Qur’anic verses: twice in Al-Naml 27:44, and once each in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 and Ghāfir 40:36. All are instances of the noun ṣarḥ; no separate verb or speech-related derivative of this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four instances of the noun ṣarḥ in three verses

1. The semantic core in early Arabic

Appearing and emerging from concealment

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s semantic core as:

يَدُلُّ عَلَى ظُهُورِ الشَّيْءِ وَبُرُوزِهِ

yadullu ʿalā ẓuhūri al-shayʾi wa-burūzihi

That is, it indicates something appearing and becoming manifest.

Within this family, something may emerge from concealment, become free of admixture, or stand clearly before others.

This appears in various expressions. صَرَّحَ بِمَا فِي نَفْسِهِ means openly expressing what one has in mind. صَرُحَ الشَّيْءُ concerns something becoming clear or pure; الصَّرِيح describes something free of the relevant admixture or ambiguity.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records ṣarraḥa for making manifest, ṣarīḥ for pure or clear, and taṣrīḥ as the opposite of taʿrīḍ.

These meanings must nevertheless be distinguished from architectural ṣarḥ. Ibn Fāris tries to explain them through appearance and prominence, but that analysis alone does not establish a historical sequence deriving every form from one initial meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: appearance, prominence, and purity

Ṣarīḥ: pure and unmixed

Arabic ṣarīḥ describes something pure and clear.

Lexicons record لَبَنٌ صَرِيحٌ for milk whose foam has subsided or been removed, leaving the milk itself plainly visible.

كَأْسٌ صُرَاحٌ is also recorded for a cup of drink unmixed with something else.

Being ṣarīḥ here means being free of the admixture in question.

That property should not be turned into ritual purity, wholesomeness, or moral superiority. A substance can be unmixed while its health effects or moral assessment remain separate questions.

Early Arabic also used رَجُلٌ صَرِيحٌ and نَسَبٌ صَرِيحٌ for a lineage considered unmixed within that society’s genealogical classifications.

This reports language and social relations; it does not establish one lineage’s human superiority over another.

Taṣrīḥ: explicit expression rather than allusion

Arabic taṣrīḥ concerns expressing something openly.

Lexicons state:

التَّصْرِيحُ خِلَافُ التَّعْرِيضِ

al-taṣrīḥu khilāfu al-taʿrīḍi

That is, explicit expression contrasts with indirect or allusive expression.

When someone states a wish or opinion openly, one can say صَرَّحَ بِالأَمْرِ.

Raising the matter through hints, allusion, or an indirect phrase is a different manner of speaking.

Taṣrīḥ here concerns how something is expressed, rather than necessarily whether its content is true.

Someone may state the truth openly or express a lie explicitly. Frankness and truthfulness are therefore related but distinct concepts.

Indirect expression can also be wise and appropriate in a particular setting. The lexical meaning of taṣrīḥ does not make every explicit statement ethical or every allusion objectionable.

In modern administrative language, taṣrīḥ sometimes denotes an official declaration and, in some languages, a permit. Those specialized uses should not be imported into Qur’anic ṣarḥ.

2. Ṣarḥ: a conspicuous building and an open space

Arabic dictionaries record several closely related architectural uses of ṣarḥ.

It can designate a large, lofty building conspicuous in its surroundings, a palace, or a freestanding structure. صَرْحَةُ الدَّارِ also names the open, level, exposed space of a house.

The variety matters: ṣarḥ cannot always be translated solely as “tower” or solely as “palace.”

A lofty, conspicuous building

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الصَّرْحُ: بَيْتٌ وَاحِدٌ يُبْنَى مُنْفَرِدًا ضَخْمًا طَوِيلًا فِي السَّمَاءِ

al-ṣarḥu baytun wāḥidun yubnā munfaridan ḍakhman ṭawīlan fī al-samāʾi

Ṣarḥ can thus be a freestanding, substantial, lofty structure.

Another definition applies it to any high, elevated building.

Its plural is صُرُوح.

In this use, the building is conspicuous because of its size and height.

That fits Ibn Fāris’s explanation through appearance and prominence.

It does not establish that every ṣarḥ was taller than neighboring buildings or had a particular height.

Ṣarḥah: an open, level area

In Maʿānī al-Qurʾān wa-Iʿrābuhu, Al-Zajjāj explains that Arabic ṣarḥ can mean either a palace or a courtyard or open area.

صَرْحَةُ الدَّارِ denotes a house’s open, level space, exposed in contrast to covered areas or surrounding rooms.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains الصَّرْحَةُ مِنَ الأَرْضِ as an exposed, level stretch of ground.

The main feature here is openness and level, visible ground, rather than a building’s height.

Ṣarḥ may therefore refer to a conspicuous building or an associated open space.

Those uses need not conflict: a building complex may contain both a lofty structure and a level courtyard. They do not mean that every Arabic ṣarḥ necessarily has both features.

Must a ṣarḥ be made from one particular material?

Some lexical explanations apply ṣarḥ to a structure that is distinct or free from admixture.

The word alone, however, does not identify its construction material.

A ṣarḥ might be built from stone, mud brick, fired brick, or another material.

The Qur’an likewise identifies material and features through accompanying words: glass in Solomon’s account, and heating clay for construction in Pharaoh’s words.

Ṣarḥ therefore names a type of structure or architectural space, rather than a particular material.

3. Poetry and pre-Islamic language

Clarity and the removal of a drink’s foam: Al-Aʿshā

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, the familiar pre-Islamic poet, reads:

كُمَيْتٌ تَكَشَّفَ عَنْ حُمْرَةٍ
إِذَا صَرَّحَتْ بَعْدَ إِزْبَادِهَا

kumaytun takashshafa ʿan ḥumratin / idhā ṣarraḥat baʿda izbādihā

An approximate rendering: “A reddish drink whose red color becomes visible when, after foaming, its foam subsides.”

Some readings of the collected poetry have كُمَيْتًا instead of كُمَيْتٌ. That difference of case does not fundamentally affect the point at issue.

صَرَّحَتْ describes the drink’s foam clearing away so that its color appears.

Appearing here does not mean speaking: a substance’s feature becomes visible after a covering has cleared.

This witness attests the branch involving appearance and purity in early poetry.

Maqāyīs and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and its case variants

Drought becoming manifest: Salāmah ibn Jandal

The pre-Islamic poet Salāmah ibn Jandal describes his people:

قَوْمٌ إِذَا صَرَّحَتْ كَحْلٌ بُيُوتُهُمُ
عِزُّ الذَّلِيلِ وَمَأْوَى كُلِّ قُرْضُوبِ

qawmun idhā ṣarraḥat kaḥlun buyūtuhumu / ʿizzu al-dhalīli wa-maʾwā kulli qurḍūbi

Translation: “A people whose homes, when harsh drought becomes manifest, are the dignity and refuge of the vulnerable and a shelter for every person in need.”

Kaḥl names a year or period of severe drought here. Lexicons transmit other readings of the second hemistich, including مَأْوَى الضُّيُوفِ and مَأْوَى الضَّرِيكِ.

صَرَّحَتْ concerns the hardship becoming fully apparent.

The drought’s effects become so evident that the condition is no longer hidden or uncertain.

The witness shows that Ṣ R Ḥ can describe a condition becoming manifest even when that condition is unwelcome.

Frankness and becoming manifest therefore do not inherently carry positive value.

Al-Azminah wa-l-Amkinah: the ʿizz al-dhalīl reading in the drought witness

Ṣurūḥ in Abū Dhuʾayb Al-Hudhalī’s poetry

A verse attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb Al-Hudhalī, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

عَلَى طُرُقٍ كَنُحُورِ الظِّبَاءِ
تَحْسَبُ آرَامَهُنَّ الصُّرُوحَا

ʿalā ṭuruqin ka-nuḥūri al-ẓibāʾi / taḥsabu ārāmahunna al-ṣurūḥā

An approximate rendering: “On paths like gazelles’ necks, you take their stone landmarks for lofty buildings.”

Ṣurūḥ appears in an image of conspicuous buildings and landmarks. Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs transmit كَنُحُورِ الظِّبَاءِ; that reading is followed here.

The use relates to a lofty, conspicuous building.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived both before and after Islam. Without dating this particular poem, it should not be presented as definite evidence preceding revelation; it nevertheless helps establish the familiar early Arabic use of ṣurūḥ.

The witnesses illustrate two semantic directions: something emerging after concealment or ambiguity clears, and a prominent structure standing visibly in its surroundings.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Abū Dhuʾayb’s witness with ka-nuḥūr al-ẓibāʾ

4. Qur’anic uses

Al-Naml 27:44: ṣarḥ in Solomon and the ruler of Sheba’s account

قِيلَ لَهَا ادْخُلِي الصَّرْحَ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَنْ سَاقَيْهَا قَالَ إِنَّهُ صَرْحٌ مُمَرَّدٌ مِنْ قَوَارِيرَ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّي ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِي وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَانَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

qīla lahā udkhulī al-ṣarḥa fa-lammā raʾathu ḥasibathu lujjatan wa-kashafat ʿan sāqayhā qāla innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīra qālat rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī wa-aslamtu maʿa sulaymāna li-allāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīna

Translation: She was told: “Enter the ṣarḥ.” When she saw it, she supposed it to be a deep pool and uncovered her lower legs. He said: “It is a smooth, polished structure made of glass.” She said: “My Lord, I have wronged myself, and with Solomon I submit to The God, Lord of the worlds.”

Ṣarḥ occurs twice in this verse.

First, in the definite form:

ادْخُلِي الصَّرْحَ

udkhulī al-ṣarḥa

That is, enter that ṣarḥ.

Second, in the indefinite form:

إِنَّهُ صَرْحٌ مُمَرَّدٌ مِنْ قَوَارِيرَ

innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīra

That is, it is a smooth, polished ṣarḥ made of glass.

Both occurrences refer to the same structure. The first identifies a particular place the ruler of Sheba is to enter; the second explains its nature and features.

Is ṣarḥ here a palace or a glass courtyard?

Lexicons and commentaries offer two main explanations.

One takes ṣarḥ as a palace or large building.

The other takes it as a courtyard or open, level surface belonging to a building.

Abū ʿUbaydah and other lexicographers propose “palace.” Al-Zajjāj records both palace and courtyard as Arabic meanings.

In the verse itself, the ruler of Sheba sees the ṣarḥ and supposes it to be a lujjah: an expanse of water that would require wading through.

It is then explained as a ṣarḥ that is مُمَرَّدٌ مِنْ قَوَارِيرَ.

The smooth glass surface clearly matters in this context.

Ṣarḥ may refer to the building complex, but what she sees and mistakes for water is a smooth glass surface.

Restricting the translation to “lofty tower” is therefore unsuitable: tower height is not the main point in this incident.

Conversely, the word should not be restricted to “glass floor” alone, since its Arabic range extends beyond that particular instance.

What do mumarrad and qawārīr add?

مُمَرَّد belongs to M R D and here describes a smooth, even, polished surface.

قَوَارِير is the plural of qārūrah and here specifies the structure’s glass material.

Smoothness and being made of glass should therefore not become fixed components of ṣarḥ itself.

Ṣarḥ names the structure; mumarrad describes its surface; min qawārīr identifies the material.

The distinction matters: incorporating all these features into ṣarḥ would obscure its other uses in Pharaoh’s words.

Study of Q R R

Was there actually water under the glass?

Some exegetical reports describe water flowing beneath a glass courtyard and sometimes mention fish or other aquatic animals.

The Qur’anic verse itself, however, states only that the ruler saw the structure and supposed she faced a pool of water.

She is then told it is a smooth glass ṣarḥ.

The actual presence of water underneath, the number of animals, and the construction method are therefore not conclusively established by the verse’s own words.

Some reports also explain its construction as a test of the ruler, an examination of her limbs, or other purposes.

Those details are absent from the Qur’an itself and should not enter the lexical meaning of ṣarḥ or the verse’s translation.

The ṣarḥ and the ruler of Sheba’s acceptance of faith

At the verse’s end, she says:

رَبِّ إِنِّي ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِي وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَانَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī wa-aslamtu maʿa sulaymāna li-allāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīna

Her statement follows her encounter with Solomon and earlier events in the account.

Al-Naml 27:42 raises recognition of the throne; Al-Naml 27:43 describes her earlier worship.

Her entire change of position cannot therefore be reduced to seeing a glass surface.

The resemblance between the ṣarḥ and water can prompt reflection on initial perception versus reality. That reflection should not become part of ṣarḥ’s lexical definition.

The word directly names a structure or architectural space here, rather than truth, insight, or guidance.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38: ṣarḥ in Pharaoh’s command

وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ مَا عَلِمْتُ لَكُمْ مِنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرِي فَأَوْقِدْ لِي يَا هَامَانُ عَلَى الطِّينِ فَاجْعَلْ لِي صَرْحًا لَعَلِّي أَطَّلِعُ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِ مُوسَىٰ وَإِنِّي لَأَظُنُّهُ مِنَ الْكَاذِبِينَ

wa-qāla firʿawnu yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu mā ʿalimtu lakum min ilāhin ghayrī fa-awqid lī yā hāmānu ʿalā al-ṭīni fa-ijʿal lī ṣarḥan laʿallī aṭṭaliʿu ilā ilāhi mūsā wa-innī la-aẓunnuhu mina al-kādhibīna

Translation: Pharaoh said: “Chiefs, I know of no deity for you other than myself. So, Haman, kindle a fire for me upon the clay and make me a lofty building, that I might ascend to look toward the deity of Moses. I indeed consider him one of the liars.”

Ṣarḥan is an indefinite noun and object of ijʿal.

Pharaoh commands Haman to build him a ṣarḥ.

Before this he says:

فَأَوْقِدْ لِي يَا هَامَانُ عَلَى الطِّينِ

fa-awqid lī yā hāmānu ʿalā al-ṭīni

The phrase concerns heating clay.

Architecturally, this can be related to firing clay bricks and producing materials such as brick. The verse does not describe the full production process, dimensions, or building technique.

Pharaoh’s purpose follows:

لَعَلِّي أَطَّلِعُ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِ مُوسَىٰ

laʿallī aṭṭaliʿu ilā ilāhi mūsā

The phrase shows that he wants a structure whose height suits his stated purpose.

The ṣarḥ’s height and prominence therefore matter here.

Study of Ṭ L ʿ

Does ṣarḥ mean only a tower here?

“Lofty tower” can suitably convey the instance Pharaoh requests.

Ṣarḥ itself, however, is a broader Arabic name for a large, conspicuous structure.

Reading it as lofty fits the elevated-building sense and is reinforced by Pharaoh’s stated purpose.

That does not make every Qur’anic ṣarḥ a lofty tower: the courtyard and glass surface are prominent in Solomon’s account.

Moreover, Pharaoh issues a construction order. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 does not itself expressly report that the building was completed or specify its final height.

Pharaoh’s words and their place in the verse

Much of the verse reports Pharaoh’s speech.

He claims to know no deity for the people besides himself, then speaks of a building from which he might look toward the deity of Moses.

That claim should not be treated as an affirmed account of the spatial location of The God.

The Qur’an reports Pharaoh’s words in a context presenting his stance toward Moses and the divine invitation.

Ṣarḥ’s lexical meaning comes from architectural usage, while the purpose and claim belong to the speaker himself.

The word cannot establish a theory about the location of The God, reaching Him through construction, or the physical shape of heaven.

Al-Ṭabarī: firing clay, constructing a building, and Pharaoh’s claim

Ghāfir 40:36: ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا

وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ يَا هَامَانُ ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا لَعَلِّي أَبْلُغُ الْأَسْبَابَ

wa-qāla firʿawnu yā hāmānu ibni lī ṣarḥan laʿallī ablughu al-asbāba

Translation: Pharaoh said: “Haman, build me a lofty structure, that I might reach the ways.”

This expresses the same request through a different construction.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 said:

فَاجْعَلْ لِي صَرْحًا

fa-ijʿal lī ṣarḥan

Ghāfir 40:36 instead says:

ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا

ibni lī ṣarḥan

Ibni is an imperative from B N Y and refers to constructing a building.

Ṣarḥan is the command’s direct object.

The continuation says لَعَلِّي أَبْلُغُ الْأَسْبَابَ, and Ghāfir 40:37 further explains what Pharaoh means by these ways.

As in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38, the building’s height thus relates to his professed aim of reaching or looking toward heaven.

Asbāb belongs to another root; a way or means of reaching should not become part of ṣarḥ’s lexical definition.

Do the two verses describe two separate buildings?

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 and Ghāfir 40:36, Pharaoh addresses Haman, and both request a ṣarḥ for a similar purpose.

It is therefore natural to read them as related presentations of one episode.

The two verses do not determine the actual number of completed buildings, their dimensions, or the execution of the order.

Repetition of ṣarḥ does not prove that two separate towers were built.

Nor should accounts of a particular height, collapse, or Pharaoh’s experiments atop it be added to the Qur’an without evidence.

5. Important forms and linguistic points

Ṣarḥ and ṣurūḥ

Architectural ṣarḥ is a noun; its plural is ṣurūḥ.

Only the singular occurs in the Qur’an, though in definite and indefinite forms and different cases.

In Al-Naml 27:44, al-ṣarḥa is accusative and the object of udkhulī. In the same verse, ṣarḥun is the predicate of inna with its pronominal subject.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38, ṣarḥan is the object of ijʿal; in Ghāfir 40:36, it is the object of ibni.

Case and definiteness differences do not produce new, independent derivatives.

Ṣarḥah

Arabic ṣarḥah denotes an open area, exposed level ground, or a house’s courtyard.

It helps clarify the root’s architectural and spatial range.

The form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣarīḥ and ṣarāḥah

Ṣarīḥ describes something clear, pure, or unmixed.

Ṣarāḥah names the corresponding quality.

صَرُحَ الشَّيْءُ also refers to something becoming pure and clear.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an: ṣarīḥ should not be presented as a word appearing in the ṣarḥ verses.

Ṣarraḥa and taṣrīḥ

Ṣarraḥa is Form II and, in speech-related uses, means expressing something openly.

Taṣrīḥ is its verbal noun.

These forms are familiar in early Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣāraḥa and muṣāraḥah

صَارَحَ فُلَانًا means facing someone and speaking openly with that person.

Muṣāraḥah likewise concerns facing another and frank expression.

Being face to face and not concealing one’s words matter in this use.

Muṣāraḥah does not necessarily imply hostility, violence, or truthful content.

A clear, cloudless day

Arabic records يَوْمٌ مُصَرِّحٌ for a cloudless day or one with a clear sky.

صَرَّحَ النَّهَارُ is also recorded for daylight becoming clear after clouds depart.

This plainly relates to appearance and the removal of concealment.

It does not make Qur’anic ṣarḥ mean a clear sky or cloudless space.

6. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرْح) and B N Y (بَنَى)

Arabic banā concerns building and erecting a structure.

Qur’anic ṣarḥ names a kind of structure.

Ghāfir 40:36 brings both families into one construction:

ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا

ibni lī ṣarḥan

Ibni expresses the act of building; ṣarḥan identifies what is to be built.

Building is therefore not itself part of the noun ṣarḥ, even though the ṣarḥ in this context is a constructed object.

Nor is every building necessarily called a ṣarḥ. Its characteristic uses emphasize size, prominence, or spatial features.

Study of B N Y

Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرْح) and Q Ṣ R (قَصْر)

Arabic qaṣr is used for a palace or large building.

Ṣarḥ also means palace in some uses.

Ṣarḥ has an additional range, however, including a courtyard or open, level area.

The words overlap in some settings without being complete synonyms.

“Palace” in Al-Naml 27:44 may convey part of the meaning, but the smooth glass surface also makes the courtyard or open interior-space reading significant.

Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرْح) and B R J (بُرْج)

Arabic burj denotes a tower or lofty, conspicuous structure; the Qur’an also uses it in terrestrial and celestial contexts.

In Pharaoh’s words, ṣarḥ can denote a lofty building rendered “tower” in Persian.

That overlap does not make every use of the roots identical.

In Al-Naml 27:44, ṣarḥ is described through its glass surface, whereas the Persian equivalent of “tower” ordinarily suggests height.

“Tower” is thus more suitable for Pharaoh’s ṣarḥ than the one in Solomon’s account.

Study of B R J

Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرْح) and B Y N (بَانَ)

Arabic bāna relates to appearing and separating.

Ṣarraḥa likewise means making something manifest in speech-related use.

The families are close in this domain.

B Y N has a broader range involving expression, separation, clarification, and the relationship between things.

Ṣ R Ḥ also includes purity and architectural ṣarḥ.

Not every Qur’anic use of bayān should therefore be treated as taṣrīḥ or ṣarḥ.

Study of B Y N

Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرْح) and Ṣ D Ḥ (صَدَحَ)

Arabic ṣadaḥa concerns raising the voice and speaking out.

Some uses approach ṣarraḥa semantically, but the vocal branch is more prominent in Ṣ D Ḥ.

Taṣrīḥ concerns explicit and open expression; ṣadḥ may focus on a voice being heard loudly and clearly.

An explicit statement need not be delivered loudly.

Nor should ṣarḥ as a structure be confused with ṣadḥ as raising the voice.

Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرْح) and Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ)

Despite similar letters, the roots are separate.

Qur’anic Ṣ R Ḥ concerns a conspicuous building or architectural space.

Ṣ R KH concerns crying out, seeking help, and shouting.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, muṣrikh belongs to Ṣ R KH, rather than Ṣ R Ḥ.

Ṣarḥ should therefore not be rendered “shout,” nor taṣrīḥ restricted to shouting.

7. Challenges in understanding and translation

The main difficulty is the difference between architectural ṣarḥ and the clarity or explicitness expressed by ṣarīḥ and taṣrīḥ.

In present-day language, frankness may be the family’s first association.

All four Qur’anic occurrences, however, name a building or architectural space.

“Open declaration,” “clear truth,” or “explicit statement” would therefore be incorrect translations of the noun.

Conversely, the architectural meaning should not be restricted to a lofty tower.

In Pharaoh’s account, height matters through his stated purpose; in Solomon’s account, the smooth glass surface is prominent.

The Persian equivalent must therefore follow context.

Is ṣarḥ always a luxurious palace?

No.

Lexicons record palace and large building, but also an open courtyard or level surface within its semantic range.

Splendor and luxury may characterize some instances, but they are not essential components of the root’s meaning.

Is ṣarḥ always a symbol of political power?

The Qur’an mentions ṣarḥ in two accounts involving rulers and governmental power.

That association does not make the Arabic word itself mean political power or rulership.

A large building might display power or serve residential, ceremonial, or practical purposes.

Its purpose and social role come from context, rather than the noun alone.

Can the glass ṣarḥ be related to truth becoming clear?

The ruler of Sheba mistakes something for water; an explanation then reveals its architectural, glass nature.

This may prompt reflection on appearance and reality.

That reflection should not become a lexical claim that ṣarḥ means truth becoming manifest in the verse.

The word still names the structure; recognition of its reality comes from other statements in the account.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

In Al-Naml 27:44, for:

ادْخُلِي الصَّرْحَ

udkhulī al-ṣarḥa

Suggested rendering:

“Enter the ṣarḥ.”

Retaining ṣarḥ with a short explanation in the study can avoid prematurely restricting it to palace or tower.

If a Persian equivalent is required, “enter the splendid building” or “enter the architectural space” can also be considered. Splendor comes from the broader context, rather than the word alone.

For the second phrase in the same verse:

إِنَّهُ صَرْحٌ مُمَرَّدٌ مِنْ قَوَارِيرَ

innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīra

Suggested rendering:

“It is a smooth, polished structure made of glass.”

Here “structure” renders ṣarḥ, “smooth and polished” renders mumarrad, and “glass” renders qawārīr.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38:

فَاجْعَلْ لِي صَرْحًا

fa-ijʿal lī ṣarḥan

Suggested rendering:

“Then make me a lofty building.”

And in Ghāfir 40:36:

ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا

ibni lī ṣarḥan

Suggested rendering:

“Build me a lofty structure.”

Height in these verses is supported by the lexical use of ṣarḥ and Pharaoh’s stated purpose.

The study should nevertheless make clear that “lofty” appropriately describes these instances without restricting every ṣarḥ to a tower.

In non-Qur’anic uses, ṣarīḥ can be translated “clear,” “pure,” or “explicit.”

Speech-related taṣrīḥ means expressing openly; ṣarāḥah can mean clarity, frankness, or purity, depending on context.

9. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ R Ḥ relates to appearance, becoming manifest, and being conspicuous.

In the descriptive branch, ṣarīḥ concerns something clear or pure. In speech, taṣrīḥ contrasts open expression with allusion and indirect wording.

Architectural ṣarḥ may denote a large, conspicuous building, a palace, or a building’s open, level space.

The uses can be explained through appearance and prominence, but that resemblance should not become a definite historical derivational sequence.

Only ṣarḥ occurs in the Qur’an: twice in Solomon and the ruler of Sheba’s account, and twice in Pharaoh’s words.

The smooth glass surface matters in Solomon’s account; the building’s height and professed purpose are prominent in Pharaoh’s words.

Short definition: In its general branch, Ṣ R Ḥ concerns appearing, being conspicuous, and being pure or explicit. Architectural ṣarḥ names a prominent building or an open, visible space. Brief root meaning: Appearing and becoming manifest, purity, and explicit expression; sarh: a prominent building or an open, level space.

Qur’anic ṣarḥ should therefore not be translated as taṣrīḥ, nor must every instance be a lofty tower or palace.

The word’s basic meaning comes from linguistic usage; height, material, shape, purpose, and the building’s setting must be established through the other words and each verse’s context.
