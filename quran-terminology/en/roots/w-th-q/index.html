Foundational Meaning

The root W TH Q points to becoming firm, becoming reliable, and binding something in a way that is not loose or abandoned. Two layers are present together: a physical layer of binding, tightening, and restraining; and an ethical or legal layer of trust, reliability, and confidence.

When something is wathīq, it is not shaky, vague, weak, or baseless. One can rely on it. If it is speech, it is trustworthy. If it is a covenant, it has been made firm. If it is a bond, it is not easily cut. If it is a restraint, it is not slack or loose.

In the Qur'an, this root appears in several fields: mīthāq, mawthiq, wathāq, and al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā. All return to one core: something becomes firm enough that trust, commitment, and accountability can be built upon it.

Forms and Qur'anic Examples

The root W TH Q appears in several main forms. In form III, as in وَاثَقَكُمْ بِهِ (wāthaqakum bih), the meaning comes close to binding, fastening into obligation, and entering into firm mīthāq. In form IV, as in يُوثِقُ (yūthiqu), it concerns binding and tightening restraint. The noun مَوْثِق (mawthiq) points to a firm guarantee. مِيثَاق (mīthāq) is the most important Qur'anic form of the root: a covenant made firm and emphatic. وَثَاق (wathāq) is a firm bond or restraint, and وُثْقَى (wuthqā) means the firmest and most reliable.

In Al-Baqarah 2:27: الَّذِينَ يَنْقُضُونَ عَهْدَ اللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مِيثَاقِهِ (alladhīna yanquḍūna ʿahda Allāhi min baʿdi mīthāqih), those who break the covenant of The God after its mīthāq.

In Al-Baqarah 2:63: وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ (wa-idh akhadhnā mīthāqakum), when We took your mīthāq.

In Al-Baqarah 2:83: وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ (wa-idh akhadhnā mīthāqa banī Isrā'īl), when We took the mīthāq of the Children of Israel.

In Ali 'Imran 3:81: وَإِذْ أَخَذَ اللَّهُ مِيثَاقَ النَّبِيِّينَ (wa-idh akhadha Allāhu mīthāqa al-nabiyyīn), when The God took the mīthāq of the prophets.

In An-Nisa 4:21: وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا (wa-akhadhna minkum mīthāqan ghalīẓā), they took from you a heavy and firm mīthāq.

In Al-Ma'idah 5:7: وَمِيثَاقَهُ الَّذِي وَاثَقَكُمْ بِهِ (wa-mīthāqahu alladhī wāthaqakum bih), His mīthāq by which He bound you.

In Yusuf 12:66: حَتَّىٰ تُؤْتُونِ مَوْثِقًا مِنَ اللَّهِ (ḥattā tu'tūni mawthiqan mina Allāh), until you give me a firm guarantee from The God.

In Muhammad 47:4: فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ (fa-shuddū al-wathāq), then tighten the bond.

In Al-Baqarah 2:256: فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ (faqadi istamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati al-wuthqā), he has grasped the firmest handhold.

The same expression appears in Luqman 31:22: فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ (faqadi istamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati al-wuthqā).

ʿAhd and Mīthāq

One of the most important points in this root is the difference between ʿahd and mīthāq. In Al-Baqarah 2:27, the Qur'an does not only say that they break the covenant of The God. It says they break the covenant of The God after its mīthāq. That means an ʿahd existed; then that ʿahd was made firm, clear, confirmed, and binding; then it was broken.

ʿAhd is the original knowledge and commitment. Through ʿahd, the human being enters responsibility. It can refer to entrusting, charging, covenanting, or standing before a clear obligation.

Mīthāq is ʿahd made firm. It is the stage in which ʿahd moves beyond initial recognition or commitment and becomes a firm, clear, accountable obligation. From this angle, the relation between three roots becomes clear: ʿ H D is knowledge joined with obligation; W TH Q is the firm securing of that obligation; N Q Ḍ is the unraveling of that bond after firmness.

Mīthāq as a Covenant Made Reliable

Mīthāq comes from W TH Q. It should not be translated only as covenant if that erases its difference from ʿahd. Mīthāq is a covenant that has been made firm, secured, and reliable, so that a person cannot easily escape it through looseness, forgetfulness, or ambiguity.

In mīthāq, trust and obligation come together. A mīthāq is both reliable and responsibility-producing. Those addressed by it enter a condition in which their speech, faith, commitment, and action are measured by it.

This is why the Qur'an speaks about taking mīthāq. It is not merely a mental state. It is taken, carried, broken, and subject to being asked about.

Taking Mīthāq

In many verses, mīthāq appears with the verb أَخَذَ (akhadha): The God takes mīthāq, or mīthāq is taken from a group. This pairing matters. Taking shows that mīthāq places the human being in a responsible relation.

A mīthāq that has been taken is not just an idea in the mind. It is a commitment that encompasses the person and rests upon them. It can be understood as responsibility made firm: something by which a person becomes bound and committed.

In Al-Baqarah 2:63 and 2:93, taking mīthāq is joined with the raising of the mountain over the Children of Israel. This image shows the weight and seriousness of the commitment. In Ali 'Imran 3:81, The God takes the mīthāq of the prophets. This shows that even prophethood stands before The God within the field of mīthāq. In Ali 'Imran 3:187, mīthāq is joined to knowledge and disclosure: a truth was entrusted and must not be concealed.

Mīthāq Ghalīẓ

The expression مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا (mīthāqan ghalīẓā) shows that mīthāq can have degrees of weight and intensity. Ghalīẓ means heavy, firm, severe, and not light; something not thin, weak, or easy to sever.

In An-Nisa 4:21, the marital bond is described as mīthāq ghalīẓ. This shows that marriage in the Qur'an is not merely a bodily, financial, or social contract. It is a bond built on trust, safekeeping, dignity, and responsibility.

In An-Nisa 4:154 and Al-Ahzab 33:7, mīthāq ghalīẓ appears in the setting of great religious and prophetic responsibilities. The heaviness of mīthāq means that the obligation is not treated lightly, and breaking it is not the breach of a simple arrangement; it harms a weighty and foundational bond.

Breaking Mīthāq

W TH Q is directly related to N Q Ḍ. Mīthāq makes firm; naqḍ unravels. Mīthāq strengthens the bond; naqḍ opens that same bond apart.

In An-Nisa 4:155 and Al-Ma'idah 5:13, the Qur'an speaks about نَقْضِهِم مِيثَاقَهُمْ (naqḍihim mīthāqahum). This shows that mīthāq, despite its firmness, can be broken by the human being. Its firmness does not mean the human being is forced into loyalty. It means that if it is broken, the responsibility is heavier.

Breaking mīthāq is wrongdoing after clarity. A person stands before something made firm and clear. For this reason, breaking mīthāq can lead to hardness of heart, cutting of bonds, and corruption in the land.

Mīthāq and Cutting Bonds

In Al-Baqarah 2:27, breaking covenant after mīthāq is joined to cutting what The God commanded to be joined. The sequence is important: the covenant of The God exists; the covenant reaches mīthāq and becomes firm; the human being breaks it; the practical result is cutting bonds that should have remained joined; and this leads to corruption in the land and loss.

This verse shows that mīthāq is not merely an individual inner pact. When divine mīthāq is broken, ethical and social order is harmed. The human being is not only disloyal before The God; they cut the bonds of truth in the earth.

So mīthāq preserves connection. When mīthāq is broken, joining becomes cutting, reform becomes corruption, and moral capital becomes loss.

Mawthiq in the Story of Joseph

In the story of Joseph, Jacob asks his sons to give him مَوْثِقًا مِنَ اللَّهِ (mawthiqan mina Allāh) before taking Benjamin. This expression shows that mawthiq is not an ordinary promise. Jacob asks them for a firm, guaranteed commitment in relation to The God.

When they give their mawthiq, Jacob says: اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَا نَقُولُ وَكِيلٌ (Allāhu ʿalā mā naqūlu wakīl). This shows that mawthiq places human speech before The God. The promise is no longer merely between father and sons; The God is made wakīl over it.

This use clarifies the difference between an ordinary promise and a secured commitment. Mawthiq is speech one should be able to rely on, and for which the human being is accountable before The God.

Wathāq: Firm Binding

The form وَثَاق (wathāq) in the Qur'an points to a bond or restraint. In Muhammad 47:4, in the context of battle and captives, it says: فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ (fa-shuddū al-wathāq). Here wathāq has a more physical meaning: tighten the bond.

This use shows that W TH Q is not only about mental or ethical covenant. At its root, it is connected with firm binding and preventing unregulated release. The Qur'an then carries the same root from the physical field into the ethical and divine field: just as a bond can restrain a body, mīthāq makes responsibility and commitment firm.

In Al-Fajr 89:26, وَثَاق (wathāq) also appears in the setting of punishment and final binding. The meaning there is also intensity and firmness of restraint.

Al-ʿUrwah al-Wuthqā

One of the most beautiful uses of this root in the Qur'an is الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ (al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā). ʿUrwah means a handle, ring, or something a person grasps. Wuthqā means the firmest and most reliable.

In Al-Baqarah 2:256, after negating compulsion in dīn, the Qur'an says that whoever rejects ṭāghūt and has faith in The God has grasped al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā, a handhold that does not break. This is important. Firmness here does not come from compulsion. It comes from recognizing truth, rejecting ṭāghūt, and having faith in The God.

In Luqman 31:22, the one who submits his face to The God while doing iḥsān has grasped al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā. So al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā is the most stable point of reliance for the human being: a handhold reached through rejecting ṭāghūt, having faith in The God, submitting one's face to The God, and doing iḥsān.

Reliability and Trust

The root W TH Q is not only about hardness and intensity. It is also about trust. Something wathīq is reliable. Because mīthāq comes from this root, it is a covenant upon which trust can be built.

If ʿahd remains without wathāqah, it may remain at the level of claim or feeling. But when ʿahd becomes mīthāq, its responsibility becomes firm. A person can no longer easily say: I did not know, I did not understand, or the commitment was unclear.

In this sense, wathāqah gives weight to ʿahd. ʿAhd brings a person into responsibility; mīthāq makes that responsibility firm and accountable.

Difference from Nearby Roots

W TH Q is close to ʿ H D, but they are not the same. ʿAhd focuses on knowledge, entrusting, commitment, remembrance, and loyalty. Mīthāq, from W TH Q, is the stage where that ʿahd becomes firm, emphasized, secured, and reliable.

W TH Q is also close to ʿ Q D, but not identical. ʿAqd focuses more on knotting, binding, and forming the two sides of a bond or contract. W TH Q emphasizes firmness, confidence, reliability, and the secure quality of that bond.

W TH Q is related to SH D D, but not identical. SH D D focuses more on intensity, strength, hardness, and tightening. In فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ (fa-shuddū al-wathāq), shadd is the action of tightening, while wathāq is the bond or firm structure being tightened.

W TH Q is also meaningfully related to ʾ M N, but they are not the same. ʾAmn points more to safety, security, and calm from danger. W TH Q points more to reliability and firmness in something one can rely upon. Something wathīq creates the ground for confidence and security.

W TH Q stands opposite N Q Ḍ. W TH Q makes firm; N Q Ḍ unravels. Mīthāq strengthens; breaking mīthāq collapses that firmness from within.

Translation Suggestions

مِيثَاق (mīthāq): mīthāq, firm covenant, secured covenant, emphasized commitment, depending on context.

أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَكُمْ (akhadhnā mīthāqakum): We took your mīthāq; We brought you into a firm and binding commitment.

مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا (mīthāqan ghalīẓā): a heavy and firm mīthāq; a commitment not light or easily severed.

مَوْثِقًا مِنَ اللَّهِ (mawthiqan mina Allāh): a firm guarantee from The God; a guarantee bound in relation to The God.

فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ (fa-shuddū al-wathāq): tighten the bond; make the restraint firm.

الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ (al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā): the firm handhold; the firmest and most reliable grip.

Summary

The root W TH Q in the Qur'an points to firmness, trust, secure binding, and reliability. It appears at the physical level, as in wathāq and the tightening of bonds; and at the ethical and divine level, as in mīthāq and al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā.

W TH Q means becoming firm, being reliable, binding in a way that is not loose, restraining what should not be released without order, turning ʿahd into a firm obligation, creating a bond one can rely on, placing the human being before clear and firm responsibility, and providing a handhold that does not break.

In the Qur'an, mīthāq is ʿahd that has reached the stage of wathāqah. ʿAhd brings the human being into responsibility; mīthāq makes that responsibility firm and accountable. For this reason, breaking covenant after mīthāq is not a light error. It is breaking something after knowledge, confirmation, and firmness.

The root may be summarized this way: W TH Q means making a bond, word, covenant, or restraint firm and reliable. In Qur'anic usage, mīthāq is covenant made firm and secured; wathāq is firm binding; and al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā is the firmest handhold that the human being grasps through faith, rejection of ṭāghūt, submission to The God, and iḥsān.
