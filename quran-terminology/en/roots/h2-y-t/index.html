Root ه ي ت — Calling and inviting someone to approach

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَيْتَ لَكَ
(hayta laka)
Come to me; imperative verb-name

From a call to come closer to hayta laka in Joseph’s story

Arabic hayta calls someone to come or approach. The speaker asks the addressee to come toward them, move closer, or accept an invitation.

The Qur’an uses it once, in Joseph’s story. After seeking to entice him and closing the doors, the woman in whose house he lived says hayta laka: “Come to me.”

The invitation is sexual in context, but hayta is not lexically restricted to sexual relations. Arabic can use it elsewhere to invite someone to come or join a group.

Its grammatical formation and historical origin are debated. The common analysis calls it an imperative verb-name: an expression with a command’s meaning that does not conjugate like an ordinary verb. Verbal analyses and possible non-Arabic origins have also been proposed.

Its clear Qur’anic function must therefore be distinguished from recorded Arabic uses and hypotheses about its historical development.

1. Early Arabic meaning

Classical hayta laka and hīta laka mean “come,” “approach,” or “turn toward me.”

Hayta performs the call itself, attracting attention and inviting proximity or participation.

Hayyata bihi is also used for calling someone.

This relates to sound and address: hayta can be the call, while hayyata describes the act of calling someone to come.

Ibn Fāris centers his entry on sound and calling, explaining hayyata bihi as calling a person and hayta laka through halumma.

The semantic connection is understandable, but it does not prove every form arose from one original sound. Establishing origins requires independent evidence.

A verse attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah reads:

«لَيْسَ قَوْمِي بِالْأَبْعَدِينَ إِذَا مَا
قَالَ دَاعٍ مِنَ الْعَشِيرَةِ هَيْتُ»

Laysa qawmī bi-al-abʿadīna idhā mā / qāla dāʿin mina al-ʿashīrati haytu.

“My people do not remain distant when a caller from the clan says: Come!”

Haytu is the caller’s invitation to the community.

The verse does not specify sexual invitation, a request for money, or a particular military command. It concerns responding to a call from one’s clan.

Hayt therefore functions as a general invitation to come closer.

The verse appears in early linguistic transmissions and among additions to Ṭarafah’s collected poetry. Such inclusion and later transmission do not establish its exact date or certain attribution.

It is evidence from Arabic poetic tradition, but this exact verse cannot confidently be dated before revelation.

An early Islamic example

A poem addressed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib says:

«أَبْلِغْ أَمِيرَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ
أَخَا الْعِرَاقِ إِذَا أَتَيْتَا

Abligh amīra al-muʾminīna / akhā al-ʿIrāqi idhā ataytā.

أَنَّ الْعِرَاقَ وَأَهْلَهُ
عُنُقٌ إِلَيْكَ فَهَيْتَ هَيْتَا»

Anna al-ʿIrāqa wa-ahlahu / ʿunuqun ilayka fa-hayta haytā.

“When you reach the Commander of the Believers, the man of Iraq, convey that Iraq and its people turn toward you: so come, come!”

Hayta again calls someone to approach.

Its repetition strengthens the call.

The address to ʿAlī as Commander of the Believers places this after Islam’s emergence, not in the pre-Islamic period.

It illustrates continuity of the general invitation sense in early Islamic Arabic.

A defensible meaning for the period of revelation

The Qur’anic use, early linguistic accounts, and poetic evidence support hayta as a recognized invitation to approach.

Its initial origin remains uncertain.

It may have been common in early Arabic before its usage narrowed. Influence from neighboring languages has also been proposed.

The Qur’anic definition rests on its clear calling function, not a certain historical origin.

2. Grammar and forms

An imperative verb-name

Arabic grammar commonly classifies hayta as an imperative verb-name.

A verb-name conveys a verb’s meaning without behaving grammatically like an ordinary conjugated verb.

Halumma likewise functions as an imperative verb-name in one Arabic usage, meaning “come.”

Hayta occupies a similar functional field.

Its form can remain fixed across different addressees.

Arabic uses:

  • هَيْتَ لَكَ: addressing one man.
  • هَيْتَ لَكِ: addressing one woman.
  • هَيْتَ لَكُمَا: addressing two people.
  • هَيْتَ لَكُمْ: addressing a male or mixed group.
  • هَيْتَ لَكُنَّ: addressing a group of women.

Hayta stays unchanged; the pronoun attached to lām distinguishes the addressees.

As a verb-name, it thus lacks ordinary imperative conjugation.

Some modern morphological indexes list it as an imperative under ه ي ت. That identifies its imperative function without proving an ordinary historical triliteral verb.

Imperative meaning and morphological formation must be distinguished.

The function of laka

Laka comprises lām and a masculine singular addressee pronoun.

Hayta calls for approach; laka identifies the person addressed.

In Yūsuf 12:23, the woman directly addresses Joseph.

Lām should not be translated as ownership: she is not declaring something his property.

Laka alone also does not establish “I have prepared myself for you.” That interpretation relates to a different reading requiring separate analysis.

In hayta laka, the straightforward sense is invitation: “Come to me.”

The Arabic verb hayyata

Lexicons record hayyata bi-al-rajuli for calling a man.

Tahyīt, written تَهْيِيت, likewise concerns calling people.

Arabic also has hawwata for calling and summoning.

Lexical explanations relate hayyata and hawwata phonetically to reproducing calls.

The existence of hayyata does not make Qur’anic hayta necessarily that verb’s imperative.

As a verb-name, it has an established independent calling function.

Non-Qur’anic hayyata and tahyīt must not be added to the list of forms actually occurring in the Qur’an.

3. Readings and historical origin

Significant differences in reading hayta laka are recorded.

Some concern vowels alone; others introduce a hamzah and permit different morphological and semantic analyses.

Readings without hamzah

Recognized readings include:

هَيْتَ لَكَ

هِيتَ لَكَ

هَيْتُ لَكَ

Lexical accounts also record forms such as هَيْتِ لَكَ and هِيتُ لَكَ.

These retain the central invitation to approach.

A different final vowel does not necessarily introduce a new meaning.

Early grammarians offered explanations involving pronunciation, verb-name formation, and resemblance to other indeclinable words.

The final vowel alone does not establish differences between a courteous invitation, a forceful command, and a romantic request.

Such distinctions require more than that vowel.

Readings with hamzah

Some readings have هِئْتَ or هِئْتُ.

Some linguists analyze hiʾtu laka with final ḍammah as a verb meaning “I have made myself ready for you.”

That analysis connects it with ه ي أ and preparation.

Other early linguists rejected this derivation as inconsistent with the Arabic formation known to them.

Some hamzah readings are still analyzed as verb-names expressing invitation.

Hamzah alone therefore does not prove derivation from ه ي أ.

How a transmitted reading is pronounced and how it can be grammatically and semantically analyzed are separate questions.

“I have made myself ready for you” belongs to the reading tradition but should not silently replace the usual reading’s direct invitation.

Was hayt borrowed?

Accounts of its historical origin differ.

Some regard it as Arabic.

Others report usage in Ḥawrān followed by entry into Hijazi Arabic.

Old sources also associate it with Syriac, Coptic, and Hebrew.

These accounts are not identical and do not all have independent historical support.

Similarity across languages alone does not prove borrowing into Arabic.

Borrowing requires evidence of the source form, chronology, sound changes, and transmission route.

A lexicographer’s report alone cannot establish a certain Syriac, Coptic, or Ḥawrānī origin.

The word’s occurrence in the Arabic Qur’an and its clear calling function are certain.

4. Qur’anic usage: the woman’s invitation to Joseph

Yūsuf 12:23

«وَرَاوَدَتْهُ الَّتِي هُوَ فِي بَيْتِهَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ وَغَلَّقَتِ الْأَبْوَابَ وَقَالَتْ هَيْتَ لَكَ قَالَ مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ إِنَّهُ رَبِّي أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَايَ إِنَّهُ لَا يُفْلِحُ الظَّالِمُونَ»

Wa-rāwadat-hu allatī huwa fī baytihā ʿan nafsihi wa-ghallaqati al-abwāba wa-qālat hayta laka qāla maʿādha Allāhi innahu rabbī aḥsana mathwāya innahu lā yufliḥu al-ẓālimūn.

“The woman in whose house he was sought to entice him, closed the doors, and said: Come to me. He said: I seek refuge in The God! He is my Lord, who has treated my dwelling well. Wrongdoers do not prosper.”

Hayta laka occurs in a specific situation.

The woman had sought to entice Joseph, then closed the doors and called him.

This is not merely an everyday invitation to approach: it invites the action she has already sought.

Its sexual meaning follows the verse’s context, not the root ه ي ت alone.

Rāwadat-hu: seeking to entice Joseph

Rāwadat-hu belongs to ر و د.

Here she seeks to incline Joseph toward her desire.

ʿAn nafsihi clarifies the object of her request.

The verse identifies her desire before hayta laka.

In isolation, the phrase means “Come to me.”

With rāwadat-hu … ʿan nafsihi, its sexual direction becomes clear.

Lexical meaning and contextual application must be distinguished.

Closing the doors: arranging the circumstances

Ghallaqat expresses closing the doors.

It belongs to غ ل ق, not ه ي ت.

She closes them before saying hayta laka.

Her invitation has preparation behind it: she has acted to establish her desired situation.

Closing doors or preparing the setting is not part of hayta’s own meaning.

They are separate actions accompanying the invitation.

The verse specifies no number of doors. Adding one without Qur’anic evidence contributes nothing to the necessary lexical or contextual meaning.

Wa-qālat hayta laka: a direct call

The phrase follows her enticement and closure of the doors.

It is brief and direct.

She calls Joseph toward herself.

“Come to me” conveys it clearly.

Replacing it with “I have prepared myself for you” relies on a different morphological analysis.

“Come and have sexual relations with me” makes her contextual intention explicit but is not a direct translation of hayta itself.

The Qur’an uses a short invitation; preceding expressions establish its purpose.

Joseph’s response

Joseph replies:

«مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ»

Maʿādha Allāh.

He seeks refuge in The God.

The reply shows his refusal.

The translation above takes rabbī to refer to God. Another recognized interpretation understands his human master, who had treated him well. This concerns the pronoun and rabb, not the meaning of hayta; in either reading, the response rejects wrongdoing.

Her invitation and his response point in different directions.

She seeks compliance; he seeks refuge in God and refuses.

Inviting is not the same as having one’s invitation accepted.

Hayta laka states the call, not agreement or movement toward the speaker.

Yūsuf 12:25 describes both racing toward the door and his shirt torn from behind, further clarifying his refusal and attempt to leave.

Invitation and response are independent actions; neither should be inferred automatically from the other.

5. Related roots and expressions

ه ي ت and ه ل م: neighboring calls

Halumma invites approach or requests that someone or something be brought forward.

Hayta can likewise mean “come” or “approach.”

Some uses are close.

Halumma occurs twice in the Qur’an: al-Anʿām 6:150 requests witnesses; al-Aḥzāb 33:18 calls people toward the speakers.

Hayta occurs only in Joseph’s story, as a direct invitation toward the speaker.

Both can function as imperative verb-names without sharing one root or historical origin.

ه ي ت and ع ل و: inviting approach

Taʿāla also calls someone to come closer.

Hayta in Yūsuf 12:23 is close in direct meaning.

That does not make their roots identical.

Taʿāla is a recognized verbal form in ع ل و; Qur’anic hayta has a debated formation, commonly analyzed as a verb-name.

Similar meaning need not mean identical morphology or history.

ه ي ت and ه ي أ: calling and preparing

The comparison matters especially because of the readings of Yūsuf 12:23.

ه ي أ expresses both form and suitable preparation in the Qur’an.

Al-Kahf 18:10 uses hayyiʾ to request a right course.

The usual hayta in Yūsuf 12:23 invites someone to come.

Certain hamzah readings may connect with readiness, without making ه ي ت and ه ي أ universally one root.

Close spelling and pronunciation do not establish identical derivational histories.

The preparation evident from closed doors does not automatically change hayta’s lexical meaning to “I prepared.”

ه ي ت and د ع و: a call and the act of inviting

One branch of daʿā concerns calling someone.

Reporting an invitation differs from performing it.

“He invited someone” reports an action.

“Come” performs the call.

Hayta in Yūsuf 12:23 does the latter: the woman directly calls her addressee.

Forms of د ع و can report inviting, its object, and its addressee in various constructions.

The families are semantically close without always being grammatically interchangeable.

ه ي ت and ر و د: calling and seeking to persuade

Both occur in Yūsuf 12:23.

Rāwadat-hu describes her effort to win Joseph over.

Hayta laka expresses her direct call.

Murāwadah can be a process of requesting and persuading; hayta is a short invitation to approach.

They are not complete synonyms.

Packing all of rāwadat-hu into hayta laka erases the separate contribution of each word.

ه ي ت and ه ا ت: coming and bringing

Hāti requests that something be brought or presented.

Hātū burhānakum, for example, requests proof.

Hayta in Yūsuf 12:23 invites the addressee themselves to approach.

Their superficial resemblance does not give them identical functions.

Nor does similar sound prove one derives from the other.

6. Translation challenges and proposals

The key issue is distinguishing literal meaning from the speaker’s intention.

Literally, the phrase calls for approach.

Contextually, the woman seeks the relationship she has pursued and prepared for by closing the doors.

Translation should not confuse these levels.

Suggested translation for Forqan:

هَيْتَ لَكَ

“Come to me.”

This is brief, natural, and consistent with the usual reading.

“Come” can suffice within the full verse, which already identifies speaker, addressee, and setting.

“Come to me” makes the direction clearer.

“Make yourself ready” is not better supported than “come” for the usual reading.

“I have made myself ready for you” relies on a particular analysis of some hamzah readings and should not silently replace the base translation.

A lexical note may state:

“Hayta: an imperative verb-name calling someone to approach; here the woman invites Joseph toward herself.”

A contextual note can add:

“Her intention is to entice Joseph into sexual relations, as indicated by her preceding efforts and closing the doors.”

This preserves the lexical meaning without concealing the verse’s clear context.

7. Conclusion

Hayta calls someone to come or approach.

Its common analysis is an imperative verb-name, remaining fixed rather than conjugating normally. Following pronouns can distinguish addressees.

Lexicons record hayyata for calling, but that does not alone establish the Qur’anic form as its imperative.

Poetic examples also illustrate calling, though some have uncertain attribution and dating.

Arabic, Ḥawrānī, Syriac, Coptic, and Hebrew origin reports remain disputed and cannot establish a certain origin without independent linguistic examination.

The word occurs only in Yūsuf 12:23.

After seeking to entice Joseph and closing the doors, the woman calls him with hayta laka. He refuses and seeks refuge in God.

Final definition for Forqan: Qur’anic ه ي ت is represented by fixed hayta, inviting someone to approach. Hayta laka means “Come to me.” By itself it neither denotes sexual relations nor establishes acceptance. The speaker’s purpose and addressee’s response follow from context.

Distinctions from halumma, taʿāla, hāti, rāwada, and hayyaʾa must remain. They may approach one another in calling, requesting, or preparing but differ in grammar, objects, and history.

In Joseph’s story, the brief, direct hayta laka stands alongside her enticement, closed doors, and his response. The word calls for approach; the accompanying actions and circumstances establish the invitation’s purpose and moral character.
