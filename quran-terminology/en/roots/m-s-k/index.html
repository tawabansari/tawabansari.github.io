M S K in early Arabic and the Qur’an: grasping and preserving, adherence to the Book, retaining marriage, withholding gifts, and sustaining the heavens and earth

The three-letter Arabic root M S K concerns grasping, retaining, preserving, and preventing release or movement. Its words describe holding something in one’s hand, an animal retaining prey for its owner, withholding wealth, and adhering to a firm covenant.

These uses involve retaining something or preventing separation, release, or loss. Holding is not always physical. One may retain control of something, withhold payment, adhere to a statement, or maintain a relationship that could end.

Early Arabic uses مَسَكَ and أَمْسَكَ for grasping and retaining, تَمَسَّكَ and اِسْتَمْسَكَ for holding fast and adhering, and مُسْكَة for something that sustains or lends firmness.

مَسْك, “hide,” is also recorded in the family; a hide can serve as a container retaining its contents. مَسَك, “bracelets,” and مَسَكَة, a firm part of a well, have likewise been connected with this semantic field.

Alongside these stands مِسْك, fragrant musk, used in the Qur’an to describe a drink in the Garden. It requires separate treatment: sharing the letters of أَمْسَكَ does not establish a common historical origin.

The family occurs twenty-seven times in twenty-four Qur’anic verses, concerning retaining a spouse, adherence to the Book, catching prey, withholding provision or gifts, sustaining birds and the heavens, and fragrant musk.

It illustrates how a basic meaning can operate in different settings without every use sharing the same ethical value or referent.

1. Forms and core meaning

Important Arabic forms include:

  • مَسَكَ، يَمْسِكُ: Grasped or retained; sometimes, did not release.
  • مَسْك: Grasping and holding; also hide, especially one used to make a container.
  • أَمْسَكَ، يُمْسِكُ: Retained, restrained, preserved, or refrained from releasing.
  • إِمْسَاك: Retaining or withholding; sometimes refraining from giving.
  • مُمْسِك: A holder or restrainer; also one who withholds from others.
  • مُمْسَك: Something held or restrained.
  • مَسَّكَ and تَمْسِيك: Held firmly or held fast.
  • تَمَسَّكَ and تَمَسُّك: Held fast, refused to release, or adhered.
  • تَمَاسَكَ: Parts remained joined and firm; of a person, maintained composure or resisted collapse and loss of balance.
  • اِسْتَمْسَكَ and اِسْتِمْسَاك: Held fast and used something as a means of adherence and stability.
  • مُسْتَمْسِك: One holding fast without letting go.
  • مِسَاك, مَسِيك, and مُمْسِك: In some uses, someone miserly or reluctant to give.
  • مَسَكَة and مَسْكَة: A bracelet; in other usage, a firm part of a well or something sustaining stability.
  • مُسْكَة: Sustenance, strength, or something to rely upon or grasp.
  • مَسْك: Animal hide, especially as material for a container.
  • مِسْك: The familiar fragrant substance, musk.

Eight morphological and lexical categories occur in the Qur’an:

The form II verb يُمَسِّكُونَ; forms of the form IV verb; form X verbs; the verbal noun إِمْسَاك; the active participles مُمْسِك and مُمْسِكَات; the active participle مُسْتَمْسِكُون; and the noun مِسْك.

These forms should remain distinct.

For example, مُمْسِك is a holder, while مُمْسَك denotes what is held. The vowel on sīn distinguishes the active and passive participles.

Likewise, مِسْك, fragrant musk, differs from مَسْك, hide, although unvocalized writing makes them look identical.

The core: retaining and withholding

An early lexical explanation identifies holding or restraining something as the root’s core.

A person or other agent prevents something’s release, separation, movement, or loss.

For example:

أَمْسَكَ الشَّيْءَ بِيَدِهِ

amsaka al-shayʾa bi-yadihi

Translation: “He held the thing in his hand.”

Physical grasping is explicit here.

But in:

أَمْسَكَ عَنِ الْإِنْفَاقِ

amsaka ʿani al-infāqi

Translation: “He refrained from giving or spending.”

The wealth need not literally be in his fist. He retains it rather than transferring it to someone else.

Likewise:

اِسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعَهْدِ

istamsaka bi-al-ʿahdi

Translation: “He adhered to the covenant.”

The object is not physical; the person’s relationship to an accepted commitment continues.

The core can therefore extend from holding an object to maintaining a relationship, control, commitment, or condition.

Not every use, however, implies ethical protection or commendable loyalty.

Someone may retain something to protect a right or withhold it from its rightful owner. Both can be described with أَمْسَكَ.

Qur’anic usage makes this distinction clear.

2. Material and social uses in early Arabic

Hide and a container that retains liquid

مَسْك can denote animal hide.

Lexical explanations connect a hide made into a waterskin or other vessel with retaining its contents.

One early explanation relates this use to holding.

The connection is intelligible: a leather waterskin prevents its liquid from spilling out.

It does not prove that every historical name for hide arose from its use as a container.

Hide also serves as clothing, a ground covering, and other materials.

Persian mashk denotes a leather water container. It too should be distinguished from مِسْك, the fragrance.

Resemblance in writing does not make the meanings identical.

Bracelets retained on the wrist

Early Arabic مَسَك denotes bracelets made of ivory, horn, or similar materials.

مَسَك is recorded as a collective or plural designation, with مَسَكَة for one bracelet.

A bracelet encircles the hand or wrist and remains there.

One lexical explanation connects this with its being held in place on the arm.

A poetic witness: a bracelet-like mark

The Umayyad poet Jarīr describes a woman in this satirical line:

تَرَى الْعَبَسَ الْحَوْلِيَّ جَوْنًا بِكُوعِهَا
لَهَا مَسَكًا مِنْ غَيْرِ عَاجٍ وَلَا ذَبْلِ

tarā al-ʿabasa al-ḥawliyya jawnan bi-kūʿihā / lahā masakan min ghayri ʿājin wa-lā dhablin

Translation: “You see the year-old grime, dark at her wrist, forming bracelets for her neither of ivory nor of horn.” The image is satirical: old accumulated dirt resembles a bracelet.

مَسَك here concerns bracelets.

The poet compares a dark accumulation on the wrist to a bracelet.

عَبَس denotes dried accumulations of dung and similar matter on an animal’s hair; the poem applies that degrading image to the wrist.

This is a transmitted poetic witness. Jarīr belongs to the Umayyad period, so his line is not pre-Islamic evidence.

Its value here is the established use of مَسَك for bracelets.

Firm ground reached when digging a well

مَسَكَةُ الْبِئْرِ denotes a hard, cohesive place reached in digging a well, requiring no additional masonry lining. It should not be restricted to an already constructed wall.

The sense concerns firmness and retention of shape.

تَمَاسَكَ الشَّيْءُ similarly means that something’s components preserved their cohesion rather than separating.

A firm well surface and a person’s fidelity to a covenant are different. They share retention and stability, but their objects and relationships differ.

An early witness: holding fast to bonds

In a line attributed to Khālid ibn Zuhayr al-Hudhalī and addressed to Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid:

فَكُنْ مَعْقِلًا فِي قَوْمِكَ ابْنَ خُوَيْلِدٍ
وَمَسِّكْ بِأَسْبَابٍ أَضَاعَ رُعَاتُهَا

fa-kun maʿqilan fī qawmika ibna Khuwaylidin / wa-massik bi-asbābin aḍāʿa ruʿātuhā

Translation: “Be a refuge among your people, son of Khuwaylid, and hold fast to bonds whose guardians have neglected them.” مَعْقِلًا evokes a refuge or stronghold and also recalls the addressee’s name.

مَسِّكْ concerns holding fast without letting go.

أَسْبَاب can denote cords, means, or connections through which someone seeks support or establishes a relationship.

Holding fast here can therefore be understood socially, beyond gripping a physical object.

The line is attributed to Khālid ibn Zuhayr, an early Hudhalī poet. Accounts place the poetic exchange within a pre-Islamic dispute.

Its historical details and the transmitted sequence of verses nevertheless require independent examination.

What directly matters here is مَسِّكْ بِأَسْبَابٍ as holding fast to bonds.

Withholding as miserliness

مُمْسِك sometimes describes a miser.

إِمْسَاك can likewise mean refusing to give.

The connection is clear: the person keeps wealth instead of giving it to others.

Not all retaining is miserliness.

Someone may save for essential personal or family needs, preserve a trust for its owner, or refrain from funding wrongdoing.

None is reprehensible merely because it involves retention.

Miserliness is a particular use and judgment in the context of wealth and giving, not the permanent meaning of every derivative.

3. Qur’anic holding, retaining, and withholding

Al-Māʾidah 5:4: retaining prey for its owner

فَكُلُوا مِمَّا أَمْسَكْنَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ

fa-kulū mimmā amsakna ʿalaykum wa-udhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalayhi

Al-Māʾidah 5:4

Translation: “Eat of what they have held for you, and mention the name of The God over it.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:4

The passage concerns hunting with trained animals.

أَمْسَكْنَ is a feminine plural past-tense form IV verb.

The trained hunting animals are its subject in context.

Holding here means catching or retaining prey for their owner.

This clearly relates to the family’s physical sense.

It should not simply be rendered “killing”: catching and holding are not identical to killing.

The kind of hunting animal, its training, and food rulings depend on other parts of the verse.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:15: keeping in houses

فَإِنْ شَهِدُوا فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ فِي الْبُيُوتِ حَتَّىٰ يَتَوَفَّاهُنَّ الْمَوْتُ أَوْ يَجْعَلَ اللَّهُ لَهُنَّ سَبِيلًا

fa-in shahidū fa-amsikūhunna fī al-buyūti ḥattā yatawaffāhunna al-mawtu aw yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:15

Translation: “If they testify, keep them in the houses until death takes them, or The God provides a way for them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:15

The verb concerns keeping people in a specified place.

فِي الْبُيُوتِ identifies that place.

This differs from retaining prey or wealth: the objects are people whose situation the verse addresses.

أَوْ يَجْعَلَ اللَّهُ لَهُنَّ سَبِيلًا must remain part of interpreting the ruling’s scope.

The verb alone does not establish perpetual punishment, the reason for the ruling, or its alternatives.

Those questions require independent study of the verse, evidentiary conditions, related passages, and legal scope.

Al-Naḥl 16:59: retaining amid humiliation

أَيُمْسِكُهُ عَلَىٰ هُونٍ أَمْ يَدُسُّهُ فِي التُّرَابِ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَحْكُمُونَ

a-yumsikuhu ʿalā hūnin am yadussuhu fī al-turābi a-lā sāʾa mā yaḥkumūna

Al-Naḥl 16:59

Translation: “Should he keep it in humiliation or bury it in the earth? How evil is their judgment!”

The context describes someone told of a daughter’s birth.

أَيُمْسِكُهُ concerns retaining the child rather than abandoning her.

Humiliation comes from عَلَىٰ هُونٍ, not M S K itself.

The alternatives expose the person’s wrongful judgment; the question should not be taken as endorsing either abusive behavior.

Keeping a person can itself accompany oppression and humiliation.

Holding therefore does not by itself demonstrate affection, justice, or respect for rights.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:100: withholding for fear of spending

قُلْ لَوْ أَنْتُمْ تَمْلِكُونَ خَزَائِنَ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّي إِذًا لَأَمْسَكْتُمْ خَشْيَةَ الْإِنْفَاقِ وَكَانَ الْإِنْسَانُ قَتُورًا

qul law antum tamlikūna khazāʾina raḥmati rabbī idhan la-amsaktum khashyata al-infāqi wa-kāna al-insānu qatūran

Al-Isrāʾ 17:100

Translation: “Say: If you possessed the treasuries of my Lord’s mercy, you would still withhold for fear of spending; the human being is miserly.”

لَأَمْسَكْتُمْ concerns refraining from giving or spending.

خَشْيَةَ الْإِنْفَاقِ supplies the reason.

Here withholding is connected with narrowness and fear of losing wealth.

This does not make every saving or retention of property a case of the censured behavior.

The verse pictures reluctance to give even under the supposition of possessing the treasuries of mercy.

Avoiding waste and meeting genuine needs are different matters, considered in passages concerning spending and economic conduct.

Ṣād 38:39: giving or retaining a gift

هَٰذَا عَطَاؤُنَا فَامْنُنْ أَوْ أَمْسِكْ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

hādhā ʿaṭāʾunā fa-mnun aw amsik bi-ghayri ḥisābin

Ṣād 38:39

Translation: “This is Our gift: give or retain, without accounting.”

The verse occurs in Solomon’s story.

أَمْسِكْ is an imperative.

It contrasts with فَامْنُنْ, giving or bestowing.

The opposition makes retaining, rather than giving, the relevant sense.

Retaining is not condemned here.

The verb used in a context of miserliness in Al-Isrāʾ 17:100 here stands alongside permission to give.

Its ethical value thus depends on the setting, object, and conditions.

Al-Mulk 67:21: withholding provision

أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا الَّذِي يَرْزُقُكُمْ إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُ

amman hādhā alladhī yarzuqukum in amsaka rizqahu

Al-Mulk 67:21

Translation: “Or who is it that will provide for you if He withholds His provision?”

Holding here means withholding sustenance.

Neither a physical grasp nor maintaining a human relationship is the subject.

The concern is preventing provision or a gift from reaching its recipient.

The root alone does not specify the kind of provision, time of its withdrawal, or natural mechanism.

The verse concerns dependence on The God, while أَمْسَكَ names the act of withholding.

4. Retaining marriage: honorably or to cause harm

An important Qur’anic use concerns maintaining or continuing a marital bond.

Al-Baqarah 2:229: honorable retention

الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ

al-ṭalāqu marratāni fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

Translation: “Divorce is twice; then retention in a fitting manner or release with kindness.”

إِمْسَاك is a form IV verbal noun.

It contrasts with تَسْرِيح from S R Ḥ.

Here it concerns maintaining marriage under the conditions discussed.

بِمَعْرُوفٍ specifies its quality.

Retention does not itself mean honorable conduct; that qualification comes from مَعْرُوف.

Release likewise carries بِإِحْسَانٍ.

Both retaining and separating are thus framed by fitting and kind conduct.

Al-Baqarah 2:231: no retaining to cause harm

وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِتَعْتَدُوا

wa-idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-balaghna ajalahunna fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw sarriḥūhunna bi-maʿrūfin wa-lā tumsikūhunna ḍirāran li-taʿtadū

Al-Baqarah 2:231

Translation: “When you divorce women and they approach the end of their term, retain them honorably or release them honorably; do not retain them to cause harm and transgress.” The phrase concerning the term must be read within the possibility of retaining the marriage; the verb alone does not establish renewed retention after the period has ended.

M S K occurs twice in this verse.

First:

فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Baqarah 2:231

“Retain them honorably.”

Second:

وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا

wa-lā tumsikūhunna ḍirāran

Al-Baqarah 2:231

“Do not retain them to cause harm.”

Both retain the core meaning of holding.

One is qualified by honorable conduct; the other by harm and transgression.

Retention alone, without regard to motive and manner, is neither necessarily virtuous nor necessarily wrong.

Maintaining a relationship may involve reconciliation, respect for rights, and consent.

Conversely, retaining it may become a means of pressure, revenge, leaving someone in uncertainty, or causing injury.

The verse prohibits such retention.

Retention here does not lexically mean owning one’s spouse.

A spouse is a person with reciprocal rights and responsibilities; maintaining the relationship must not become an instrument of harm.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37: أَمْسِكْ عَلَيْكَ زَوْجَكَ

وَإِذْ تَقُولُ لِلَّذِي أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَأَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيْهِ أَمْسِكْ عَلَيْكَ زَوْجَكَ وَاتَّقِ اللَّهَ

wa-idh taqūlu li-alladhī anʿama Allāhu ʿalayhi wa-anʿamta ʿalayhi amsik ʿalayka zawjaka wa-ittaqi Allāha

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

Translation: “When you said to the one whom The God had favored and you had favored: Keep your wife with you and be mindful of The God.”

أَمْسِكْ is an imperative.

Its object is زَوْجَكَ, “your spouse.”

It refers to continuing the marriage rather than ending it.

It does not require every marriage to continue in every circumstance regardless of rights or harm.

This is a particular situation; general marital rulings require comparison with other Qur’anic passages.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:2: honorable retention or separation

فَإِذَا بَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ

fa-idhā balaghna ajalahunna fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw fāriqūhunna bi-maʿrūfin

Al-Ṭalāq 65:2

Translation: “When they approach the end of their term, retain them honorably or part from them honorably.” Here too the wording about the term must be read within its context, rather than used alone to establish retention after the term has ended.

The passage closely resembles Al-Baqarah 2:231.

Retention contrasts with separation.

Al-Baqarah used تَسْرِيح, while this verse uses فِرَاق.

That lexical difference should remain visible, although the words converge in the context of marital separation.

Retention still means continuing the relationship.

Its limits and manner are qualified by honorable conduct.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10: وَلَا تُمْسِكُوا بِعِصَمِ الْكَوَافِرِ

وَلَا تُمْسِكُوا بِعِصَمِ الْكَوَافِرِ وَاسْأَلُوا مَا أَنْفَقْتُمْ

wa-lā tumsikū bi-ʿiṣami al-kawāfiri wa-isʾalū mā anfaqtum

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10

Translation: “Do not hold to the marriage bonds of disbelieving women, and ask for what you have spent.”

تُمْسِكُوا is followed by the preposition بِـ.

Its object is عِصَم, plural of عِصْمَة, referring here to the bonds sustaining marriage.

The verb concerns adherence to the marital bonds under discussion, rather than physically holding a woman.

This distinction matters.

Other passages use أَمْسِكُوهُنَّ for maintaining the relationship with women.

Here عِصَم is the object, highlighting the relationship’s binding structure.

The context concerns migrating believing women, marriages, and groups with different religious affiliations.

A universal ruling about every marriage in every circumstance should not be derived without examining the audience, historical conditions, and related verses.

5. Holding fast to the Book

Al-Aʿrāf 7:170: adherence to the Book

وَالَّذِينَ يُمَسِّكُونَ بِالْكِتَابِ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ إِنَّا لَا نُضِيعُ أَجْرَ الْمُصْلِحِينَ

wa-alladhīna yumassikūna bi-al-kitābi wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta innā lā nuḍīʿu ajra al-muṣliḥīna

Al-Aʿrāf 7:170

Translation: “Those who hold firmly to the Book and establish prayer: We do not let the reformers’ reward go to waste.”

يُمَسِّكُونَ is form II.

It takes بِالْكِتَابِ.

Holding fast here means adhering to the Book, not merely keeping a physical copy in one’s hand or house.

The preceding verses discuss heirs of the Book and their conduct toward it.

Adherence therefore concerns a real relationship with its message and commitments.

Establishing prayer and the designation “reformers” appear in the same sentence.

This confirms that preserving an object’s outward form is not the subject.

“Reformer” is not M S K’s independent meaning. Reform and reward are expressed by other phrases.

Readings with and without a doubled sīn are transmitted. The Ḥafṣ wording used here is يُمَسِّكُونَ, with the doubled consonant.

The morphological variation does not fundamentally alter contextual adherence to the Book.

Al-Baqarah 2:256: grasping the firm handhold

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا

lā ikrāha fī al-dīni qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi fa-man yakfur bi-al-ṭāghūti wa-yuʾmin bi-Allāhi fa-qadi istamsaka bi-al-ʿurwati al-wuthqā lā infiṣāma lahā

Al-Baqarah 2:256

Translation: “There is no compulsion in religion. Right guidance has become clear from error. Whoever rejects false authority and believes in The God has grasped the firmest handhold, which cannot break.”

اِسْتَمْسَكَ is form X.

It takes بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ.

عُرْوَة denotes a handle or something that can be grasped.

وُثْقَىٰ belongs to W TH Q, concerning firmness and reliability.

The image is grasping a secure handhold.

The context places it within faith in The God and rejection of false authority.

No literal handle is being grasped; the physical image expresses adherence to a secure way.

No compulsion in religion and holding fast

The juxtaposition of rejecting compulsion and holding fast matters.

Faith and rejection of false authority are described after guidance has become distinguishable from error.

A person choosing that position is described as grasping a secure handhold.

Holding fast here concerns a real relationship to truth and faith.

The root itself does not mean faith or knowledge of truth.

Those concepts come from accompanying expressions.

The grasping image does not justify compelling others to believe; the verse opens by explicitly denying compulsion in religion.

Luqmān 31:22: directing oneself to The God and doing good

وَمَنْ يُسْلِمْ وَجْهَهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ وَإِلَى اللَّهِ عَاقِبَةُ الْأُمُورِ

wa-man yuslim wajhahu ilā Allāhi wa-huwa muḥsinun fa-qadi istamsaka bi-al-ʿurwati al-wuthqā wa-ilā Allāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūri

Luqmān 31:22

Translation: “Whoever submits his face to The God while doing good has grasped the firmest handhold; to The God belongs the outcome of all affairs.”

The same expression اِسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ appears.

Its preceding clause differs from Al-Baqarah 2:256.

Here the themes are submission of one’s face to The God and doing good.

Al-Baqarah mentions rejection of false authority and faith in The God.

Together they place grasping the firm handhold within faith, orientation toward The God, and good conduct.

The root still means holding fast and adhering.

Good conduct comes from وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ, not the verb’s formation.

Al-Zukhruf 43:43: adherence to revelation

فَاسْتَمْسِكْ بِالَّذِي أُوحِيَ إِلَيْكَ إِنَّكَ عَلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

fa-stamsik bi-alladhī ūḥiya ilayka innaka ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīmin

Al-Zukhruf 43:43

Translation: “Hold fast to what has been revealed to you; you are indeed on a straight path.”

فَاسْتَمْسِكْ is a singular form X imperative.

It addresses the Prophet.

بِالَّذِي أُوحِيَ إِلَيْكَ identifies what is grasped: what has been revealed to him.

The sense is maintaining adherence to revelation.

This exceeds physical preservation of written material: revelation guides conduct and orientation in this context.

Merely claiming adherence to revelation does not, by the verb’s meaning alone, establish freedom from error in every interpretation and action.

Sound understanding, practical fidelity, and the relationship of each interpretation to the text require separate examination.

Al-Zukhruf 43:21: a claim to adhere to a book

أَمْ آتَيْنَاهُمْ كِتَابًا مِنْ قَبْلِهِ فَهُمْ بِهِ مُسْتَمْسِكُونَ

am ātaynāhum kitāban min qablihi fa-hum bihi mustamsikūna

Al-Zukhruf 43:21

Translation: “Or did We give them a book before it, to which they hold fast?”

مُسْتَمْسِكُونَ is a plural form X active participle.

Unlike the earlier examples, the sentence is a question.

In a context challenging false beliefs and claims about The God, the Qur’an asks whether they possess an earlier book to which they adhere.

The verse does not endorse their claim.

Neither the verb nor the participle proves that what is held to is true or divine.

Someone may sincerely adhere to a false statement.

Here the very existence of the claimed scriptural basis is questioned.

Adherence is therefore not inherently virtuous; its value also depends on what is being grasped.

6. Sustaining, withholding, and preserving creation

Several verses attribute holding or withholding to The God.

They concern sustaining birds and the heavens and earth, withholding provision and mercy, and retaining the soul at death.

Al-Naḥl 16:79 and Al-Mulk 67:19: birds in the air

Al-Naḥl 16:79 says:

أَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ مُسَخَّرَاتٍ فِي جَوِّ السَّمَاءِ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا اللَّهُ

a-lam yaraw ilā al-ṭayri musakhkharātin fī jawwi al-samāʾi mā yumsikuhunna illā Allāhu

Al-Naḥl 16:79

Translation: “Have they not observed the birds made subject in the space of the sky? None holds them up except The God.”

Al-Mulk 67:19 likewise says:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ فَوْقَهُمْ صَافَّاتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا الرَّحْمَٰنُ

a-wa-lam yaraw ilā al-ṭayri fawqahum ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna mā yumsikuhunna illā al-Raḥmānu

Al-Mulk 67:19

Translation: “Have they not observed the birds above them, spreading and folding their wings? None holds them up except the Entirely Merciful.”

Both passages apply holding to birds in the air.

It means sustaining them, preventing their fall or loss of flight.

The root does not imply a physical hand holding birds aloft.

Nor does its literal meaning specify the natural laws of flight, wing shape, or airflow.

The Qur’an presents flight within divine power and governance.

Natural science may investigate its mechanisms, but those mechanisms do not replace the verse’s lexical meaning.

Al-Ḥajj 22:65: keeping the sky from falling

وَيُمْسِكُ السَّمَاءَ أَنْ تَقَعَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ

wa-yumsiku al-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍi illā bi-idhnihi

Al-Ḥajj 22:65

Translation: “He holds the sky lest it fall upon the earth, except by His permission.”

Holding is qualified by preventing a particular occurrence:

أَنْ تَقَعَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ

an taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍi

Al-Ḥajj 22:65

“That it fall upon the earth.”

The verb can therefore express preventing a condition from occurring, beyond retaining possession of something.

The verse alone does not identify the sky’s physical structure, the kinds of bodies involved, or the mechanism sustaining them.

سَمَاء has several Qur’anic uses; its referent requires contextual and intertextual study.

Fāṭir 35:41: preventing the heavens and earth from passing away

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُمْسِكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ أَنْ تَزُولَا وَلَئِنْ زَالَتَا إِنْ أَمْسَكَهُمَا مِنْ أَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ

inna Allāha yumsiku al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa an tazūlā wa-la-in zālatā in amsakahumā min aḥadin min baʿdihi

Fāṭir 35:41

Translation: “The God holds the heavens and earth lest they pass away; if they were to pass away, no one after Him could hold them.”

M S K occurs twice.

The first concerns keeping the heavens and earth from passing away.

The second concerns everyone else’s inability to sustain them if that happens.

Holding relates to maintaining a condition and preventing disintegration or departure.

Zawāl belongs to Z W L and requires separate study.

Holding does not itself imply absolute stillness.

Something can move while maintaining order and stability.

Sustaining the heavens and earth therefore does not imply that every body is motionless or nature undergoes no change.

Al-Zumar 39:42: retaining one soul and sending another

اللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى الْأَنْفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا وَالَّتِي لَمْ تَمُتْ فِي مَنَامِهَا فَيُمْسِكُ الَّتِي قَضَىٰ عَلَيْهَا الْمَوْتَ وَيُرْسِلُ الْأُخْرَىٰ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى

Allāhu yatawaffā al-anfusa ḥīna mawtihā wa-allatī lam tamut fī manāmihā fa-yumsiku allatī qaḍā ʿalayhā al-mawta wa-yursilu al-ukhrā ilā ajalin musamman

Al-Zumar 39:42

Translation: “The God takes souls fully at their death, and those that have not died during their sleep. He retains those for whom He has decreed death and sends the others until an appointed term.”

فَيُمْسِكُ contrasts with وَيُرْسِلُ.

Holding concerns retention.

Sending concerns dispatch or release.

The contrast helps define the root.

Retention does not identify where the soul is held or the nature of the soul.

The verse alone does not conclusively explain the physical structure of sleep or death, or how soul and body relate.

The sentence distinguishes retaining those for whom death is decreed from sending others until a specified term.

Fāṭir 35:2: withholding mercy

مَا يَفْتَحِ اللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِنْ رَحْمَةٍ فَلَا مُمْسِكَ لَهَا وَمَا يُمْسِكْ فَلَا مُرْسِلَ لَهُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ

mā yaftaḥi Allāhu li-al-nāsi min raḥmatin fa-lā mumsika lahā wa-mā yumsik fa-lā mursila lahu min baʿdihi

Fāṭir 35:2

Translation: “Whatever mercy The God opens for people, none can withhold; whatever He withholds, none can send after Him.”

مُمْسِك and يُمْسِك belong to the same family.

The former is an active participle naming a restrainer.

The latter is a verb expressing withholding.

Opening and sending stand opposite withholding.

Here the sense is preventing a gift or mercy from reaching its recipient, not gripping a physical object.

Mercy needs study across relevant verses; the root does not restrict it to rain, wealth, or one particular material benefit.

Al-Zumar 39:38: false deities cannot withhold mercy

أَوْ أَرَادَنِي بِرَحْمَةٍ هَلْ هُنَّ مُمْسِكَاتُ رَحْمَتِهِ

aw arādanī bi-raḥmatin hal hunna mumsikātu raḥmatihi

Al-Zumar 39:38

Translation: “Or if He intends mercy for me, can they withhold His mercy?”

مُمْسِكَات is a feminine plural active participle.

The pronoun refers to the false objects of worship questioned earlier in the verse.

The Qur’an challenges their independent ability to prevent divine mercy.

Holding itself does not mean association of partners, divinity, or power.

It means withholding; the questioned deities’ inability comes from the context.

The passage has a clear semantic relationship to Fāṭir 35:2.

What these creation and mercy passages show

Holding sometimes maintains a condition and prevents falling or passing away; elsewhere it withholds a gift from reaching someone.

Both relate to retention.

Their difference must remain visible.

Sustaining a bird in flight, retaining a soul, and withholding provision are three distinct uses.

A fixed rendering such as “imprisoning” is therefore unsuitable for all these verses.

7. مِسْك: fragrant musk, not holding

خِتَامُهُ مِسْكٌ وَفِي ذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَتَنَافَسِ الْمُتَنَافِسُونَ

khitāmuhu miskun wa-fī dhālika fa-l-yatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūna

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

Translation: “Its ending or seal is musk; for that let those who compete compete.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

This is the Qur’an’s sole occurrence of مِسْك as the fragrant substance.

The preceding verses describe a pure, sealed drink.

Then comes:

خِتَامُهُ مِسْكٌ

khitāmuhu miskun

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

“Its ending or seal is musk.”

The meaning of مِسْك

مِسْك names the familiar fragrance.

Persian also has mushk.

Traditionally, musk was an aromatic substance obtained from an animal.

The verse itself does not identify its natural source, production method, or animal species.

The direct translation should therefore retain “musk.”

Is musk derived from grasping and retaining?

Arabic dictionaries register مِسْك under the letters M S K.

Some early lexical analyses treat it separately from the core meaning of retaining.

Another early account identifies fragrant مِسْك as an Arabic adaptation of a Persian word.

That account has a notable connection with Persian mushk.

The precise route of borrowing and earliest attestations require independent historical linguistic investigation.

For Qur’anic study, مِسْك in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26 denotes a fragrance and must not be translated as grasping or retaining.

Shared letters do not transfer every property of مَسْك to مِسْك or vice versa.

خِتَامُهُ مِسْكٌ: a fragrant seal or ending?

Two principal explanations are transmitted.

One takes the vessel’s seal or closure to be musk.

The other understands the drink’s final taste or scent: drinking ends with a pleasant musk-like aroma or flavor.

The disagreement concerns خِتَام from KH T M, not مِسْك itself.

Both readings retain musk as a fragrance.

M S K study can clarify musk, while determining خِتَام precisely requires separate study of KH T M and the verse’s context.

8. Comparison with related roots

M S K and Q B Ḍ

Qabḍ concerns grasping, gathering, or taking possession.

M S K can also mean grasping and holding.

Qabḍ often emphasizes the act of taking or gathering, while imsāk can highlight continued retention or prevention of release.

Someone can grasp something and then release it.

Imsāk can instead emphasize continuing to hold it.

This is a tendency, and the families overlap in some settings.

M S K and Ḥ B S

Ḥabs concerns restraint, limited movement, and keeping something in a condition.

Imsāk can operate similarly, as in keeping people in houses in Al-Nisāʾ 4:15.

Not every use is confinement of a person.

Honorable marital retention, adherence to the Book, sustaining the heavens, and withholding provision are different contexts.

“Confinement” is not a suitable universal translation.

M S K and M N ʿ

Manʿ concerns preventing something from arriving or happening.

Imsāk performs that role in passages such as Fāṭir 35:2 and Al-Mulk 67:21.

But it does not always mean prevention.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 concerns maintaining a relationship honorably.

Al-Māʾidah 5:4 concerns retaining prey.

Prevention is one use within the family, not the whole field.

M S K and ʿ Ṣ M

ʿṢM concerns holding, protection, and preventing separation or harm.

This approaches istimsāk.

In Al-Baqarah 2:256, a person grasps a firm handhold.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10 uses عِصَم for marital bonds.

The roots remain independent.

Istimsāk can describe grasping, while ʿṢM can name a bond or means preventing separation or injury.

The aspects can coexist in one sentence without becoming identical.

M S K and W TH Q

WTHQ concerns firmness, trust, and security.

الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:256 and Luqmān 31:22.

Istimsāk expresses grasping the handhold.

Wuthqā expresses that handhold’s firmness.

Grasping does not itself establish that the object is secure.

Someone may grasp something fragile.

In these verses, firmness comes from the adjective and the setting of faith and good conduct.

M S K and S R Ḥ

These stand opposite each other in divorce passages.

Imsāk retains the relationship.

Tasrīḥ releases or separates.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 qualifies retention with fitting conduct and release with kindness.

Al-Baqarah 2:231 qualifies both with fitting conduct.

The contrast does not make retaining invariably better than releasing.

In some circumstances continuing may be fitting; in others, separation with fairness and kindness.

Judgment depends on conditions and the parties’ rights, not an unchanging moral valuation of the root.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

M S K cannot take one fixed Persian equivalent everywhere.

Physical contexts permit “grasped,” “held,” or “restrained.”

Wealth contexts may call for “refrained from giving” or “kept for oneself.”

Marriage contexts favor “maintaining the relationship” or “continuing the marital bond.”

Tamsīk and istimsāk can be “adhered firmly” or “held fast,” according to the image.

Heaven and earth passages permit “sustained” or “prevented from falling or passing away”; the latter details must come from the accompanying clause.

مِسْك in Al-Muṭaffifīn is “fragrant musk.”

Brief root meaning

Grasping and retaining; preventing release, separation, movement, or loss. Extended uses include adherence and holding fast, withholding a gift, and maintaining a condition.

This defines the principal branch. Fragrant مِسْك requires its independent meaning.

Meanings in Qur’anic contexts

Al-Baqarah 2:231: Retaining women within marriage, first honorably and then in the prohibition against harmful retention.

Al-Baqarah 2:256: Grasping the firm handhold, in faith in The God and rejection of false authority.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:15: Keeping people in houses.

Al-Māʾidah 5:4: Catching and retaining prey for the trained animal’s owner.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:170: Firm adherence to the Book.

Al-Naḥl 16:59: Keeping a child within a situation described as humiliating.

Al-Naḥl 16:79 and Al-Mulk 67:19: Sustaining birds in the air.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:100: Withholding wealth for fear of spending.

Al-Ḥajj 22:65: Keeping the sky from falling upon the earth.

Luqmān 31:22: Grasping the firm handhold while submitting one’s face to The God and doing good.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37: Continuing the marriage.

Fāṭir 35:2: Withholding mercy, emphasizing others’ inability to obstruct divine giving.

Fāṭir 35:41: Sustaining the heavens and earth against passing away.

Ṣād 38:39: Retaining a gift rather than giving it.

Al-Zumar 39:38: Withholding divine mercy, in a question challenging false deities’ ability.

10. Summary

The principal M S K field concerns grasping, holding, and preventing release or loss.

It appears concretely in retaining prey, containing liquid, and a bracelet remaining on the wrist.

Extended uses include keeping wealth, withholding gifts, maintaining marriage, and preserving a condition.

Tamsīk and istimsāk express holding fast and adhering.

The Qur’an applies the family to both commendable and reprehensible conduct.

Honorable marital retention in Al-Baqarah 2:229 is commendable, while harmful retention is prohibited in Al-Baqarah 2:231.

Withholding for fear of spending in Al-Isrāʾ 17:100 relates to miserliness. In Ṣād 38:39, retaining contrasts with giving without retention itself being condemned.

Book-related uses concern meaningful adherence, not merely storing written objects. Grasping itself does not prove virtue; its object and validity matter.

Creation passages concern sustaining birds and the heavens and earth, withholding mercy and provision, and retaining the soul. They express preservation and restraint without independently specifying physical mechanisms or the soul’s nature.

Fragrant مِسْك stands alongside that principal branch. Early accounts distinguish it from holding, and some report Persian origin. Grasping and preservation should not be imposed upon it.

Final definition: In its principal branch, M S K concerns grasping and retaining, preventing release or separation, and maintaining a condition or relationship. Human uses include adherence, preserving a bond, and refraining from giving. Creation and mercy passages emphasize sustaining and withholding. Ethical value depends on the object, motive, and circumstances. Fragrant مِسْك is registered under the same written letters but has an independent meaning and should not be conflated with the other derivatives.
