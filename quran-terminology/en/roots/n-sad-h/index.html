Sincere goodwill and purity: truthfulness, freedom from deception, prophetic counsel, false claims of goodwill, and sincere repentance

1. Introducing the root and its semantic centers

Arabic N Ṣ Ḥ concerns goodwill, truthfulness, purity, and freedom from deception. In human relations, naṣaḥa lahu describes sincerely seeking another person’s welfare without intending to deceive.

This may involve speaking, warning, and guiding, but is not confined to verbal advice. Responsible conduct, caring for someone, preventing harm, and properly fulfilling a duty can also demonstrate goodwill.

Another application, naṣaḥa al-shayʾu, describes something becoming pure and unadulterated. Early Arabic ʿasalun nāṣiḥun, for example, describes pure honey.

A further branch concerns sewing and joining pieces of cloth. Naṣaḥa al-thawba means sewing a garment; al-niṣāḥ names sewing thread.

Connections between the applications are conceivable: purity contrasts with adulteration, goodwill with deception, and sewing with separation.

These connections do not establish a certain historical origin for all branches. Early lexical explanations themselves offer differing accounts of the root’s central meaning.

Qur’anic uses chiefly concern goodwill and truthfulness, but a crucial distinction applies: someone who calls himself nāṣiḥ is not necessarily a genuine well-wisher.

Satan claims to be a well-wisher in Adam’s story, and Joseph’s brothers make the same claim. By contrast, the prophets and the person warning Moses of danger illustrate genuine goodwill.

The word’s meaning, a person’s claim, and the truth of that claim should therefore remain distinct.

Tawbatan naṣūḥan also concerns a genuine, sincere return, rather than advising someone else.

Thus, purity and goodwill free of deceit form an important shared axis of Qur’anic N Ṣ Ḥ, while verbal guidance, practical care, and genuine repentance are different applications.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:79 and Hūd 11:34 each contain the root twice, so occurrences outnumber verses.

The familiar noun naṣīḥah does not occur in the Qur’an, although the verb and active participle nāṣiḥ do.

Arabic also has naṣīḥ, nuṣaḥāʾ, niṣāḥ, manṣūḥ, minṣaḥah, intaṣaḥa, and istanṣaḥa. These do not occur in the Qur’an.

All Qur’anic verbs in this family are Form I. Other forms recorded in dictionaries should not be counted as Qur’anic merely because they exist.

2. Early Arabic uses: purity, goodwill, and sewing

Naṣaḥa al-shayʾu: becoming pure

A lexical explanation states:

نَصَحَ الشَّيْءُ: خَلَصَ

naṣaḥa al-shayʾu: khalaṣa

Meaning:

“Something became pure.”

Also:

وَالنَّاصِحُ: الْخَالِصُ مِنَ الْعَسَلِ وَغَيْرِهِ

wa-al-nāṣiḥu: al-khāliṣu mina al-ʿasali wa-ghayrihi

Meaning:

“Nāṣiḥ describes what is pure, including honey and other things.”

Arabic nāṣiḥ therefore does not always describe a person giving advice.

A substance may be called nāṣiḥ because it is pure and unadulterated.

In human relations, the concept connects with freedom from deception or impurity of intention and conduct.

Material purity and human moral sincerity are nevertheless not identical.

Pure honey has no moral intention or will, whereas a sincere person makes decisions affecting others’ welfare.

Material purity is a property of the substance; interpersonal goodwill concerns a person’s intentions and conduct.

A poetic example from Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah

A verse by Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah al-Hudhalī reads:

فَأَزَالَ مُفْرِطَهَا بِأَبْيَضَ نَاصِحٍ
مِنْ مَاءِ أَلْهَابٍ بِهِنَّ التَّأْلَبُ

fa-azāla mufriṭahā bi-abyaḍa nāṣiḥin / min māʾi alhābin bihinna al-taʾlabu

Translation of the cited phrase: “With something clear and pure ... from water in mountain clefts where taʾlab is found.” Details of the image and the referent of nāṣiḥ depend on the verse’s transmitted wording and explanation.

Lexical works transmit this verse to illustrate nāṣiḥ as pure.

Explanations differ over which part of the image—water or the substance mixed with it—the description applies to.

One early explanation takes nāṣiḥ here as nāṣiʿ, clear and pure.

Its value is therefore in attesting purity as a sense of nāṣiḥ, rather than conclusively determining every detail of the poetic image.

Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah was a Hudhalī poet generally counted among those who lived through both pre-Islamic and Islamic times. Without dating this verse, it should not be treated as certainly pre-Qur’anic.

That caution does not prevent examining the linguistic usage transmitted in the poem and early dictionaries.

Nuṣḥ as the opposite of deceit

Arabic contrasts nuṣḥ with ghishsh.

Ghishsh can concern deception, adulteration, or dishonest conduct, especially presenting something contrary to truth or another person’s interests.

Nuṣḥ, conversely, accompanies sincerity and goodwill.

A lexical explanation states:

وَالنُّصْحُ نَقِيضُ الْغِشِّ

wa-al-nuṣḥu naqīḍu al-ghishshi

Meaning:

“Nuṣḥ is the opposite of ghishsh.”

This is important for understanding the Qur’anic uses.

Goodwill does not mean merely saying something pleasant. A sincere person may warn of danger or say something difficult for the listener to hear.

Conversely, pleasant and hopeful speech may look benevolent while actually seeking to deceive.

Gentle speech, a pleasant message, and professed affection therefore do not alone establish genuine nuṣḥ.

Genuine nuṣḥ depends on sincerity and goodwill, rather than the outward form of speech alone.

Naṣaḥa and naṣīḥah: guidance for another’s welfare

Naṣaḥa lahu is recorded for wishing someone well and guiding them toward their welfare.

Naṣaḥahu occurs in the same semantic field.

Lexical explanations recognize the construction with li as an established, eloquent way of expressing goodwill.

For example:

نَصَحْتُ لَهُ

naṣaḥtu lahu

Meaning:

“I wished him well and offered him guidance.”

Li does not establish that the person must have spoken to the beneficiary.

Nuṣḥ may appear in conduct, care, trustworthiness, or fulfilling a responsibility for another.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12 is particularly significant because it concerns caring for a child, rather than merely advising him.

An example from al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

نَصَحْتُ بَنِي عَوْفٍ فَلَمْ يَتَقَبَّلُوا
رَسُولِي وَلَمْ تَنْجَحْ لَدَيْهِمْ وَسَائِلِي

naṣaḥtu banī ʿawfin fa-lam yataqabbalū / rasūlī wa-lam tanjaḥ ladayhim wasāʾilī

Translation:

“I sincerely counselled Banū ʿAwf, but they did not accept my messenger, and my efforts with them did not succeed.”

The verse clearly illustrates nuṣḥ as benevolent warning and guidance.

It also shows that counsel may be rejected.

Successfully persuading the listener is therefore not an essential part of nuṣḥ’s meaning.

Someone may sincerely wish another well and speak truly, while the listener rejects it.

Some transmissions of the collected poems have waṣātī instead of rasūlī. The variant does not change the general sense of goodwill and rejected guidance.

Nāṣiḥ al-jayb: purity within

Literary Arabic uses nāṣiḥu al-jaybi for someone inwardly free of deception and impurity.

A verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

أَبْلِغِ الْحَارِثَ بْنَ هِنْدٍ بِأَنِّي
نَاصِحُ الْجَيْبِ بَاذِلٌ لِلثَّوَابِ

ablighi al-ḥāritha bna hindin bi-annī / nāṣiḥu al-jaybi bādhilun li-al-thawābi

A translation close to the sense:

“Tell al-Ḥārith ibn Hind that I am sincere at heart, generous and willing to exert myself for recompense.”

Nāṣiḥ al-jayb here does not mean sewing a pocket or advising a pocket.

Jayb represents the person’s inward or hidden state in this expression.

The description thus concerns inward sincerity and freedom from guile.

Nāṣiḥ is consequently not confined to someone giving verbal advice.

Inward purity can itself be a claim someone makes about himself. Its truth requires conduct and evidence.

Naṣaḥa al-thawba: sewing and joining

Naṣaḥa al-thawba means sewing a garment.

Al-niṣāḥ names sewing thread, while minṣaḥah and manṣaḥah are recorded for a needle or sewing implement.

Thawbun manṣūḥun describes a sewn garment.

Here thread joins pieces of cloth, repairing their separation or a tear.

One early analysis uses sewing and joining to explain the root’s central meaning:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مُلَاءَمَةٍ بَيْنَ شَيْئَيْنِ وَإِصْلَاحٍ لَهُمَا

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā mulāʾamatin bayna shayʾayni wa-iṣlāḥin lahumā

Meaning:

“A root denoting fitting two things together and repairing them.”

The analysis takes the tailor and sewing thread as examples, then explains goodwill and sincere repentance through that image.

This is intelligible through joining and repairing.

A hasty conclusion should nevertheless be avoided.

Using sewing as an explanatory image does not establish that goodwill historically developed from tailoring in Arabic.

Other lexical explanations emphasize purity and freedom from deception.

Both branches and their possible connections can be recognized without asserting a certain historical origin independently of evidence.

Further non-Qur’anic applications

Intaṣaḥa is recorded for accepting advice or taking someone as a sincere adviser.

The two uses differ syntactically.

Istanṣaḥahu also means asking someone for counsel.

Naṣaḥa al-ghaythu al-bilāda describes rain producing continuous vegetation, as though new growth fills the gaps between plants.

That can be compared with the sewing image of joining separated parts.

None of these further derivatives occurs in the Qur’an.

3. False claims of goodwill: Satan and Joseph’s brothers

Nāṣiḥ in the Qur’an is not always spoken by truthful people.

Someone may present himself as benevolent while his actual conduct contradicts the claim.

These cases are particularly significant for identifying the root’s limits.

A. Al-Aʿrāf 7:21: Satan claims to be a well-wisher

وَقَاسَمَهُمَا إِنِّي لَكُمَا لَمِنَ النَّاصِحِينَ

wa-qāsamahumā innī lakumā la-mina al-nāṣiḥīna

Translation:

“He swore to the two: I am indeed one of your sincere well-wishers.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:21

Al-nāṣiḥīna is the plural active participle.

Satan presents himself as a well-wisher to Adam and his wife.

The surrounding verses must be examined to assess the claim.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:20, Satan whispers to them and offers an apparently beneficial reason to approach the tree.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 states:

فَدَلَّاهُمَا بِغُرُورٍ

fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin

“He drew the two down through deception.”

This makes clear that Satan’s professed goodwill was not genuine.

Al-nāṣiḥīna therefore does not mean the Qur’an itself identifies Satan as a genuine well-wisher.

The verse quotes his speech while its context exposes his deception.

B. An oath and a claim of goodwill

Wa-qāsamahumā shows Satan swearing to gain their trust.

An oath can emphasize a claim to truthfulness in human relations.

Swearing alone, however, does not establish that speech is true.

The story combines an oath with professed nuṣḥ, but the conduct’s result contradicts the claim.

The context thus supports a clear principle: professed goodwill, even accompanied by an emphatic oath, cannot replace examining truth and the consequences of conduct.

This follows from the narrative; nuṣḥ itself does not mean swearing or deception.

C. Yūsuf 12:11: the brothers’ claim

قَالُوا يَا أَبَانَا مَا لَكَ لَا تَأْمَنَّا عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَنَاصِحُونَ

qālū yā abānā mā laka lā taʾmannā ʿalā yūsufa wa-innā lahu la-nāṣiḥūna

Translation:

“They said: our father, why do you not trust us with Joseph, when we are indeed his well-wishers?”

Yūsuf 12:11

The speakers again present themselves as sincere well-wishers.

The preceding verses, however, show them deciding to remove Joseph.

Yūsuf 12:9 includes:

اقْتُلُوا يُوسُفَ أَوِ اطْرَحُوهُ أَرْضًا يَخْلُ لَكُمْ وَجْهُ أَبِيكُمْ

uqtulū yūsufa awi iṭraḥūhu arḍan yakhlu lakum wajhu abīkum

Translation:

“Kill Joseph or cast him into some land, so that your father’s attention will be yours alone.”

When they profess goodwill in Yūsuf 12:11, the text has therefore already revealed their motives and decision.

The claim conflicts with their actual conduct.

D. Goodwill and trust

Their speech places two concepts together:

لَا تَأْمَنَّا

lā taʾmannā

And:

لَنَاصِحُونَ

la-nāṣiḥūna

The first belongs to ʾ M N and concerns trust and trustworthiness.

The second belongs to N Ṣ Ḥ and concerns goodwill.

Trust and goodwill are connected but distinct.

Someone may honestly safeguard another’s property while lacking enough knowledge about their interests in another matter.

Someone may also profess goodwill while being untrustworthy.

Joseph’s brothers emphasize goodwill to secure their father’s trust.

The Qur’an separates the claim from reality by showing their decisions and conduct.

Nāṣiḥ should therefore not automatically become “trustworthy person” in every use: these related qualities remain independent.

4. Prophetic nuṣḥ: goodwill in conveying the message

Several Qur’anic verses describe prophets’ nuṣḥ toward their people.

These uses connect goodwill with conveying the message, warning, and calling for reform.

Nuṣḥ itself is not identical with the message’s full content or its acceptance by the audience.

A. Noah: conveying the message and wishing well

أُبَلِّغُكُمْ رِسَالَاتِ رَبِّي وَأَنْصَحُ لَكُمْ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

uballighukum risālāti rabbī wa-anṣaḥu lakum wa-aʿlamu mina allāhi mā lā taʿlamūna

Translation:

“I convey my Lord’s messages to you and sincerely counsel you; I know from The God what you do not know.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:62

The verse has two verbs:

أُبَلِّغُكُمْ

uballighukum

And:

أَنْصَحُ لَكُمْ

anṣaḥu lakum

Conveying concerns delivering the message.

Nuṣḥ concerns goodwill toward the addressees.

The two can coexist without being complete synonyms.

Someone may pass on speech without wishing the recipient well.

The prophet here describes both conveying the message and wishing his people well.

Nuṣḥ should therefore not simply be rendered “I conveyed,” since the verse expresses conveying separately.

B. Hūd: a trustworthy well-wisher

أُبَلِّغُكُمْ رِسَالَاتِ رَبِّي وَأَنَا لَكُمْ نَاصِحٌ أَمِينٌ

uballighukum risālāti rabbī wa-anā lakum nāṣiḥun amīnun

Translation:

“I convey my Lord’s messages to you, and I am a trustworthy well-wisher to you.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:68

Two descriptions stand together:

نَاصِحٌ

nāṣiḥun

And:

أَمِينٌ

amīnun

Nāṣiḥ concerns goodwill.

Amīn concerns trustworthiness and being worthy of confidence.

They should not be collapsed into one equivalent: the Qur’an expresses related but distinct qualities.

Their proximity does not establish that everyone calling himself trustworthy or benevolent speaks truly.

Satan professed nuṣḥ in Adam’s story, while his conduct contradicted it.

C. Ṣāliḥ: rejected counsel

فَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا قَوْمِ لَقَدْ أَبْلَغْتُكُمْ رِسَالَةَ رَبِّي وَنَصَحْتُ لَكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ لَا تُحِبُّونَ النَّاصِحِينَ

fa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā qawmi la-qad ablaghtukum risālata rabbī wa-naṣaḥtu lakum wa-lākin lā tuḥibbūna al-nāṣiḥīna

Translation:

“He turned away from them and said: my people, I conveyed my Lord’s message to you and wished you well, but you do not love sincere advisers.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:79

The root occurs twice here:

نَصَحْتُ

naṣaḥtu

And:

النَّاصِحِينَ

al-nāṣiḥīna

The first form is a perfect verb.

The second is a plural active participle.

After his people’s conduct and their fate, Ṣāliḥ speaks of his previous goodwill.

They had not accepted his counsel and warning.

Nuṣḥ therefore does not depend on the recipient’s acceptance.

Someone may warn sincerely while the audience dislikes what he says.

Lā tuḥibbūna al-nāṣiḥīna matters: opposition may arise because the message challenges the audience’s desires and conduct, rather than because it is false.

That does not make every unpleasant statement or harsh criticism necessarily sound counsel.

The speech’s truth and benevolence still depend on reality, intention, and the speaker’s conduct.

D. Shuʿayb: goodwill and his people’s opposition

فَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا قَوْمِ لَقَدْ أَبْلَغْتُكُمْ رِسَالَاتِ رَبِّي وَنَصَحْتُ لَكُمْ فَكَيْفَ آسَىٰ عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ كَافِرِينَ

fa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā qawmi la-qad ablaghtukum risālāti rabbī wa-naṣaḥtu lakum fa-kayfa āsā ʿalā qawmin kāfirīna

Translation:

“He turned away from them and said: my people, I conveyed my Lord’s messages to you and wished you well; how, then, should I grieve over a people who disbelieved?”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:93

Its construction resembles Ṣāliḥ’s speech.

Shuʿayb speaks of conveying messages and wishing his people well.

Nuṣḥ here does not guarantee acceptance.

Nor does it establish that the prophet bore responsibility for all his people’s choices.

The message has been conveyed, and the people have taken their position toward it.

Nuṣḥ describes the prophet’s benevolent conduct; acceptance or rejection concerns the audience’s conduct.

E. Noah in Hūd 11:34: goodwill and its benefit

وَلَا يَنْفَعُكُمْ نُصْحِي إِنْ أَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَنْصَحَ لَكُمْ إِنْ كَانَ اللَّهُ يُرِيدُ أَنْ يُغْوِيَكُمْ هُوَ رَبُّكُمْ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

wa-lā yanfaʿukum nuṣḥī in aradtu an anṣaḥa lakum in kāna allāhu yurīdu an yughwiyakum huwa rabbukum wa-ilayhi turjaʿūna

Translation:

“My sincere counsel will not benefit you, even if I wish to counsel you, if The God wills to leave you in error. He is your Lord, and to Him you are returned.”

Hūd 11:34

The root again occurs twice:

نُصْحِي

nuṣḥī

And:

أَنْ أَنْصَحَ

an anṣaḥa

Nuṣḥī is the verbal noun with a first-person pronoun: my sincere counsel or goodwill.

An anṣaḥa lakum concerns Noah’s wish to counsel and guide his people sincerely.

The verse distinguishes the prophet’s goodwill from his people’s benefiting from it.

A prophet may warn with sincere intentions while people reject the warning and gain no benefit.

The verse also raises divine will and error, requiring examination alongside guidance, error, choice, and human responsibility elsewhere in the Qur’an.

The root alone does not establish that prophetic goodwill is worthless or that The God compels error independently of human conduct and choices.

The clear lexical distinction is between the speaker’s goodwill and the audience’s benefiting from it.

5. Nuṣḥ in action, beyond speech

Two important Qur’anic applications show that nuṣḥ is not confined to verbal guidance.

A. Al-Tawbah 9:91: sincerity toward The God and the Messenger

لَيْسَ عَلَى الضُّعَفَاءِ وَلَا عَلَى الْمَرْضَىٰ وَلَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يَجِدُونَ مَا يُنْفِقُونَ حَرَجٌ إِذَا نَصَحُوا لِلَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ مَا عَلَى الْمُحْسِنِينَ مِنْ سَبِيلٍ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

laysa ʿalā al-ḍuʿafāʾi wa-lā ʿalā al-marḍā wa-lā ʿalā alladhīna lā yajidūna mā yunfiqūna ḥarajun idhā naṣaḥū li-allāhi wa-rasūlihi mā ʿalā al-muḥsinīna min sabīlin wa-allāhu ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation:

“There is no blame upon the weak, the sick, or those unable to find what they could spend, provided they are sincere toward The God and His Messenger. There is no ground for reproach against those who do good; The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Tawbah 9:91

This is important evidence for nuṣḥ’s breadth.

The context concerns people unable to accompany the Prophet on a military expedition because of weakness, illness, or lack of financial means.

Lack of capacity alone is not treated as evidence of insincerity or opposition to the Prophet.

The verse removes blame if they remain sincere toward The God and His Messenger.

Can a person give advice to The God?

Restricting nuṣḥ to instructing or advising the recipient would make naṣaḥū li-allāhi difficult to understand.

The Qur’an presents The God as knowing all things; a person does not teach Him something He does not know.

The root is not restricted to verbal advice, however.

Nuṣḥ can concern sincere intention, truthfulness, loyalty, and goodwill in fulfilling responsibilities.

Here the appropriate sense is acting sincerely and without deceit toward The God and the Prophet, rather than offering The God advice.

Thus, nuṣḥ can characterize intention and conduct even where no verbal advice is involved.

Genuine inability and sincere intention

Al-Tawbah 9:92 describes people who sought a means of travel from the Prophet and returned sorrowful because none was available.

The sequence distinguishes genuine inability from knowingly refusing responsibility.

Nuṣḥ here concerns sincerity and truthfulness toward one’s responsibility.

It does not establish that every claim of inability, without examining circumstances, necessarily proves genuine nuṣḥ.

Nor does sincerity imply equal capacity for everyone.

Health, resources, and living conditions differ; judgments about conduct should account for those differences.

B. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12: a family seeking the child’s welfare

وَحَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَرَاضِعَ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَقَالَتْ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ أَهْلِ بَيْتٍ يَكْفُلُونَهُ لَكُمْ وَهُمْ لَهُ نَاصِحُونَ

wa-ḥarramnā ʿalayhi al-marāḍiʿa min qablu fa-qālat hal adullukum ʿalā ahli baytin yakfulūnahu lakum wa-hum lahu nāṣiḥūna

Translation:

“We had previously prevented him from accepting wet nurses. She said: shall I direct you to a household that will care for him for you and wish him well?”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12

This belongs to the story of Moses’ infancy.

His sister offers to identify a family to those seeking someone to care for and nurse the child.

She then says:

وَهُمْ لَهُ نَاصِحُونَ

wa-hum lahu nāṣiḥūna

“They wish him well.”

The infant is not yet able to benefit from verbal counsel about a complex matter.

The family’s nāṣiḥ quality therefore concerns care, protection, and his welfare.

Nuṣḥ can thus manifest itself in practical conduct.

Yakfulūnahu belongs to K F L and concerns caring for someone and assuming responsibility for them.

Nuṣḥ and care appear together, but are not complete synonyms.

Someone may hold responsibility for another’s care while acting negligently or deceptively.

A family seeking the child’s welfare goes beyond the title of guardian and attends to his well-being.

Moses is subsequently returned to his mother.

The proposed family’s goodwill thus accords with its real relationship to the child.

Nāṣiḥūna itself does not establish that every family member must have explained his identity to Pharaoh’s officials.

That detail does not follow from the root.

C. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:20: a warning to save Moses’ life

وَجَاءَ رَجُلٌ مِنْ أَقْصَى الْمَدِينَةِ يَسْعَىٰ قَالَ يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنَّ الْمَلَأَ يَأْتَمِرُونَ بِكَ لِيَقْتُلُوكَ فَاخْرُجْ إِنِّي لَكَ مِنَ النَّاصِحِينَ

wa-jāʾa rajulun min aqṣā al-madīnati yasʿā qāla yā mūsā inna al-malaʾa yaʾtamirūna bika li-yaqtulūka fa-ukhruj innī laka mina al-nāṣiḥīna

Translation:

“A man came running from the farthest part of the city and said: Moses, the leaders are consulting about killing you. Leave, for I am one of your well-wishers.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:20

Being nāṣiḥ here accompanies warning of serious danger.

A man tells Moses that a group is deliberating about killing him.

He then urges Moses to leave.

Unlike the brothers’ claim in Joseph’s story, the warning accompanies conduct serving the recipient’s safety.

The next verse includes:

فَخَرَجَ مِنْهَا خَائِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ

fa-kharaja minhā khāʾifan yataraqqabu

“He left it, fearful and watchful.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:21, excerpt

The man’s goodwill involves practical action and conveying important information.

Nuṣḥ therefore need not be calm or pleasant speech alone.

Sometimes goodwill means warning of danger and calling for immediate action.

This does not make every person giving a warning necessarily truthful: evidence and context must establish its accuracy.

6. Sincere repentance: purity in returning

Al-Taḥrīm 66:8

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا تُوبُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ تَوْبَةً نَصُوحًا عَسَىٰ رَبُّكُمْ أَنْ يُكَفِّرَ عَنْكُمْ سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ وَيُدْخِلَكُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū tūbū ilā allāhi tawbatan naṣūḥan ʿasā rabbukum an yukaffira ʿankum sayyiʾātikum wa-yudkhilakum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru

Translation:

“You who have believed, return to The God in a sincere and genuine return. Perhaps your Lord will cover your misdeeds and admit you to gardens beneath which rivers flow.”

Al-Taḥrīm 66:8, opening excerpt

This is the Qur’an’s only instance of naṣūḥ.

Tawbatan belongs to T W B and concerns returning.

Naṣūḥan describes the quality of that return.

The form naṣūḥ

Ḥafṣ reads naṣūḥan with fatḥah on nūn.

It follows faʿūl, a pattern that can express intensification or an established quality.

The description is naṣūḥan even though tawbah is feminine.

Some Arabic faʿūl descriptions can qualify feminine nouns without a feminine suffix.

Its outwardly masculine shape therefore does not make it describe a man instead of repentance itself.

Shuʿbah’s transmission from ʿĀṣim also has nuṣūḥan with ḍammah on nūn.

That reading can be a verbal noun: repentance accompanied by sincerity and nuṣḥ.

Both forms connect semantically with a genuine and sincere return.

Sincere repentance: pure and genuine

One lexical explanation describes repentance free from deceit and impurity.

That fits nāṣiḥ as a description of pure material.

On this reading, sincere repentance is not merely a verbal declaration while inwardly intending to continue the same misconduct.

The word alone does not establish that anyone repeating an error must have been lying from the start in their earlier repentance.

Someone may sincerely intend to stop when repenting but later suffer a new lapse.

Judging sincerity at the time of repentance and judging later behavior are connected but distinct issues.

Repentance and the image of sewing a garment

One lexical analysis proposes a connection between sincere repentance and sewing a garment.

As sewing repairs torn cloth, a genuine return seeks to repair disruption caused by wrongdoing.

The image is semantically intelligible.

It should not become a certain account of the expression’s historical origin.

Nor does it establish that repentance is possible in every case through something resembling sewing or material compensation.

The kind of repair depends on the wrong and the rights infringed.

Some cases require stopping the misconduct and changing direction; others also involve repairing harm to other people.

Sincere repentance and reform

Al-Baqarah 2:160 includes:

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ تَابُوا وَأَصْلَحُوا وَبَيَّنُوا فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِمْ

illā alladhīna tābū wa-aṣlaḥū wa-bayyanū fa-ulāʾika atūbu ʿalayhim

Translation:

“Except those who return, put things right, and make clear; those are the ones toward whom I turn.”

Repentance here accompanies reform and making the truth clear.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:146 also places repentance alongside reform, holding fast to The God, and making devotion sincere to Him.

In some wrongs, a genuine return therefore relates to reforming conduct and repairing its effects.

Those concepts should not all become the lexical meaning of naṣūḥ.

Naṣūḥ describes sincerity and purity in returning.

Reform and clarification are distinct actions that accompany repentance in relevant situations.

Sincere repentance and regret

Nadāmah belongs to N D M and concerns regret about the past.

Tawbah, however, concerns turning back from a previous path.

Naṣūḥ describes that return’s sincerity and purity.

Someone may regret the consequences of an act without yet intending to stop it.

Sincere repentance, by contrast, is a return incompatible with deceiving oneself or others.

Regret, return, and purity are therefore related but different concepts.

The distinction should remain clear when translating and explaining Al-Taḥrīm 66:8.

7. Comparison with related roots

N Ṣ Ḥ and GH SH SH: goodwill and deception

The nearest lexical contrast to nuṣḥ is ghishsh.

Interpersonal nuṣḥ concerns sincerity and goodwill.

Ghishsh concerns deception, adulteration, and presenting something contrary to reality.

Satan’s professed nuṣḥ alongside actual deception in Al-Aʿrāf 7:21 is significant.

Not every error in guiding someone is necessarily deceit or intentional deception.

Someone may wish another well but give mistaken advice through inadequate knowledge.

Sincere intention, accurate information, and the action’s outcome should therefore remain distinct.

N Ṣ Ḥ and Ṣ D Q: goodwill and truthfulness

Ṣ D Q concerns truthfulness and correspondence between speech or conduct and reality.

Nuṣḥ connects with sincerity but particularly foregrounds benevolent conduct toward another.

Someone may speak accurately while using the truth to humiliate or harm.

Accurate content alone therefore does not establish benevolent intention.

Conversely, someone’s intention may be good while their information is mistaken.

Complete nuṣḥ in human conduct requires sincerity, knowledge, and attention to the recipient’s welfare, although the root itself guarantees none of these in every individual case.

Study of Ṣ D Q

N Ṣ Ḥ and ʾ M N: goodwill and trustworthiness

The roots come together in Al-Aʿrāf 7:68 and Joseph’s brothers’ speech.

Trustworthiness concerns preserving trust and avoiding betrayal.

Goodwill concerns seeking another’s welfare and acting sincerely toward them.

They may coexist but are not complete synonyms.

Both descriptions should therefore remain present when translating nāṣiḥun amīnun.

Study of ʾ M N

N Ṣ Ḥ and KH L Ṣ: goodwill and making pure

KH L Ṣ concerns purity, becoming free of admixture, and setting something apart.

One branch of nuṣḥ likewise concerns purity.

The senses approach each other in sincere repentance.

Qur’anic ikhlāṣ, however, may concern making religion and one’s devotion sincere to The God.

Beyond purity, nuṣḥ has particular uses in goodwill, guidance, and caring for others.

Its entire family cannot therefore be equated with ikhlāṣ.

Study of KH L Ṣ

N Ṣ Ḥ and H D Y: goodwill and guidance

Guidance concerns directing and showing a path.

Nuṣḥ may accompany guidance, since a sincere person can direct another toward the right path.

The concepts are nevertheless distinct.

Someone may show another a route without concern for their welfare.

Goodwill can also appear through practical care and preventing harm, without verbal guidance.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12 clearly illustrates this distinction.

N Ṣ Ḥ and W ʿ Ẓ: goodwill and admonition

W ʿ Ẓ concerns admonition and reminding people of conduct’s consequences.

Nuṣḥ can accompany admonition.

Admonition more particularly describes a kind of speech and warning, while nuṣḥ foregrounds benevolent intention and conduct.

Someone may admonish without sincere goodwill.

Someone may also act responsibly and benevolently without direct admonition.

Admonition is therefore one possible expression of nuṣḥ, rather than its entire meaning.

Study of W ʿ Ẓ

8. Translation questions and proposals for Forqan

Must naṣaḥa always mean giving advice?

No.

In modern Persian, naṣīḥat usually suggests giving verbal counsel to improve another person’s behavior.

Qur’anic nuṣḥ also concerns intention, loyalty, and practical care.

Naṣaḥū li-allāhi wa-rasūlihi in Al-Tawbah 9:91 cannot mean “they gave advice to The God and His Messenger.”

Wa-hum lahu nāṣiḥūna in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12 likewise concerns a household caring for a child.

Goodwill or sincerity is therefore usually a more suitable general equivalent than verbal advice alone.

Is every claimant a genuine well-wisher?

No.

Satan in Al-Aʿrāf 7:21 and Joseph’s brothers in Yūsuf 12:11 profess goodwill.

Context shows their claims conflicting with their actual conduct.

Translation should retain their speech as a claim.

Nāṣiḥ should not be changed to “deceiver” in those verses to remove the discrepancy.

It still means a well-wisher; the speaker falsely claims that quality.

Must nuṣḥ always be accepted?

No.

A claim of goodwill requires examination.

Unexamined acceptance of such a claim can be harmful in the stories of Satan and Joseph’s brothers.

Rejecting every unpleasant warning is also unwise: Ṣāliḥ and Shuʿayb were rejected despite their goodwill.

The criteria should be truth, evidence, the speaker’s conduct, and the recipient’s real welfare, rather than whether the message is pleasant.

9. Conclusion

Arabic N Ṣ Ḥ has three important branches: purity, goodwill as opposed to deception, and sewing or joining separated parts.

Lexical explanations differ over the central axis.

Some emphasize purity, while others foreground joining and repairing.

These explanations can approach each other semantically without making one the certain historical source of every application.

The root occurs 13 times in 11 Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:21, Satan presents himself as a well-wisher to Adam and his wife, while context exposes the claim as deceptive.

Joseph’s brothers likewise profess goodwill in Yūsuf 12:11 after planning to remove him.

By contrast, Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and Shuʿayb speak of wishing their people well.

Nuṣḥ accompanies conveying messages, warning, and calling for reform, but is not identical with conveying or acceptance.

Al-Tawbah 9:91 concerns people excused from joining a military expedition through weakness, illness, or lack of resources. Sincere intention is prominent there.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12, being nāṣiḥ concerns caring for an infant.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:20, goodwill takes the form of warning Moses of serious danger.

In Al-Taḥrīm 66:8, sincere repentance is a genuine return, rather than merely announcing regret or uttering words without a real change of direction.

Nuṣḥ is therefore not confined to verbal counsel.

Speech, warning, care, trustworthiness, and sincere action can all express goodwill.

Professed goodwill is nevertheless distinct from actual goodwill.

Benevolent intentions alone also do not guarantee that every piece of information or decision is correct.

Recognizing genuine nuṣḥ therefore requires considering sincere intention, truthful speech, responsibility, and conduct together.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

In human relations, N Ṣ Ḥ concerns sincere goodwill free of deception: seeking another’s welfare through speech and conduct with sincere intention.

Another branch concerns material purity, while a distinct application describes sewing and joining cloth.

Qur’anic naṣaḥa concerns wishing well and offering sincere guidance; nāṣiḥ means a sincere well-wisher, nuṣḥ means goodwill or sincere counsel, and naṣūḥ describes genuine, sincere repentance.

The root alone does not mean verbal admonition, unquestioning obedience, or accepting speech without examination.

Purity and goodwill free of deception appropriately express the shared Qur’anic field. Whether a person’s claim is true or false, and how goodwill appears in practice, require examining context and conduct.
