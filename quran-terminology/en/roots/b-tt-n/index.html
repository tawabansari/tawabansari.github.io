Qur’anic Root B Ṭ N

The interior and unseen side of things: from the belly and lining to secrets, intimacy, and knowledge of what is hidden

In Arabic, the root ب ط ن points to the inside or inner side of something, in contrast with its surface and visible part. This meaning first appears in tangible things: the belly as opposed to the back, a garment’s lining as opposed to its outer fabric, and the interior of a territory as opposed to its exterior. The same relationship also applies to nonmaterial matters, such as the inner reality of an issue, an act performed secretly, or someone familiar with another person’s confidential affairs.

In the Qur’an, this root describes human and animal bodies, the inner parts of places and objects, open and hidden conduct, and The God. This range does not make the word a collection of unrelated meanings. In most uses, a clear relationship is present: something has an inside and an outside, or an evident and an unseen aspect. The precise meaning of “inside,” however, changes with the subject.

“Being internal” should also be distinguished from “being hidden.” Everything internal is not necessarily unknown, and everything hidden is not necessarily inside a physical object. A garment’s lining, for example, may become visible while remaining its lining. Conversely, a person’s hidden intention cannot be reduced to a physical location.

The root occurs 25 times in the Qur’an in five principal forms: بَطَنَ; بِطَانَة and its plural بَطَائِن; بَطْن and its plural بُطُون; بَاطِن; and بَاطِنَة. These forms show how the concept of the interior extends from the tangible world to social relationships, moral responsibility, and knowledge.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twenty-five occurrences in five forms

1. The semantic core and its tangible image

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one semantic principle for ب ط ن and expresses it as follows:

«إِنْسِيُّ الشَّيْءِ وَالْمُقْبِلُ مِنْهُ»

The reference is to the inner side, or the part of something that stands opposite its exterior and back.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the core of baṭn

In bodily usage, Arabic بَطْن means the belly and the lower or front part of the body. It contrasts with ظَهْر, the back. This opposition extends to other things: land, valleys, containers, and clothing can also be described in terms of an interior and exterior, or baṭn and ẓahr.

The relationship does not always mean that something is enclosed in a sealed space. In بطن الوادي, the reference is to the interior or low part of a valley, rather than an actual belly. In بطن الأمر, it is the internal aspect or less apparent reality of an affair.

بَاطِن belongs to the same family. It can refer to an inner part, an unseen aspect, or something beyond a person’s direct sight and knowledge. Its counterpart is ظَاهِر: the surface or apparent aspect.

Yet the outward and inward are not always two separate things. They may be two aspects of one thing, such as a garment’s outer fabric and lining; two ways conduct appears, such as open and hidden sin; or a contrast concerned with the limits of human knowledge, distinguishing what is apparent to a person from what remains concealed.

The root’s semantic core can therefore be understood as “the inside or inner side of something, as opposed to its outside and surface.” In extended usage it also denotes the latent and less apparent aspects of affairs.

In each branch, this definition must be read with its subject. When the inside of a body is at issue, spatial meaning predominates. With conduct and intention, concealment comes to the fore. With knowledge of affairs, access to or awareness of their inner reality becomes important.

2. The root in early Arabic and everyday life

In early Arabic, ب ط ن was not confined to abstract concepts. It appeared in descriptions of bodies, travel through territory, travel equipment, clothing, kinship, and close social relationships.

One early witness appears in the poetry of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd:

وَبِلَادٍ زَعِلٍ ظِلْمَانُهَا
كَالْمَخَاضِ الْجُرْبِ فِي الْيَوْمِ الْخَدِرِ

wa-bilādin zaʿilin ẓilmānuhā / ka-al-makhāḍi al-jurbi fī al-yawmi al-khadiri

قَدْ تَبَطَّنْتُ وَتَحْتِي جَسْرَةٌ
تَتَّقِي الْأَرْضَ بِمَلْثُومٍ مَعِرْ

qad tabaṭṭantu wa-taḥtī jasratun / tattaqī al-arḍa bi-malthūmin maʿir

Translation: “Many a land whose male ostriches are restless, like mange-stricken pregnant she-camels on a cold day: I traversed its interior with a sturdy she-camel beneath me, keeping the ground at bay with her worn, hairless footpad.”

Dīwān of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd: the poem and lexical commentary

In this poem, تَبَطَّنْتُ refers to entering and advancing through a territory. The poet is not speaking about a bodily belly; he describes himself traversing the interior of a spatial expanse.

This witness matters because it shows the extension of “inside” from a body to a place, already intelligible in pre-Qur’anic poetry. There are, however, differences in the transmission and wording of some similar lines in early sources. All transmitted versions should not be treated as one poem with a single certain text.

The expression قَدْ تَبَطَّنْتُ وَتَحْتِي جَسْرَةٌ also appears in the poetry of Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, with a different description of the mount. This poetic resemblance further places the expression within descriptions of travel and passage through territory.

Ashʿār al-Shuʿarāʾ al-Sittah: Labīd’s witness and transmission variants

In the language of camel travel, بِطَان names the strap or girth that passes beneath a camel’s belly and secures its load or saddle. Its name is intelligible from its position in relation to the animal’s belly.

In the Arabic proverb الْتَقَتْ حَلْقَتَا الْبِطَانِ, the meeting of the girth’s two rings becomes an image for an affair growing intense or reaching a difficult stage. The proverb’s precise date of origin is uncertain. Its inclusion in early dictionaries alone does not establish that the exact expression is pre-Islamic.

In descriptions of bodies, بَطِين denotes someone with a large belly, and مِبْطَان a glutton. Mutammim ibn Nuwayrah, a poet of the early Islamic period, says of a man he praises:

لَقَدْ كَفَّنَ الْمِنْهَالُ تَحْتَ رِدَائِهِ
فَتًى غَيْرَ مِبْطَانِ الْعَشِيَّاتِ أَرْوَعَا

la-qad kaffana al-Minhālu taḥta ridāʾihi / fatan ghayra mibṭāni al-ʿashiyyāti arwaʿā

Translation: “Al-Minhāl shrouded beneath his cloak a gallant young man who was no glutton at evening meals.”

Ibn Rashīq, Al-ʿUmdah: Mutammim ibn Nuwayrah’s witness

This witness shows مِبْطَان used for gluttony. The same family includes مَبْطُون for someone suffering from an abdominal illness, while مُبَطَّن is also recorded in some uses for a person with a lean or slender belly.

These derivatives must not be treated as interchangeable. مِبْطَان and مُبَطَّن, for example, can describe different conditions in the uses just mentioned despite their similar forms.

Another social use is بَطْن in genealogical divisions. Arabic sometimes describes kinship groups using names of body parts. Here, baṭn is a branch of a larger kinship group. This usage is intelligible through the image of a whole with interconnected parts, though genealogical ranks should not be assumed identical across all periods and tribes.

بِطَانَة likewise denotes a garment’s lining and, by extension, intimates who have access to someone’s life and private affairs.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: lexical forms and biṭānah

These witnesses show that the transition from a physical interior to a spatial and social interior is at home in Arabic.

3. Baṭn in human and animal bodies

Most Qur’anic occurrences use بَطْن and its plural بُطُون. These sometimes refer directly to the belly. Elsewhere, the verse’s subject identifies the interior where a fetus develops or an inner part of an animal’s body.

Inside the mother and fetal development

Āl ʿImrān 3:35

إِذْ قَالَتِ امْرَأَتُ عِمْرَانَ رَبِّ إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لَكَ مَا فِي بَطْنِي مُحَرَّرًا فَتَقَبَّلْ مِنِّي ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

idh qālati imraʾatu ʿImrāna rabbi innī nadhartu laka mā fī baṭnī muḥarraran fa-taqabbal minnī innaka anta al-samīʿu al-ʿalīmu

Translation: “When the wife of ʿImrān said, ‘My Lord, I have vowed to You what is in my belly, freed and dedicated to Your service. Accept it from me. You are indeed the Hearing, the Knowing.’”

Here, مَا فِي بَطْنِي refers to a child not yet born. Baṭn broadly names the inner region of the mother’s body, and the context clarifies the intended referent.

The same use appears in Al-Naḥl 16:78, Al-Zumar 39:6, and Al-Najm 53:32.

Al-Naḥl 16:78 speaks of human beings emerging from their mothers’ bellies knowing nothing. Al-Zumar 39:6 describes successive stages of creation in mothers’ bellies, within three darknesses. Al-Najm 53:32 recalls human beings when they were fetuses in their mothers’ bellies.

The sense is clear in these passages. Baṭn refers to the inner location of fetal development, but the word itself is not the technical name of a particular organ such as the womb. That identification comes from the subject of the verse and knowledge of the body.

An explanation may therefore identify the place of fetal development within the mother, without claiming that baṭn means “womb” in all its lexical uses.

Animal bellies and internal bodily processes

Al-Naḥl 16:66

وَإِنَّ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَنْعَامِ لَعِبْرَةً ۖ نُسْقِيكُمْ مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ

wa-inna lakum fī al-anʿāmi la-ʿibratan nusqīkum mimmā fī buṭūnihi min bayni farthin wa-damin labanan khāliṣan sāʾighan li-al-shāribīna

Translation: “There is indeed a lesson for you in livestock: We give you to drink, from what is inside them, between digestive matter and blood, pure milk, pleasant for those who drink.”

Here, بُطُونِهِ concerns the interior of livestock bodies. The subject is the production of milk and its relationship to processes within the animal’s body.

Grammatically, the masculine singular pronoun in بُطُونِهِ relates to الْأَنْعَام, a collective noun. Al-Muʾminūn 23:21 uses بُطُونِهَا for the same livestock. This difference in pronouns does not, by itself, create two meanings for buṭūn.

Here too, buṭūn should not be restricted to a single organ such as the stomach. The phrase speaks of the bodily interior; the context specifies milk and the substances mentioned alongside it.

Al-Naḥl 16:69

ثُمَّ كُلِي مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا ۚ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ بُطُونِهَا شَرَابٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهُ فِيهِ شِفَاءٌ لِلنَّاسِ ۗ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

thumma kulī min kulli al-thamarāti fa-uslukī subula rabbiki dhululan yakhruju min buṭūnihā sharābun mukhtalifun alwānuhu fīhi shifāʾun li-al-nāsi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yatafakkarūna

Translation: “Then eat of every kind of fruit and follow your Lord’s paths, made easy. From within them comes a drink of varied colors, in which there is healing for people. In this is indeed a sign for people who reflect.”

In this verse, بُطُونِهَا refers to bees. The expression describes a drink coming from within their bodies.

Al-Naḥl 16:66 concerns the interior of livestock bodies, whereas Al-Naḥl 16:69 concerns bees. This difference shows that baṭn does not name a digestive apparatus specific to one animal species. The same word is used for different creatures while retaining the broad meaning of bodily interior.

A lexical study should not infer from buṭūn itself that the Qur’an is specifying every anatomical pathway involved in milk or honey production. Such an account requires independent investigation and does not alter the root’s lexical meaning.

The belly as the underside of the body

Al-Nūr 24:45 presents a different use of baṭn. It mentions creatures that move on their bellies, in contrast to those that walk on two legs or four.

Here, baṭn concerns the body’s underside: a surface that may touch the ground during movement.

This use clearly illustrates the old opposition between baṭn and ẓahr. Baṭn does not always mean the inner space of a container. It can denote the body’s underside as opposed to its back.

The fish’s belly and the account of Jonah

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:144 speaks of Jonah remaining in بَطْنِهِ. In light of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142, the pronoun refers to the great fish that swallowed him.

This too is a clear bodily use. Baṭn is the place inside the fish’s body where Jonah was located.

In the verse, the word does not name a symbolic concept or spiritual stage. Broader moral or interpretive meanings of the account, if explored, should be investigated separately from the direct meaning of baṭn.

4. Baṭn in eating and the consequences of conduct

Several Qur’anic verses use buṭūn in connection with eating and filling the belly. Some concern food; others use warning language to describe the consequences of human conduct as eating fire.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:10

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ الْيَتَامَىٰ ظُلْمًا إِنَّمَا يَأْكُلُونَ فِي بُطُونِهِمْ نَارًا ۖ وَسَيَصْلَوْنَ سَعِيرًا

inna alladhīna yaʾkulūna amwāla al-yatāmā ẓulman innamā yaʾkulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran wa-sa-yaṣlawna saʿīran

Translation: “Those who unjustly consume the property of orphans are only consuming fire into their bellies, and they will enter a blazing fire.”

The verse connects consuming orphans’ property with eating fire. This statement sets the grave consequences of injustice before the audience through a bodily image.

Lexically, بُطُونِهِمْ still denotes bellies. The moral force and warning arise from the whole sentence and the link between unjustly consuming property and fire, rather than from the root ب ط ن itself meaning punishment.

Al-Baqarah 2:174 speaks of eating fire into the belly in relation to people who conceal what has been sent down of the Book and exchange it for a small price. The two verses have similar imagery, while addressing different forms of wrongdoing.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:66, Al-Dukhān 44:45, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:53 also mention bellies in scenes of food and punishment in the hereafter. Al-Ḥajj 22:20 speaks of what is inside bellies, along with the skins, being melted.

These uses preserve the bodily meaning of baṭn, although the nature of each event and its relationship to human destiny come from the individual verse’s context.

A lexical study should therefore not simply turn baṭn into “the inner self,” “heart,” “moral interior,” or “conscience.” Where the Qur’an names the belly, the bodily sense should be considered first. Its relationship to moral or otherworldly consequences may then be explained if the context permits.

5. The interior of a place, fabric lining, and social intimacy

Three Qur’anic uses show the extension of “inside” from the body to places, fabric, and human relationships.

The interior of a territory: Al-Fatḥ 48:24

Verse text:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي كَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ عَنْهُمْ بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ أَظْفَرَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرًا

wa-huwa alladhī kaffa aydiyahum ʿankum wa-aydiyakum ʿanhum bi-baṭni Makkata min baʿdi an aẓfarakum ʿalayhim wa-kāna Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīran

Translation:

“He is the One who restrained their hands from you and your hands from them within the bounds of Mecca, after giving you the upper hand over them. The God sees what you do.”

بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ is a spatial expression. Here, baṭn refers to the interior or central, inner part of a geographical area.

It has a clear connection with بطن الوادي in early Arabic. In both, the word extends from the body to a place.

Identifying the event’s exact location within historical Mecca and its surroundings is, however, a geographical and historical question. The root ب ط ن itself supplies neither coordinates nor precise boundaries.

Renderings such as “within the bounds of Mecca” or “in the inner area of Mecca” preserve the lexical meaning. Identification with a particular historical location requires separate evidence.

Lining and outer fabric: Al-Raḥmān 55:54

Verse text:

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ فُرُشٍ بَطَائِنُهَا مِنْ إِسْتَبْرَقٍ وَجَنَى الْجَنَّتَيْنِ دَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā furushin baṭāʾinuhā min istabraqin wa-janā al-jannatayni dānin

Translation:

“Reclining on bedding whose linings are of rich brocade, with the fruit of the two gardens close at hand.”

بَطَائِنُهَا is the plural of بِطَانَة and refers to the bedding’s inner linings.

In clothing and bedding, biṭānah contrasts with ẓihārah: the inner lining as opposed to the outer fabric.

This is one of the clearest witnesses to the root’s semantic core. A lining is an inner or lower part of something, rather than necessarily a part no one can see.

بَطَائِنُهَا cannot therefore be translated here as “the bedding’s hidden secrets.” The word names an actual component of its construction.

Istabraq is a kind of costly fabric. The verse explicitly specifies the linings’ material; the outer fabric’s material is not identified in this phrase itself.

Biṭānah: intimacy and access to internal affairs

Āl ʿImrān 3:118

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَتَّخِذُوا بِطَانَةً مِنْ دُونِكُمْ لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ قَدْ بَدَتِ الْبَغْضَاءُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَمَا تُخْفِي صُدُورُهُمْ أَكْبَرُ ۚ قَدْ بَيَّنَّا لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tattakhidhū biṭānatan min dūnikum lā yaʾlūnakum khabālan waddū mā ʿanittum qad badati al-baghḍāʾu min afwāhihim wa-mā tukhfī ṣudūruhum akbaru qad bayyannā lakumu al-āyāti in kuntum taʿqilūna

Translation:

“You who have believed, do not take as confidants people from outside your community. They spare no effort to undermine you and wish you hardship. Hatred has already appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater. We have made the signs clear to you, if you exercise reason.”

بِطَانَة here no longer denotes a garment’s lining. It refers to people who gain access to the internal and confidential affairs of an individual or community.

The connection with lining is clear. As a lining lies inside a garment and close to the body, a trusted person gains entry to another’s life, decisions, and private affairs.

In social usage, biṭānah can denote a confidant, trusted intimate, or someone informed of internal matters and important decisions.

It therefore means more than an ordinary friend or acquaintance. The word conveys a closer relationship with access to the interior of affairs.

The same verse also describes the people’s blameworthy qualities: they spare no effort to cause harm, desire the audience’s suffering, and reveal hostility in their speech, while concealing something greater.

These descriptions matter for understanding the warning’s scope. The verse does not stop at naming biṭānah; it discusses the danger of entrusting internal affairs to ill-intentioned people.

Biṭānah itself should therefore not be equated with “enemy.” One confidant may wish someone well, while another may exploit the same closeness to cause harm.

Nor should everyone outside a group be assumed to share one intention and pattern of conduct without considering the verse’s descriptions. مِنْ دُونِكُمْ marks a boundary between the audience’s community and outsiders, but the verse goes on to identify particular qualities of hostility and harmful conduct.

The lexical meaning of biṭānah involves closeness and access to internal affairs. Determining who deserves that trust depends on conduct, trustworthiness, and the relationship’s circumstances.

Here, ب ط ن develops from a bodily image into an important concept in human relationships while retaining its semantic core.

6. Outward and inward conduct and moral responsibility

Several verses set ب ط ن against ظ ه ر. The subject is no longer a body or place, but human conduct and its open or hidden aspects.

Al-Anʿām 6:120

Verse text:

وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ ۚ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَكْسِبُونَ الْإِثْمَ سَيُجْزَوْنَ بِمَا كَانُوا يَقْتَرِفُونَ

wa-dharū ẓāhira al-ithmi wa-bāṭinahu inna alladhīna yaksibūna al-ithma sa-yujzawna bi-mā kānū yaqtarifūna

Translation:

“Leave both the outward and inward aspects of sin. Those who earn sin will be recompensed for what they used to commit.”

ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ and بَاطِنَهُ express two aspects of sin.

Open sin can be conduct performed before others or with effects known to them. Hidden sin can be conduct occurring out of sight, or with an aspect not apparent to other people.

Bāṭin should not, however, be restricted here to inner thoughts and intentions. A sin committed secretly can also be a clear instance.

Nor does the verse alone establish that every passing thought or feeling, before becoming conduct for which a person is responsible, receives the same recompense as a committed act. Responsibility and earning sin must be examined through the sentence and other verses.

The central point is that whether an act is visible to people does not determine whether it is permitted or prohibited. Concealing conduct does not change its nature.

Al-Anʿām 6:151 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:33

These two verses use the verbal form بَطَنَ.

Al-Anʿām 6:151 prohibits approaching indecencies, whether open or hidden. Al-Aʿrāf 7:33 likewise includes الْفَوَاحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ among prohibited things.

Linguistically, ظَهَرَ and بَطَنَ are contrasting verbs. The first concerns becoming apparent, and the second remaining concealed.

In these uses, مَا بَطَنَ concerns what remains hidden of the conduct under discussion. The expression should not be restricted to one particular indecency without independent grounds.

Some historical explanations identify particular instances of open and hidden indecencies. Such examples alone do not restrict the two verbs’ general meaning.

A clear semantic principle emerges: concealment from people does not remove conduct from moral evaluation and responsibility.

Yet baṭn itself does not name sin. It identifies only the subject’s hidden aspect. What is blameworthy or forbidden is established by ithm, fawāḥish, and the verse’s ruling.

Outward and hidden blessings: Luqmān 31:20

Verse text:

أَلَمْ تَرَوْا أَنَّ اللَّهَ سَخَّرَ لَكُمْ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَأَسْبَغَ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعَمَهُ ظَاهِرَةً وَبَاطِنَةً ۗ وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يُجَادِلُ فِي اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَابٍ مُنِيرٍ

a-lam taraw anna Allāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi wa-asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu ẓāhiratan wa-bāṭinatan wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīrin

Translation:

“Have you not seen that The God has made what is in the heavens and on earth serviceable to you and has bestowed His blessings upon you abundantly and fully, both outward and hidden? Yet among people is one who disputes concerning The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating book.”

Here, ظَاهِرَةً and بَاطِنَةً describe The God’s blessings.

An outward blessing has manifestations and effects clear to people. A hidden blessing may be one a person does not directly see, does not fully recognize, or cannot easily connect with their own life.

Some explanations relate outward blessings to material resources and inward blessings to nonmaterial human capacities. These may be useful illustrations, but the division should not be presented as the verse’s exclusive meaning.

A bodily blessing can also be hidden from a person, such as many internal processes that occur without conscious attention. A nonmaterial blessing can likewise have apparent, observable effects.

Outward and inward are therefore not necessarily restricted here to two entirely separate categories, material and spiritual. The subject is the breadth of blessings and their differing degrees of visibility to people.

Alongside the passages concerning sin and indecencies, this verse shows that bāṭin has no inherently negative value. In one place it qualifies sin, and in another a blessing. Its evaluative force comes from the thing to which it is attributed.

7. Bāṭin as the inner side in contrast with the outer

Another use of this family appears in Al-Ḥadīd 57:13.

Verse text:

يَوْمَ يَقُولُ الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالْمُنَافِقَاتُ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا انْظُرُونَا نَقْتَبِسْ مِنْ نُورِكُمْ قِيلَ ارْجِعُوا وَرَاءَكُمْ فَالْتَمِسُوا نُورًا فَضُرِبَ بَيْنَهُمْ بِسُورٍ لَهُ بَابٌ بَاطِنُهُ فِيهِ الرَّحْمَةُ وَظَاهِرُهُ مِنْ قِبَلِهِ الْعَذَابُ

yawma yaqūlu al-munāfiqūna wa-al-munāfiqātu li-alladhīna āmanū unẓurūnā naqtabis min nūrikum qīla irjiʿū warāʾakum fa-iltamisū nūran fa-ḍuriba baynahum bi-sūrin lahu bābun bāṭinuhu fīhi al-raḥmatu wa-ẓāhiruhu min qibalihi al-ʿadhābu

Translation:

“On the day when hypocritical men and women say to those who have believed, ‘Wait for us, so that we may take some of your light,’ it will be said, ‘Go back behind you and seek light.’ A wall with a gate will then be set between them: on its inner side is mercy, while on its outer side, facing it, is punishment.”

بَاطِنُهُ and ظَاهِرُهُ describe two sides of a separating boundary. The image of a wall and gate places the relationship between inside and outside spatially before the reader.

Bāṭin here does not mean a hidden secret, an inner intention, or a reality nobody can see. The subject is the inner side of a separating structure, where mercy is present.

This example matters because it shows that even in a context concerning the hereafter, the word can retain its spatial meaning.

Mercy and punishment are different outcomes attributed to the wall’s two sides. The meaning of bāṭin is not derived from mercy, nor is the meaning of ẓāhir constructed from punishment.

It therefore cannot be said that, within this root, everything inward is good and everything outward blameworthy. In Al-Anʿām, bāṭin may qualify sin; in Luqmān, a blessing; and in Al-Ḥadīd, the inner side of a structure where mercy is found.

The root’s meaning is more stable than these contexts’ differing evaluations.

8. الْبَاطِن as a description of The God

Al-Ḥadīd 57:3

هُوَ الْأَوَّلُ وَالْآخِرُ وَالظَّاهِرُ وَالْبَاطِنُ ۖ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

huwa al-awwalu wa-al-ākhiru wa-al-ẓāhiru wa-al-bāṭinu wa-huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmun

Translation:

“He is the First and the Last, the Manifest and the Hidden, and He knows all things.”

This verse presents one of the root’s most sensitive uses. الْبَاطِن describes The God alongside الْأَوَّل, الْآخِر, and الظَّاهِر.

The bodily meaning of baṭn cannot simply be attributed to The God to understand this description. The God does not have an inner organ opposed to an outer organ, and bāṭin does not turn Him into a hidden body located inside the world.

We must begin with the root’s linguistic core without transferring the physical features of material examples to The God.

In Arabic, bāṭin can refer to what lies beyond direct, outward perception. It can also describe someone who knows the inside of an affair. Accordingly, when explaining this divine description, Ibn Fāris emphasizes The God’s knowledge of the interior and reality of affairs.

Maqāyīs and Al-Mufradāt: knowledge of the interior of affairs

Both directions matter for studying the verse.

First, The God is not one of the perceptible objects that a person can encompass with sight and other senses like a body. What a person observes of the world does not amount to sensory comprehension of The God’s reality.

Second, The God knows the interior of affairs and what remains concealed from limited human knowledge. The verse’s ending, وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ, emphasizes the comprehensiveness of His knowledge.

This final sentence is an important contextual indication: the preceding descriptions must not be interpreted as implying The God’s remoteness, ignorance, or limitation.

At the same time, الْبَاطِن should not be restricted to “the Knower of secrets” in a way that removes its semantic potential in contrast with الظَّاهِر. The Qur’an brings four descriptions together, and each should be studied in relation to the others.

الْأَوَّلُ وَالْآخِرُ concern beginning and end, while الظَّاهِرُ وَالْبَاطِنُ introduce a contrast between manifestation and concealment. Their placement together expresses that The God is not confined to one side of either opposition.

This relationship should not, however, be turned without evidence into a definitive philosophical theory of The God’s location, His indwelling in things, or a bodily identity between The God and the world. Such theories do not follow from the lexical meaning of bāṭin alone.

Later philosophical and mystical terminology, although it may offer interpretations of this verse, should likewise not be the starting point for translating it.

In a basic translation, “the Hidden” opposite “the Manifest” preserves the verse’s verbal contrast. An explanation can then clarify that no body hidden in a place is intended and that the verse’s ending supports The God’s knowledge of both apparent and hidden affairs.

Two principles should therefore be preserved together: The God is not limited by bodily and sensory measures, and no internal or concealed matter lies outside His knowledge.

This conclusion accords with the Qur’anic network of statements about The God’s knowledge of open and hidden matters. Al-Taghābun 64:4, for example, describes His knowledge of what people conceal and reveal, and of what is within their breasts.

9. Distinguishing ب ط ن from related roots

ب ط ن (baṭan) and ظ ه ر (ẓahar)

Study of Ẓ H R

These roots stand opposite each other in many uses.

Baṭn denotes the interior or unseen aspect, while ẓahr denotes the surface, exterior, or what has become apparent.

Bodily uses concern belly and back; clothing, lining and outer fabric; conduct, concealment and openness; and knowledge, the known and evident aspect as opposed to the latent or less known.

This opposition is one of the clearest internal relationships in the Qur’an’s lexical network. It should not, however, be turned into one fixed nonphysical meaning in every verse.

ب ط ن (baṭan) and خ ف ي (khafiya)

Study of KH F Y

Khafiya concerns remaining concealed from sight or knowledge. Bāṭin can also be unseen in many uses, but its semantic field is broader than concealment.

A garment’s lining, for example, remains its inner part even if the garment is turned inside out and the lining becomes visible. What is called bāṭin is then no longer hidden from sight.

Khafāʾ thus concerns inaccessibility or lack of visibility more directly; baṭn can also express a position or inner side independently of whether it is seen.

The two fields draw closer in matters of ethics and knowledge.

ب ط ن (baṭan) and س ر ر (sirr)

Study of S R R

Sirr concerns a secret: something kept concealed or not apparent to everyone.

Bāṭin is not necessarily a secret. The body’s interior, a garment’s lining, and a wall’s inner side can be expressed through this root without any intention to conceal them.

Conversely, when human confidential affairs are at issue, bāṭin and sirr can draw close. Through this relationship, biṭānah also denotes a confidant or trusted intimate.

Being a confidant is nevertheless only one of the root’s social uses and should not be generalized to the entire family.

ب ط ن (baṭan) and ج و ف (jawf)

Study of J W F

Jawf concerns the internal, hollow space of a body or container. In physical uses it can denote the bodily interior or something’s empty space.

Baṭn overlaps with it in some uses but does not always emphasize an empty cavity. Sometimes the underside or inner side is intended, in contrast with back and surface.

Jawf and baṭn are therefore close in some sentences, but equating them without considering the sentence’s structure and subject is imprecise.

ب ط ن (baṭan) and د خ ل (dukhūl)

Study of D KH L

Dakhala concerns entering or being inside. Baṭn can name the inner part itself.

Their difference is clear in a spatial image: entering represents movement from outside to inside, whereas baṭn identifies the interior or inner side itself.

The fields draw closer in expressions such as تبطنت الوادي, since a verb derived from ب ط ن can express movement through the interior of a place.

That proximity does not imply that every use of baṭn means entering.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For bodily بَطْن, “belly” is a natural equivalent. In expressions about a fetus and its mother, “in the mother’s belly” can be retained, with an explanation identifying the site of fetal development.

For livestock and bees, “within the body” can also be appropriate, especially when an internal process is intended and restriction to one particular organ would be unwarranted.

For بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ, proposed translations are “within the bounds of Mecca” or “in the inner area of Mecca.” These preserve the spatial meaning and leave historical identification of the location to separate research.

For بَطَائِنُهَا in Al-Raḥmān, “their linings” is suitable, since the verse uses a specific sense relating to fabric and bedding.

For بِطَانَةً in Āl ʿImrān, “confidant,” “trusted intimate,” or “someone with access to internal affairs” are defensible equivalents. The full sentence should make clear that the issue is more than ordinary friendship: it involves closeness and access to private and important affairs.

For مَا بَطَنَ in Al-Anʿām and Al-Aʿrāf, “what is hidden” or “what is done secretly” is suitable. The precise choice depends on the subject and sentence structure.

For بَاطِنَةً in Luqmān, “hidden” opposite “outward” is appropriate. It should not be translated exclusively as a spiritual blessing without grounds.

For بَاطِنُهُ in Al-Ḥadīd 57:13, “its inner side” is suitable, since the contrast with an outer side is spatial.

For الْبَاطِنُ in Al-Ḥadīd 57:3, “the Hidden” is a brief, accessible basic rendering that preserves its contrast with al-Ẓāhir. The divine meaning of this description should be explained separately, avoiding both attribution of physical embodiment to The God and restriction of the word’s full potential to a philosophical term.

These differences show why a single fixed equivalent is unsuitable for every derivative. The family’s shared meaning remains, while each form should be translated according to its subject and sentence structure.

11. Conclusion

In Arabic, ب ط ن rests on the inside or inner side of something in contrast with its outside and surface. This core appears tangibly in bodies, clothing, places, and everyday equipment, extending to internal and latent aspects of social, moral, and cognitive matters.

In the Qur’an, baṭn most often concerns the belly and bodily interior. Uses involving mothers, livestock, bees, animal movement, and the fish’s belly show how broad and active this bodily meaning remains.

Spatial بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ concerns the inner part of an area. بَطَائِنُهَا denotes the lining of bedding. In بِطَانَة, the interior extends to close human relationships and access to confidential affairs.

In the opposition between ẓāhir and bāṭin, the root concerns apparent and concealed aspects of conduct or blessings. The opposition itself has no fixed moral value: bāṭin can qualify hidden sin, a less recognized blessing, or a structure’s inner part.

For the divine description الْبَاطِنُ, the root’s meaning must be understood without transferring physical properties. Concealment from limited human perception and The God’s knowledge of the interior and reality of affairs are two directions to explore. The verse itself does not authorize turning The God into a body hidden in the world or confining Him to a later philosophical theory.

Final root definition: ب ط ن denotes the interior and inner side of something in contrast with its exterior and surface; in extended uses, it expresses an affair’s latent or less apparent aspect, or what is accessible to intimates and those familiar with it.

Within this family, “inside” is central, but it does not always mean “secret” or “unknown thing.” Each use should be understood and translated according to whether its subject is a body, place, garment, act, blessing, human relationship, or description of The God.
