Silence and cessation of speech: from human silence to noiseless objects and closed, solid structures

1. Introducing the root and its forms

The Arabic root Ṣ M T is best known for silence and not speaking. It can describe someone who does not speak or stops speaking. Elsewhere the family describes things without sound, or whose usual sound is not heard during movement or contact.

Early Arabic also records other meanings, including a firmly closed lock, a solid body without cavities, an unmixed color, and things containing no admixture.

These meanings have connections with silence, but should not all be reduced to not speaking.

Ṣ M T occurs only once in the Qur’an, as صَامِتُونَ (ṣāmitūna) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • صَمَتَ / يَصْمُتُ (ṣamata / yaṣmutu): became or remained silent; does not speak.
  • صَمْت (ṣamt): silence and refraining from speech.
  • صُمُوت / صُمَات (ṣumūt / ṣumāt): other verbal nouns for remaining silent.
  • صَامِت (ṣāmit): silent; someone who does not speak.
  • صَامِتُونَ (ṣāmitūna): silent people; the family’s sole Qur’anic form.
  • صَمُوت / صِمِّيت (ṣamūt / ṣimmīt): taciturn or frequently silent.
  • أَصْمَتَ (aṣmata): became silent; in some uses, lost the ability to speak.
  • صَمَّتَ / تَصْمِيت (ṣammata / taṣmīt): silencing or causing another to be silent.
  • مُصْمَت (muṣmat): closed and impenetrable, solid and without cavities; also containing no admixture.
  • صَمُوت (ṣamūt): of armor, swords, or certain objects, quiet or noiseless in use.

Only ṣāmitūna occurs in the Qur’an. The other forms clarify the Arabic root’s semantic field but should not be counted as Qur’anic derivatives.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Silence: the absence of speech

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records ṭūl al-sukūt for ṣamt: prolonged silence.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explain ṣamata through sakata.

These witnesses establish not speaking as ṣamt’s familiar meaning. Some lexicographers’ emphasis on prolonged silence should nevertheless not become an absolute rule that every ṣamt must last a long time.

Someone can remain silent briefly or continue in silence. Its actual duration follows the situation and sentence.

Ṣamata alone also does not identify the cause. Someone may fall silent to listen, refrain from speaking thoughtfully or deliberately, or be unable to speak because of a particular condition.

These situations differ in cause and value, although speech may be absent in all of them.

Is ṣamt always voluntary?

In ordinary human usage, ṣamt can be voluntary: someone can speak but remains silent.

Lexicons nevertheless also record aṣmata for someone whose tongue has ceased to speak.

Ṣamt therefore cannot be declared invariably voluntary throughout its historical uses or never applicable to an inability to speak.

Conversely, describing someone as ṣāmit does not alone establish that they cannot speak.

This distinction matters for Qur’anic usage: ṣāmitūna in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 describes the addressees’ silence rather than a physical inability to speak.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: silence and cessation of speech

Closure and absence of an outlet

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris proposes ibhām wa-ighlāq as Ṣ M T’s semantic principle: being closed or obstructed.

On this analysis, human silence can be explained through speech’s outlet remaining closed. Applied to objects, muṣmat also describes something without an opening or means of penetration.

The connection is intelligible, but Ibn Fāris’s lexical analysis should be distinguished from proving the root’s historical emergence.

Comparing silence to a closed outlet does not establish that the earliest Arabic speakers derived every use from the image of a closed mouth.

The definition should retain silence and closure or solidity as attested uses without making their relationship more certain than the evidence allows.

3. Ṣamt in early Arabic life and language

Noiseless armor: a pre-Islamic poetic witness

Describing military equipment, Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī refers to armor as ṣamūt:

وَكُلِّ صَمُوتٍ نَثْلَةٍ تُبَّعِيَّةٍ
وَنَسْجِ سُلَيْمٍ كُلَّ قَضَّاءَ ذَائِلِ

wa-kulli ṣamūtin nathlatin tubbaʿiyyatin / wa-nasji sulaymin kulla qaḍḍāʾa dhāʾili

A rendering close to the sense:

“And every broad, supple, noiseless coat of armor made by Tubbaʿ, and the long, firmly woven armor of Sulaym.”

Ṣamūt describes armor here.

Lexical explanations say that its suppleness and workmanship prevent a conspicuous sound when it is donned or moved.

Ṣ M T therefore extends beyond human silence. An inanimate object can be called ṣamūt for lacking sound.

Ṣamūt here does not mean inability to speak. Armor has no speech apparatus; the description concerns the sound its components produce during movement.

The witness is transmitted in Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī’s collected poetry and lexicons. As a pre-Islamic poet’s verse, it matters for the root’s usage before the Qur’an.

Commentary on Sībawayh: Al-Nābighah’s witness and Sulaym as a poetic form of Sulaymān

A sword and a quieter blow

Arabic sources also record sayfun ṣamūtun.

They explain it as a sword that does not produce the usual sound of impact and rebound when entering its target or meeting bone.

A line attributed to Al-Zubayr ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib reads:

وَيَنْفِي الْجَاهِلَ الْمُخْتَالَ عَنِّي
رِقَاقُ الْحَدِّ وَقْعَتُهُ صَمُوتُ

wa-yanfī al-jāhila al-mukhtāla ʿannī / riqāqu al-ḥaddi waqʿatuhu ṣamūtu

Translation:

“A fine, keen blade whose blow is silent keeps the ignorant, conceited man away from me.”

Ṣamūt concerns the sword blow’s character rather than its wielder’s silence.

A root explanation must therefore identify what the adjective describes. Human silence, noiseless armor, and a quieter sword blow differ, while approaching one another through absent or reduced sound.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the witness attributed to Al-Zubayr and a ṣamūt blow

Silent and speaking property

One familiar Arabic expression is:

مَا لَهُ صَامِتٌ وَلَا نَاطِقٌ (mā lahu ṣāmitun wa-lā nāṭiqun)

He has neither silent nor speaking property; in other words, he owns nothing.

Here ṣāmit denotes gold and silver, and nāṭiq animals such as camels, sheep, and horses.

Nāṭiq does not necessarily mean speaking a human language. Animals produce sounds, whereas gold and silver do not produce such vocal sounds.

This contrast categorized kinds of property within Arabic financial and social usage.

It does not make gold or silver ṣāmit’s basic meaning in every sentence. That meaning belongs to a particular property idiom.

Muṣmat: a closed, solid body

Arabic also uses muṣmat for a body that is not hollow or offers no opening for penetration.

Jismun muṣmatun, for instance, denotes a solid body without cavities.

Quflun muṣmatun describes a closed lock that is difficult or complicated to open.

Elsewhere thawbun muṣmatun or lawnun muṣmatun describes fabric or a color without another color mixed into it. Farasun muṣmatun can describe a uniformly colored horse without markings.

Silence alone does not explain these uses: closure, solidity, or freedom from admixture is prominent.

A single-colored fabric is not muṣmat because it makes no sound; the adjective concerns the color’s uniformity and purity.

Alfun muṣmatun likewise means a full thousand without deficiency.

Classical Ṣ M T thus extends beyond silence. These senses should nevertheless not be imposed on Qur’anic ṣāmitūna, whose context concerns not speaking.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, and Asās al-Balāghah: silent property and solid bodies

4. The Qur’anic use: am antum ṣāmitūna

The sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in Al-Aʿrāf:

وَإِنْ تَدْعُوهُمْ إِلَى الْهُدَىٰ لَا يَتَّبِعُوكُمْ ۚ سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْكُمْ أَدَعَوْتُمُوهُمْ أَمْ أَنْتُمْ صَامِتُونَ

wa-in tadʿūhum ilā al-hudā lā yattabiʿūkum sawāʾun ʿalaykum a-daʿawtumūhum am antum ṣāmitūna

“If you call them to guidance, they do not follow you; it is the same for you whether you call them or remain silent.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:193

The meaning of ṣāmitūna

Ṣāmitūna is the sound masculine plural of the active participle ṣāmit.

It appears with the pronoun antum:

أَنْتُمْ صَامِتُونَ (antum ṣāmitūna)

You are silent, or you remain silent.

Opposite it stands a-daʿawtumūhum: have you called them?

The sentence contrasts calling and summoning them with remaining silent and saying nothing.

Ṣāmitūna does not describe people physically unable to speak. Nor does it describe the invoked deities: it describes those who may call them or remain silent.

The distinction matters because the word’s meaning must follow its syntactic role.

Why are calling and remaining silent equivalent?

The preceding statements clarify the verse.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:191 says:

أَيُشْرِكُونَ مَا لَا يَخْلُقُ شَيْئًا وَهُمْ يُخْلَقُونَ

a-yushrikūna mā lā yakhluqu shayʾan wa-hum yukhlaqūna

“Do they associate what creates nothing, while they themselves are created?”

The next verse says:

وَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ لَهُمْ نَصْرًا وَلَا أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَنْصُرُونَ

wa-lā yastaṭīʿūna lahum naṣran wa-lā anfusahum yanṣurūna

“They can neither help them nor help themselves.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:192

The passage chiefly concerns the inability of supposed partners.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 then says that calling them and remaining silent yield the same result.

Sawāʾun ʿalaykum therefore does not declare speaking and silence equivalent in every life situation.

The equivalence concerns the fruitlessness of calling those unable to respond or help.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:194 challenges them to respond; Al-Aʿrāf 7:195 questions their ability to move, grasp, see, and hear; Al-Aʿrāf 7:198 again raises their failure to hear and see.

These verses examine the supposed divine partners’ capacity to receive requests and respond.

Silence in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 is consequently neither a devotional command nor praise of being silent. It is one of two compared conditions exposing the futility of invoking supposed partners.

Verbal and nominal constructions

A-daʿawtumūhum is a perfect verb, whereas antum ṣāmitūna is a nominal clause.

The distinction should remain without establishing calling as necessarily brief or silence as necessarily permanent.

The nominal clause presents the addressees’ silence as a condition. Its continuation or duration cannot be established from the active participle alone.

Whether you call them or remain silent therefore naturally conveys the contrast, although Arabic uses a verb on one side and a nominal clause on the other.

5. Ṣamt compared with sukūt, inṣāt, and ṣawm

Ṣ M T (ṣamata) and S K T (sakata)

Ṣamata and sakata are very close in Arabic; lexicographers sometimes define one through the other.

Some uses nevertheless permit a distinction between remaining silent and the cessation of speech or sound.

S K T occurs once in the Qur’an:

وَلَمَّا سَكَتَ عَنْ مُوسَى الْغَضَبُ أَخَذَ الْأَلْوَاحَ

wa-lammā sakata ʿan mūsā al-ghaḍabu akhadha al-alwāḥa

“When Moses’ anger subsided, he took up the tablets.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154

Sakata’s subject here is anger rather than a person speaking.

Becoming silent can therefore also express something subsiding and settling.

Ṣāmitūna in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 instead describes people who say nothing.

This does not make S K T invariably about sound ending and Ṣ M T invariably about speech not beginning. Arabic usage exceeds such absolute boundaries.

Ṣ M T (ṣamata) and N Ṣ T (anṣata)

The Qur’an says:

وَإِذَا قُرِئَ الْقُرْآنُ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ وَأَنْصِتُوا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

wa-idhā quriʾa al-qurʾānu fa-istamiʿū lahu wa-anṣitū laʿallakum turḥamūna

“When the Qur’an is recited, listen to it and remain silent, so that you may receive mercy.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:204

Anṣitū accompanies listening here.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, a group arriving to hear the Qur’an also says to one another:

أَنْصِتُوا

anṣitū

“Be silent and listen.”

In both cases, inṣāt concerns attention and listening to the recited speech.

Ṣamt alone, however, does not mean attentive listening or understanding. Someone may remain silent without attending to another’s words.

Inṣāt in these contexts is silence for listening, whereas ṣamt covers a more general condition of silence.

This does not mean every inṣāt throughout Arabic usage guarantees complete comprehension. Hearing, attending, and understanding are distinguishable stages.

Ṣ M T (ṣamata) and Ṣ W M (ṣāma)

These roots resemble one another in sound and some uses but remain distinct.

Ṣ M T concerns silence and not speaking.

Ṣ W M concerns refraining and holding oneself back from doing something, whose object and limits follow the context.

Maryam says:

فَقُولِي إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ صَوْمًا فَلَنْ أُكَلِّمَ الْيَوْمَ إِنْسِيًّا

fa-qūlī innī nadhartu li-al-raḥmāni ṣawman fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyan

“Then say: I have vowed a ṣawm to Al-Raḥmān, so today I will speak to no human being.”

Maryam 19:26

Mary’s abstention accompanies not speaking here.

Ṣawm can therefore include refraining from speech in a particular situation, without making ṣawm and ṣamt interchangeable everywhere.

Likewise, the prescribed ṣiyām in Al-Baqarah 2:183, Al-Baqarah 2:184, Al-Baqarah 2:185, Al-Baqarah 2:186, and Al-Baqarah 2:187 must not be reduced to silence. Those verses examine the worship’s practical limits.

Ṣamt may thus be one behavior within a particular abstention without constituting the whole meaning of ṣawm.

Study of Ṣ W M

Ṣ M T (ṣamata) and B K M (bakima)

B K M concerns muteness and an inability to express oneself clearly.

Arabic abkam describes someone without clear speech or whose ability to speak is obstructed.

Ordinary ṣāmit instead describes someone not speaking, without necessarily lacking the capacity.

The distinction also matters when understanding people. Someone’s silence does not prove inability to speak, absence of thought, or lack of an answer.

Qur’anic ṣāmitūna in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 contrasts calling with remaining silent rather than describing a speech impairment.

The separate Forqan study of B K M examines muteness, silence, and obstruction of expression in detail.

Ṣ M T (ṣamata) and Ṣ M M (ṣamma)

Ṣ M M in its familiar use concerns deafness and an obstructed reception of sound.

Ṣ M T instead concerns silence and absence of speech.

Someone silent may have full hearing; someone who cannot hear is not necessarily unable to speak.

Ṣāmit and aṣamm are therefore different descriptions.

Qur’anic ṣumm appears in contexts of not hearing or receiving the message, whereas ṣāmitūna concerns not speaking.

Shared ṣād and mīm do not establish one root; hearing characteristics should not be transferred into ṣamt’s definition.

Study of Ṣ M M

Ṣ M T (ṣamata) and N Ṭ Q (naṭaqa)

Nuṭq concerns speaking and expressing speech, often contrasting with silence.

Arabic ṣāmit wa-nāṭiq applies that contrast to categorizing property.

Qur’anic nuṭq has broader uses, including human speech and the speech of beings or things God causes to speak.

Ṣāmit instead describes someone or something from which no speech proceeds.

That distinction does not establish every silent thing as lacking perception, life, or a reality beyond appearances. Silence alone does not determine a being’s nature.

6. Silence and its moral value

Arabic ṣamt can accompany dignity, reflection, and avoidance of thoughtless words. Silence may be wise behavior in such circumstances.

Ṣamt itself nevertheless means neither wisdom, piety, nor virtue.

Someone may remain silent to avoid lying or hurting others, to listen to another, or in the face of injustice or a truth that should be spoken.

These situations are not morally equivalent.

The Qur’an sometimes addresses listening and silence during recitation, but elsewhere prohibits knowingly concealing truth:

وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ

wa-lā taktumū al-shahādata

“And do not conceal testimony.”

Al-Baqarah 2:283

Al-Baqarah 2:42 also says:

وَلَا تَلْبِسُوا الْحَقَّ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَتَكْتُمُوا الْحَقَّ وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-lā talbisū al-ḥaqqa bi-al-bāṭili wa-taktumū al-ḥaqqa wa-antum taʿlamūna

“Do not mix truth with falsehood or conceal the truth while you know.”

Silence in different situations can therefore have different consequences.

Ṣamt and kitmān should nevertheless remain distinct. Silence may simply be absence of speech, whereas concealment concerns hiding something whose disclosure matters in that situation.

Not every silence is virtuous, and not every silence conceals truth.

The moral criteria concern the silence’s subject, the person’s knowledge and responsibility, and the consequences of their conduct, rather than simply whether they spoke.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Equivalents for Ṣ M T should follow the form and its particular use.

Ṣamata: became silent, remained silent, or did not speak.

Ṣamt: silence.

Ṣāmit: silent; someone who does not speak.

Ṣāmitūna: silent people; in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193, you are silent or remain silent.

Ṣamūt: taciturn or silent; of certain objects, noiseless.

Ṣimmīt: taciturn and often silent.

Aṣmata: became silent; in some uses, ceased to be able to speak.

Ṣammatahu: silenced him.

Muṣmat: solid, without cavities, completely closed, or single-colored, according to subject and context.

Mālun ṣāmitun: in Arabic property terminology, gold and silver or nonanimal wealth.

For Al-Aʿrāf 7:193, a suitable rendering is:

“It is the same for you whether you call them or remain silent.”

Remain silent naturally and clearly conveys ṣāmitūna here.

Be mute is unsuitable, since the verse does not concern a physical inability to speak.

Listen to their words is also inaccurate: ṣamt alone does not mean inṣāt or auditory attention.

Remain silent about truth or choose wise silence likewise introduces meaning beyond the expression.

8. Conclusion

Ṣ M T chiefly concerns silence and not speaking. Early uses extend this to noiseless objects, such as armor or a sword producing little sound in use.

Another branch applies muṣmat to something closed, solid, without cavities, or unmixed. Closure and absence of an opening can explain the lexical connection without establishing a definitive history of every meaning.

The Qur’an uses only ṣāmitūna. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 it describes the silence of those who may call supposed partners or say nothing.

The context equates invoking those partners with remaining silent in outcome: they cannot respond or help.

The verse’s ṣamt is therefore neither a general command to silence nor praise of it nor a physical inability to speak.

Ṣamt differs from inṣāt, B K M, Ṣ M M, and ṣawm. Silence alone establishes neither attention and understanding, inability to speak, nor positive or negative moral value.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Being silent and not speaking; in other uses, being noiseless, closed, or solid.

Meaning in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193:

صَامِتُونَ (ṣāmitūna): those who are silent or say nothing; here, you remain silent contrasts with you call them.
