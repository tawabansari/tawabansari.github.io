The Qur’anic Root Ḥ R M

Prevention, inviolability, protected bounds, and deprivation

The root ح ر م (Ḥ R M) has an extensive Qur’anic family, including حرام (ḥarām), تحريم (taḥrīm), حرمة (ḥurmah), حرم (ḥaram), and محروم (maḥrūm). These derivatives seem to concern several different subjects: something is forbidden to a person, a place is a sanctuary, a time has inviolability, people are in a state described as حُرُم (ḥurum), and someone else is deprived. Yet these branches share a field: prevention, the closing of access, and the establishment of protected bounds that may not freely be crossed or violated.

Ibn Fāris identifies the origin of this family as “prevention and intensification.” This definition lets us consider two important directions together. Sometimes something is closed off from a person’s access: food forbidden to them, the Garden barred to someone, or a benefit of which they have been deprived. In the other direction, a person, place, time, or right is itself placed within protected bounds that restrict infringement: as in المسجد الحرام (al-masjid al-ḥarām), الشهر الحرام (al-shahr al-ḥarām), and حرمات الله (ḥurumāt Allāh).

We should therefore not begin with only the later legal definition of ḥarām as “an action whose performance is sinful.” That is one important use of the root, but not its entire field.

Qur’anic forms

The root ح ر م (Ḥ R M) occurs 83 times in the Qur’an. The morphological index records 39 instances of the Form II verb حَرَّمَ (ḥarrama), 33 in the nominal family of حَرَام (ḥarām), two of حُرُمَات (ḥurumāt), four of مَحْرُوم (maḥrūm), four of مُحَرَّم (muḥarram), and one of مُحَرَّمَة (muḥarramah).

حَرَّمَ (ḥarrama) — 39 occurrences

حَرَام (ḥarām) — forbidden or inviolable

حَرَم (ḥaram) — protected precinct

حُرُم (ḥurum) — months and people

حُرُمَات (ḥurumāt) — two occurrences

مَحْرُوم (maḥrūm) — four occurrences

مُحَرَّم (muḥarram) — four occurrences

مُحَرَّمَة (muḥarramah) — one occurrence

This morphological classification is not sufficient for semantic analysis, however. Within the nominal group, حَرَام (ḥarām), حَرَم (ḥaram), and حُرُم (ḥurum) have different roles: one may mean forbidden or inviolable, another a protected precinct, and another applies to inviolable months or people in a particular state.

1. The early field: prevention and the closing of a way

In early Arabic, حَرَمَهُ الشيءَ (ḥaramahu al-shayʾa) means withholding something from someone and not letting it reach them. From this field come حرمان (ḥirmān) for deprivation and محروم (maḥrūm) for someone from whom something has been withheld or who has not obtained access to it.

حريم (ḥarīm) is also the area around a house, well, or other thing over which others have no right of unrestricted use. This shows that prevention in Ḥ R M is not merely a relationship between a person and an action; it can establish a boundary around something. This capacity matters for understanding ḥaram and ḥurmah.

Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays contains the lines:

جالَت لِتَصرَعَني فَقُلتُ لَها اِقصِري
إِنّي اِمرُؤٌ صَرعي عَلَيكِ حَرامِ

Jālat li-taṣraʿanī fa-qultu lahā iqṣirī / Innī imruʾun ṣarʿī ʿalayki ḥarāmi.

“She turned to throw me down, and I said to her: desist; I am a man whom it is beyond you to throw down.”

Here ḥarām is not a religious legal ruling. The poet says that throwing him down is beyond the she-camel’s reach and ability. This usage shows that before legal classifications, the field of ḥarām could convey being barred, closed off, and inaccessible.

The notions of a sanctuary and inviolable months were also known in pre-Islamic Arabic. The Qur’an did not invent these words; it used them within the semantic field of its earliest audience.

2. حَرَّمَ (ḥarrama): not every prohibition is a legal ruling

The most frequent derivative in this family is the verb حَرَّمَ (ḥarrama).

In many verses, prohibition does indeed concern what is permitted and forbidden:

إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةَ وَالدَّمَ وَلَحْمَ الْخِنْزِيرِ

Innamā ḥarrama ʿalaykumu l-maytata wa-d-dama wa-laḥma l-khinzīri.

“He has only forbidden you carrion, blood, and the flesh of swine.”

Al-Baqarah 2:173

And:

وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا

Wa-aḥalla llāhu l-bayʿa wa-ḥarrama r-ribā.

“And The God has permitted trade and forbidden ribā.”

Al-Baqarah 2:275

Yet the Qur’an itself contains uses showing that the lexical meaning of حرّم (ḥarrama) is broader than “a legal ruling that something is sinful.”

In the account of Moses:

وَحَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَرَاضِعَ مِنْ قَبْلُ

Wa-ḥarramnā ʿalayhi l-marāḍiʿa min qablu.

“And We had previously barred wet nurses to him.”

Al-Qasas 28:12

The infant Moses is not facing the commission of a sin. The context shows that he would not take the wet nurses’ breasts. Here حرمنا (ḥarramnā) expresses a way being closed and access prevented.

In Al-Maidah 5:26, a land is likewise described:

فَإِنَّهَا مُحَرَّمَةٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً

Fa-innahā muḥarramatun ʿalayhim arbaʿīna sanatan.

“Then it will be barred to them for forty years.”

Here too, the land has not become a “sinful object”; their access to it is closed.

Concerning the Garden:

فَقَدْ حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ الْجَنَّةَ

Fa-qad ḥarrama llāhu ʿalayhi l-jannata.

“The God has indeed barred the Garden to him.”

Al-Maidah 5:72

And concerning the water and provision of the Garden’s inhabitants:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ حَرَّمَهُمَا عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ

Inna llāha ḥarramahumā ʿalā l-kāfirīna.

“The God has barred both to those who commit kufr.”

Al-Araf 7:50

These are not commands telling an addressee, “Do not do this.” They describe access: the way to something has been closed to a person.

The general core of ḥarrama is therefore better understood as “barring and closing off.” Whether this prevention is legislation, punishment, deprivation, impossibility, or restricted access in a particular verse is determined by context.

3. The construction “ḥarrama + ʿalā”: what is closed off to whom?

One very frequent Qur’anic construction is:

حَرَّمَ الشيءَ عَلَى شخص (ḥarrama al-shayʾa ʿalā shakhs)

Something is forbidden or barred to someone.

In Al-Baqarah 2:173:

حَرَّمَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةَ

Ḥarrama ʿalaykumu l-maytata.

“He has forbidden you carrion.”

In Al-Qasas 28:12:

حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَرَاضِعَ

Ḥarramnā ʿalayhi l-marāḍiʿa.

“We barred wet nurses to him.”

In Al-Maidah 5:72:

حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ الْجَنَّةَ

Ḥarrama llāhu ʿalayhi l-jannata.

“The God has barred the Garden to him.”

And in Al-Araf 7:50:

حَرَّمَهُمَا عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ

Ḥarramahumā ʿalā l-kāfirīna.

“He has barred both to those who commit kufr.”

The construction is the same, but the kind of prevention is not.

In the first case, a food is forbidden to the addressees; in the account of Moses, wet nurses become inaccessible to the child; in the case of the Garden, a person is denied entry; and in Al-Araf, water and provision are placed beyond a group’s reach.

The preposition على (ʿalā) therefore identifies the person to whom the prevention or restriction applies, but does not itself determine the kind of prohibition.

This prevents us from automatically translating every occurrence of حرّم عليه (ḥarrama ʿalayhi) into the language of religious law.

4. Ḥarām and ḥalāl: being closed off and being open

The family’s most important Qur’anic contrast is حرام / حلال (ḥarām / ḥalāl):

وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا

Wa-aḥalla llāhu l-bayʿa wa-ḥarrama r-ribā.

“And The God has permitted trade and forbidden ribā.”

And:

لَا تُحَرِّمُوا طَيِّبَاتِ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ

Lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla llāhu lakum.

“Do not forbid the good things that The God has permitted you.”

Al-Maidah 5:87

The second verse is especially significant. Something can be طيب (ṭayyib), good or wholesome, while a person attempts to make it ḥarām for themselves. Thus:

Ḥarām does not equal bad or impure;
nor does ḥalāl equal good or pure.

This family concerns above all whether the way to use or action is open or closed. Goodness, badness, purity, and impurity may be other attributes.

The Qur’an even says of humanly constructed prohibitions:

فَجَعَلْتُمْ مِنْهُ حَرَامًا وَحَلَالًا

Fa-jaʿaltum minhu ḥarāman wa-ḥalālan.

“Then you made some of it forbidden and some permitted.”

Yunus 10:59

And:

هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ

Hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun.

“This is permitted, and this is forbidden.”

An-Nahl 16:116

The word ḥarām by itself therefore does not establish that the prohibition comes from The God. Who imposed it, and its source, must be examined separately.

5. Divine prohibition and humanly constructed prohibition

Surah Al-Anam provides a particularly clear example of this distinction.

Groups had devised restrictions concerning their livestock and crops:

وَأَنْعَامٌ حُرِّمَتْ ظُهُورُهَا

Wa-anʿāmun ḥurrimat ẓuhūruhā.

“And livestock whose backs have been forbidden.”

Al-Anam 6:138

And:

وَحَرَّمُوا مَا رَزَقَهُمُ اللَّهُ افْتِرَاءً عَلَى اللَّهِ

Wa-ḥarramū mā razaqahumu llāhu ftirāʾan ʿalā llāhi.

“And they forbade what The God had provided them, fabricating lies against The God.”

Al-Anam 6:140

The Qur’an then asks:

آلذَّكَرَيْنِ حَرَّمَ أَمِ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

Ā-dh-dhakarayni ḥarrama ami l-unthayayni.

“Did He forbid the two males or the two females?”

And:

قُلْ هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ الَّذِينَ يَشْهَدُونَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ حَرَّمَ هَٰذَا

Qul halumma shuhadāʾakumu lladhīna yashhadūna anna llāha ḥarrama hādhā.

“Say: bring your witnesses who testify that The God has forbidden this.”

Al-Anam 6:150

The Qur’an therefore distinguishes two things:

A human being declares something forbidden;
and The God has forbidden something.

These statements are not identical.

An-Nahl 16:116 states the principle directly:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ

Wa-lā taqūlū li-mā taṣifu alsinatukumu l-kadhiba hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun.

“And do not say, concerning the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is permitted, and this is forbidden.’”

Even in At-Tahrim 66:1, the Prophet is asked:

لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ

Li-ma tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu laka.

“Why do you forbid what The God has permitted you?”

The verb تحرم (tuḥarrimu) therefore does not itself mean issuing a universal divine law. A person may restrict something for themselves, a community may devise prohibitions, and The God may also prohibit something. The source of the prohibition is not determined by the verb itself.

6. Al-masjid al-ḥarām, al-bayt al-ḥarām, and ḥaraman āminan

Another branch of the root moves from forbidding something to a person toward establishing protected bounds around something.

The Qur’an repeatedly speaks of:

الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ (al-masjid al-ḥarām)

and:

الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ (al-bayt al-ḥarām)

as places possessing inviolability.

Ḥarām here cannot simply be translated as “forbidden.” Al-masjid al-ḥarām is not “the forbidden mosque.” This place has inviolability: protected bounds that prohibit certain infringements and actions in relation to it.

The noun حَرَم (ḥaram) makes this direction clear:

أَوَلَمْ نُمَكِّنْ لَهُمْ حَرَمًا آمِنًا

A-wa-lam numakkin lahum ḥaraman āminan.

“Have We not established for them a secure sanctuary?”

Al-Qasas 28:57

And:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا جَعَلْنَا حَرَمًا آمِنًا وَيُتَخَطَّفُ النَّاسُ مِنْ حَوْلِهِمْ

A-wa-lam yaraw annā jaʿalnā ḥaraman āminan wa-yutakhaṭṭafu n-nāsu min ḥawlihim.

“Have they not seen that We have made a secure sanctuary, while people are snatched away all around them?”

Al-Ankabut 29:67

The Qur’an itself qualifies ḥaram with آمن (āmin), secure. Ḥaram and security are therefore not exact synonyms. A ḥaram is a precinct possessing inviolability; security is another quality attributed to it in these verses.

An-Naml 27:91 also says of that city:

رَبَّ هَٰذِهِ الْبَلْدَةِ الَّذِي حَرَّمَهَا

Rabba hādhihi l-baldati lladhī ḥarramahā.

“The Lord of this city, who made it inviolable.”

There is thus a direct relationship between the verb حرّم (ḥarrama) and the noun حرم (ḥaram): protected bounds can be established for something, closing it to certain uses or infringements.

7. Months that are ḥurum and people who are ḥurum

The same field applies to time:

الشَّهْرُ الْحَرَامُ بِالشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ

Ash-shahru l-ḥarāmu bi-sh-shahri l-ḥarāmi.

“The inviolable month for the inviolable month.”

Al-Baqarah 2:194

And:

مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ

Minhā arbaʿatun ḥurumun.

“Four of them are inviolable.”

At-Tawbah 9:36

An inviolable month is not one whose existence or use is itself forbidden. It is a time possessing inviolability, within which certain conduct is restricted.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 directly raises one such restriction:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ قِتَالٍ فِيهِ

Yasʾalūnaka ʿani sh-shahri l-ḥarāmi qitālin fīhi.

“They ask you about the inviolable month: fighting within it.”

Protected bounds can therefore encompass time as well as place.

وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ (wa-antum ḥurumun)

In Al-Maidah:

غَيْرَ مُحِلِّي الصَّيْدِ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ

Ghayra muḥillī ṣ-ṣaydi wa-antum ḥurumun.

“Not permitting hunting while you are in a state of ḥurum.”

Al-Maidah 5:1

لَا تَقْتُلُوا الصَّيْدَ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ

Lā taqtulū ṣ-ṣayda wa-antum ḥurumun.

“Do not kill game while you are in a state of ḥurum.”

Al-Maidah 5:95

وَحُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَرِّ مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا

Wa-ḥurrima ʿalaykum ṣaydu l-barri mā dumtum ḥuruman.

“And land game is forbidden to you as long as you remain in a state of ḥurum.”

Al-Maidah 5:96

In these three passages, the Qur’an does not say مُحْرِمون (muḥrimūn), and the word إحرام (iḥrām) does not appear in the verses either. The Qur’anic form is حُرُم (ḥurum).

From the verses themselves, we can say that these people are in a state of inviolability and restriction, one explicit effect of which is the restriction on land hunting.

In later ritual language, iḥrām became the established term for this state. Using it in translation may be practical, but the entire body of later legal details about iḥrām should not be incorporated into the meaning of ḥurum itself.

8. حُرُمَات (ḥurumāt): protected bounds that must not be violated

Ḥurumāt occurs twice in the Qur’an:

وَالْحُرُمَاتُ قِصَاصٌ

Wa-l-ḥurumātu qiṣāṣun.

“And inviolable matters are subject to equal requital.”

Al-Baqarah 2:194

And:

وَمَنْ يُعَظِّمْ حُرُمَاتِ اللَّهِ فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَهُ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِ

Wa-man yuʿaẓẓim ḥurumāti llāhi fa-huwa khayrun lahu ʿinda rabbihi.

“Whoever honors the inviolable matters of The God, that is better for him with his Lord.”

Al-Hajj 22:30

Ḥurumāt does not mean only “forbidden acts.” Its field includes things and boundaries whose inviolability must not be breached.

For this reason, حرمات الله (ḥurumāt Allāh) cannot be reduced without explanation to “The God’s prohibitions.” “The inviolable matters of The God” or “things possessing inviolability” better preserves its scope.

This branch also makes the relationship between prevention and احترام (iḥtirām), respect, understandable: something possesses inviolability because it cannot be treated as though it had no protected bounds. Yet iḥtirām itself does not occur as a derivative of this root in the Qur’an, and its modern meaning should not replace the root’s definition.

9. مَحْرُوم (maḥrūm): someone to whom something remains inaccessible

The four occurrences of maḥrūm show the other side of the same relationship.

In Adh-Dhariyat 51:19:

وَفِي أَمْوَالِهِمْ حَقٌّ لِلسَّائِلِ وَالْمَحْرُومِ

Wa-fī amwālihim ḥaqqun li-s-sāʾili wa-l-maḥrūmi.

“And in their wealth is a right for the one who asks and the deprived.”

And in Al-Maarij 70:24–25:

وَالَّذِينَ فِي أَمْوَالِهِمْ حَقٌّ مَعْلُومٌ
لِلسَّائِلِ وَالْمَحْرُومِ

Wa-lladhīna fī amwālihim ḥaqqun maʿlūmun / Li-s-sāʾili wa-l-maḥrūmi.

“And those in whose wealth is a known right, for the one who asks and the deprived.”

Later sources offer different explanations of who maḥrūm refers to in these verses: someone who does not ask, someone whose earning has failed, someone who has lost their property, and others. None of these examples should become the word’s lexical definition.

Two other Qur’anic uses clarify the basic meaning. In Al-Qalam 68:27, the owners of the lost garden say:

بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ

Bal naḥnu maḥrūmūna.

“Rather, we have been deprived.”

In Al-Waqiah 56:67, after imagining the destruction of a crop, it is likewise said:

بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ

Bal naḥnu maḥrūmūna.

“Rather, we have been deprived.”

In both cases, people have missed out on a benefit they expected.

Thus:

Ḥarām may describe something to which access has been closed;
maḥrūm is the person who has been denied that access.

10. Differences from nearby roots

Ḥ R M (ḥaram) compared with Ḥ L L (ḥalal): being closed and being open

The most important contrast within the Qur’an is between Ḥ R M and ح ل ل (Ḥ L L).

The Qur’an says:

وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا

Wa-aḥalla llāhu l-bayʿa wa-ḥarrama r-ribā.

“And The God has permitted trade and forbidden ribā.”

And:

لَا تُحَرِّمُوا طَيِّبَاتِ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ

Lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla llāhu lakum.

“Do not forbid the good things that The God has permitted you.”

The lexical core of Ḥ L L is connected with unfastening and opening. From this field, حلال (ḥalāl) is something whose way is not closed.

The two families’ tendencies can be understood as:

Ḥ L L: opening and the presence of permission;
Ḥ R M: closure and the presence of prevention or protected bounds.

This is a difference in tendency. Every use of ḥalāl should not become an image of an untied knot, nor every use of ḥarām an actual wall.

Ḥ R M (ḥaram) compared with M N AYN (manaʿa): direct prevention and protected bounds

The root م ن ع (M N AYN; M N ʿ) is one of the closest families to Ḥ R M. Ibn Fāris identifies its origin as خلاف إعطاء (khilāf iʿṭāʾ), the opposite of giving: something is not given or handed over to another. The Qur’an also uses it in direct constructions of prevention:

مَا مَنَعَكَ أَلَّا تَسْجُدَ

Mā manaʿaka allā tasjuda.

Al-Araf 7:12

“What prevented you, so that you did not prostrate?”

And:

وَمَا مَنَعَ النَّاسَ أَنْ يُؤْمِنُوا

Wa-mā manaʿa n-nāsa an yuʾminū.

“And what prevented people from believing?”

Al-Isra 17:94

And concerning the measure:

مُنِعَ مِنَّا الْكَيْلُ

Muniʿa minnā l-kaylu.

“The measure has been withheld from us.”

Yusuf 12:63

In these cases, منع (manʿ) emphasizes the act of preventing or withholding itself.

Ḥ R M can have the same result: حرمنا عليه المراضع (ḥarramnā ʿalayhi al-marāḍiʿa) effectively bars wet nurses. Yet the family of Ḥ R M has another range that M N AYN does not express in the same way: ḥaram, shahr ḥarām, masjid ḥarām, ḥurumāt, and the state of ḥurum. These cases involve more than an act of prevention; there is also a protected precinct or a state carrying restrictions.

The distinction should therefore not be framed as “prevention is always temporary, while ḥarām is always permanent or institutional.” The Qur’an supplies no such absolute rule. A more cautious distinction is:

M N AYN primarily expresses preventing, withholding, or obstructing itself;
Ḥ R M, in addition to prevention, can describe a state in which access or infringement is restricted because of inviolability and protected bounds.

Ḥ R M (ḥaram) compared with Q D S (qadas): protected bounds and purity

In religious English, words such as “sacred” and “holy” may be used for ḥarām, ḥaram, and muqaddas so interchangeably that their distinct Arabic roots disappear from view.

The Qur’an, however, uses these as separate families.

Concerning Mecca, it says:

حَرَمًا آمِنًا

Ḥaraman āminan.

“A secure sanctuary.”

It also speaks of المسجد الحرام (al-masjid al-ḥarām) and البيت الحرام (al-bayt al-ḥarām).

By contrast, concerning the place where Moses was addressed, it says:

إِنَّكَ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

Innaka bi-l-wādi l-muqaddasi ṭuwan.

“You are in the purified valley, Ṭuwā.”

Ta-Ha 20:12

And:

إِذْ نَادَاهُ رَبُّهُ بِالْوَادِ الْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

Idh nādāhu rabbuhu bi-l-wādi l-muqaddasi ṭuwan.

“When his Lord called to him in the purified valley, Ṭuwā.”

An-Naziat 79:16

The root ق د س (Q D S) has its own Qur’anic family, and early lexical sources connect it with cleanness and purity.

Muqaddas and ḥarām should therefore not be assumed to be lexical synonyms.

In Ḥ R M, prevention and protected bounds are prominent.
In Q D S, according to the available lexical evidence, purity and freedom from impurity are prominent.

A place may possess inviolability and also be considered holy in religious language and culture. But a shared referent does not make two roots synonymous. Determining the precise field of Q D S requires studying that root independently.

11. The legal term ḥarām and the Qur’an’s broader meaning

In later legal systems, ḥarām became a particular category of the ruling governing human conduct, with a technical definition alongside obligatory, recommended, discouraged, and permissible.

This is a real historical use, but it should not be the starting point for defining the root in the Qur’an.

In the Qur’an:

food is forbidden;
ribā is forbidden;
expelling people is forbidden;
wet nurses are barred to a child;
a land becomes inaccessible to a people;
the Garden is closed to someone;
a mosque and a House are ḥarām, possessing inviolability;
a time is an inviolable month;
people are ḥurum;
and another person is maḥrūm, deprived.

No single narrow legal definition covers this breadth.

When examining a verse, we should therefore first ask: What has been barred to whom, or from what action, or what has been placed within protected bounds? If the context then establishes a legislative prohibition, it becomes appropriate to discuss a legal ruling.

Translation guidance

حَرَّمَ (ḥarrama) in legislative verses may be rendered “forbade” or “prohibited.” In passages such as Al-Qasas 28:12, “barred” is more precise.

حَرَام (ḥarām) concerning food or conduct is often “forbidden” or “prohibited.” Concerning the mosque, the House, or a month, the element of “possessing inviolability” must remain clear.

حَرَم (ḥaram) is “sanctuary” or “an inviolable precinct,” not merely “a forbidden place.”

حُرُم (ḥurum) concerning months means “inviolable.” Concerning people in Al-Maidah, it describes a particular state of inviolability and restriction for which the term iḥrām later became established.

حُرُمَات (ḥurumāt) is “inviolable matters” or “things possessing inviolability,” not simply “prohibitions.”

مَحْرُوم (maḥrūm) is “deprived,” someone denied access or a benefit; the cause of the deprivation is not determined by the word itself.

مُحَرَّم (muḥarram) may mean “forbidden,” “inaccessible,” or “inviolable,” depending on context.

Conclusion

The root ح ر م (Ḥ R M) cannot be explained solely through the later definition “ḥarām is an act whose performance is sinful.” The family’s core is broader: prevention, the closing of access, and the establishment of protected bounds that cannot or may not freely be crossed or violated.

The Qur’an itself reveals this breadth. حرمنا عليه المراضع (ḥarramnā ʿalayhi al-marāḍiʿa) concerns an infant to whom wet nurses are inaccessible. محرمة عليهم أربعين سنة (muḥarramatun ʿalayhim arbaʿīna sanatan) bars a people’s access to a land. حرم الله عليه الجنة (ḥarrama Allāhu ʿalayhi al-jannata) deprives someone of the Garden. Alongside these, carrion, ribā, and certain relationships are prohibited at the level of a ruling.

The construction ḥarrama + ʿalā shows that something can be barred to a person or group, but ʿalā itself does not determine the kind of prevention. Legislation, punishment, lack of access, and deprivation can all appear in this construction.

In the other direction, masjid al-ḥarām, bayt al-ḥarām, al-shahr al-ḥarām, and ḥaram āmin show that the root does more than withhold something from a person: it can establish protected bounds around a place, time, or matter, restricting infringement. Ḥurumāt Allāh can be understood in the same direction.

Maḥrūm is the personal side of this relationship: someone who has missed out on something or has not received an expected benefit.

Comparison with nearby roots clarifies the boundaries. Ḥ L L tends toward opening and permission, standing opposite Ḥ R M. M N AYN primarily expresses preventing and withholding itself, while Ḥ R M can also establish protected bounds and a state of inviolability. Q D S is a separate family connected in lexical evidence with purity and freedom from impurity; muqaddas and ḥarām should therefore not be treated as synonyms merely because of their cultural proximity.

A cautious synthesis of the root is:

Ḥ R M: a family centered on prevention and the establishment of protected bounds. Something can be closed off to a person and made forbidden to them, or can itself be placed within bounds that restrict infringement. This field makes sense of prohibition, ḥarām, ḥaram, inviolability, the state of ḥurum, and deprivation. The precise kind of prevention—ruling, deprivation, lack of access, a restriction in time or place, or protection from infringement—is determined by each verse’s construction and context.
