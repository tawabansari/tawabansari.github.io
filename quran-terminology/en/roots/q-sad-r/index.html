Early Arabic Q Ṣ R has two closely related semantic branches. One concerns shortness, shortening, and falling short of a full extent. The other concerns restricting something, keeping it within a defined range, and preventing it from extending beyond that range.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains this line:

لَوْلَا حَبَائِلُ مِنْ نُعْمٍ عَلِقْتُ بِهَا / لَأَقْصَرَ الْقَلْبُ عَنْهَا أَيَّ إِقْصَارِ

Were it not for the bonds of attachment to Nuʿm in which I am caught, my heart would have turned away from her altogether.

Nuʿm is the beloved’s name. Iqṣār here means ceasing and relinquishing attachment to her. The line is also transmitted with minor differences in wording.

This transmitted poem illustrates a non-material use of the root in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Shortening objects, restricting movement, and naming large enclosed buildings qaṣr are also recorded in classical Arabic lexicons. The evidence presented here does not independently establish the precise pre-Islamic date of every usage or of this particular poetic line.

Forms examined: قَصَرَ, قَصُرَ, قَصْر, قِصَر, قُصُور, قَصِير, قَاصِر, قَاصِرَة, قَصَّرَ, تَقْصِير, مُقَصِّر, أَقْصَرَ, إِقْصَار, مَقْصُور, مَقْصُورَة, اِقْتَصَرَ, and اِقْتِصَار.

Qur’anic forms: تَقْصُرُوا, يُقْصِرُونَ, مُقَصِّرِين, قَصْر, قُصُور, قَاصِرَات, and مَقْصُورَات. The root occurs eleven times in eleven verses. Qaṣr and its plural quṣūr together occur four times, qāṣirāt three times, and each of the remaining four forms once. Verse 7:201 is quoted for context and is not an additional occurrence of this root.

1. Core meanings and semantic branches

Being short and shortening

Qiṣar contrasts with length. Something short in size, height, or extent is called qaṣīr.

Qaṣṣara l-ḥabl means to shorten a rope; qaṣṣara l-shaʿr means to shorten the hair.

The idea is not limited to physical dimensions. Speech may be shortened, as may the time taken to perform a task.

Qaṣara ʿani l-shayʾ can mean falling short of something or failing to reach it. For example, an arrow that falls before reaching its target can be described as follows:

Qaṣara l-sahmu ʿani l-hadaf: the arrow fell short of the target.

Here the arrow has not reached the intended endpoint of its course.

Taqṣīr in a responsibility similarly denotes failing to perform a task to the extent expected.

Restricting and ceasing

Qaṣara l-shayʾa ʿalā kadhā means to restrict something to a particular use or scope, without extending it further.

For example, someone who dedicates an income to meeting family needs limits it to a defined use.

Iqtaṣara ʿalā l-shayʾ means to be content with something or confine oneself to it.

Aqṣara ʿanhu can mean to desist from an activity even while still able to continue it.

Failing through inability differs from stopping despite the ability to continue. Some formations and uses of the root make this distinction, although the Arabic forms also overlap.

Qaṣr: a large, enclosed building

Qaṣr denotes a palace, mansion, or substantial building, sometimes fortified, whose space is set apart from its surroundings.

Maqṣūrah can denote part of a building separated from other parts by a wall or barrier.

This branch has a semantic connection with delimiting space and keeping something within a defined area.

That explains a relationship among meanings; it does not, by itself, establish the historical origin of the building’s name.

Quṣūr meaning palaces must also be distinguished from quṣūr meaning inability or falling short. Their written form is the same, but syntax and context distinguish their meanings.

2. Shortening salat and shortening hair

Shortening salat

وَإِذا ضَرَبتُم فِى الأَرضِ فَلَيسَ عَلَيكُم جُناحٌ أَن تَقصُروا مِنَ الصَّلوٰةِ إِن خِفتُم أَن يَفتِنَكُمُ الَّذينَ كَفَروا إِنَّ الكٰفِرينَ كانوا لَكُم عَدُوًّا مُبينًا

Wa-idhā ḍarabtum fī l-arḍi fa-laysa ʿalaykum junāḥun an taqṣurū mina l-ṣalāti in khiftum an yaftinakumu lladhīna kafarū. Inna l-kāfirīna kānū lakum ʿaduwwan mubīnā.

When you travel through the land, there is no blame upon you for shortening salat if you fear that those who disbelieve may subject you to harm. The disbelievers have indeed been an open enemy to you.

An-Nisa 4:101

Taqṣurū is a form I imperfect verb made subjunctive after an, with the final nūn omitted.

The construction involving taqṣurū and salat denotes reducing or shortening it relative to its usual form.

The verse describes travel in conditions of danger. Its no-blame construction removes blame for shortening salat under the circumstances stated.

The condition “if you fear” belongs to the verse and must not be omitted from its translation or from discussion of what it states.

The following verse, 4:102, explains the performance of salat in confrontation with an enemy and the need to remain prepared and vigilant.

The verb qaṣr alone does not specify the amount of shortening, the number of units, or procedural details. It denotes a reduction from the usual form.

This usage illustrates attention to people’s actual circumstances. An act of worship under danger need not be performed in the same manner or extent as under security and calm.

Shortening hair in the Sacred Mosque setting

لَقَد صَدَقَ اللَّهُ رَسولَهُ الرُّءيا بِالحَقِّ لَتَدخُلُنَّ المَسجِدَ الحَرامَ إِن شاءَ اللَّهُ ءامِنينَ مُحَلِّقينَ رُءوسَكُم وَمُقَصِّرينَ لا تَخافونَ فَعَلِمَ ما لَم تَعلَموا فَجَعَلَ مِن دونِ ذٰلِكَ فَتحًا قَريبًا

La-qad ṣadaqa llāhu rasūlahu l-ruʾyā bi-l-ḥaqq. La-tadkhulunna l-masjida l-ḥarāma in shāʾa llāhu āminīna muḥalliqīna ruʾūsakum wa-muqaṣṣirīna lā takhāfūn. Fa-ʿalima mā lam taʿlamū fa-jaʿala min dūni dhālika fatḥan qarībā.

The God has indeed fulfilled His Messenger’s vision in truth: you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, if The God wills, in safety, with your heads shaved or your hair shortened, without fear. He knew what you did not know and granted, before that, a near opening.

Al-Fath 48:27

Muqaṣṣirīn is a masculine plural active participle of form II, denoting those who shorten their hair.

The verse uses two different roots side by side:

Muḥalliqīn refers to shaving the head, while muqaṣṣirīn refers to shortening the hair.

Ḥalq concerns removing hair by shaving; taqṣīr concerns reducing its length without necessarily removing it completely.

Both words should therefore not be translated as shaving the head.

Taqṣīr here does not mean negligence or falling short in a duty. A word that can concern a failure of responsibility in another context here denotes a specific physical act.

3. Iqṣār: ceasing a continuing behaviour

وَإِخوٰنُهُم يَمُدّونَهُم فِى الغَىِّ ثُمَّ لا يُقصِرونَ

Wa-ikhwānuhum yamuddūnahum fī l-ghayyi thumma lā yuqṣirūn.

As for their companions, they draw them further into error, then do not desist.

Al-A'raf 7:202

Yuqṣirūna is a form IV imperfect verb. Here iqṣār means ceasing, desisting, or not continuing an activity.

The preceding verse states:

إِنَّ الَّذينَ اتَّقَوا إِذا مَسَّهُم طٰئِفٌ مِنَ الشَّيطٰنِ تَذَكَّروا فَإِذا هُم مُبصِرونَ

Inna lladhīna ittaqaw idhā massahum ṭāʾifun mina l-shayṭāni tadhakkarū fa-idhā hum mubṣirūn.

Those who practise taqwa, when a visitation from Satan touches them, remember, and then they see clearly.

Al-A'raf 7:201

Together, the verses describe two different responses to error.

Some remember and recover their insight when exposed to temptation; the other group continues to be drawn into error and does not break the pattern.

The translation of 7:202 follows the grammatical reading in which the devils draw their human companions further into error: ikhwānuhum is the topic, and the clause that follows reports what is done to those companions. The reference of “they do not desist” has also been read in two ways: the devils do not cease encouraging error, or the misguided people do not cease their conduct.

These readings can illuminate the two sides of the relationship, but they are grammatical interpretations of the pronouns; they should not be merged into a claim that the wording explicitly names both subjects at once.

In either case, iqṣār retains the sense of desisting from an activity. Its negation expresses continuation.

4. Qaṣr and quṣūr: palaces and large buildings

The palaces of Thamūd

وَاذكُروا إِذ جَعَلَكُم خُلَفاءَ مِن بَعدِ عادٍ وَبَوَّأَكُم فِى الأَرضِ تَتَّخِذونَ مِن سُهولِها قُصورًا وَتَنحِتونَ الجِبالَ بُيوتًا فَاذكُروا ءالاءَ اللَّهِ وَلا تَعثَوا فِى الأَرضِ مُفسِدينَ

Wa-dhkurū idh jaʿalakum khulafāʾa min baʿdi ʿĀdin wa-bawwaʾakum fī l-arḍi tattakhidhūna min suhūlihā quṣūran wa-tanḥitūna l-jibāla buyūtā. Fa-dhkurū ālāʾa llāhi wa-lā taʿthaw fī l-arḍi mufsidīn.

Remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and settled you in the land: you make palaces on its plains and carve the mountains into houses. Remember the blessings of The God, and do not act ruinously upon the earth, spreading corruption.

Al-A'raf 7:74

The verse distinguishes two ways of making dwellings: building palaces on plains and carving houses into mountains.

Quṣūr is the broken plural of qaṣr, meaning palaces or substantial buildings.

Such construction was among Thamūd’s capabilities and achievements. Ṣāliḥ does not call them to abandon building homes and palaces; he connects their resources with responsibility before The God and avoidance of corruption.

Architectural achievement and extensive material resources do not, in themselves, prove either a society’s corruption or its righteousness.

A lofty palace and a destroyed community

فَكَأَيِّن مِن قَريَةٍ أَهلَكنٰها وَهِىَ ظالِمَةٌ فَهِىَ خاوِيَةٌ عَلىٰ عُروشِها وَبِئرٍ مُعَطَّلَةٍ وَقَصرٍ مَشيدٍ

Fa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā wa-hiya ẓālimatun fa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā wa-biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa-qaṣrin mashīd.

How many a settlement We destroyed while it was doing wrong, leaving it collapsed upon its roofs; and how many an abandoned well and a lofty palace!

Al-Hajj 22:45

Qaṣrin mashīd describes a lofty, firmly built, or plastered palace. Different lexical explanations of mashīd concern its elevation or construction and finish.

The unused well and the palace provide two images of what remains of a community.

A building may remain standing long after its community has perished. Strong palace walls do not guarantee the survival of their owners’ social and political order.

The following verse, 22:46, calls people to travel through the land and reflect on such histories.

A palace as a material resource

تَبارَكَ الَّذى إِن شاءَ جَعَلَ لَكَ خَيرًا مِن ذٰلِكَ جَنّٰتٍ تَجرى مِن تَحتِهَا الأَنهٰرُ وَيَجعَل لَكَ قُصورًا

Tabāraka lladhī in shāʾa jaʿala laka khayran min dhālika jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā l-anhāru wa-yajʿal laka quṣūrā.

Blessed is He who, if He wills, can provide you with better than that: gardens beneath which rivers flow, and provide you with palaces.

Al-Furqan 25:10

In the preceding verses, Muhammad’s opponents use his lack of a treasure or garden as grounds for rejecting his mission.

The response speaks of The God’s power to provide better resources, including gardens and palaces.

Qaṣr here is neither a symbol of sin nor a symbol of virtue. A palace is a material resource.

A person’s claim to a divine mission is not established by the size of a home, wealth, or economic standing.

Sparks like palaces

إِنَّها تَرمى بِشَرَرٍ كَالقَصرِ

Innahā tarmī bi-shararin ka-l-qaṣr.

It casts sparks like a palace.

Al-Mursalat 77:32

Qaṣr is used here in a simile.

In the familiar reading, the sparks are compared with a palace in size.

Lexical discussions also record an explanation involving large pieces of wood or tree trunks. A transmitted report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās connects the term with wood stored for winter. This is reported linguistic evidence, not an independently dated record proving the word’s earliest meaning.

A reported variant vocalization with a short a on ṣād, ka-l-qaṣar, is also associated with trunks or thick portions of trees. This is distinguished here from the received text quoted above, ka-l-qaṣr, with a silent ṣād.

Both explanations convey an image of enormous, terrifying sparks.

Palace fits the other Qur’anic uses of qaṣr and is the rendering adopted here. Large pieces of wood remain a reported lexical alternative in the explanation, rather than being silently substituted into the verse.

5. Qāṣirāt al-ṭarf and maqṣūrāt: restricting the gaze and being kept within a place

Qāṣirāt al-ṭarf

The Qur’an uses this expression in three verses concerning the blessings of the Garden.

وَعِندَهُم قٰصِرٰتُ الطَّرفِ عينٌ

Wa-ʿindahum qāṣirātu l-ṭarfi ʿīn.

With them are wide-eyed companions who restrict their gaze.

As-Saffat 37:48

وَعِندَهُم قٰصِرٰتُ الطَّرفِ أَترابٌ

Wa-ʿindahum qāṣirātu l-ṭarfi atrāb.

With them are companions of equal age who restrict their gaze.

Sad 38:52

فيهِنَّ قٰصِرٰتُ الطَّرفِ لَم يَطمِثهُنَّ إِنسٌ قَبلَهُم وَلا جانٌّ

Fīhinna qāṣirātu l-ṭarfi lam yaṭmithhunna insun qablahum wa-lā jānn.

In them are companions who restrict their gaze, whom no human or jinn has had sexual contact with before them.

Ar-Rahman 55:56

Qāṣirāt is the feminine plural active participle of qaṣara. Al-ṭarf here concerns the gaze and its reach.

In Arabic, qāṣirat al-ṭarf can describe a woman who confines her gaze and attention to her spouse rather than directing them toward others.

The expression does not describe short eyes, impaired vision, or a physical inability to see other things.

Qaṣr here concerns restricting the gaze rather than extending it beyond a particular scope.

The morphology matters: qāṣirāt is an active participle, attributing the restriction of the gaze to those described.

Replacing this with a passive expression such as “those whose gaze has been restricted” loses that grammatical distinction. The active form alone, however, does not settle every question about freedom, compulsion, or the circumstances of the action.

In all three verses the description belongs to the Garden. It does not, by itself, establish a general rule for restricting women’s freedom in worldly life.

Maqṣūrāt fī l-khiyām

حورٌ مَقصورٰتٌ فِى الخِيامِ

Ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī l-khiyām.

Ḥūr kept within the tents.

Ar-Rahman 55:72

Maqṣūrāt is the feminine plural passive participle of qaṣara.

Unlike the gaze expression, this verse describes the condition of those kept or placed within tents, not the act of restricting their own gaze.

The difference between the forms is clear:

  • Qāṣirāt: those who restrict something, here their gaze.
  • Maqṣūrāt: those placed or kept within a defined space.

Lexical explanations describe maqṣūrāt in this context through being kept, reserved, or sheltered in a particular place.

The passive form alone does not establish coercion, imprisonment, unhappiness, or loss of choice. It describes a condition resulting from an action, not every circumstance or quality of that condition.

The verse depicts the Garden. The actual nature of these dwellings and the life of their occupants cannot be determined from the word maqṣūrāt alone.

6. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṣ R (qaṣr) and N Q Ṣ (naqṣ)

Naqṣ concerns decrease or deficiency in quantity, number, or completeness.

Qaṣr more particularly concerns shortening an extent, limiting a dimension, or falling short of the endpoint of a course or activity.

Shortening a rope can be described through qaṣr and also as a decrease in length. A decrease in the number of people or the amount of wealth, however, is not necessarily expressed through qaṣr.

Q Ṣ R (taqṣīr) and Ḥ L Q (ḥalq)

In the hair-related branch, ḥalq means shaving or removing hair, while taqṣīr means shortening it.

The Qur’an uses both forms together in 48:27.

Taqṣīr here is not incomplete shaving; it is a different way of changing the length of the hair.

Q Ṣ R (qaṣr) and Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣr)

Ḥaṣr concerns restricting, enclosing, or preventing movement or action.

One branch of qaṣr likewise concerns limiting something so it does not extend beyond a defined scope.

The roots overlap substantially. Qaṣr also highlights shortening an extent or keeping to a defined range, whereas ḥaṣr can place greater emphasis on an obstacle and restricted possibilities of movement or action.

Q Ṣ R (qaṣr al-ṭarf) and Gh Ḍ Ḍ (ghaḍḍ al-baṣar)

Ghaḍḍ concerns reducing, lowering, or restraining something.

In 24:30 and 24:31, believing men and women are called to lower their gaze.

Qaṣr al-ṭarf more particularly concerns keeping the gaze within a defined range rather than directing it beyond that range.

The expressions are close but not identical: ghaḍḍ al-baṣar emphasizes reduction and restraint; qaṣr al-ṭarf emphasizes limiting the direction and object of attention.

Qāṣirāt al-ṭarf describes the Garden, whereas lowering the gaze in Sūrat al-Nūr is a command concerning believing men and women in worldly life.

Q Ṣ R (qaṣr) and B Y T (bayt)

Bayt denotes a house or dwelling and has a broad semantic range.

Qaṣr denotes a substantial building, palace, or mansion and may include features such as strength or enclosure.

In 7:74, the Qur’an uses quṣūr for the buildings made on the plains and buyūt for dwellings carved into mountains.

A palace can be a dwelling, but not every house is a palace.

7. Translation choices for Forqan

Render taqṣurū in 4:101 as reduce or shorten; yuqṣirūna in 7:202 as desist; and muqaṣṣirīn in 48:27 as those shortening their hair.

In architectural contexts, render qaṣr and quṣūr as palace, mansion, and palaces.

Render qāṣirāt al-ṭarf as those who restrict their gaze, and maqṣūrāt fī l-khiyām as those kept within the tents.

Preserve the distinction between the active and passive participles.

Brief root meaning: Being short or shortening; falling short of a full extent; restricting or keeping within a defined scope; and ceasing an activity. In the nominal branch, a palace or large enclosed building.

Conclusion

Q Ṣ R has two related semantic axes: shortening something’s extent and restricting it to a defined measure or scope.

The Qur’an uses this family for shortening salat and hair, ceasing a behaviour, naming palaces, restricting the gaze, and being kept within a place.

The root does not in itself imply inability, negligence, or coercion. Shortening salat in danger, cutting hair, and restricting the gaze are uses that do not carry such a negative meaning in themselves.

Qaṣr as the name of a building likewise does not mean that the building is short in height. It belongs to the branch concerning substantial, enclosed buildings.

To understand each derivative, ask what is shortened or restricted, who ceases an activity, and in what context the limit is described.
