Qur’anic Root J H D

Serious effort, exerting one’s capacity, enduring hardship, and perseverance toward a goal

The Arabic root ج ه د concerns effort involving exertion and difficulty. It may concern accomplishing something, pursuing a goal, defending a right, confronting opposition, or even pressing someone to accept a wrongful demand. The root therefore neither means war exclusively nor guarantees that the effort’s goal is right.

Qur’anic settings vary: limited capacity for financial contribution, emphatic oaths, parental pressure upon a child, striving in God’s way, confronting opponents through the divine message, and activities in contexts of battle and defense.

This breadth is fundamental to understanding jihad. Calling a war jihad does not alone make it lawful or divine; nor can military effort in some Qur’anic uses be denied. Root meaning, word structure, accompanying expressions, and verse context must establish each meaning.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important forms

The principal Arabic forms include:

  • جَهَدَ ـ يَجْهَدُ: strove, subjected oneself or another to hardship, exerted capacity.
  • جَهْد: hardship, intense effort, utmost capacity, intensity or emphasis.
  • جُهْد: capacity, ability, and the amount of energy a person can exert.
  • جَاهَدَ ـ يُجَاهِدُ: strove seriously, persevered and confronted difficulty or opposition.
  • جِهَاد: serious effort and confrontation involving exertion.
  • مُجَاهِد: someone undertaking such effort or confrontation.
  • مُجَاهَدَة: serious striving, confrontation, and exertion of capacity.
  • أَجْهَدَ: subjected to hardship, exhausted or pushed someone’s or something’s capacity to its limit.
  • اِجْتَهَدَ ـ اِجْتِهَاد: exerted one’s utmost to achieve a purpose.
  • مَجْهُود: something effort has been spent on, or someone exhausted through pressure and hardship.

The Qur’an uses the root 41 times in 36 verses, in five morphological categories: جَهْد, جُهْد, inflected forms of جَاهَدَ, جِهَاد, and مُجَاهِدِين/مُجَاهِدُون. The counts are five jahd, one juhd, 27 Form III verbs, four jihād, and four active participles.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 41 occurrences in five morphological groups

اِجْتَهَدَ, اِجْتِهَاد, أَجْهَدَ, and مَجْهُود do not occur in the Qur’an, although they became important in Arabic usage and later scholarly and legal terminology.

Semantic core: effort in the face of difficulty

Ibn Fāris in Maqāyīs al-Lughah relates ج ه د to مَشَقَّة, hardship. Other dictionaries also explain the family through capacity, scope, utmost extent, and intensity.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: the core of hardship

These meanings are related, not identical. Capacity concerns available ability; hardship concerns experienced difficulty and pressure; utmost extent and intensity indicate effort or pressure reaching a high degree.

The relationship among these uses supports this semantic core: seriously exerting capacity in an undertaking requiring effort, pressure, or confrontation. Brief root meaning: Serious effort and exertion of capacity in the face of difficulty; jahd also expresses intensity and hardship, and juhd ability and capacity.

Hardship matters, but every occurrence need not imply the most extreme suffering or exhaustion. One concerns available capacity, another intensity of effort, another difficulty imposed upon someone.

The family differs from slight, passing, inconsequential effort. Its presence nevertheless does not prove that someone’s entire physical and psychological capacity has been exhausted.

Distinguishing جَهْد and جُهْد

Some Arabic lexical traditions distinguish the vocalizations: jahd, with fatḥah on jīm, more often concerns hardship, intensity, and utmost exertion; juhd, with ḍammah, capacity and ability.

The distinction is not uniform or absolute across sources. Some lexicographers use both for capacity, and some also record hardship for both.

One therefore cannot establish that jahd always means only suffering and juhd only capacity.

Qur’anic جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ emphasizes an oath’s intensity, whereas جُهْدَهُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:79 concerns people’s limited capacity and resources. This fits the lexical tendency without separating all historical uses absolutely.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān: vocalization and range of jahd and juhd

2. Early Arabic usage

Effort and pressure in everyday life

Arabic جَهَدَ الرَّجُلُ فِي الْأَمْرِ describes someone exerting serious effort in an undertaking.

جَهَدَ دَابَّتَهُ or أَجْهَدَهَا concerns pressing a riding or pack animal and, in lexical explanations, driving it beyond its capacity. Hardship is especially visible: the act involves imposing pressure on another being, not only the actor’s own effort.

Dictionaries also record جَهَدَهُ الْمَرَضُ for illness exhausting and weakening a person. Here the person is not pursuing a goal: hardship is imposed upon them.

The family can thus express active striving and being subjected to pressure.

Early Arabic poetic evidence

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes a line attributed to Al-Aʿshā about movement and pressure upon a mount, explaining جَهَدَ and أَجْهَدَ. It shows the family’s use for bodily exertion and pressure.

Lexical sources also quote Al-Shammākh describing abundant camel milk, with variants including مَجْهُود and غَيْرِ مَجْهُود. One explanation connects majhūd with extracting its butter through effort. Other explanations take it as desirable milk eagerly drunk, or read “not majhūd” as milk so abundant that milking does not exhaust it or the camel.

Lisān and Al-Miṣbāḥ: variant readings and explanations of Al-Shammākh’s witness

These textual and interpretive differences prevent the line alone from securely proving one particular historical meaning. The witnesses show a family of effort, drawing on capacity, pressure, and exhaustion, extending beyond warfare terminology.

جِهَاد in Arabic

Dictionaries connect jihād with exerting one’s utmost in effort and confrontation. Fighting an enemy is an established use, while the lexical field is broader.

Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-ʿArab explains jihad through warfare, speech, and other exertions of capacity.

Lexical breadth must be distinguished from a religious ruling. The inclusion of military confrontation alone does not determine when war is permissible, who may command it, or its ethical limits. Those require the relevant verses about fighting, oppression, justice, covenants, and prohibited transgression.

Some dictionaries also record جَهَاد, jahād with an open jīm, for hard, level, or barren ground. It differs in vocalization from جِهَاد, jihād. The attestation does not establish that Qur’anic jihād derives directly from this landscape image; the available evidence does not prove such a history.

Lexical sources: jahād with an open jīm describing ground

3. Qur’anic جَهْد and جُهْد

جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ: emphatic swearing

The expression appears in several passages. For example:

Al-Anʿām 6:109

وَأَقْسَمُوا بِاللَّهِ جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ لَئِنْ جَاءَتْهُمْ آيَةٌ لَيُؤْمِنُنَّ بِهَا قُلْ إِنَّمَا الْآيَاتُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَمَا يُشْعِرُكُمْ أَنَّهَا إِذَا جَاءَتْ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

wa-aqsamū bi-Allāhi jahda aymānihim la-in jāʾat-hum āyatun la-yuʾminunna bi-hā qul innamā al-āyātu ʿinda Allāhi wa-mā yushʿirukum annahā idhā jāʾat lā yuʾminūna

Proposed translation for this study: “They swore by God with the utmost emphasis in their oaths that, if a sign came to them, they would certainly believe in it. Say: The signs are only with God. What makes you aware that, when it comes, they will not believe?”

جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ concerns intensity and emphasis in swearing. The speakers emphatically claim that seeing a sign would make them believe.

The oath’s force does not establish the claim’s truth. The continuation shows that even a sign’s arrival does not guarantee their belief.

The same expression occurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:53, Al-Naḥl 16:38, Al-Nūr 24:53, and Fāṭir 35:42. The subjects differ: solidarity with believers, resurrection, obedience, and accepting a warning.

In all these, جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ indicates the speakers’ emphatic assertion, not its truth.

Al-Nūr 24:53 makes the distinction clear:

وَأَقْسَمُوا بِاللَّهِ جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ لَئِنْ أَمَرْتَهُمْ لَيَخْرُجُنَّ قُلْ لَا تُقْسِمُوا طَاعَةٌ مَعْرُوفَةٌ إِنَّ اللَّهَ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-aqsamū bi-Allāhi jahda aymānihim la-in amartahum la-yakhrujunna qul lā tuqsimū ṭāʿatun maʿrūfatun inna Allāha khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūna

Proposed translation for this study: “They swore by God with the utmost emphasis in their oaths that, if you commanded them, they would certainly go out. Say: Do not swear; recognized obedience. God is indeed aware of what you do.”

Strong verbal assertion is confronted with actual obedience. Loudness or force of assertion does not establish an oath’s value; real conduct makes it clear.

جُهْدَهُمْ: their available capacity

Al-Tawbah 9:79

الَّذِينَ يَلْمِزُونَ الْمُطَّوِّعِينَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ فِي الصَّدَقَاتِ وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَجِدُونَ إِلَّا جُهْدَهُمْ فَيَسْخَرُونَ مِنْهُمْ سَخِرَ اللَّهُ مِنْهُمْ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

alladhīna yalmizūna al-muṭṭawwiʿīna mina al-muʾminīna fī al-ṣadaqāti wa-alladhīna lā yajidūna illā juhdahum fa-yaskharūna minhum sakhira Allāhu minhum wa-lahum ʿadhābun alīmun

Proposed translation for this study: “Those who find fault with believers who contribute voluntarily to charity, and with those who find nothing beyond their capacity, and ridicule them—God ridicules them, and theirs is a painful punishment.”

Juhd here concerns neither fighting nor oaths, but people’s limited means and capacity.

لَا يَجِدُونَ إِلَّا جُهْدَهُمْ indicates that they find nothing to give beyond what their effort and limited resources provide.

The contribution’s value cannot be judged solely by its amount. Someone wealthy may give little; another with limited means may give a share of what they can afford.

The verse condemns those who ridicule limited but real effort.

Juhd thus relates to the person’s capacity. Effort must be assessed with actual ability, rather than a uniform measure ignoring different resources.

4. جَاهَدَ: effort and confrontation whose direction depends on context

Pressing someone toward associationism

The verb جَاهَدَ describing parents is important evidence of the root’s breadth.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:8

وَوَصَّيْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ بِوَالِدَيْهِ حُسْنًا وَإِنْ جَاهَدَاكَ لِتُشْرِكَ بِي مَا لَيْسَ لَكَ بِهِ عِلْمٌ فَلَا تُطِعْهُمَا إِلَيَّ مَرْجِعُكُمْ فَأُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-waṣṣaynā al-insāna bi-wālidayhi ḥusnan wa-in jāhadāka li-tushrika bī mā laysa laka bihi ʿilmun fa-lā tuṭiʿhumā ilayya marjiʿukum fa-unabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūna

Proposed translation for this study: “We enjoined goodness toward parents upon the human being. If they press you hard to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, do not obey them. To Me is your return; I will inform you of what you used to do.”

جَاهَدَاكَ describes their strong effort to make their child accept associationism.

The goal is wrongful, so جَاهَدَ alone is not praise. People can exert themselves fully toward a demand the Qur’an rejects.

Luqmān states the issue more explicitly:

Luqmān 31:15

وَإِنْ جَاهَدَاكَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ تُشْرِكَ بِي مَا لَيْسَ لَكَ بِهِ عِلْمٌ فَلَا تُطِعْهُمَا وَصَاحِبْهُمَا فِي الدُّنْيَا مَعْرُوفًا وَاتَّبِعْ سَبِيلَ مَنْ أَنَابَ إِلَيَّ ثُمَّ إِلَيَّ مَرْجِعُكُمْ فَأُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

wa-in jāhadāka ʿalā an tushrika bī mā laysa laka bihi ʿilmun fa-lā tuṭiʿhumā wa-ṣāḥibhumā fī al-dunyā maʿrūfan wa-ittabiʿ sabīla man anāba ilayya thumma ilayya marjiʿukum fa-unabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūna

Proposed translation for this study: “If they press you hard to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, do not obey them; accompany them honorably in this world, and follow the way of whoever turns back to Me. Then to Me is your return, and I will inform you of what you used to do.”

عَلَىٰ in جَاهَدَاكَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ تُشْرِكَ clarifies the direction of pressure: the parents seek to make their child do something the child must not accept.

While rejecting obedience to the wrongful demand, the verse preserves good companionship with the parents.

Mujāhadah in these verses is therefore neither military war nor praised religious striving, but strong pressure to change another’s belief.

This alone establishes that jāhada in Qur’anic language does not mean exclusively “fought.”

Striving through the divine message

Al-Furqān 25:52

فَلَا تُطِعِ الْكَافِرِينَ وَجَاهِدْهُمْ بِهِ جِهَادًا كَبِيرًا

fa-lā tuṭiʿi al-kāfirīna wa-jāhidhum bihi jihādan kabīran

Proposed translation for this study: “Do not obey those who deny; through it, undertake a great and persistent striving against them.”

جَاهِدْهُمْ commands striving and confrontation against opponents, but بِهِ specifies the means.

In the preceding context of message and warning, the Qur’an is the stronger referent of بِهِ. Early commentary records this reading, while another transmitted reading refers to Islam. The reference must be stated as a contextual interpretation, not a noun explicitly written in the verse.

Al-Ṭabarī: two readings of the referent of bihi

The confrontation concerns conveying the message, explaining, reasoning, answering opposition, and resisting pressure to abandon or alter truth.

جِهَادًا كَبِيرًا is an emphatic verbal noun qualified by “great,” emphasizing the effort’s magnitude and importance.

The verse names no weapon; the identified means is the divine message in that contextual reading.

Rendering جَاهِدْهُمْ بِهِ as “wage military war against them” consequently fails to preserve بِهِ and the context.

The verse clearly demonstrates jihad as extensive, serious, difficult work in conveying a message and addressing thought.

A person’s striving benefits themselves

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:6

وَمَنْ جَاهَدَ فَإِنَّمَا يُجَاهِدُ لِنَفْسِهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَغَنِيٌّ عَنِ الْعَالَمِينَ

wa-man jāhada fa-innamā yujāhidu li-nafsihi inna Allāha la-ghaniyyun ʿani al-ʿālamīna

Proposed translation for this study: “Whoever strives, strives only for himself. God is indeed independent of all worlds.”

جَاهَدَ appears without naming a human enemy or weapon. The sentence emphasizes the relationship between someone’s effort and their own benefit and outcome.

The surah’s context of tested faith, pressures, perseverance, and conduct clarifies the striving.

Human effort for God does not supply a need of God. Its benefit returns to the person.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69

وَالَّذِينَ جَاهَدُوا فِينَا لَنَهْدِيَنَّهُمْ سُبُلَنَا وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ لَمَعَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-alladhīna jāhadū fīnā la-nahdiyannahum subulanā wa-inna Allāha la-maʿa al-muḥsinīna

Proposed translation for this study: “Those who strove in Our cause—We will certainly guide them to Our ways. God is indeed with those who do good.”

جَاهَدُوا concerns effort directed toward God. فِينَا specifies its orientation, while the continuation connects it with guidance and doing good.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt, faith encounters testing, social pressure, threat, and perseverance against opposition. The verse cannot therefore be restricted to military operations alone.

جَاهَدُوا فِينَا itself nevertheless provides no closed list such as worship, teaching, struggle against the self, or military battle. Under appropriate conditions these may be instances of striving in God’s way, but identifying or authorizing an instance requires independent evidence.

حَقَّ جِهَادِهِ: fitting effort, not duty beyond capacity

Al-Ḥajj 22:78

وَجَاهِدُوا فِي اللَّهِ حَقَّ جِهَادِهِ هُوَ اجْتَبَاكُمْ وَمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ مِنْ حَرَجٍ مِلَّةَ أَبِيكُمْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ هُوَ سَمَّاكُمُ الْمُسْلِمِينَ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَفِي هَٰذَا لِيَكُونَ الرَّسُولُ شَهِيدًا عَلَيْكُمْ وَتَكُونُوا شُهَدَاءَ عَلَى النَّاسِ فَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِاللَّهِ هُوَ مَوْلَاكُمْ فَنِعْمَ الْمَوْلَىٰ وَنِعْمَ النَّصِيرُ

wa-jāhidū fī Allāhi ḥaqqa jihādihi huwa ijtabākum wa-mā jaʿala ʿalaykum fī al-dīni min ḥarajin millata abīkum Ibrāhīma huwa sammākumu al-muslimīna min qablu wa-fī hādhā li-yakūna al-rasūlu shahīdan ʿalaykum wa-takūnū shuhadāʾa ʿalā al-nāsi fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātū al-zakāta wa-iʿtaṣimū bi-Allāhi huwa mawlākum fa-niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīru

Proposed translation for this study: “Strive in God’s cause as is fitting for striving in His cause. He chose you and imposed no hardship upon you in religion—the way of your father Abraham. He named you Muslims before, and in this, so that the Messenger may witness over you and you over people. Establish ṣalāh, give zakāh, and hold fast to God. He is your protector: an excellent protector and excellent helper.”

حَقَّ جِهَادِهِ emphasizes striving appropriately and in accordance with its due.

The verse immediately says وَمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ مِنْ حَرَجٍ. Fitting striving must therefore not be turned into limitless suffering or a burden beyond human capacity.

The command accompanies Abraham’s way, witnessing, establishing ṣalāh, giving zakāh, and holding fast to God. Religious responsibility here extends beyond a military activity.

Linguistically, حَقَّ جِهَادِهِ emphasizes the quality and appropriateness of effort, rather than treating every added hardship or duty as a virtue.

5. Striving with wealth and selves, and its relationship to fighting

بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنْفُسِهِمْ

A major Qur’anic expression concerns striving with wealth and selves.

Al-Tawbah 9:41

انْفِرُوا خِفَافًا وَثِقَالًا وَجَاهِدُوا بِأَمْوَالِكُمْ وَأَنْفُسِكُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

infirū khifāfan wa-thiqālan wa-jāhidū bi-amwālikum wa-anfusikum fī sabīli Allāhi dhālikum khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūna

Proposed translation for this study: “Go forth lightly and heavily burdened, and strive with your wealth and selves in God’s way. That is better for you, if you know.”

انْفِرُوا accompanies جَاهِدُوا. In this setting, nafr concerns going out and mobilizing for a difficult mission; surrounding verses establish the military confrontation.

Jāhidū here therefore cannot be detached from its wartime setting.

بِأَمْوَالِكُمْ وَأَنْفُسِكُمْ identifies two means of committing capacity: spending property and personally taking part, with one’s life at stake.

The expression need not describe one identical activity everywhere. Wealth can support and supply needs; personal participation may involve activity, risk, perseverance, or confrontation. Context determines the particular instance.

The wording about wealth and selves also appears in Al-Anfāl 8:72, Al-Tawbah 9:20, Al-Tawbah 9:88, Al-Ḥujurāt 49:15, and Al-Ṣaff 61:11. Al-Baqarah 2:218, Al-Anfāl 8:74, Al-Anfāl 8:75, and Al-Tawbah 9:19 mention striving without that particular pairing; Al-Anfāl 8:73 belongs to the surrounding discussion of mutual support but contains no ج ه د derivative. These passages concern faith, migration, support, or striving in God’s way, with mobilization and military conflict evident in some contexts.

Just as jihad cannot be restricted to fighting, fighting must not be removed from every instance of Qur’anic jihad.

الْمُجَاهِدُون and الْقَاعِدُون

Al-Nisāʾ 4:95

لَا يَسْتَوِي الْقَاعِدُونَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ غَيْرُ أُولِي الضَّرَرِ وَالْمُجَاهِدُونَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنْفُسِهِمْ فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ الْمُجَاهِدِينَ بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنْفُسِهِمْ عَلَى الْقَاعِدِينَ دَرَجَةً وَكُلًّا وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الْحُسْنَىٰ وَفَضَّلَ اللَّهُ الْمُجَاهِدِينَ عَلَى الْقَاعِدِينَ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

lā yastawī al-qāʿidūna mina al-muʾminīna ghayru ulī al-ḍarari wa-al-mujāhidūna fī sabīli Allāhi bi-amwālihim wa-anfusihim faḍḍala Allāhu al-mujāhidīna bi-amwālihim wa-anfusihim ʿalā al-qāʿidīna darajatan wa-kullan waʿada Allāhu al-ḥusnā wa-faḍḍala Allāhu al-mujāhidīna ʿalā al-qāʿidīna ajran ʿaẓīman

Proposed translation for this study: “Believers who remain seated, other than those with injury or impairment, are not equal to those striving in God’s way with their wealth and selves. God has favored those striving with wealth and selves over those seated by a degree; to both He has promised good, and He has favored the strivers over those seated with a great reward.”

الْمُجَاهِدُون contrasts with الْقَاعِدُون: those actively participating in the responsibility in question and those remaining behind or sitting out.

غَيْرُ أُولِي الضَّرَرِ is crucial: the verse excludes from the comparison people genuinely prevented from participating by impairment or harm.

This connects with the root’s core: genuine effort cannot be assessed without considering human capacity.

The verse also says وَكُلًّا وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الْحُسْنَىٰ. Preference in the particular responsibility does not deny all good to other believers.

Mujāhid here concerns participation in difficult, costly effort whose military setting is established by context.

The active participle appears several times in Al-Nisāʾ 4:95 and also in Muḥammad 47:31, where الْمُجَاهِدِينَ accompanies الصَّابِرِينَ in the testing of people.

Striving and perseverance under pressure

Al-Naḥl 16:110

ثُمَّ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لِلَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا فُتِنُوا ثُمَّ جَاهَدُوا وَصَبَرُوا إِنَّ رَبَّكَ مِنْ بَعْدِهَا لَغَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

thumma inna rabbaka li-alladhīna hājarū min baʿdi mā futinū thumma jāhadū wa-ṣabarū inna rabbaka min baʿdihā la-ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed translation for this study: “Your Lord, toward those who migrated after being subjected to persecution, then strove and persevered—your Lord thereafter is indeed forgiving and merciful.”

Migration, striving, and patience share one context. جَاهَدُوا emphasizes active, serious effort, while صَبَرُوا emphasizes perseverance and steadfastness.

The words are related but not fully synonymous. Jihad emphasizes exertion and difficult action; patience emphasizes firmness and not abandoning the right course.

Their association shows complementary effort and perseverance, without making every jihad armed battle.

6. Confronting opponents: the boundary between jihad and warfare

جَاهِدِ الْكُفَّارَ وَالْمُنَافِقِينَ

Al-Tawbah 9:73

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ جَاهِدِ الْكُفَّارَ وَالْمُنَافِقِينَ وَاغْلُظْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَمَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu jāhidi al-kuffāra wa-al-munāfiqīna wa-ughluẓ ʿalayhim wa-maʾwāhum jahannamu wa-biʾsa al-maṣīru

Proposed translation for this study: “Prophet, confront those who deny and the hypocrites with serious effort, and be firm toward them. Their refuge is Hell—an evil destination.”

Al-Taḥrīm 66:9 repeats the command in the same construction.

جَاهِدْ has human objects, الْكُفَّارَ وَالْمُنَافِقِينَ: groups confronting the message and believing community.

The verb itself does not specify one method. This especially matters for hypocrites, whose outward profession differs from inward belief; not all necessarily engage in open battle.

The verse alone therefore does not authorize armed war against everyone identified as a hypocrite.

وَاغْلُظْ عَلَيْهِمْ emphasizes firmness, understood within the subject and Qur’anic limits for human conduct. Firmness does not mean killing or transgression.

Confrontation may be political, social, legal, or verbal in some circumstances, and military in war. Each application requires evidence from that situation. Early commentaries also report differing methods, including verbal confrontation and enforcement of legal penalties; these should remain distinguished as interpretations.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing interpretations of confronting hypocrites

Jihad and fighting are different words

For fighting and killing, the Qur’an uses another family: قَاتَلَ, قِتَال, قَتَلَ, and قَتْل from ق ت ل.

Qitāl directly concerns fighting and armed confrontation, whereas jihād’s lexical foundation concerns effort and exertion.

Fighting can, under some conditions, be an instance of jihad; every jihad is not fighting.

The Qur’an does not separate them entirely. In warfare passages, wealth, personal participation, and battlefield presence can form part of jihad. In Al-Furqān 25:52 the means is بِهِ; in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:8 parents جَاهَدَاكَ to impose associationism.

Jihad’s lexical field is therefore broader than fighting.

The distinction alone does not determine all warfare rules. Defense, transgression, covenants, peace, and prohibited compulsion require their own verses.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 is central because it joins fighting with prohibiting transgression. Al-Ḥajj 22:39 and Al-Ḥajj 22:40 connect permission to fight with oppression and expulsion; Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9 distinguish those who fought or expelled believers from those who did not.

Jihad cannot therefore be defined merely by the opponent’s label. Striving in God’s way must be assessed through its orientation, method, and Qur’anic limits.

فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ: the direction of striving

فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ accompanies jihad in many verses, specifying its direction and purpose.

Sabīl means a way or path; its relation to God identifies the effort with God’s way.

That relationship is not established simply by personal claims or a group’s name. Calling an act involving oppression, transgression, or religious compulsion jihad does not establish its Qur’anic rightness.

Nor does the qualification make every effort toward any goal jihad in God’s way. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:8 and Luqmān 31:15 describe intense effort to impose associationism.

Jāhada names a kind of action; فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ specifies its desired orientation, which must be recognized through Qur’anic criteria.

7. Comparing related roots

ج ه د (جَهَدَ) and س ع ي (سَعَى): effort and purposeful movement

Study of S ʿ Y

Qur’anic سَعَى can concern movement, going, and striving toward a goal.

ج ه د makes intensity of exertion and difficulty more prominent.

Not every saʿy necessarily involves severe pressure or hardship, whereas that aspect is important within ج ه د.

The distinction is not absolute: saʿy can also be difficult and costly. Context determines actual intensity.

ج ه د (جَهَدَ) and ص ب ر (صَبَرَ): action and perseverance

Study of Ṣ B R

Patience emphasizes firmness, restraint, and maintaining a course despite pressure and obstacles.

Jihad concerns seriously exerting capacity and confronting difficulty.

Al-Naḥl 16:110 joins the aspects in جَاهَدُوا وَصَبَرُوا. People may strive toward right and persevere along the same course.

Their association establishes a relationship, not an invariant sequence in which one must precede the other.

ج ه د (جَهَدَ) and ق ت ل (قَتَلَ): effort and deadly confrontation

Study of Q T L

Qatala concerns killing; qātala fighting. This family describes a more specific field of confrontation than ج ه د.

Fighting may require jihad and exertion, but intense effort does not always lead to killing or warfare.

This matters when translating جَاهِدُوا or جَاهِدْهُمْ. Rendering effort through the Qur’an as armed war is inaccurate; where context concerns mobilization and battle, translation must also preserve that setting.

ج ه د (جَهَدَ) and ج د ل (جَدَلَ): effort and reasoned discussion

Study of J D L

Qur’anic jidāl concerns debate, argument, and verbal confrontation, while jihad encompasses more kinds of activity.

Verbal and reasoned confrontation can be a means of jihad, but jihad is not confined to discussion, and not every debate is striving in God’s way.

Al-Furqān 25:52 identifies the means of confrontation, connecting this use with effort in conveying and reasoning, without making ج ه د and ج د ل identical.

ج ه د (جَهَدَ) and و س ع (وَسِعَ): effort and capacity

Study of W S ʿ

Wusʿ concerns scope and capacity; jahd and juhd can concern exerting that capacity or a degree of pressure and effort.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 says God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity. In Al-Tawbah 9:79, جُهْدَهُمْ concerns the limited resources of those who have nothing beyond what they can give.

The families are related, not identical. Wusʿ emphasizes capacity; juhd in the charity passage concerns what someone provides within their means.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ, “with the utmost emphasis in their oaths” is appropriate. “With their strongest oaths” can also be understandable, but should not imply classification into distinct legal types of oath.

For جُهْدَهُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:79, “within their means” or “what they can afford” is natural.

For جَاهَدَاكَ in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:8 and Luqmān 31:15, “press you hard” or “pressure you to…” fits. “Wage war against you” is inaccurate there.

For جَاهِدْهُمْ بِهِ جِهَادًا كَبِيرًا in Al-Furqān 25:52, “through it, undertake a great and persistent striving against them” or “through it, confront them with great, serious effort” is appropriate.

For جَاهَدُوا فِينَا in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69, “strove in Our cause” is natural; explanation can examine فِينَا more closely.

For جَاهِدُوا بِأَمْوَالِكُمْ وَأَنْفُسِكُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:41, “strive with your wealth and selves” preserves the basic sense, while analysis must clarify mobilization and battle.

Depending on context, الْمُجَاهِدُون can be rendered “those striving,” “those confronting opposition in God’s way,” or, in clear military settings, “those undertaking jihad.” Persian can also retain “mujāhidān,” provided the meaning is not confined beforehand to a modern political or military title.

A fixed translation of war for every derivative is therefore inappropriate. Rendering should begin with the basic meaning and become precise through context.

9. Conclusion

Arabic ج ه د concerns serious effort, exertion, hardship, and confrontation with difficulty. It can describe emphatic oaths, limited capacity, pressure upon another, perseverance in right, and battlefield effort.

جَهْدَ أَيْمَانِهِمْ expresses emphatic swearing and جُهْدَهُمْ limited capacity. جَاهَدَاكَ can describe wrongful pressure to impose associationism on a child; جَاهِدْهُمْ بِهِ concerns confronting opposition through the divine message.

Elsewhere, striving with wealth and selves accompanies migration, support, mobilization, and war. Military effort can be a form of jihad without exhausting its meaning.

Therefore, jihad fundamentally names serious effort and confrontation; its ethical orientation depends on goal, method, and context.

Not every difficult effort is jihad in God’s way, nor does calling every war jihad make it lawful. The Qur’an describes pressure to impose associationism alongside effort through the Qur’an to convey a message and striving with wealth and selves in difficulty.

Translation must preserve this breadth. Reducing every ج ه د use to war conceals part of Qur’anic language; removing the military dimension everywhere also conflicts with the evidence.

Precise understanding joins effort and exertion with the direction and limits supplied by each verse.
