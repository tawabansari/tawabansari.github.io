The Qur’anic Root A W N

Now, the present time, and when an event occurs: awān and other branches of the family in earlier Arabic

The Qur’anic word الْآنَ (al-āna) refers to the time of speaking or to a newly established situation: “now,” “right now,” or, in some constructions, “from now on.” It can locate a statement, the disclosure of truth, a changed ruling, an opportunity to act, or the moment an event transforms a previous situation.

Some Qur’anic root indexes place al-ān under A W N. The original Quranic Arabic Corpus data, however, records it as a temporal word without assigning a root. Arabic أَوَان (awān) also means a thing’s time or occasion. Earlier linguists disagreed over al-ān’s historical derivation; deriving it from awān is not the only explanation proposed.

Earlier dictionaries also record an A W N branch concerning gentleness, slowness, repose, and taking time. These meanings deserve recognition, but shared letters alone do not turn Qur’anic al-ān into calmness or slowness.

1. The Qur’anic form and earlier Arabic usage

All Qur’anic occurrences discussed here belong to al-āna: Al-Baqarah 2:71, Al-Baqarah 2:187, Al-Nisāʾ 4:18, Al-Anfāl 8:66, Yūnus 10:51, Yūnus 10:91, Yūsuf 12:51, and Al-Jinn 72:9. There are eight occurrences in eight verses across six surahs.

The Qur’an also uses the attached forms فَالْآنَ (fa-al-āna) and آلْآنَ (āl-āna). These are not new derivatives: fa- is a connective particle, while āl-āna adds an interrogative hamzah to the word.

Forms such as أَوَان (awān), آوِنَة (āwinah), أَوْن (awn), and تَأَوَّنَ (taʾawwana) are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

Awān: time and occasion

Awān means a time, occasion, or period. أَوَانُ الْبَرْدِ (awānu al-bardi) means the time of cold weather, while جَاءَ أَوَانُ الْأَمْرِ (jāʾa awānu al-amri) expresses the arrival of the time for something.

Āwinah is also used for different times or occasions. آوِنَةً بَعْدَ آوِنَةٍ (āwinatan baʿda āwinatin) describes something occurring at different times or from time to time.

Awān therefore need not denote a very brief instant. It may mean a period or the appropriate time for an action. Al-ān, by contrast, specifically identifies the present relative to the moment of speaking or the current situation.

An example from earlier Arabic poetry

A poem by Labīd ibn Rabīʿah contains this line:

فَأَيَّ أَوَانٍ لَا تَجِئْنِي مَنِيَّتِي
بِقَصْدٍ مِنَ الْمَعْرُوفِ لَا أَتَعَجَّبِ

fa-ayya awānin lā tajiʾnī maniyyatī / bi-qaṣdin mina al-maʿrūfi lā ataʿajjabi

Its sense is: “Whenever my death comes to me in a familiar, ordinary course, I shall not be surprised.”

Awānin identifies when an event occurs. The poet does not specify a known time for his death, but considers whatever time it may arrive.

The poem is recorded in Labīd’s collected verse. He lived through both the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, but this particular line cannot be dated with certainty. Another transmission reads mā tajiʾnī in place of lā tajiʾnī; the variant does not change awān’s temporal meaning.

هَذَا أَوَانُ الْجِدِّ (hādhā awānu al-jiddi), “this is the time for serious effort,” is also recorded in rajaz verse attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj, an Islamic-period poet. Here, awān identifies the appropriate or newly arrived time for an action. This attests a recorded usage, rather than by itself establishing a pre-Qur’anic date.

These examples show awān’s established use for locating when something occurs or is done.

Awn: repose, gentleness, and slowness

Another branch associates awn with repose, gentleness, slowness, and avoiding haste.

Lexicons record آنَ يَؤُونُ أَوْنًا (āna / yaʾūnu / awnan) for treating gently or acting calmly. أُنْ عَلَى نَفْسِكَ (un ʿalā nafsika) advises someone to be gentle with themselves and avoid haste in movement or effort.

A rajaz fragment transmitted in earlier sources reads:

وَسَفَرٌ كَانَ قَلِيلَ الْأَوْنِ

wa-safarun kāna qalīla al-awni

That is, “a journey with little ease or gentleness.” The citation examined does not securely establish the poet’s identity or the fragment’s date.

Awn here characterizes the journey’s quality, rather than when it occurred. Qalīla al-awni means that ease or gentle treatment was scarce during it.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris grounds A W N in gentleness and repose. Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records slow walking, rest, ease, and taking time.

Some lexical reports connect awn, gentle movement, with hawn. These reports assist historical investigation, but do not alone establish a derivational link between repose and temporal al-ān.

The two established fields should therefore remain distinct: time and occasion, and gentleness, repose, and slowness. An unsupported single definition deriving them all from an imagined common image would reduce precision.

2. Al-ān: the present in Arabic grammar

Al-ān is a temporal noun functioning as an adverbial of time. In its regular Qur’anic use it is indeclinable with final fatḥah: its final vowel remains fixed across constructions.

It identifies the present, but the present need not be a mathematical instant with no duration.

“Now the truth has become clear” describes the current situation relative to the past. “Now this is permitted for you” may mark the beginning of a situation continuing afterward.

Three things should therefore be distinguished: “now” itself, the event occurring at that time, and the event’s consequences for the future.

Al-ān specifies time. Whether a truth is disclosed, a ruling changes, someone repents, or a situation ends comes from the sentence’s other words.

Disagreement over al-ān’s origin

Earlier linguists offered several analyses of al-ān’s historical formation.

One derives it from awān, with sound changes and the loss of part of the form producing al-ān. Explanations along these lines are attributed to Al-Farrāʾ and Ibn Al-Anbārī.

Another connects it with āna, a verb meaning that the time has arrived. Lexical tradition also records this verb as āna / yaʾīnu, semantically close to anā / yaʾnī.

A separate discussion concerns al-ān’s grammatical status. Al-Khalīl, Sībawayh, and others treat it as a temporal noun indeclinable with fatḥah, but accounts differ over al- and the reason for its fixed form.

These theories do not approach a single question from the same direction: some concern historical derivation, others the established word’s grammatical structure.

Grouping al-ān under A W N in some indexes can therefore organize Qur’anic occurrences without resolving the historical disagreement over its derivation.

For reading the Qur’an, the firmer shared conclusion remains clear: al-ān refers to the present and functions as a temporal adverbial.

3. Now contrasted with before: disclosure and changed situations

Al-āna jiʾta bi-al-ḥaqq: now you have brought the truth

Surah Al-Baqarah states:

قَالَ إِنَّهُ يَقُولُ إِنَّهَا بَقَرَةٌ لَا ذَلُولٌ تُثِيرُ الْأَرْضَ وَلَا تَسْقِي الْحَرْثَ مُسَلَّمَةٌ لَا شِيَةَ فِيهَا قَالُوا الْآنَ جِئْتَ بِالْحَقِّ فَذَبَحُوهَا وَمَا كَادُوا يَفْعَلُونَ

qāla innahu yaqūlu innahā baqaratun lā dhalūlun tuthīru al-arḍa wa-lā tasqī al-ḥartha musallamatun lā shiyata fīhā qālū al-āna jiʾta bi-al-ḥaqqi fa-dhabaḥūhā wa-mā kādū yafʿalūna

“He said: He says it is a cow neither trained to plow the earth nor to water the field, sound and without a contrasting marking. They said: Now you have brought the truth. So they slaughtered it, though they nearly failed to do so.” (Al-Baqarah 2:71)

Al-ān belongs to the Children of Israel’s reported speech. After repeated questions and further descriptions of the cow, they say, “Now you have brought the truth.”

The word places their present statement against the earlier stages of the exchange. They now consider the answer clear or sufficient.

Al-ān itself does not mean that truth has become clear. That comes from jiʾta bi-al-ḥaqq.

The quoted statement is also those speakers’ own claim. It does not establish that Moses’s earlier answers lacked truth and only the final answer was true. The verse reports what they said at this stage.

The precise translation of al-ān is therefore “now”; the speakers’ assessment of truth is a separate part of the sentence.

Al-āna ḥaṣḥaṣa al-ḥaqq: now the truth has become clear

The account of Joseph states:

قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُنَّ إِذْ رَاوَدْتُنَّ يُوسُفَ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ قُلْنَ حَاشَ لِلَّهِ مَا عَلِمْنَا عَلَيْهِ مِنْ سُوءٍ قَالَتِ امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ الْآنَ حَصْحَصَ الْحَقُّ أَنَا رَاوَدْتُهُ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ وَإِنَّهُ لَمِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

qāla mā khaṭbukunna idh rāwadtunna yūsufa ʿan nafsihi qulna ḥāsha li-llāhi mā ʿalimnā ʿalayhi min sūʾin qālati imraʾatu al-ʿazīzi al-āna ḥaṣḥaṣa al-ḥaqqu anā rāwadtuhu ʿan nafsihi wa-innahu la-mina al-ṣādiqīna

“He said: What was your affair when you sought to entice Joseph? They said: Glory be to The God; we know of no evil on his part. The powerful official’s wife said: Now the truth has become clear. I sought to entice him, and he is indeed among the truthful.” (Yūsuf 12:51)

After questions and answers about the past incident, the official’s wife openly states the truth.

Al-ān locates this disclosure, contrasting it with the period when the truth was hidden or disputed.

Ḥaṣḥaṣa is a separate verb expressing the truth’s emergence and clarification. Its meaning should not be absorbed into al-ān.

Their distinction is clear: al-ān answers “when,” while ḥaṣḥaṣa states what happened.

In both Al-Baqarah 2:71 and Yūsuf 12:51, al-ān appears in human speech and locates a claim that truth has become clear. The claim’s value and accuracy must be assessed from the relevant context and evidence.

Fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna: the beginning of a new situation

Al-Baqarah 2:187 discusses marital intimacy during fasting nights, The God’s knowledge of the audience’s conduct, and His accepting their repentance and pardoning them, before stating fa-al-āna bāshirūhunna.

Fa- connects the clause with what precedes; al-ān locates the new situation.

“Now” here does not grant merely one brief moment for the action. The verse states the ruling for fasting nights, and fa-al-āna marks the beginning of the situation addressed.

“So now” is the direct rendering. “From now on” may bring out the new situation’s continuation, provided this comes from context rather than turning al-ān’s lexical meaning into the future.

Its presence distinguishes the current situation from what preceded it, but does not alone identify the origin of the earlier situation or establish that an existing Qur’anic verse has been abrogated. Those questions require independent examination of rulings and context.

Al-āna khaffafa allāhu ʿankum: now the burden has been lightened

Surah Al-Anfāl states:

الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ وَعَلِمَ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ ضَعْفًا فَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ مِائَةٌ صَابِرَةٌ يَغْلِبُوا مِائَتَيْنِ وَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ أَلْفٌ يَغْلِبُوا أَلْفَيْنِ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

al-āna khaffafa allāhu ʿankum wa-ʿalima anna fīkum ḍaʿfan fa-in yakun minkum miʾatun ṣābiratun yaghlibū miʾatayni wa-in yakun minkum alfun yaghlibū alfayni bi-idhni allāhi wa-allāhu maʿa al-ṣābirīna

“Now The God has lightened your burden, knowing that there is weakness among you. If a hundred of you are steadfast, they will overcome two hundred; if a thousand of you are steadfast, they will overcome two thousand by the permission of The God. The God is with the steadfast.” (Al-Anfāl 8:66)

Al-ān opens the sentence and relates to the lightening of the burden.

The preceding verse, Al-Anfāl 8:65, gives a different numerical ratio between the steadfast and their opponents. Al-ān in verse 66 therefore emphasizes a difference in time and situation.

Its lexical meaning is not itself “lightening”: that comes from khaffafa.

Nor does al-ān alone explain the change’s cause. The verse itself mentions weakness among the audience. That explanation belongs to the statement, rather than to the temporal adverbial’s meaning.

Together with Al-Baqarah 2:187, this illustrates how al-ān can mark the beginning of a new situation whose effects continue beyond the moment of speaking.

4. Āl-ān: now that the moment of response has arrived?

In two verses of Yūnus, al-ān carries an interrogative hamzah, reflected in the spelling and pronunciation āl-āna.

The word still identifies the present, but the interrogative hamzah and particular context make the sentence a critical or disapproving question.

Believing after what was denied has occurred

Surah Yūnus states:

أَثُمَّ إِذَا مَا وَقَعَ آمَنْتُمْ بِهِ آلْآنَ وَقَدْ كُنْتُمْ بِهِ تَسْتَعْجِلُونَ

a-thumma idhā mā waqaʿa āmantum bihi āl-āna wa-qad kuntum bihi tastaʿjilūna

“Then, when it has occurred, will you believe in it? Now? When previously you were demanding that it be hastened!” (Yūnus 10:51)

The question concerns people who demanded an event’s swift arrival, only to face the question of believing in it once it occurs.

Āl-ān highlights when they react. The questioning tone reveals the lateness or inconsistency of their position.

Nevertheless, al-ān still lexically means “now.” Delay, objection, and rebuke arise from the question and the contrast with past conduct.

Āl-āna wa-qad ʿaṣayta qablu: now, after that disobedience?

The account of Pharaoh continues:

آلْآنَ وَقَدْ عَصَيْتَ قَبْلُ وَكُنْتَ مِنَ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

āl-āna wa-qad ʿaṣayta qablu wa-kunta mina al-mufsidīna

“Now? When you disobeyed before and were among the corrupters?” (Yūnus 10:91)

In Yūnus 10:90, Pharaoh professes faith as drowning overtakes him. Verse 91 responds by setting the time of that profession against his previous disobedience and corruption.

Āl-ān is a rebuking question here. The word and the sentence’s tone nevertheless remain distinct. “Now” does not itself mean “far too late”; Pharaoh’s condition, past disobedience, and imminent drowning create that significance in this sentence.

These occurrences show how the interrogative hamzah can make al-ān highlight a reaction’s lateness without changing its temporal core.

Innī tubtu al-ān: repentance and its opportunity

Surah Al-Nisāʾ states:

وَلَيْسَتِ التَّوْبَةُ لِلَّذِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَهُمُ الْمَوْتُ قَالَ إِنِّي تُبْتُ الْآنَ وَلَا الَّذِينَ يَمُوتُونَ وَهُمْ كُفَّارٌ أُولَٰئِكَ أَعْتَدْنَا لَهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

wa-laysati al-tawbatu li-lladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāti ḥattā idhā ḥaḍara aḥadahumu al-mawtu qāla innī tubtu al-āna wa-lā alladhīna yamūtūna wa-hum kuffārun ulāʾika aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāban alīman

“Repentance is not for those who keep doing evil until, when death comes to one of them, he says, ‘I have repented now’; nor for those who die while disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.” (Al-Nisāʾ 4:18)

Al-ān occurs in the words of someone professing repentance as death arrives.

It again refers to the present, but a particular situation defines that present: death has reached the person, who speaks of repentance after persisting in evil deeds.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:17 also discusses those who return soon after wrongdoing. Reading the two verses together helps distinguish repentance’s opportunity from postponing it until death.

This distinction comes from the verses’ context; al-ān itself does not mean “the moment of death” or “unaccepted repentance.”

5. Al-ān and a changed situation in the jinn’s report

Surah Al-Jinn states:

وَأَنَّا كُنَّا نَقْعُدُ مِنْهَا مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ فَمَنْ يَسْتَمِعِ الْآنَ يَجِدْ لَهُ شِهَابًا رَصَدًا

wa-annā kunnā naqʿudu minhā maqāʿida li-l-samʿi fa-man yastamiʿi al-āna yajid lahu shihāban raṣadan

“We used to sit there in places for listening, but whoever listens now finds a flame lying in wait for him.” (Al-Jinn 72:9)

Al-ān contrasts with kunnā naqʿudu, “we used to sit.” The speakers compare their previous and current situations.

The linguistic construction is clear: we previously acted this way, but now anyone attempting to listen encounters something different.

Al-ān locates the new situation. It neither denotes the flame itself nor explains the change’s cause.

Nor can al-ān alone establish the precise date the situation began or its physical mechanism. Such conclusions need other evidence.

This is the same temporal function seen in Al-Baqarah 2:187 and Al-Anfāl 8:66: placing the present situation against an earlier one.

6. Related families and translation proposals

A W N (awān) compared with A N Y (anā)

Study of A N Y

Qur’anic A N Y is associated with forms such as yaʾni, ināhu, and ānāʾ.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, a-lam yaʾni concerns the arrival of time for an action. In Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, ināhu concerns the food’s readiness. Ānāʾ al-layl in Āl ʿImrān 3:113, Ṭā Hā 20:130, and Al-Zumar 39:9 denotes portions of the night.

Al-ān identifies the present; yaʾni concerns a time’s arrival, and ānāʾ identifies portions of time.

Their meanings may overlap in translation, but their forms and grammatical positions differ. Lexical disagreement over al-ān’s historical relationship to similarly shaped verbs does not justify merging all these forms under one root.

A W N (awān) compared with W Q T (waqt)

Study of W Q T

Waqt concerns a specified time or a time appointed for an event or action.

Awān may identify an action’s time without necessarily fixing its length or limits in advance. Al-ān likewise identifies the present without itself specifying its duration.

An action may therefore have an appointed time, while al-ān only describes the current position relative to that time.

A W N (awān) compared with Ḥ Y N (ḥīn)

Study of Ḥ Y N

Ḥīn is a broad Arabic word for time or a period. Depending on context, that period may be short or long.

Awān may likewise describe a time or period. Al-ān, by contrast, specifically relates to the present.

Al-ān is therefore close to “now” in translation, whereas ḥīn often becomes “a time,” “when,” or “a period.”

A W N (awān) compared with A N F (ānifan)

Study of A N F

In Muḥammad 47:16, ānifan means “just a little while ago.” The Qur’anic index assigns it to A N F, rather than A W N.

Ānifan identifies the recent past, while al-ān identifies the present. Their shared temporal reference does not make their roots identical.

Nor should āniyah, “vessels,” āniyah, “intensely hot,” in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:5, and ānāʾ, “portions of time,” be merged into A W N merely because of similar spellings.

Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal, natural equivalent for al-āna is “now.” “Right now” suits a sentence that especially emphasizes the present moment.

In Al-Baqarah 2:71 and Yūsuf 12:51, “now” conveys when the statement is made or truth becomes clear.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187 and Al-Anfāl 8:66, “now” is direct, while context also conveys a new situation’s beginning. “From now on” may clarify its continuation in some fluent renderings, without replacing the word’s basic meaning everywhere.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:18, “I have repented now” is precise. The repentance’s lateness should be clarified in the explanation.

In Yūnus 10:51 and Yūnus 10:91, preserve the question: “Now?” or “Only now?” The latter brings out the rebuke, but “only” comes from context rather than being an independent component of al-ān.

“Now” fits Al-Jinn 72:9, where the present situation contrasts with previous conduct.

For non-Qur’anic awān, “time,” “occasion,” or “season” are the main equivalents. Āwinah may denote an occasion or portion of time. Awn in the gentleness and ease branch instead becomes “repose,” “gentleness,” “slowness,” or “ease,” according to the sentence.

Conclusion

In indexes accepting this assignment, A W N appears only through al-ān and its attached forms. The word means “now” or “the present,” identified relative to the speaking situation or current circumstances.

Qur’anic usage shows that al-ān may contrast with the past, mark a new situation’s beginning, locate the disclosure of truth, or highlight a late response within a question. None of these effects changes its lexical meaning.

Arabic awān concerns time and occasion and was one proposed basis for explaining al-ān’s formation among earlier linguists. The historical derivation remains disputed; a possibility should not become a certain conclusion.

Another A W N branch records gentleness, repose, slowness, and taking time. These belong to the Arabic linguistic heritage, but the evidence does not justify deriving calmness from Qur’anic al-ān.

Al-ān’s established Qur’anic meaning is “now: the present relative to the speaking situation or event.” The differences among its eight occurrences arise from the verbs, particles, speakers, and contexts, rather than a change in its semantic core.
