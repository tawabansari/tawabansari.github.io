Bread, baking dough, and early uses for striking and driving

The Arabic root KH B Z is best known through bread and baking it. Khubz names bread made from dough and cooked with heat; khabz, with an initial fatḥah, denotes baking bread; and khabbāz is someone who bakes bread.

Early Arabic also used this family for striking something with the hand and driving animals forcefully. Some lexicographers connected working the dough with these other uses, but there is no agreement that the name for bread historically originated in striking.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Joseph’s account with his two fellow prisoners. One reports dreaming of carrying bread on his head while birds eat from it. Joseph’s interpretation makes the bread part of an image referring to the fate of that same dreamer.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing khubz itself from the meaning bread’s image acquires in the dream. In Arabic, bread remains the familiar food, although alongside other dream images it may point to a different event.

1. Forms and semantic core

KH B Z occurs only once in the Qur’an, as khubzan in Yūsuf 12:36.

The word is a singular accusative noun. Arabic khubz can function as a generic noun, denoting bread as food without determining the number of loaves.

Important Arabic derivatives include:

  • خَبَزَ ـ يَخْبِزُ (khabaza–yakhbizu): to bake bread; in some early uses, strike something by hand or drive an animal forcefully.
  • خَبْز (khabz): baking bread; also, in some uses, striking or forceful driving.
  • خُبْز (khubz): bread, the food made from baked dough.
  • خُبْزَة (khubzah): a loaf or piece of bread; in some lexical uses, a dish prepared with bread.
  • خَبَّاز (khabbāz): a baker, someone whose occupation is baking bread.
  • خِبَازَة (khibāzah): the baker’s craft or profession.
  • خَابِز (khābiz): someone who bakes bread; in another use, someone possessing bread.
  • خَبِيز (khabīz): bread baked from grain, rather than a general name for every cooked food.
  • مَخْبُوز (makhbūz): baked as bread; the passive participle of khabaza.
  • اِخْتَبَزَ (ikhtabaza): to bake bread or prepare bread for oneself.
  • تَخَبَّزَ (takhabbaza): in some uses, striking and trampling with an animal’s feet.
  • اِنْخَبَزَ (inkhabaza): in a limited use, a place’s lying low or becoming depressed.

Arabic dictionaries also record khubbāzā as the familiar plant called panīrak in Persian, mallow. Its form places it under KH B Z, but similarity alone does not establish that the plant-name derives directly from baking bread.

These derivatives help map the root’s lexical field, but only khubzan occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence, Yūsuf 12:36, word eighteen

Khubz and khabz distinguished

The first consonant’s vowel matters in these two words.

Khubz, with ḍammah, names the food: bread.

Khabz, with fatḥah, is the verbal noun of khabaza and means baking bread.

In «خَبَزَ الْخَبَّازُ الْخُبْزَ» (khabaza al-khabbāzu al-khubza), “the baker baked the bread,” the first word expresses baking, the second identifies the baker, and the third names the product.

The act of baking, the person baking, and the baked bread are therefore distinct.

In Yūsuf 12:36, khubzan denotes bread, rather than the act of baking.

This also matters for understanding the dream: the person does not report baking bread, but carrying it on his head.

2. The root in early dictionaries

Bread: dough cooked with heat

Lisān al-ʿArab identifies al-khubz as the bread people eat, and khabazahu yakhbizuhu as preparing and baking it.

Citing Tahdhīb al-Lughah, it describes someone preparing flour, making dough, and baking it in hot ashes or an oven.

Arabic baking terminology thus relates to a familiar practical process.

Flour is mixed with water or other suitable ingredients to make dough, which is then cooked with heat.

Methods varied, however. Early Arabic bread could be made in an oven or among ashes and earth heated by fire.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tahdhīb al-Lughah: dough, baking, and bread

Khubzu mallah: bread baked in hot ashes

Mallah denotes ashes and hot earth in which a fire has been kindled.

Khubzu mallah is bread baked in that setting.

Dictionaries also explain khubzah through ṭulmah: dough placed in hot ashes until cooked.

Baking bread therefore did not require a constructed oven or bakery.

Travellers and desert dwellers could prepare bread with fire and hot ashes.

Qur’anic khubz nevertheless does not name a particular baking method. Yūsuf 12:36 specifies neither oven baking, preparation under ashes, nor the kind of flour.

“Bread” therefore suffices in the translation.

Was bread necessarily made from wheat?

Dictionaries also record khabīz for bread baked from different kinds of grain.

KH B Z thus does not restrict bread to wheat.

Bread can be made from different grains. Khubz denotes the baked food, without necessarily identifying a particular grain as its ingredient.

Joseph’s account likewise specifies neither wheat nor barley, nor the bread’s size or shape.

Historical or exegetical reports may discuss such details, but they are not part of khubzan’s direct meaning.

3. Striking with the hand: another semantic branch

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for KH B Z:

«خَبْطُ الشَّيْءِ بِالْيَدِ» (khabṭu al-shayʾi bi-al-yadi).

That is, striking or beating something with the hand.

He also relates breadmaking to this meaning.

The practical connection is understandable, since preparing some breads involves handling, pressing, and kneading dough.

Caution is nevertheless needed in claiming that the name for bread historically developed from striking with the hand.

After reporting that possibility, Lisān al-ʿArab says the derivational explanation is not particularly strong.

Two facts should remain separate: khabz is recorded for striking, while the historical derivation of the name for bread from that act is uncertain.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: striking and caution about bread’s historical derivation

Camels striking the ground with their feet

Arabic sources use khabaza al-baʿīru al-arḍa for a camel striking the ground with its “hands,” meaning its forefeet.

Takhabbazati al-ibilu al-ʿushba also describes camels trampling vegetation beneath their feet.

These uses concern striking and exerting pressure, rather than bread or human food.

Some lexicographers treat this as a comparison: the camel’s foot movements resemble hand movements during breadmaking.

Ibn Fāris, by contrast, presents striking as the family’s central meaning.

This disagreement prevents establishing a certain historical sequence in which striking necessarily came before bread, or vice versa.

Both uses are clearly recorded, but the precise direction of semantic development requires independent historical evidence.

Driving animals forcefully

Another use of khabaza al-dābbata means driving an animal forcefully.

The animal’s movement here involves pressure and urging it onward.

This can relate to feet striking the ground, but forceful driving and baking bread remain different actions.

Verbs in one early Arabic family may denote actions related through their movements or results.

This proximity does not make Qur’anic khubz mean speed, pressure, violence, or driving.

The Qur’anic form names a familiar food.

4. Early Arabic witnesses and traditions near the Qur’anic period

Bread in food and hospitality

Ibn Saʿd’s Al-Ṭabaqāt al-Kubrā attributes a poem to Wahb ibn ʿAbd Quṣayy describing food supplied to the people of Mecca:

فَأَوْسَعَ أَهْلَ مَكَّةَ مِنْ هَشِيمٍ
وَشَابَ الْخُبْزَ بِاللَّحْمِ الْغَرِيضِ

fa-awsaʿa ahla makkata min hashīmin / wa-shāba al-khubza bi-al-laḥmi al-gharīḍi

Translation:

“He plentifully supplied Mecca’s people with broken bread and mixed the bread with fresh meat.”

The line occurs in the account of Hāshim’s hospitality and provisioning Mecca’s people.

Al-khubz is a familiar food that can be broken and mixed with meat.

This witness matters lexically because bread is actual food here, rather than a metaphor or moral description.

It also shows bread’s relationship with other foods: it can be eaten alone or accompanied by other ingredients.

The poem, however, is transmitted in a historical source written after Islam, and its attribution to Wahb ibn ʿAbd Quṣayy comes from that report. Although the subject and figures belong to the pre-Islamic period, neither its date nor its exact wording can be established with certainty without independent evidence.

This caution does not prevent using the lexical witness; it clarifies its historical standing.

Ibn Saʿd, Al-Ṭabaqāt al-Kubrā: Hāshim’s provisioning and the poem attributed to Wahb

The rajaz verse lā takhbizā khabzan

Dictionaries record the following rajaz verse:

لَا تَخْبِزَا خَبْزًا وَبُسَّا بَسًّا
وَلَا تُطِيلَا بِمُنَاخٍ حَبْسًا

lā takhbizā khabzan wa-bussā bassan / wa-lā tuṭīlā bi-munākhin ḥabsan

Two recognized lexical readings exist for this verse.

In one, khabz concerns forcefully driving animals, and bass denotes gentler, slower movement.

Its approximate meaning is then:

“Do not drive the animals forcefully; drive gently, and do not linger excessively at the stopping place.”

Another reading concerns preparing food while travelling. Khabz means baking bread, while bass means mixing flour or sawīq with water or oil.

On this reading:

“Do not spend time baking bread; prepare a simple, quick meal, and do not linger excessively at the stopping place.”

Some transmissions also vary the wording, replacing wa-bussā bassan with related expressions.

This matters because early lexicographers could interpret a witness differently according to accompanying words and the assumed setting.

Nor has the verse been securely attributed to a particular poet or date; it should not be presented without qualification as pre-Islamic evidence.

Both baking bread and forceful driving are recorded in the Arabic lexical tradition, but this verse alone does not establish their historical sequence.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: two interpretations of the rajaz and wording variants

5. Qur’anic use: bread in Joseph’s fellow prisoner’s dream

وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ السِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَا إِنِّي أَرَانِي أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا وَقَالَ الْآخَرُ إِنِّي أَرَانِي أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِي خُبْزًا تَأْكُلُ الطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ نَبِّئْنَا بِتَأْوِيلِهِ إِنَّا نَرَاكَ مِنَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-dakhala maʿahu al-sijna fatayāni qāla aḥaduhumā innī arānī aʿṣiru khamran wa-qāla al-ākharu innī arānī aḥmilu fawqa raʾsī khubzan taʾkulu al-ṭayru minhu nabbiʾnā bi-taʾwīlihi innā narāka mina al-muḥsinīna

Installed Forqan translation:

Two young men entered prison with him. One said, “I see myself pressing wine.” The other said, “I see myself carrying bread upon my head, from which birds eat. Tell us its interpretation. We see you among those who do good.”

Yūsuf 12:36

Joseph’s two fellow prisoners recount their dreams to him in this verse.

One sees himself pressing wine; the other sees himself carrying bread above his head while birds eat from it.

Khubzan occurs in the second person’s dream.

Its direct meaning remains the familiar food. The verse does not say that he is baking bread, preparing dough, or carrying flour.

His principal verb is aḥmilu, “I carry.”

Bread should therefore be distinguished from the action performed with it.

Khubzan: bread rather than flour or dough

Arabic khubz is the baked product, whereas ʿajīn denotes dough and daqīq flour.

This distinction matters in Yūsuf 12:36 because the person says birds eat from what is above his head.

The dream names it khubzan.

“Flour,” “dough,” or “grain” would therefore be inaccurate translations.

The verse also does not specify how many loaves there are.

Arabic khubz can be a generic noun without requiring a single loaf.

The natural Persian equivalent nānī, “some bread,” is therefore appropriate, but it should not establish one loaf of a particular size and shape.

Fawqa raʾsī: the bread’s position in the dream

The person says:

«أَحْمِلُ فَوْقَ رَأْسِي خُبْزًا» (aḥmilu fawqa raʾsī khubzan).

That is, he carries bread above his head.

Fawqa raʾsī locates what is being carried.

Some exegetical reports describe the bread as lying in baskets carried upon the head.

The verse itself, however, uses neither “basket” nor “tray.”

A vessel for carrying bread is therefore a practical possibility or an exegetical detail, rather than the meaning of khubzan or fawqa raʾsī.

“I carry bread upon my head” suffices in a direct translation.

Taʾkulu al-ṭayru minhu: birds eat from the bread

The verse continues:

«تَأْكُلُ الطَّيْرُ مِنْهُ» (taʾkulu al-ṭayru minhu).

That is, birds eat from it.

Minhu, “from it,” refers to the bread.

In the dream image, bread has thus become food for birds.

KH B Z itself does not mean bird food, however. Bread may feed humans or animals; the accompanying sentence identifies who eats it.

Nor does the verse identify the birds’ species or number, or say that all the bread was eaten.

Such details should not be added to the Arabic to complete the dream image.

Does khubz mean death in this dream?

Joseph’s response in Yūsuf 12:41 describes the outcome for his two fellow prisoners:

يَا صَاحِبَيِ السِّجْنِ أَمَّا أَحَدُكُمَا فَيَسْقِي رَبَّهُ خَمْرًا وَأَمَّا الْآخَرُ فَيُصْلَبُ فَتَأْكُلُ الطَّيْرُ مِنْ رَأْسِهِ قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ الَّذِي فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ

yā ṣāḥibayi al-sijni ammā aḥadukumā fa-yasqī rabbahu khamran wa-ammā al-ākharu fa-yuṣlabu fa-taʾkulu al-ṭayru min raʾsihi quḍiya al-amru alladhī fīhi tastaftiyāni

Installed Forqan translation:

My two prison companions, one of you will serve wine to his lord; the other will be crucified, and birds will eat from his head. The affair about which you seek an answer has been decided.”

Yūsuf 12:41

Joseph explains that one prisoner will serve wine to his master, while the other will be crucified so that birds eat from his head.

The connection between the images is clear.

In the dream, the person carries bread on his head and birds eat from it.

In Joseph’s interpretation, birds eat from the crucified person’s head.

The scenes are related, but the lexical definition of bread does not change.

Arabic khubz remains bread. The person’s fate comes from interpreting the whole dream, rather than khubz lexically meaning a head, body, execution, or death.

This distinction matters. Turning a dream image’s interpreted meaning into the word’s definition could make Qur’anic bread mean a human corpse, although no independent lexical evidence establishes that meaning.

Nor does bread’s connection with an adverse event in this dream make seeing bread in every other dream a sign of death or punishment.

The Qur’an concerns two particular people’s dreams and Joseph’s interpretation, rather than a universal, permanent dictionary of dream symbols.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpreting the same prisoner’s dream, distinct from bread’s lexical meaning

Was the dreamer a baker?

Early exegetical reports identify one prisoner as the king’s baker or food official and the other as his cupbearer.

These reports may belong to commentators’ historical understanding of their identities.

Yūsuf 12:36 itself, however, speaks only of two young prisoners and does not name their occupations.

Khubzan alone therefore cannot establish that the person was necessarily a baker. Many people can carry bread without baking professionally.

Nor does the verse specify the number of baskets or loaves, the prisoners’ names, or their charges.

Understanding khubz does not depend on proving these historical reports.

6. Distinctions from related words

KH B Z (khubz) and Ṭ ʿ M (ṭaʿām): bread and food

Qur’anic ṭaʿām concerns food and has a broader field than bread.

Bread is a food, but not every food is bread.

Yūsuf 12:36 specifically uses khubzan.

Yūsuf 12:37 then has Joseph speaking of ṭaʿāmun turzaqānihi, food provided to the pair.

These two verses use a specific food and then a general word for food.

Not every ṭaʿām should therefore mean bread, nor khubz all foods.

The ṭaʿām in Yūsuf 12:37 is also not necessarily the bread seen in the second prisoner’s dream; its precise relationship requires examining Joseph’s speech in context.

Study of Ṭ ʿ M

KH B Z (khubz) and R GH F (raghīf): bread and a loaf

Arabic raghīf denotes a loaf or unit of bread.

Khubz, by contrast, can name bread generically.

Someone may speak of khubz generally or have a particular raghīf in mind.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule about size, thickness, or shape, since bread types and word usage may differ across times and places.

Yūsuf 12:36 uses khubzan rather than raghīfan.

Its wording therefore does not determine the number of loaves.

KH B Z (khubz) and ʿ J N (ʿajn): making dough and baking

ʿAjn concerns preparing and kneading dough.

In food-related usage, khabz concerns baking bread.

The actions are connected in preparing many breads, but differ.

Dough may be ready without being baked; bread has undergone baking.

Some lexical explanations also connect pressing and beating dough with khabz. This should not erase the established distinction between dough and baked bread in the Qur’anic use.

KH B Z (khabz) and Ṭ B KH (ṭabkh): baking bread and cooking food

Arabic ṭabkh has broad application in cooking food, particularly with heat.

In its primary bread-related use, khabz denotes baking dough into bread.

Bread baking is therefore a kind of preparing food with heat, but not every ṭabkh means baking bread.

Cooking meat or a liquid dish may be ṭabkh without producing bread.

Persian likewise distinguishes baking bread from cooking food: the acts are related, but not every food is bread.

KH B Z (khabz) and KH B Ṭ (khabṭ): striking and beating

Ibn Fāris uses khabṭ to explain KH B Z.

Khabṭ concerns striking and beating; some uses may include irregular movement or forceful collision.

This proximity appears in descriptions of camels’ movement or striking something.

Their root consonants nevertheless differ: one ends with zāy, the other with ṭāʾ.

The established Qur’anic use of khabṭ concerns behavior and movement, whereas khubz in Yūsuf 12:36 names bread.

Partial semantic proximity therefore does not allow translating Joseph’s dream bread as striking or beating.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Khubzan in Yūsuf 12:36 means bread, for which Persian nān is familiar and natural.

The installed Persian Forqan translation, “I carry bread upon my head, and birds eat from it,” clearly conveys the expression.

No direct replacement with “a basket of bread,” “several loaves,” “wheat bread,” or “oven-baked bread” is needed, since the wording specifies none of these details.

Nor should the dream’s interpretation be inserted into khubz. Yūsuf 12:36 describes bread being seen; Yūsuf 12:41 explains the person’s fate. These stages should remain separate.

Brief root meaning and the word’s meaning in the verse

Khubzan — Yūsuf 12:36

Root: KH B Z.

Grammatical form: A singular, indefinite accusative noun; the object of aḥmilu, “I carry.”

Brief root meaning: Bread and baking dough into bread; other recorded lexical uses include striking and forceful driving.

Meaning in this verse: The bread one of Joseph’s fellow prisoners dreams of carrying on his head while birds eat from it.

The root meaning, grammatical form, and verse’s particular referent remain distinct here.

8. Conclusion

Arabic KH B Z is known through bread and baking it. Khubz names the baked food; khabz denotes baking bread.

Early dictionaries also record striking with the hand, camels striking the ground with their forefeet, and forcefully driving animals.

Ibn Fāris makes striking the root’s central meaning, but other sources qualify deriving the name for bread from that act. A possible material image should therefore not become the certain historical origin of every use.

Poetry and early reports show bread as a familiar food in Arab life, eaten alone or with other foods.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Yūsuf 12:36, where bread belongs to a prisoner’s dream. In Yūsuf 12:41, Joseph interprets the dream and explains the person’s fate.

This interpretive connection does not change bread’s lexical meaning or establish a general rule for interpreting every dream involving bread.

Final definition: In its familiar food-related branch, KH B Z indicates preparing and baking bread; khubz names the baked bread. Other recorded Arabic branches express striking and forceful driving, although their precise historical relationship remains uncertain. Khubzan in Yūsuf 12:36 means bread, a food present in the dream image whose interpretation must be understood through Joseph’s words and the story’s context. Brief root meaning: Bread and preparing and baking it; other early uses include striking and forceful driving.
