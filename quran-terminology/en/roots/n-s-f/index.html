Uprooting and scattering: from windblown soil and winnowing grain to dispersing an artifact and removing mountains

1. Introducing the root and its semantic center

The Arabic root ن س ف concerns uprooting, separating, scattering, and removing something from its place. نَسَفَ is used when something is detached from its previous position or arrangement and set in motion or scattered by pulling, impact, wind, or another action.

One clear use concerns wind lifting and scattering soil and light particles from the ground. Another concerns a building or other object uprooted and dismantled from its position.

Everyday Arabic also uses نَسَفَ for sifting or winnowing grain: shaking and tossing food to separate light or unwanted material from the rest.

These uses share several features: something occupied a position or belonged to an arrangement; a force then detached or moved it; and it may be dispersed in different directions.

These stages should nevertheless be distinguished.

Uprooting does not necessarily mean pulverizing. A building may be dismantled without every component becoming dust.

Scattering likewise does not necessarily mean destroying. In winnowing, the aim may be to preserve the desired grain while removing impurities.

When something is dispersed after burning or crushing, its former shape may be completely lost. Burning and crushing, however, must be identified from accompanying verbs and the material’s condition, rather than نَسَفَ alone.

A defensible semantic center is uprooting or separating something from its former position, potentially accompanied by scattering it. The force of movement, fragmentation, complete destruction, and purpose of dispersal depend on context.

The Qur’an uses this root for dispersing an artifact that had been worshipped and for removing mountains from their position in a scene of the Hereafter.

Qur’anic forms

The root ن س ف occurs five times in three verses, in four surface forms:

  • لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ: We shall certainly scatter it; Ṭā Hā 20:97.
  • نَسْفًا: a scattering; Ṭā Hā 20:97 and Ṭā Hā 20:105.
  • يَنْسِفُهَا: He uproots and scatters them; Ṭā Hā 20:105.
  • نُسِفَتْ: they were uprooted and scattered; Al-Mursalāt 77:10.

All the Qur’anic verbs from this root are Form I. نَسْف is the corresponding verbal noun.

لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ is a first-person plural imperfect verb, accompanied by lām and the heavy emphatic nūn.

يَنْسِفُهَا is a third-person masculine singular imperfect verb. Its attached pronoun refers to the mountains.

نُسِفَتْ is a feminine singular passive perfect verb, in grammatical agreement with the nonhuman plural الْجِبَال.

Other attested Arabic forms include نَسِيف, نُسَافَة, مِنْسَف, مِنْسَفَة, نَسُوف, اِنْتَسَفَ, and تَنَاسَفَ, none of which occurs in the Qur’an.

The study must therefore distinguish actual Qur’anic forms from derivatives attested only elsewhere in Arabic.

2. Early Arabic uses and semantic branches

Wind lifting soil from the ground

One early lexical explanation describes nasf as wind carrying something away:

النَّسْفُ: انْتِسَافُ الرِّيحِ الشَّيْءَ كَأَنَّهُ يَسْلُبُهُ

al-nasfu intisāfu al-rīḥi al-shayʾa ka-annahu yaslubuhu

Translation:

“Nasf is the wind’s carrying something away, as though snatching it.”

The wind detaches soil, dust, or another light object from the ground and carries it along.

Another lexical explanation centers the root on كَشْفِ شَيْءٍ: uncovering or removing something from its position.

In that explanation, wind lifts soil and chaff from the ground, as though stripping them from its surface and taking them away.

The explanations are close, but view the action from different angles.

One highlights being lifted and dispersed by the wind.

The other emphasizes removal from a former position.

This does not make wind essential to every instance of nasf.

Arabic also uses the verb for dismantling buildings, uprooting plants, and sifting food.

Wind is thus one familiar agent of nasf, rather than a fixed component of the root’s meaning.

Nasf of a building: uprooting and dismantling

نَسَفَ الْبِنَاءَ is attested for uprooting or demolishing a building.

اِنْتَسَفَ الْبِنَاءَ can likewise denote dismantling and uprooting a structure.

مِنْسَفَة names an implement used to pull out or detach parts of a building.

Nasf here concerns dismantling something established in a particular place.

The connection with wind lifting soil is clear: in each case, something is removed from its former condition or position.

Yet nasf of a building does not necessarily mean an explosion.

In contemporary Persian, the expression often evokes blasting and severe demolition. That modern association should not be imposed on every early Arabic use.

Uprooting and dismantling can be accomplished in different ways.

The Qur’an’s use for mountains therefore does not by itself establish explosives, fire, or a process resembling modern blasting.

The manner of the event requires textual or independent evidence.

Nasf of food: sifting and separating

Another important use concerns preparing food and removing impurities.

نَسَفَ الطَّعَامَ describes shaking or tossing food or grain to separate unwanted material.

مِنْسَف names the implement used, described in some accounts as a large sieve or a vessel in which grain is shaken.

نُسَافَة denotes material that falls away during this sifting or winnowing.

The following Arabic expression is attested:

اعْزِلِ النُّسَافَةَ وَكُلْ مِنَ الْخَالِصِ

iʿzili al-nusāfata wa-kul mina al-khāliṣi

Translation:

“Set aside the separated impurities and eat from the pure part.”

Nasf here involves moving and separating components of food.

Grain may be tossed into the air so that wind separates lighter material, or a sieve may be shaken to separate different components.

The desired food is not destroyed in the process.

“Destroying” therefore cannot serve as the root’s general semantic center.

Nasf may dismantle something in one context and separate components to preserve the desired part in another.

The final result must be understood from the action’s object and purpose.

An animal pulling up plants with its mouth

نَسَفَ الْبَعِيرُ الْكَلَأَ describes a camel plucking vegetation from the ground with the front of its mouth.

بَعِيرٌ نَسُوفٌ likewise describes a camel that pulls vegetation up from its roots or growing place.

Nasf here concerns detaching a plant from its place.

Scattering it through the air need not form part of the action.

Some uses thus emphasize uprooting even without an explicitly mentioned stage of dispersal.

An animal pulling plants with its mouth is feeding. This use does not make nasf intrinsically destructive or blameworthy.

A mount’s movement and hair worn away

Early Arabic also uses نَسُوف in descriptions of certain horses and camels.

A horse whose elbows come close to its girth while running, for example, may receive this description.

نَسِيف also denotes a mark left when hair is rubbed or pulled away from an animal’s body.

These uses concern contact, rubbing, and the removal of part of an animal’s hair or covering.

A verse by Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim

The pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim describes a horse’s movement:

نَسُوفٌ لِلْحِزَامِ بِمِرْفَقَيْهَا
يَسُدُّ خَوَاءَ طُبْيَيْهَا الْغُبَارُ

nasūfun li-al-ḥizāmi bi-mirfaqayhā / yasuddu khawāʾa ṭubyayhā al-ghubāru

Translation:

“A mare whose elbows approach her girth as she runs; dust fills the space between her two teats.”

نَسُوف describes a feature of the horse’s movement.

It does not mean soil scattered by the wind. The poet describes rapid movement and the position of the animal’s limbs.

Some derivatives of ن س ف thus had specialized uses in describing animals.

This does not make rapid equine movement or bodily contact the meaning of nasf in the Qur’an.

Each derivative must be understood in its own context.

A verse attributed to al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī says:

وَقَدْ تَخِذَتْ رِجْلِي لَدَى جَنْبِ غَرْزِهَا
نَسِيفًا كَأُفْحُوصِ الْقَطَاةِ الْمُطَرِّقِ

wa-qad takhidhat rijlī ladā janbi gharzihā / nasīfan ka-ufḥūṣi al-qaṭāti al-muṭarriqi

Translation:

“My foot has made a bare, rubbed patch beside the camel’s stirrup, like the hollow nest of a sandgrouse.”

نَسِيف denotes a mark caused by the rider’s foot rubbing against the camel’s body.

It points to the removal of some of the animal’s hair or covering.

The verse is attributed to al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī. Early transmissions differ over part of its wording and over its attribution to al-Mumazzaq or al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī.

Nevertheless, nasīf for a mark left by rubbing and hair loss is independently attested in lexical traditions.

These two poetic examples show that the family was not limited to scattered soil or demolished buildings.

Other established uses

Arabic lexical traditions record نُسَّاف for a bird that takes and detaches things with its beak or claws.

نِسْفَة also denotes a rough, porous stone used to remove dirt from the skin. Some accounts record a related form with the consonant shīn.

In one Arabic dialect, كَلَامٌ نَسِيفٌ denotes quiet or secret speech.

A commentary on a poem by Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī connects this use with people speaking briefly and quietly for fear of revealing their location.

A connection can be imagined between taking off portions of speech and uttering it incompletely or softly. This does not establish the historical origin of that use.

These branches help reveal the family’s breadth, but none of these particular forms occurs in the Qur’an.

3. Ṭā Hā 20:97: dispersing a manufactured object of worship

قَالَ فَاذْهَبْ فَإِنَّ لَكَ فِي الْحَيَاةِ أَنْ تَقُولَ لَا مِسَاسَ وَإِنَّ لَكَ مَوْعِدًا لَنْ تُخْلَفَهُ وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِكَ الَّذِي ظَلْتَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفًا لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًا

qāla fa-idhhab fa-inna laka fī al-ḥayāti an taqūla lā misāsa wa-inna laka mawʿidan lan tukhlafahu wa-unẓur ilā ilāhika alladhī ẓalta ʿalayhi ʿākifan la-nuḥarriqannahu thumma la-nansifannahu fī al-yammi nasfan

Translation:

“He said: Go, then. In this life it is for you to say, ‘No contact’; and you have an appointment from which you will not be released. Look at your god, to whose worship you remained devoted: We shall certainly burn it, then scatter it in the water, a thorough scattering.”

Ṭā Hā 20:97

This is the first verse containing ن س ف in the order of the muṣḥaf.

Moses is speaking, addressing al-Sāmirī and the object of worship he had introduced among the Children of Israel.

A. What is being scattered?

Ṭā Hā 20:88 says:

فَأَخْرَجَ لَهُمْ عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ فَقَالُوا هَٰذَا إِلَٰهُكُمْ وَإِلَٰهُ مُوسَىٰ فَنَسِيَ

fa-akhraja lahum ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun fa-qālū hādhā ilāhukum wa-ilāhu mūsā fa-nasiya

Translation:

“He brought out for them a calf, a bodily form with a lowing sound. They said: This is your god and the god of Moses, but he forgot.”

The passage concerns an artifact shaped like a calf that a group presented as their god.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 also speaks of a calf made from the people’s ornaments.

The object in Ṭā Hā 20:97 is therefore something placed in the position of a god.

ن س ف does not name a calf, idol, or deity.

لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ expresses the action of dispersing that artifact.

Nasf should not be defined solely as “destroying an idol,” since other verses use the same root for mountains.

B. لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ

The expression contains two different verbs:

لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ

la-nuḥarriqannahu

and:

ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ

thumma la-nansifannahu

The first belongs to ح ر ق and, in the Ḥafṣ reading, denotes burning.

The second belongs to ن س ف and concerns uprooting and scattering.

ثُمَّ also places the actions in sequence.

Rendering both simply as “We shall destroy it” would erase their difference.

Nasf does not itself mean burning.

Burning is mentioned before dispersal and may prepare the artifact to become separable components.

The verse does not explain the precise process.

Exegetical reports differ between burning with fire and filing or grinding the artifact. These differences are partly connected with different readings of the verb from ح ر ق.

Some accounts describe burning with fire; others discuss filing down and reducing the material.

These differences do not change the independent meaning of nasf.

The second verb clearly concerns dispersing the resulting material in water.

The verse alone does not establish every component’s precise material, the temperature, particle size, or chemical process.

C. فِي الْيَمِّ: dispersing in water

لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًا contains the preposition فِي and the noun الْيَمِّ.

Yamm denotes a body of water, especially the sea.

It identifies the destination or setting in which the artifact’s components are dispersed.

“We shall scatter it in the water” or “in the sea” are therefore natural renderings.

Nasf itself does not establish that scattering always occurs in water.

Other verses use the same root for mountains without mentioning any sea.

Nor does this action independently establish a general requirement to throw every manufactured object of worship into the sea.

The narrative concerns a particular act and a particular object.

D. The final نَسْفًا

نَسْفًا is the verbal noun of nasafa, functioning here as a cognate accusative.

A verbal noun following its verb emphasizes the action itself.

لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ ... نَسْفًا thus emphatically asserts the dispersal.

The cognate accusative alone does not determine particle size, wind strength, or the manner of scattering.

Repeating “We shall scatter it, a scattering” follows the Arabic structure, but may sound cumbersome in translation.

A fluent rendering may say “We shall scatter it thoroughly,” provided thoroughness conveys emphasis rather than a scientific claim that every particle ceases to exist.

E. The narrative’s contextual message

In the preceding verses Moses discusses the calf with al-Sāmirī, then points to the god to whose worship he had remained devoted.

Burning and scattering the artifact ends its presence as an object of worship in this setting.

Something people had placed in a divine position is shown vulnerable to change, demolition, and dispersal.

This conclusion comes from the narrative as a whole.

Nasf still means scattering and uprooting at the lexical level.

It should not be translated directly as “invalidating polytheism,” “purifying belief,” or “liberation from idolatry.”

These may be contextual messages or results, but they are not equivalents of the word itself.

4. Ṭā Hā 20:105: nasf of the mountains

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْجِبَالِ فَقُلْ يَنْسِفُهَا رَبِّي نَسْفًا

wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-jibāli fa-qul yansifuhā rabbī nasfan

Translation:

“They ask you about the mountains. Say: My Lord will uproot and scatter them.”

Ṭā Hā 20:105

This is the second verse containing ن س ف in the order of the muṣḥaf.

Unlike verse 97, which concerned a human artifact, the objects here are mountains.

A. يَنْسِفُهَا: uprooting and scattering the mountains

يَنْسِفُ is an imperfect verb, with رَبِّي as its subject.

The attached pronoun refers to الْجِبَال, the mountains.

Lexical evidence supports understanding nasf here as uprooting mountains from their places and scattering them.

The following verses must also be considered to understand the event’s result.

Ṭā Hā 20:106 says:

فَيَذَرُهَا قَاعًا صَفْصَفًا

fa-yadharuhā qāʿan ṣafṣafan

Translation:

“Then He leaves it a level, even plain.”

And Ṭā Hā 20:107:

لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا

lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajan wa-lā amtan

Translation:

“You see in it neither unevenness nor elevation.”

These verses show that nasf of the mountains removes elevations and changes the shape of the ground.

Nasf in Ṭā Hā 20:105 therefore means more than mountains merely shaking.

A slight shaking that left the mountains in place would not explain the result described in Ṭā Hā 20:106 and Ṭā Hā 20:107.

“Uprooting and scattering” is consequently more accurate than “shaking.”

B. Does nasf mean being crushed into dust?

Some lexical and exegetical explanations describe the mountains’ nasf as their reduction to scattered particles.

This accords with nasf of windblown soil and with other verses about the mountains’ transformation.

The verb’s own meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the event’s complete result.

Arabic can use نَسَفَ for uprooting a building without requiring its complete pulverization.

In Ṭā Hā, the following verses make clear that the mountains’ elevations disappear.

They do not specify the resulting particles’ size, the remaining material’s nature, or the physical mode of dispersal.

The passage thus describes uprooting and scattering mountains and removing their elevations, without establishing a particular scientific theory of the process.

C. Does nasf mean the complete annihilation of matter?

Nasf can have a destructive result, but the verb does not necessarily mean matter ceasing to exist.

When wind carries soil from the ground, for example, the soil leaves its former position without necessarily ceasing to exist.

Dismantling may likewise erase a building’s form while leaving some of its materials.

The mountain passages describe an immense transformation and the loss of their familiar shape and position.

Nasf alone does not reveal the final state of all the mountains’ matter or whether any trace of their components remains.

“Scattering” should therefore not be turned without evidence into “annihilating.”

D. Is this an event in the present world?

The preceding verses of Ṭā Hā mention the trumpet blast, humanity’s gathering, and the Resurrection.

The question about mountains then follows.

Their nasf here belongs to the setting of events concerning the Hereafter.

This does not identify every modern earthquake, landslide, or natural disaster with the Qur’anic event.

Nor should the event of the Hereafter be restricted without evidence to a familiar geological phenomenon.

The verse depicts the mountains’ transformation without explaining its precise mechanism.

5. Al-Mursalāt 77:10: nasf among immense transformations

وَإِذَا الْجِبَالُ نُسِفَتْ

wa-idhā al-jibālu nusifat

Translation:

“And when the mountains are uprooted and scattered.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:10

This is the third and final verse containing ن س ف.

Unlike Ṭā Hā 20:105, the verb is passive.

A. The meaning of نُسِفَتْ

نُسِفَتْ is a passive perfect verb.

الْجِبَال is a nonhuman plural, hence the feminine singular verb. One grammatical analysis treats this noun as the passive subject of an omitted verb explained by نُسِفَتْ; the expressed verb’s passive subject is an implicit pronoun referring to the mountains.

The mountains undergo the action, but its agent is not explicitly named in the same sentence.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 names رَبِّي as the subject; Al-Mursalāt 77:10 uses a passive sentence.

This difference does not mean that the verses posit conflicting agents.

The passive can focus attention on the event and what happens to the mountains.

It also does not establish that the mountains scatter spontaneously, independently of every other agent.

B. The preceding verses

Al-Mursalāt 77:8 says:

فَإِذَا النُّجُومُ طُمِسَتْ

fa-idhā al-nujūmu ṭumisat

Translation:

“Then when the stars are effaced.”

The next verse says:

وَإِذَا السَّمَاءُ فُرِجَتْ

wa-idhā al-samāʾu furijat

Translation:

“And when the sky is opened apart.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:9

Then:

وَإِذَا الْجِبَالُ نُسِفَتْ

wa-idhā al-jibālu nusifat

“And when the mountains are uprooted and scattered.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:10

The following passage speaks of the messengers’ appointed time and the Day of Judgment.

Together these describe transformations involving stars, sky, and mountains.

Each verb nevertheless retains its own meaning.

طُمِسَتْ belongs to ط م س and concerns effacement.

فُرِجَتْ belongs to ف ر ج and concerns being opened or split apart.

نُسِفَتْ belongs to ن س ف and concerns uprooting and scattering.

Translating them all as “were destroyed” would erase the differences between their images.

C. Is nasf of mountains identical to the stars’ scattering?

Al-Infiṭār 82:2 says:

وَإِذَا الْكَوَاكِبُ انْتَثَرَتْ

wa-idhā al-kawākibu intatharat

“And when the stars are scattered.”

The verb here belongs to ن ث ر, rather than ن س ف.

Both roots can concern scattering, but they are not identical.

Lexical examples of nasf highlight removal from a place and dispersal.

Nathr more particularly emphasizes strewing and the scattering of components.

Al-Infiṭār describes celestial bodies scattering.

Al-Mursalāt describes nasf of mountains.

This difference does not prove that stars and mountains undergo transformation according to two entirely separate physical laws; the verses give no such details.

The verbs’ semantic difference must nevertheless be preserved in lexical study.

6. Nasf of mountains compared with other Qur’anic verses

The Qur’an uses several roots to describe the mountains’ transformation in the Hereafter.

These verses illuminate nasf’s semantic field without making the verbs complete synonyms.

ن س ف and ب س س: uprooting and crushing

Al-Wāqiʿah says:

وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا

wa-bussati al-jibālu bassan

“And the mountains are crushed thoroughly.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5

The next verse says:

فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththan

“Then they become scattered dust.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6

The first verse uses بُسَّتْ, followed by its result in هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا.

ب س س here concerns crushing and breaking up the mountains.

Nasf in Ṭā Hā and Al-Mursalāt emphasizes uprooting and scattering them.

These images can describe an immense transformation in a common setting without establishing a definite physical sequence.

The expressions alone do not show that nasf necessarily precedes bass, or vice versa.

Such an order requires independent evidence.

Study of B S S

ن س ف and د ك ك: uprooting and pounding down

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14 says:

وَحُمِلَتِ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ فَدُكَّتَا دَكَّةً وَاحِدَةً

wa-ḥumilati al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu fa-dukkatā dakkatan wāḥidatan

Translation:

“The earth and mountains are lifted and pounded down in a single pounding.”

د ك ك concerns pounding, breaking down, and leveling.

This approaches the result of nasf in Ṭā Hā: removing the ground’s elevations.

Dakk and nasf remain different verbs.

Pounding and collapse are prominent in dakk; uprooting and dispersal in nasf.

Their overlap does not make nasf itself mean pounding with a single blow.

Study of D K K

ن س ف and ن ف ش: scattering and loosening a texture

Al-Qāriʿah 101:5 says:

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-al-ʿihni al-manfūshi

Translation:

“The mountains become like fluffed, colored wool.”

الْمَنْفُوش belongs to ن ف ش.

Applied to wool, nafsh concerns opening, separating, and fluffing its fibers.

Nasf, by contrast, concerns uprooting and scattering something.

The roots are close in spelling but should not be confused.

The wool image helps convey dispersal and the loss of the mountains’ solidity, without making nasf lexically identical to nafsh.

Nor does the comparison establish that the mountains necessarily become wool as a material.

The verse uses a simile, whose limits must be respected.

ن س ف and ن ث ر: uprooting and scattering components

ن ث ر denotes strewing or scattering the components of something.

The Qur’an uses مَنْثُورًا of scattered dust and pearls, and اِنْتَثَرَتْ of celestial bodies scattering.

Nasf may also lead to dispersal, but its established uses prominently include detachment from a former place or uprooting.

Wind may, for example, lift soil from the ground by nasf, after which the soil is seen as dispersed particles.

These aspects can coexist in one event without being lexically identical.

Nathr need not mean destruction: in Al-Insān 76:19, scattered pearls form part of a favorable image.

Nasf likewise has no intrinsically negative moral value in every use: the family also expresses sifting food and separating impurities.

Study of N TH R

ن س ف and ذ ر و: uprooting and winnowing

ذ ر و concerns scattering and strewing, especially by wind.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:1 says:

وَالذَّارِيَاتِ ذَرْوًا

wa-al-dhāriyāti dharwan

“By those that scatter, a scattering.”

Lexical and exegetical explanations also use تَذْرِيَة, dispersing particles, for nasf of the calf in Ṭā Hā 20:97.

The connection is clear because nasf may result in particles scattered by wind or a hand’s movement.

Other uses of nasf, however, concern uprooting buildings or plants.

Dharw and nasf overlap in some settings without sharing their entire semantic fields.

ن س ف and ن ز ع: uprooting and pulling out

Nazʿ concerns pulling, extracting, and separating something from its place.

One use of nasf likewise concerns uprooting.

Nazʿ may concern drawing a hand from a garment, taking away sovereignty, or removing rancor, whereas Qur’anic nasf concerns dispersing an artifact or uprooting mountains.

Though they overlap in removal, nazʿ more prominently evokes pulling out and separating, while nasf highlights uprooting with dismantling or dispersal.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute rule for every Arabic use.

7. Interpretive difficulties and translation proposals for Forqan

Does nasf always mean an explosion?

No.

In contemporary Persian, the expression often concerns severe demolition, especially with explosives.

Early Arabic also attests nasf for wind carrying soil, uprooting plants, dismantling buildings, and sifting food.

The modern technical meaning of explosion should not be imposed on every Qur’anic use.

The mountain verses concern immense transformation and the removal of elevations, without specifying tools or physical mechanism.

In Ṭā Hā 20:97, nasf also follows burning, but nasf itself does not mean exploding or burning.

Does nasf always mean reducing something to extremely fine particles?

No.

Fragmentation may precede or result from nasf in certain events.

In Moses’ account, the artifact is dispersed after being burned.

Parallel verses also illuminate the mountains’ transformation and loss of their former shape.

Yet uprooting plants or dismantling buildings does not require complete pulverization as part of the word’s meaning.

A rendering such as “turns them into extremely fine particles” therefore needs support from the particular verse’s context and evidence.

Does nasf necessarily mean complete destruction?

The root itself concerns uprooting, separating, and scattering.

The action may erase something’s former shape. Loss of form or structure, however, is not identical to the absolute annihilation of matter.

The mountain verses clearly describe elevations disappearing and the ground’s shape changing.

They do not provide scientific details about every material component’s survival or final condition.

“Destroying” may convey a general result in some translations, but “uprooting and scattering” better explains the root precisely.

Translation proposals

For نَسَفَ generally, “uprooted,” “removed,” “scattered,” or “dismantled” may fit, depending on object and context.

“Uprooting and scattering” is a suitable basic equivalent for نَسْف.

For مِنْسَف in food preparation, “sieve” or “implement for tossing and cleaning grain” is appropriate.

نُسَافَة naturally means “material separated during sifting,” or, where appropriate, “separated impurities.”

نَسُوف applied to a camel needs explanation of the particular use, such as a camel that pulls up vegetation by the roots.

The following are proposed for the three Qur’anic verses:

VerseQur’anic phraseProposed rendering
Ṭā Hā 20:97ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًاThen We shall scatter it thoroughly in the sea
Ṭā Hā 20:105يَنْسِفُهَا رَبِّي نَسْفًاMy Lord will uproot and scatter them
Al-Mursalāt 77:10وَإِذَا الْجِبَالُ نُسِفَتْAnd when the mountains are uprooted and scattered

“We shall scatter it” is natural in Ṭā Hā 20:97, since something is dispersed in water after burning.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 benefits from retaining both uprooting and scattering, since mountains and the removal of elevations are involved.

The same rendering suits Al-Mursalāt 77:10, while retaining its passive construction.

Using the root for mountains does not justify imposing a mountain-like image on the calf narrative’s translation.

Nasf should not be restricted to “exploding,” “burning,” “annihilating,” or “powdering” without sufficient independent support for the particular verse.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root ن س ف concerns uprooting, separating, and scattering something from its former place or condition.

Wind can lift and scatter soil and chaff. People can dismantle a building or sift grain to separate impurities. An animal may also pluck a plant from the ground with its mouth.

Other branches concern animals’ movement and bodily contact, hair worn away, and certain uses involving speech and movement.

Early examples, including poetry by Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim and attributed to al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī, illustrate specialized uses describing horses and camels.

Those specialized uses should not determine the Qur’anic meaning of nasf.

The root occurs five times in three Qur’anic verses.

In Ṭā Hā 20:97, Moses speaks of burning and then dispersing the god introduced by al-Sāmirī among the Children of Israel.

Burning and scattering are distinct actions. Nasf concerns dispersing the artifact in water, rather than directly meaning burning or the complete annihilation of matter.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 applies nasf to mountains. The following verses show that elevations disappear and the ground is described as level.

Al-Mursalāt 77:10 also concerns mountains, with a passive verb. The event forms part of an image of the Hereafter alongside transformations of stars and sky.

Parallel verses about crushed mountains, scattered dust, the pounding down of earth, and fluffed wool illuminate the event’s semantic setting.

Nasf, bass, dakk, nafsh, and nathr should not all be merged into one meaning, or combined to establish a definite physical sequence for the mountains’ transformation.

Nasf also need not mean destruction in Arabic. Some uses concern separating and dispersing to preserve what is desired, as in sifting food.

The action’s value and final result must be understood from its object and context.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

ن س ف denotes uprooting, separating, and removing something from its place, potentially with dispersal of its components or dismantling of its former structure.

Early Arabic attests this in wind carrying soil, uprooting plants, dismantling buildings, and sifting food.

In the Qur’an, نَسْف expresses uprooting and scattering.

Ṭā Hā 20:97 uses it for dispersing the manufactured calf in water.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 and Al-Mursalāt 77:10 apply it to mountains and the loss of their former shape and position.

The root alone does not imply explosion, burning, complete pulverization, or matter ceasing to exist. Any such features require evidence from the verse’s other elements or independent sources.

Uprooting and scattering are suitable shared senses for Qur’anic ن س ف. Each verse should be rendered with attention to its object, verbal construction, and the result the text itself describes.
