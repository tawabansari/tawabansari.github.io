Main meanings: nail and claw; in another branch, attaining what is sought, overcoming an opponent, and gaining the upper hand in a confrontation.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root ẓ f r has two established semantic branches. Ẓufur denotes a human nail or an animal's nail or claw. Ẓafar denotes attaining what is sought, succeeding in confrontation, or overcoming an opponent.

The branches can be connected through imagery: nails and claws serve for grasping, holding, and sometimes overcoming prey or an enemy. This resemblance alone does not establish that the historical meaning of victory developed directly from that of a nail.

Older Arabic dictionaries likewise present the family through attainment and overcoming on one hand, and the nail and associated strength on the other. Both branches should be examined without forcing either into the other.

Nails and claws in pre-Islamic Arabic

Ẓufur denotes the hard structure at the tips of human and many animal digits. It was used for human nails and for the nails or claws of birds and other animals.

Nails were used in everyday life for grasping, scratching, and scraping. In animals they can help grip surfaces, dig, obtain food, or hunt.

Figurative uses draw on these properties. A sharp nail can suggest the ability to inflict harm; a blunt one can depict inability to confront an enemy.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd contains:

لَا كَبِيرٌ دَالِفٌ مِنْ هَرَمٍ

lā kabīrun dālifun min haramin

أَرْهَبُ اللَّيْلَ وَلَا كَلُّ الظُّفُرْ

arhabu al-layla wa-lā kallu al-ẓufur

Translation: I am no decrepit old man who fears the night, nor one whose claw has grown blunt and powerless.

Kallu al-ẓufur describes a blunt or ineffective nail. The poet uses the image to deny his own weakness.

He is not describing the physical shape of his nail. The blunt nail depicts someone who has lost the power to confront others and defend himself.

The line is preserved in the collection attributed to Ṭarafah and in older lexical quotations. Variants include أَرْهَبُ النَّاسَ instead of أَرْهَبُ اللَّيْلَ. For this study the relevant image is كَلُّ الظُّفُر, which that variation does not alter.

In this verse attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, the nail thus represents the ability to confront or harm, beyond its bodily meaning.

The line does not prove that ẓafar, victory, originated from this image. Attainment and overcoming form an independently established branch of classical Arabic, whose presence in the language of revelation is also attested by the Quran.

Ẓafar: attaining and overcoming

In the second branch, ظَفِرَ بِالشَّيْءِ means attaining something sought.

When the object is something lost or desired, ẓafar can mean finding or obtaining it.

When the object is an enemy or rival, it refers to overcoming them or gaining the upper hand.

In ظَفِرَ بِعَدُوِّهِ, a person has overcome the enemy or gained possession and control of them.

This branch therefore extends beyond warfare, although success and overcoming are prominent when an enemy is involved.

Here ẓafar expresses attainment or an advantage actually gained, rather than merely hoping or striving for victory.

2. Lexical forms and the word family

Important forms include ظُفُر, ظُفْر, أَظْفَار, أَظَافِير, ظَفَر, ظَفِرَ, يَظْفَرُ, ظَافِر, مَظْفُور, أَظْفَرَ, ظَفَّرَ, مُظَفَّر, and تَظَافَرَ.

Ẓufur: nail and claw

Ẓufur is a singular noun, commonly pluralized as aẓfār. Aẓāfīr is also recorded in this lexical family.

Ẓufr, with a vowelless fāʾ, is another established pronunciation.

The word is applied to humans and animals, although their nails differ in shape.

A human nail is usually a relatively flat surface at a fingertip. In many birds the corresponding structure is curved and sharp, serving to grasp or hunt. Other animals have differently shaped hard structures at their toe tips.

Arabic may use a general term for related structures while also distinguishing particular forms through words such as mikhlab, ẓilf, and ḥāfir.

Ẓafar: attainment and overcoming

Ẓafar, with short a vowels on ẓāʾ and fāʾ, is the verbal noun of ẓafira.

Its main sense is attaining what is sought, overcoming, or gaining the upper hand.

Ẓāfir is the active participle: someone who has attained or prevailed.

In this branch the passive participle commonly occurs as مَظْفُورٌ بِهِ, naming the person or thing attained or brought under another's control.

The object may be a person, property, goal, or outcome whose attainment had not previously been assured.

Aẓfara: causing someone to prevail

Aẓfara is a form IV verb.

Its difference from ẓafira becomes clear through the roles of subject and object.

In ظَفِرَ فُلَانٌ بِعَدُوِّهِ, the person himself prevails over his enemy.

In أَظْفَرَهُ بِعَدُوِّهِ, the subject causes another person to prevail over or gain hold of his enemy.

In the branch of overcoming, aẓfara therefore expresses causing attainment or victory.

This distinction matters in the Quranic verse: The God is the subject of aẓfara, and the people granted the advantage are identified by the object pronoun.

Ẓaffara: different uses

Ẓaffara has several Arabic uses.

One branch means causing someone to prevail or granting victory. Muẓaffar can describe someone repeatedly victorious or successful against enemies.

In the nail-related branch, ẓaffara can mean pressing a nail into something and leaving its mark, such as scratching skin or pressing a nail into fruit.

The morphological form is the same, but object and context distinguish causing victory from marking with a nail.

Taẓāfara: joining forces and mutual support

Taẓāfara is recorded for a group joining in an undertaking or supporting one another.

In تَظَافَرَ الْقَوْمُ عَلَيْهِ, people join forces against someone to overcome or act against him.

This relates to overcoming and attainment, but additionally expresses the participation of several people.

Taẓāfara should be distinguished from taẓāhara, from ẓ h r, even though some uses overlap.

Other uses of the family

Lexical sources record ẓafarah for a fleshy growth or membrane extending from the corner of the eye, explaining the name through its resemblance to a nail.

Aẓfār also named an aromatic material with nail-like pieces, used for fragrance or fumigation.

Some descriptions of plants use forms of this family for nail-like shoots or projections.

These show naming based on resemblance to a nail, but none of these senses occurs in the Quran.

3. Quranic occurrences

The root occurs twice, in two morphological forms, in the Quran:

Ẓufur in Al-Anʿām 6:146 and aẓfarakum in Al-Fatḥ 48:24.

The first is the noun for a nail; the second is a form IV verb meaning to cause to prevail.

A. Ẓufur: animal nails and claws

وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ هَادُوا حَرَّمْنَا كُلَّ ذِي ظُفُرٍ ۖ وَمِنَ الْبَقَرِ وَالْغَنَمِ حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ شُحُومَهُمَا إِلَّا مَا حَمَلَتْ ظُهُورُهُمَا أَوِ الْحَوَايَا أَوْ مَا اخْتَلَطَ بِعَظْمٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَاهُمْ بِبَغْيِهِمْ ۖ وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

wa-ʿalā alladhīna hādū ḥarramnā kulla dhī ẓufurin wa-mina al-baqari wa-l-ghanami ḥarramnā ʿalayhim shuḥūmahumā illā mā ḥamalat ẓuhūruhumā awi al-ḥawāyā aw mā ikhtalaṭa bi-ʿaẓmin dhālika jazaynāhum bi-baghyihim wa-innā la-ṣādiqūn

Al-Anʿām 6:146

And for those who became Jews We prohibited every nail-bearing animal. Of cattle and sheep We prohibited their fat to them, except what their backs or intestines carry, or what is mixed with bone. Thus We recompensed them for their transgression; and indeed We are truthful.

Al-Anʿām 6:146

Ẓufur here refers to the nail or hard structure at the end of an animal's digits.

Kulla dhī ẓufur comprises kull, expressing inclusion; dhī, meaning possessing; and ẓufur, nail.

Its literal sense is therefore “every possessor of a nail” or “every nail-bearing animal.”

The important question is which animals this designation identifies in historical usage and the verse's context.

Which animals fall under dhī ẓufur?

Lexical sources and early explanations record several understandings of its scope.

Some apply it to animals whose digits were described as not divided or separated, including camels and certain birds.

Others include clawed animals under the designation.

Broader explanations place nails, claws, and certain hoof forms in a common semantic field through structural resemblance.

These differences caution against turning ẓufur directly into a fixed modern zoological category.

A human nail, a bird of prey's talon, and the hard structure at a camel's toe differ in shape and function. Arabic has specific names for some of these structures, while ẓufur can also be used more generally.

Conversely, taking “every possessor of a nail” without context to include everything with any nail-like structure would greatly broaden the designation.

After كُلَّ ذِي ظُفُرٍ, the verse separately mentions cattle and sheep, specifying a prohibition on fat and its exceptions.

This separates the ruling on the first group from the ruling on cattle and sheep fat.

Although the lexical sense of ẓufur is clear, identifying every species historically included under dhī ẓufur requires further examination. The noun alone provides no definitive list.

The prohibition's scope in the verse

The verse begins وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ هَادُوا.

This specifies the people subject to the ruling: those who became Jews.

The subsequent pronoun in ʿalayhim refers to the same group.

The verse thus reports a prohibition imposed on a specified group, rather than issuing a general command to all humanity.

This accords with the preceding context.

In Al-Anʿām 6:145, the addressee of revelation is commanded to state what he finds prohibited in what was revealed to him. Al-Anʿām 6:146 then describes prohibitions imposed upon those who became Jews.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:160 likewise connects the prohibition of certain good things for a group of Jews with their wrongdoing.

The noun ẓufur and the animals it designates should therefore not be detached from the verse and made the sole basis for a universal prohibition.

The reported ruling has a specified historical and personal scope. The verse itself attributes it to that group's transgression through bi-baghyihim.

Lexically, ẓufur names a bodily feature. Prohibition, those subject to it, and its stated reason come from other words and constructions in the verse.

Nails and claws in this verse

Persian nākhun usually denotes human nails and similar structures, while changāl more readily evokes sharp, curved animal claws, especially those of predatory birds.

Arabic likewise has mikhlab as a specific term for a claw or talon.

Ẓufur is lexically broader than a predatory claw. Translating it simply as “clawed” may emphasize one group while excluding other possible uses.

“Nail-bearing” stays closer to the Arabic wording, although readers need explanation of the animals intended.

“Every nail-bearing animal” is suitable for a literal base translation, with the distinctions between nails, claws, and related structures explained separately.

B. Aẓfarakum: causing to prevail

وَهُوَ الَّذِي كَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ عَنْهُمْ بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ أَظْفَرَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرًا

wa-huwa alladhī kaffa aydiyahum ʿankum wa-aydiyakum ʿanhum bi-baṭni makkata min baʿdi an aẓfarakum ʿalayhim wa-kāna allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīrā

Al-Fatḥ 48:24

And He is the One who restrained their hands from you and your hands from them in the valley of Makkah, after He had caused you to prevail over them; and The God sees what you do.

Al-Fatḥ 48:24

Aẓfarakum is a past-tense form IV verb.

Its subject is The God, and kum refers to the group addressed.

ʿAlayhim identifies the opposing group over whom the believers had prevailed.

Aẓfarakum ʿalayhim therefore means that The God caused them to prevail over their opponents or granted them the advantage and ability to gain hold of them.

Ẓafira and aẓfara

In the basic verb ẓafira, the subject attains what is sought or prevails over the opponent.

Form IV aẓfara means causing another to prevail.

The God is the subject in this verse, attributing the believers' advantage to Him.

This attribution does not change the lexical meaning; it identifies the cause of the victory and its recipients.

Here the word means more than helping: it expresses an outcome in which one side has gained the upper hand.

Prevailing alongside restraint from conflict

A significant feature is the pairing of aẓfarakum with كَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنْكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ عَنْهُمْ.

Kaffa, from k f f, here means restraining or preventing.

The verse says that The God restrained their hands from you and yours from them after causing you to prevail over them.

Iẓfār here is consequently not equivalent to destroying or killing the opponent.

A group can gain an advantage and the capacity to harm or capture its opponents while being restrained from further confrontation.

The verse places one side's advantage and the restraint of both sides' hands together.

Ẓafar can thus denote a real advantage without necessarily implying killing, destruction, or final conquest.

This does not make peace or abstention from fighting the lexical meaning of ẓafar. Attainment and prevailing remain its meaning; restraint is expressed separately by kaffa.

The relationship between ẓafar and fatḥ

Al-Fatḥ 48:1 opens the surah with إِنَّا فَتَحْنَا لَكَ فَتْحًا مُبِينًا.

Fatḥ, from f t ḥ, concerns opening. In confrontational settings it can express an opening, victory, or the removal of an obstacle.

Ẓafar emphasizes attaining what is sought or prevailing over an opponent.

Al-Fatḥ 48:24 specifically describes gaining the upper hand over an opposing group. Al-Fatḥ 48:1 describes a clear opening granted by The God; determining its precise historical referent requires separate contextual study.

The concepts can be related in one event without being identical in root or semantic range.

Prevailing and subsequent responsibility

The verse ends وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرًا.

This recalls that human actions remain under The God's sight.

A contextual inference is that gaining the upper hand does not mean unlimited freedom to act against the opponent. The verse reporting victory also reports the restraint of hands.

The clauses thus distinguish having power over an opponent from using that power.

Ẓafar itself expresses advantage and attainment. How such power is exercised is understood through context and other Quranic teachings.

4. Distinctions from related roots

Ẓ f r (ẓafar) and f w z (fawz): attainment and ultimate success

F w z concerns success, escape from loss, and reaching a desired outcome.

Āl ʿImrān 3:185 uses fa-qad fāza for someone kept away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden. There fawz concerns a person's ultimate success.

Ẓafar can describe overcoming a rival or obtaining something sought.

Someone may prevail in battle without succeeding morally or in life's ultimate outcome.

Conversely, someone may attain salvation without prevailing over another person in worldly life.

“Victory” and “salvation” therefore cannot replace one another in every context.

Ẓafar particularly emphasizes attainment or prevailing in confrontation; fawz denotes success in reaching a desired outcome, especially escaping loss.

Ẓ f r (ẓafar) and gh l b (ghalaba): attainment and overcoming

Ghalaba denotes one force, person, or group overcoming another.

Al-Rūm 30:2 and Al-Rūm 30:3 describe defeat and subsequent victory.

Ẓafar can express the same advantage, while more explicitly evoking attaining a goal or achieving a sought outcome.

In military use, ghalabah emphasizes one side's dominance; ẓafar can highlight the attained advantage or bringing the opponent under one's control.

This is not an absolute distinction, since both can mean victory over an enemy.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:24, aẓfarakum ʿalayhim conveys an advantage placing the opposing group within the believers' power.

Ẓ f r (ẓafar) and n ṣ r (naṣr): prevailing and assistance

N ṣ r concerns helping, supporting, and giving assistance.

Āl ʿImrān 3:126 speaks of assistance that comes from The God.

Helping and prevailing are distinct concepts.

Someone may receive help without yet attaining the desired result. Ẓafar normally refers to attainment and an achieved outcome.

Naṣr may take the form of support, strengthening, or providing means; ẓafar in confrontation concerns gaining the upper hand.

“He caused you to prevail over them” therefore suits Al-Fatḥ 48:24. “He helped you” conveys only part of the relationship and leaves the achieved advantage unclear.

Ẓ f r (ẓafar) and f t ḥ (fatḥ): attainment and opening

The basic sense of fatḥ concerns opening.

Opening a door or path, removing an obstacle, and providing an opening belong to this family.

In battle or confrontation, fatḥ can involve victory, although its basic image differs from that of ẓafar.

Ẓafar emphasizes obtaining what is sought or prevailing; fatḥ can express the opening of a path or removal of a barrier.

Both can occur in one event, but conflating their roots erases the distinction between their linguistic images.

Ẓ f r (ẓufur) and kh l b (mikhlab): nail and claw

Mikhlab, from kh l b, names an animal claw, especially the sharp, curved claw used to grasp prey.

Ẓufur is a broader term for a nail and is applied to humans and different animals.

A mikhlab can be structurally related to a nail, but not every ẓufur is a predatory claw.

This matters in Al-Anʿām 6:146, where “every clawed animal” could restrict the designation to animals possessing hunting claws.

“Every nail-bearing animal” stays closer to the wording, leaving the exact referents for separate explanation.

Ẓ f r (ẓufur) and ẓ l f (ẓilf): nail and cloven hoof

Ẓilf, from ẓ l f, names the hard terminal foot structure of animals such as cattle and sheep.

Ẓilf and ẓufur are distinct Arabic words, although both concern hard coverings at animal digit tips.

Ḥāfir names the hoof or hard foot structure of animals such as horses.

These terms show Arabic distinguishing different forms of animal locomotor structures.

Nevertheless, broader usage can draw analogies between similar structures, leading some lexicographers to give ẓufur a wider animal-related scope.

This breadth should not erase the distinctions between ẓufur, ẓilf, ḥāfir, and mikhlab.

5. Morphological and semantic observations

Ẓufur and ẓafar

The vowels of these forms matter.

Ẓufur, with u on ẓāʾ and fāʾ, is the noun for a nail.

Ẓafar, with a on both consonants, is the verbal noun of attainment and prevailing.

Both appear as ظفر without vowel marks, but their pronunciation and meaning must remain distinct.

Ẓufur in Al-Anʿām 6:146 is a genitive noun.

Aẓfarakum in Al-Fatḥ 48:24 is a form IV verb from the attainment and overcoming branch.

These two uses therefore require different translations.

The role of ʿalā in Al-Fatḥ 48:24

Ẓafira bihi is used for attaining a thing or gaining hold of a person.

Ẓafira ʿalayhi is also recorded for overcoming an opponent.

The verse combines ʿalā with the pronoun him.

This construction clarifies one group's advantage over the other.

“He caused you to prevail over them” is therefore more accurate than “He brought you to them,” since confrontation and advantage are at issue.

Form IV and attribution of the outcome to The God

In aẓfarakum, The God is the subject and kum the object.

The construction attributes the addressees' prevailing over their opponents to Him.

Form IV alone does not specify the means, strength, or method through which this occurred.

Such details require context and independent evidence.

The verse itself describes restraining both groups' hands, so prevailing here accompanies the cessation or prevention of confrontation.

Evaluative implications

Ẓafar does not itself identify the victor as good or bad.

A person may rightly overcome an aggressor; an oppressor may also overcome another person.

The lexical meaning of attainment and advantage holds in both cases, while moral judgment depends on purpose, conduct, and circumstances.

Al-Fatḥ 48:24 attributes the outcome to The God in a particular context. It does not make every human victory, at any time and by any means, evidence of divine moral approval.

Likewise, a nail or claw does not itself signify an animal's predatory nature, impurity, or prohibition. Those properties do not follow from ẓufur alone.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The root's two semantic branches must remain distinct.

Ẓufur: Nail; for animals, nail or claw according to the structure.

Aẓfār: Nails.

Ẓafira: Attained, obtained what was sought, prevailed, or won.

Ẓafar: Attainment, success in obtaining a goal, or victory over an opponent.

Aẓfara: Caused to prevail, granted victory, or enabled attainment.

Muẓaffar: Granted victory, victorious, or favored with success.

Translation in Al-Anʿām 6:146

For كُلَّ ذِي ظُفُرٍ, the principal proposal is:

“Every nail-bearing animal.”

This retains the literal structure without restricting it to predatory clawed animals or a single specific animal group.

A lexical note can explain the use for animal nails and claws and the need for separate study of the precise historical referents.

Translation in Al-Fatḥ 48:24

For مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ أَظْفَرَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ, the principal proposal is:

“After He had caused you to prevail over them.”

This preserves both the causative force of form IV and the advantage expressed through ʿalayhim.

“After He had granted you victory over them” is also possible. “Caused you to prevail” suits this context particularly well, since the same verse speaks of restraining both groups' hands without limiting the outcome to the opponent's destruction or complete military defeat.

Brief meanings for word boxes

Brief root meaning: Two main branches: nail and claw; attaining what is sought and overcoming an opponent.

Ẓufur — Al-Anʿām 6:146

Context: A nail or analogous animal structure; in dhī ẓufur, a nail-bearing animal.

Aẓfarakum — Al-Fatḥ 48:24

Context: He caused you to prevail over them; The God granted the addressees an advantage and power over the opposing group.

7. Conclusion

Ẓ f r has two established branches: nails and claws, and attaining a goal or overcoming an opponent.

The first names the hard structure at the tips of human and animal digits. The verse attributed to Ṭarafah uses a blunt nail to depict weakness in confrontation, showing the nail as an image of strength as well as a body part.

The second branch emphasizes achieved attainment or advantage. The causative aẓfara denotes causing another to prevail.

Grasping with a claw and attaining a goal can be connected through imagery, but that resemblance alone does not establish their historical derivation. Both branches must remain in the definition.

The Quran uses this root twice.

In Al-Anʿām 6:146, ẓufur in kulla dhī ẓufur names a nail or analogous animal structure. The verse reports prohibitions imposed on those who became Jews; its animal designation should not be detached from that scope and turned into a universal prohibition.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:24, aẓfarakum describes causing the believers to prevail. The same verse reports The God's restraint of both sides' hands, showing that prevailing need not imply destruction or killing.

Ẓafar is related to ghalabah, fawz, naṣr, and fatḥ, but is not identical to any of them throughout their uses. Its prominent feature is attained success or an achieved advantage in confrontation.

Final definition: Ẓ f r has two main branches: ẓufur, nail or claw; and ẓafar, attaining a goal, successfully obtaining something, or prevailing over an opponent. The Quran uses the former to describe an animal category and the latter through aẓfara for causing one group to prevail over another.
