Containing, retaining, and attentive reception

The Arabic root w-ʿ-y denotes containing something, gathering it in a receptacle, and retaining it. When its object is speech or knowledge, it takes on the sense of receiving, understanding, and remembering. Within this family, وِعَاء means a container or receptacle, وَعَى means receiving and retaining, and أَوْعَى means putting into a container and storing up.

The Qur’an uses this family for material containers, attentive reception of speech, storing up possessions, and what people retain within themselves. It occurs seven times across four verses.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

In evidence attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic, أَوْعَى appears in connection with storing and retaining something as provisions. A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ says:

الْخَيْرُ يَبْقَى وَإِنْ طَالَ الزَّمَانُ بِهِ
وَالشَّرُّ أَخْبَثُ مَا أَوْعَيْتَ مِنْ زَادِ

al-khayru yabqā wa-in ṭāla al-zamānu bihi / wa-al-sharru akhbathu mā awʿayta min zādi

Translation: “Good endures, however long time passes over it; evil is the foulest provision you have stored.”

Here أَوْعَيْتَ concerns storing up and keeping something with oneself. The poet draws on provisions for a journey, but treats human deeds as provisions in place of food and equipment. Evil becomes something a person can accumulate and carry along.

This attestation shows that using īʿāʾ for storing, and extending it from material things to moral matters, is known in early poetic tradition. The verse has reached us through later transmission, however, so its precise date should not be treated as certain.

Classical Arabic also records وَعَيْتُ الْعِلْمَ for receiving and retaining knowledge. Its connection with containing is clear: just as a receptacle holds something, a person can receive speech or knowledge and remember it. That semantic connection does not by itself establish that the mental meaning historically developed after the material one.

Arabic lexicons record other uses under this entry: وَعَى الْعَظْمُ for a broken bone knitting together, including with a crooked union, وَعَى الْجُرْحُ for a wound discharging pus, and الْوَعَى for noise and loud sounds. Their historical relationship to receiving and retaining is not equally certain. The noisy sense, in particular, has been connected with w-gh-y. These subsidiary branches should not be imposed on Qur’anic words without evidence.

2. Qur’anic forms and semantic core

The forms of this family in the Qur’an are:

  • وِعَاء and its plural أَوْعِيَة: a container or receptacle.
  • تَعِيَهَا: that it receive and retain it.
  • وَاعِيَة: receiving and retaining.
  • أَوْعَى: he stored up.
  • يُوعُونَ: they gather and retain within themselves.

Two principal settings appear here: material retention and mental retention.

The shared core of the principal uses can be expressed as gathering something within a receptacle or receiving space and retaining it. That space may be an actual container or, in mental usage, a person’s memory and inner life.

Nevertheless, وَعَى and أَوْعَى are not interchangeable in every verse. Each form’s meaning also depends on its verbal structure and object.

3. Qur’anic uses

1. Wiʿāʾ and awʿiyah: containers for keeping things

Yūsuf 12:76

فَبَدَأَ بِأَوْعِيَتِهِمْ قَبْلَ وِعَاءِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ اسْتَخْرَجَهَا مِنْ وِعَاءِ أَخِيهِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ كِدْنَا لِيُوسُفَ ۖ مَا كَانَ لِيَأْخُذَ أَخَاهُ فِي دِينِ الْمَلِكِ إِلَّا أَنْ يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ ۚ نَرْفَعُ دَرَجَاتٍ مَنْ نَشَاءُ ۗ وَفَوْقَ كُلِّ ذِي عِلْمٍ عَلِيمٌ

fa-badaʾa bi-awʿiyatihim qabla wiʿāʾi akhīhi thumma istakhrajahā min wiʿāʾi akhīhi kadhālika kidnā li-Yūsufa mā kāna li-yaʾkhudha akhāhu fī dīni al-maliki illā an yashāʾa Allāhu narfaʿu darajātin man nashāʾu wa-fawqa kulli dhī ʿilmin ʿalīm

Yūsuf 12:76

Translation: “He began with their containers before his brother’s container, then brought it out of his brother’s container. Thus We devised a plan for Joseph. He could not have taken his brother under the king’s system, unless The God willed. We raise in degrees whomever We will; and above everyone possessing knowledge is one who knows.”

Here أَوْعِيَة is the plural of وِعَاء. It refers to containers in which the travelers kept their belongings or loads.

The Qur’an does not specify their material, shape, or size. They might have been bags, saddlebags, or another kind of baggage receptacle. The word denotes something capable of containing and retaining its contents.

The repetition of وِعَاءِ أَخِيهِ highlights the spatial role of the word: an object is brought out of Joseph’s brother’s container.

Here wiʿāʾ names a container and does not mean awareness, memory, or understanding. Despite its resemblance to أَوْعَى, أَوْعِيَة is not a verb; it is the broken plural of وِعَاء.

2. Taʿiyahā and wāʿiyah: attentive reception and retention

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:12

لِنَجْعَلَهَا لَكُمْ تَذْكِرَةً وَتَعِيَهَا أُذُنٌ وَاعِيَةٌ

li-najʿalahā lakum tadhkiratan wa-taʿiyahā udhunun wāʿiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:12

Translation: “So that We may make it a reminder for you, and an attentive ear may receive and retain it.”

تَعِيَهَا comes from وَعَى يَعِي. The pronoun hā refers to what the preceding verses present as a reminder. وَاعِيَة is the feminine active participle of the same root, modifying أُذُن, ear.

The Qur’an is not speaking merely of sound reaching an ear. Someone may hear a sound without grasping the speech, or may subsequently forget it.

أُذُنٌ وَاعِيَةٌ is an ear that receives and retains what it hears. The ear is named as the means of reception, but the meaning is not confined to its bodily activity.

For example, two people may hear an instructive story. One simply passes over it; the other remembers its content and reflects upon it. This illustrates the sense of waʿy in the verse.

Lexicons also record al-wāʿiyah for crying out or wailing. In this verse, however, its association with تَعِيَهَا makes reception and retention clear. Identical written forms do not justify transferring the meaning of a cry to an attentive ear.

3. Awʿā: gathering and storing up

Al-Maʿārij 70:18

وَجَمَعَ فَأَوْعَىٰ

wa-jamaʿa fa-awʿā

Al-Maʿārij 70:18

Translation: “And gathered, then stored up.”

أَوْعَى is a Form IV perfect verb. In material use, أَوْعَى الشَّيْءَ means putting something into a receptacle and retaining it.

Here it follows جَمَعَ. Jamaʿa denotes gathering, while fa-awʿā focuses on storing and retaining what has been gathered.

The verbs are close but not identical. Someone may gather things and then use, distribute, or transfer them. Fa-awʿā draws attention to retaining and accumulating what has been gathered.

The context matters. Al-Maʿārij 70:17 describes someone who turns away. Al-Maʿārij 70:21 describes a person who withholds when good comes to him; Al-Maʿārij 70:24 and Al-Maʿārij 70:25, by contrast, describe people whose possessions contain a known right for the petitioner and the deprived.

This connection places gathering and storing in a description of blameworthy behavior. The root w-ʿ-y itself, however, does not mean miserliness, injustice, or a prohibition on saving. The criticism emerges from the overall conduct and context.

Simply translating أَوْعَى as “was miserly” substitutes a contextual implication for the verb. “Stored up” or “kept in store” is closer to its lexical structure; its connection with withholding generosity belongs in the explanation.

4. Yūʿūna: what they retain within themselves

Al-Inshiqāq 84:23

وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يُوعُونَ

wa-Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā yūʿūn

Al-Inshiqāq 84:23

Translation: “And The God knows best what they retain within themselves.”

يُوعُونَ is the plural imperfect of أَوْعَى, the same verb that appears as فَأَوْعَى in al-Maʿārij.

Here no material possessions are explicitly named as the object. The preceding verses concern people who do not believe when the Qur’an is recited to them and who deny it. This context supports an inward reading of what they retain.

In this context, بِمَا يُوعُونَ concerns what people gather and retain within themselves, including beliefs, intentions, and what they harbor concerning the truth.

The image of receptacle and contents is applied to human inwardness. A person can retain speech or a belief without everything within them being visible to others.

In explanations of this verse, يُوعُونَ is sometimes rendered “they conceal.” The context makes this understandable, but īʿāʾ and kitmān are not exactly identical.

Īʿāʾ emphasizes gathering and retaining within oneself. The contents remaining hidden may be a consequence; the verb does not invariably denote deliberate concealment.

“What they retain within themselves” therefore preserves the root’s principal image. The reflection can explain that The God knows their inward reality, intentions, and beliefs.

4. Semantic branches and interpretive challenges

Comparison of the uses in these four verses brings out three points.

First, w-ʿ-y should not be restricted to mental awareness. In Yūsuf 12:76, wiʿāʾ is a material container. Al-Ḥāqqah 69:12 concerns receiving and retaining speech; Al-Maʿārij 70:18 concerns storing up; Al-Inshiqāq 84:23 concerns what people retain within themselves.

Second, وَعَى and أَوْعَى differ in use. In al-Ḥāqqah, waʿā concerns receiving and retaining a message; in al-Maʿārij, awʿā concerns accumulation. Yet these verses do not establish an absolute rule that waʿā is always mental and awʿā always material. Yūʿūna in al-Inshiqāq resists such a rigid division.

Third, modern “awareness” can cover a broad range, from alertness and cognition to social or political consciousness. Not all these senses can be placed directly within Qur’anic waʿy. In al-Ḥāqqah, the specific meaning is receiving and retaining a reminder. Modern expressions such as “awareness raising” should not replace a Qur’anic derivative’s meaning without examination.

5. Comparison with related roots

W-ʿ-y (waʿy) and s-m-ʿ (samʿ): reception and hearing

Samʿ concerns hearing, whereas cognitive waʿy emphasizes receiving and retaining content. An ear can hear sound, but attentive reception of speech involves more than sound arriving. Qur’anic hearing can itself include acceptance and attention in context, so the distinction is not absolute.

W-ʿ-y (waʿy) and ḥ-f-ẓ (ḥifẓ): remembering and preserving

Ḥifẓ encompasses keeping, protecting, and preventing loss. Cognitive waʿy emphasizes receiving something and retaining it in memory. They overlap in retaining speech and knowledge, but not every use of ḥifẓ can be replaced by waʿy.

W-ʿ-y (waʿy) and f-h-m (fahm): reception and understanding

Fahm denotes grasping the meaning of speech or a problem. Waʿy can include understanding, but retention is prominent in its semantic range. Someone may memorize speech without understanding it fully, or understand a matter and later forget its details. Understanding and retention are related but are not one identical activity.

W-ʿ-y (waʿy) and j-m-ʿ (jamʿ): gathering and storing

Jamʿ concerns bringing scattered things together. In material use, īʿāʾ emphasizes putting and retaining something in a receptacle or place of storage. Their combination in وَجَمَعَ فَأَوْعَىٰ shows how gathering and storing can be distinct aspects of one action.

Study of j-m-ʿ

W-ʿ-y (waʿy) and k-t-m (katm): inward retention and concealment

Kitmān concerns concealing something. Īʿāʾ primarily concerns retaining it within oneself. What is held within may be hidden from others, but inward retention need not amount to deliberately concealing the truth. In Al-Inshiqāq 84:23, the context of denial connects these meanings.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For وِعَاء and أَوْعِيَة, “container,” “containers,” or, with traveling baggage, “baggage receptacle” are suitable. A particular material or shape should not be specified without evidence.

For تَعِيَهَا, depending on the sentence, “receive and retain it” or “keep it in memory” is appropriate.

For وَاعِيَة in أُذُنٌ وَاعِيَةٌ, “an attentive ear” is natural and understandable. The explanation should clarify that reception and retention of what is heard are intended.

For فَأَوْعَى, “then stored up” or “then kept in store” is appropriate. Miserliness and withholding generosity should be explained from the context rather than directly substituted for the verb.

For يُوعُونَ, “they retain within themselves” is appropriate. Where beliefs or intentions being hidden is intended, that meaning should be explained contextually.

7. Conclusion

In its principal uses, w-ʿ-y concerns containing and retaining something. Material containers, storing possessions, receiving speech, and retaining inward contents express different aspects of this meaning.

Wiʿāʾ is a receptacle; cognitive waʿā conveys receiving and retaining; awʿā can denote gathering and storing in material or inward settings.

The root itself is neither inherently commendatory nor condemnatory. Attentive reception of a reminder is praised in al-Ḥāqqah, whereas storing up in a context of turning away and withholding is condemned in al-Maʿārij. In al-Inshiqāq, The God knows what people retain within themselves.

Thus the defensible core is “taking in and retaining.” What is retained, how it is received, and its moral value are determined by the derivative and the context of each verse.
