Intense fear, dread filling the heart, and the loss of a sense of security

In its best-known Arabic usage, the root R ʿ B denotes fear or dread that overwhelms a person and disrupts their sense of security. Ruʿb is closely related to khawf and fazaʿ, but many uses emphasize the intensity of fear and its hold over a person.

A person may recognize danger and still make a considered decision despite feeling afraid. When dread overwhelms them, however, they may lose the ability to assess the situation or stand firm, and turn to flight, retreat, or another response.

Nevertheless, ruʿb should not always be restricted to flight or the complete loss of decision-making ability. Intense fear can have different consequences; the word itself does not determine all of them.

The root has other Arabic uses as well. Raʿaba al-ḥawḍa means to fill a pool; saylun rāʿibun describes a flood filling a valley; and raʿʿaba al-sanāma means to cut a camel’s hump into pieces.

These uses show that the family is not lexically confined to fear. Their resemblance does not, without sufficient historical evidence, establish one certain original meaning for all its branches.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only in the sense of dread.

Al-ruʿba occurs four times in connection with casting fear into the hearts of groups of opponents; ruʿban occurs once in the account of the Companions of the Cave.

The latter is especially significant: the Qur’an says that someone who looked upon them would flee and be filled with dread. Qur’anic ruʿb is therefore not confined to warfare or an enemy’s fear of a military force.

Qur’anic forms: الرُّعْبَ, al-ruʿba (Āl ʿImrān 3:151; Al-Anfāl 8:12; Al-Aḥzāb 33:26; Al-Ḥashr 59:2), and رُعْبًا, ruʿban (Al-Kahf 18:18).

Other recorded Arabic forms include raʿaba, yarʿabu, raʿb, ruʿb, ruʿub, ruʿiba, raʿuba, raʿʿaba, tarʿīb, tarʿāb, irtaʿaba, irtiʿāb, marʿūb, murtaʿib, raʿīb, rāʿib, raʿʿāb, tirʿābah, ruʿbūbah, and raʿābīb.

Their structures and uses differ. In particular, fear, filling, and cutting must be distinguished as separate semantic branches.

1. Semantic cores and early Arabic usage

Three semantic branches

Lexical analysis identifies three distinct branches within this family:

الرَّاءُ وَالْعَيْنُ وَالْبَاءُ أُصُولٌ ثَلَاثَةٌ: أَحَدُهَا الْخَوْفُ، وَالثَّانِي الْمَلْءُ، وَالْآخَرُ الْقَطْعُ

al-rāʾu wa-al-ʿaynu wa-al-bāʾu uṣūlun thalāthatun: aḥaduhā al-khawfu, wa-al-thānī al-malʾu, wa-al-ākharu al-qaṭʿu

“Rāʾ, ʿayn, and bāʾ have three semantic foundations: fear, filling, and cutting.”

This classification matters methodologically.

Etymological research cannot always reduce every recorded use of a family to one simple image.

Sometimes a three-letter sequence has several semantic branches whose precise historical relationship remains unclear.

For R ʿ B, these branches are:

  • Fearing and frightening;
  • Becoming full or filling something;
  • Cutting and separating pieces.

Only the first branch—dread—is directly used in the Qur’an.

The other uses nevertheless matter for understanding the breadth of Arabic and should neither be omitted nor forced into the meaning of fear.

Ruʿb: fear and dread

One concise lexical definition states:

الرُّعْبُ: الْخَوْفُ

al-ruʿbu: al-khawfu

“Ruʿb means fear.”

Another definition states:

الرُّعْبُ وَالرُّعُبُ: الْفَزَعُ وَالْخَوْفُ

al-ruʿbu wa-al-ruʿubu: al-fazaʿu wa-al-khawfu

“Ruʿb and ruʿub mean fright and fear.”

These definitions identify khawf and fazaʿ as the familiar core of this branch.

In its clear uses, however, ruʿb often conveys intense, overwhelming fear.

Another explanation describes its intensity as follows:

الرُّعْبُ: الِانْقِطَاعُ مِنِ امْتِلَاءِ الْخَوْفِ

al-ruʿbu: al-inqiṭāʿu mini imtilāʾi al-khawfi

“Ruʿb is incapacitation or being cut off through being filled with fear.”

This explanation highlights fear’s overwhelming force and its possible effect on behavior or capacity.

A description of usage should nevertheless be distinguished from an etymological analysis.

This explanation connects fear, fullness, and being cut off. Its imagery is understandable, but it does not by itself establish that all three historical branches developed from a single original meaning.

A Qur’anic definition can therefore emphasize intense, pervasive dread without making complete incapacity or loss of ability a necessary feature of every use.

Raʿabahu: frightening another person

Raʿabahu yarʿabuhu is recorded in Arabic for frightening someone or causing them dread.

Marʿūb describes someone who has been frightened or is in a state of dread.

Raʿīb is also recorded for a frightened person.

Structurally, the experiencer of fear must be distinguished from its cause.

For example:

Raʿabahu: he frightened him.

Fa-raʿaba: so he became afraid.

This distinction matters in Qur’anic usage: ruʿb is a noun, while verbs such as alqā and qadhafa express how it is attributed to the hearts of those concerned.

Al-ruʿb should therefore not independently be understood as “casting fear.”

The word denotes dread; casting comes from the accompanying verb.

Raʿʿaba and irtaʿaba

Raʿʿabahu is recorded for frightening another person, and irtaʿaba for becoming afraid or terrified.

Tarʿīb and tarʿāb are also recorded for frightening.

Here, verbal structure can change the direction of the action.

Irtaʿaba describes the person who experiences fear.

Raʿʿabahu concerns an agent that frightens someone else.

The forms should not, however, be assumed equally common throughout every period of Arabic.

Some early dictionaries, for example, disagree over arʿabahu in the sense of frightening.

Some reject this usage and prefer raʿabahu.

Others also record arʿabahu.

One dictionary alone cannot establish that another form is invalid throughout all periods and dialects of Arabic.

This disagreement does not affect the Qur’anic analysis: no verb from this root occurs there, and all five occurrences use the noun ruʿb.

Tirʿābah: a very fearful person

Tirʿābah is recorded for a very timid or easily frightened person.

It is explained by al-farūqah, meaning very fearful.

This describes a disposition or condition in which danger, or even a small stimulus, quickly provokes fear.

A relatively lasting disposition of timidity differs from a single experience of dread.

A courageous person may experience terror in a highly unexpected situation.

An easily frightened person may, conversely, perceive danger in an ordinary situation.

An episode of intense fear does not prove that someone is inherently timid or lacks courage.

Qur’anic ruʿb likewise denotes an experienced or arising state, not necessarily a permanent trait.

2. Filling as a meaning of R ʿ B

Raʿabtu al-ḥawḍa

One recorded branch concerns filling.

Its lexical explanation states:

وَرَعَبْتُ الْحَوْضَ إِذَا مَلَأْتَهُ

wa-raʿabtu al-ḥawḍa idhā malaʾtahu

“Raʿabtu al-ḥawḍa means: I filled the pool.”

Here raʿaba does not mean frightening.

A pool is a receptacle or place filled with water.

Thus raʿabtu al-ḥawḍa belongs to the sense of filling.

In the same domain, saylun rāʿibun describes a flood filling a valley.

A word form can therefore have a different meaning depending on what it concerns.

If the object is a person, raʿabahu in the fear branch can mean frightening them.

If the object is a pool, the recorded usage concerns filling it.

Root letters alone cannot determine meaning; sentence structure and the object of the verb must also be examined.

Saylun rāʿibun

In Arabic, saylun rāʿibun describes a flood filling a valley.

The description concerns the volume and spread of the water.

It does not lexically mean that the flood is frightened or has frightened someone.

A flood may actually be dangerous and inspire fear, but rāʿib in this expression concerns fullness and the spread of water.

Nor does this usage establish that Qur’anic ruʿb simply means fullness.

In the Qur’an, ruʿb concerns dread and appears alongside hearts, flight, and confrontation.

The pool and flood uses should therefore be recognized as a separate lexical branch.

3. Early poetry: filling valleys and non-Qur’anic uses

A poem by Mulayḥ ibn Ḥakam al-Hudhalī

A verse attributed to Mulayḥ ibn Ḥakam al-Hudhalī reads:

بِذِي هَيْدَبٍ أَيْمَا الرُّبَى تَحْتَ وَدْقِهِ
فَتَرْوَى وَأَيْمَا كُلُّ وَادٍ فَيَرْعَبُ

bi-dhī haydabin aymā al-rubā taḥta wadqihi / fa-tarwā wa-aymā kullu wādin fa-yarʿabu

“With a laden, trailing cloud: every hill beneath its rain is watered, and every valley fills with water.”

Wadq in this line concerns rain.

Haydab describes a cloud with dense, trailing parts.

Al-rubā is the plural of rabwah and denotes elevations or hills.

Wādin denotes a valley or natural watercourse.

The crucial word here is the verb yarʿabu.

Its lexical explanation allows the sense of a valley becoming filled with water.

Some transmissions have kullu wādin in the nominative: the valley itself becomes full.

Another reading has kulla wādin in the accusative, with the flood or rain understood as filling the valley.

Transmissions also differ between fa-yarʿabu and fa-yurʿabu.

These differences affect syntax but not the general image: rain or a flood fills the valleys.

Lexical transmissions attribute this verse to Mulayḥ ibn Ḥakam al-Hudhalī.

Its precise date is not sufficiently established to assign it definitively to the pre-Islamic period. It is better treated as evidence from early Arabic than as independently verified pre-Qur’anic poetry.

A witness attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī

A poetic witness attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī reads:

يُقَاتِلُ جُوعَهُمْ بِمُكَلَّلَاتٍ
مِنَ الْفُرْنِيِّ يَرْعَبُهَا الْجَمِيلُ

yuqātilu jūʿahum bi-mukallalātin / mina al-furniyyi yarʿabuhā al-jamīlu

“He combats their hunger with large bowls of food filled with fat.”

Mukallalāt concerns large dishes or bowls of food here.

Al-furnī is explained lexically as a kind of food or bread.

Al-jamīl in this context concerns fat or cooking grease.

Yarʿabuhā means filling the dishes.

This witness likewise supports the branch concerned with filling.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The attribution is not uniform, however: a transmission with a different opening attributes it to Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī. A definitive pre-Islamic dating requires independent evidence.

The wording and explanation of some terms also vary. Neither the attribution nor a broad claim about the historical origin of every root meaning should be treated as certain on this basis.

Ruʿbūb in poetry attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ reads:

إِذَا حَرَّكْتَهَا السَّاقُ قُلْتَ نَعَامَةٌ
وَإِنْ زُجِرَتْ يَوْمًا فَلَيْسَتْ بِرُعْبُوبِ

idhā ḥarrakathā al-sāqu qulta naʿāmatun / wa-in zujirat yawman fa-laysat bi-ruʿbūbi

“When urged forward by the leg, you would call her a swift ostrich; but when driven or shouted at, she is no flighty, frightened camel.”

Ruʿbūb describes a camel here.

In this usage, it is recorded for a flighty, highly restless camel.

The behavioral image can be associated with agitation and sudden responses.

It does not establish that Qur’anic ruʿb means swift running or flightiness.

Ruʿbūb is a particular animal description here and should be understood within that usage.

The verse is attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ in lexical transmissions and belongs among witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic poetry.

What the poetic witnesses show

Early witnesses show that R ʿ B was not used only for fear and dread.

The poem by Mulayḥ and the witness attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb concern filling a valley or a dish.

The witness attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ contains a specific description of a camel’s behavior.

These witnesses should not change the Qur’anic meaning of ruʿb from dread into filling with water or animal flightiness.

All five Qur’anic occurrences have clear contextual indications of fear.

4. Cutting and dividing into pieces

Raʿaba al-sanāma

The third recognized branch concerns cutting.

Arabic records:

رَعَبَ السَّنَامَ: قَطَعَهُ

raʿaba al-sanāma: qaṭaʿahu

“Raʿaba the hump: he cut it.”

Raʿʿaba al-sanāma is also recorded for cutting a camel’s hump into pieces.

Tirʿībah denotes a piece of the hump.

Ruʿbūbah also names a portion of the hump in one usage.

This branch differs semantically from fear and filling.

Some lexical explanations nevertheless connect being cut off with fear-induced incapacity.

The possibility of such imagery does not historically prove that all three meanings arose from one original event or image.

It is therefore preferable to record cutting as a separate branch.

None of the five Qur’anic occurrences of ruʿb means cutting a hump or dividing something into pieces.

Ruʿbūbah: another use describing a woman

Ruʿbūbah and ruʿbūb are also recorded for a tall, well-proportioned, or soft and delicate woman.

Lexical explanations connect this description with portions of a camel’s hump or with bodily softness and fullness.

They disagree, however, about the exact characteristic intended.

Some emphasize height and proportion, others softness and beauty.

These differences caution against treating one bodily or etymological image as certain for every derivative.

This usage has no role in the direct meaning of Qur’anic al-ruʿb.

Other secondary uses

Raʿʿabat al-ḥamāmatu is recorded for a pigeon’s call becoming loud and strong.

Al-rāʿibī is also recorded as the name of a kind of pigeon.

Another use connects raʿb with threats or frightening speech.

Some lexical records also give ruʿb a sense concerning the part of an arrow where its head is fitted.

These secondary uses show that the lexical family is broader than its Qur’anic sense.

They should not be imported unnecessarily into explanations of the verses.

In the Qur’an, ruʿb names a state of dread, not a pigeon, arrowhead, hump, or filled receptacle.

5. “We shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved”: Āl ʿImrān

The first Qur’anic occurrence reads:

سَنُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ بِمَا أَشْرَكُوا بِاللَّهِ مَا لَمْ يُنَزِّلْ بِهِ سُلْطَانًا وَمَأْوَاهُمُ النَّارُ وَبِئْسَ مَثْوَى الظَّالِمِينَ

sanulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba bi-mā ashrakū bi-Allāhi mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānan wa-maʾwāhumu al-nāru wa-biʾsa mathwā al-ẓālimīna

Āl ʿImrān 3:151

“We shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved because they associated with The God something for which He sent down no authority. Their refuge is the Fire; how wretched is the abode of the wrongdoers.”

This verse belongs to a passage about confrontation between believers and their opponents.

The surrounding verses discuss divine help, fighting, steadfastness, defeat, disagreement, and trial.

Ruʿb therefore appears in the context of an actual confrontation.

Sanulqī: casting dread

Sanulqī belongs to the root L Q Y.

It includes the future marker sa- and concerns casting or placing something in a location.

What is cast in this verse is al-ruʿba.

Its location is specified by:

فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū

Āl ʿImrān 3:151

“Into the hearts of those who disbelieved.”

Three distinct elements are therefore present:

The God is the agent of casting; dread is what is cast; and the hearts of the group concerned are its stated location.

Not all these elements belong to the meaning of ruʿb itself.

Ruʿb denotes dread, while sanulqī attributes its arising to divine casting.

Fī qulūbi: the location of dread

Qur’anic qalb is frequently associated with understanding, faith, doubt, tranquility, and fear.

Here dread is placed in hearts.

This expression alone is not a medical account of the heart organ or the neurological mechanisms of fear.

In Arabic, the heart can represent a person’s inward seat of feeling and decision.

“He cast dread into their hearts” is therefore a natural and understandable expression.

It should not be interpreted in a wholly physical manner, as though an object called ruʿb were thrown inside the heart organ.

The Qur’an describes the arising of an inward state.

Bi-mā ashrakū bi-Allāh

The verse states a reason for the dread cast into them:

بِمَا أَشْرَكُوا بِاللَّهِ مَا لَمْ يُنَزِّلْ بِهِ سُلْطَانًا

bi-mā ashrakū bi-Allāhi mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānan

Āl ʿImrān 3:151

“Because they associated with The God something for which He sent down no authority.”

Within this context, dread is linked to associating partners with The God without authority for doing so.

The meaning of ruʿb must be distinguished from the reason given for it.

Ruʿb does not lexically mean associating partners with The God.

Nor does this verse establish that everyone holding a false belief experiences manifest dread at every moment of life.

It concerns a particular group in a confrontation.

Such a statement should not be generalized to every person and situation without evidence.

Does ruʿb mean military defeat here?

The context makes its connection with confrontation understandable.

Ruʿb itself, however, does not mean defeat.

Dread may cause retreat, hesitation, or reduced resistance; the precise outcome must be learned from the event and its account.

The next verse, Āl ʿImrān 3:152, also describes faltering, disagreement, and disobedience among the believers.

The passage should not become a simple rule that every fearful group necessarily loses, or that every defeated group necessarily experienced ruʿb.

It is more accurate to understand ruʿb here as inward dread in a situation of confrontation.

6. “I shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved”: Al-Anfāl

The second occurrence reads:

إِذْ يُوحِي رَبُّكَ إِلَى الْمَلَائِكَةِ أَنِّي مَعَكُمْ فَثَبِّتُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا سَأُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ فَاضْرِبُوا فَوْقَ الْأَعْنَاقِ وَاضْرِبُوا مِنْهُمْ كُلَّ بَنَانٍ

idh yūḥī rabbuka ilā al-malāʾikati annī maʿakum fa-thabbitū alladhīna āmanū sa-ulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba fa-iḍribū fawqa al-aʿnāqi wa-iḍribū minhum kulla banānin

Al-Anfāl 8:12

“When your Lord revealed to the angels: I am with you, so make those who believe stand firm. I shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved. Strike above the necks and strike every fingertip of theirs.”

This verse concerns warfare and support for the believers.

The preceding verses mention appeals for help, The God’s response, and the presence of angels.

Sa-ulqī and sanulqī

Āl ʿImrān 3:151 uses sanulqī.

Al-Anfāl 8:12 uses sa-ulqī.

Both belong to L Q Y.

The former has a first-person plural speaker, the latter a first-person singular speaker.

Translation should preserve this morphological difference.

Sanulqī means “We shall cast.”

Sa-ulqī means “I shall cast.”

In both, al-ruʿba is the verb’s object and is cast into the hearts of the group concerned.

The meaning of ruʿb itself has not changed.

Believers’ steadfastness and opponents’ dread

The verse says:

فَثَبِّتُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا

fa-thabbitū alladhīna āmanū

Al-Anfāl 8:12

“So make those who believe stand firm.”

Then:

سَأُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ

sa-ulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba

Al-Anfāl 8:12

“I shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved.”

The expressions form a semantic contrast.

On one side stand the believers’ firmness and perseverance.

On the other stands their opponents’ dread.

This contrast shows that inward states can affect confrontation.

Thabāt and ruʿb nevertheless belong to distinct lexical roots.

One should not define either merely by negating the other.

A person may remain steadfast despite fear.

Someone else may abandon an undertaking for another reason without experiencing intense dread.

Thus steadfastness and dread contrast here, but they are not wholly incompatible in every human experience.

Fa-iḍribū: another verb in the same verse

After mentioning dread, the verse speaks of striking in a wartime setting.

Ḍarb belongs to Ḍ R B.

Ruʿb belongs to R ʿ B.

The two concepts should not be merged.

Ruʿb expresses inward dread.

Ḍarb concerns delivering a blow.

They share a wartime context here, but ruʿb itself does not mean striking, killing, or wounding.

Nor does this verse alone authorize a general instruction to frighten or harm every person with an opposing belief.

It must be examined within its particular confrontation and alongside other verses concerning fighting.

This study concerns lexical meaning and should not replace a separate investigation of the rules of warfare.

7. “You would have been filled with dread of them”: the Companions of the Cave

The third occurrence differs in subject from the other four.

In the account of the Companions of the Cave, the Qur’an says:

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ وَكَلْبُهُمْ بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ بِالْوَصِيدِ لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli wa-kalbuhum bāsiṭun dhirāʿayhi bi-al-waṣīdi lawi iṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim la-wallayta minhum firāran wa-la-muliʾta minhum ruʿban

Al-Kahf 18:18

“You would think them awake, though they are asleep. We turn them to the right and to the left, while their dog stretches its forelegs at the threshold. Had you looked upon them, you would have turned away fleeing and been filled with dread of them.”

The verse describes the sleepers’ condition.

They are asleep, but may appear awake to an observer.

Their dog is also at the threshold.

The Qur’an then states the consequence of supposing that an observer looked upon them.

Ruʿban: an indefinite accusative noun

The word here appears as ruʿban.

Unlike the other four occurrences, it lacks the definite article al-.

It also accompanies the following verb:

وَلَمُلِئْتَ

wa-la-muliʾta

Al-Kahf 18:18

“And you would surely have been filled.”

Muliʾta is a second-person masculine singular passive perfect verb from M L ʾ.

One syntactic analysis treats ruʿban as an accusative specification, identifying what fills the person; analysis as a second object is also proposed.

The expression thus conveys pervasive dread.

The noun’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from its accompanying verb.

Ruʿb means dread.

Muliʾta expresses being filled.

The whole meaning of filling therefore cannot be treated as a constant component in every definition of R ʿ B.

Ruʿb and the branch concerned with filling

The lexical section showed that raʿaba al-ḥawḍa is also recorded for filling a pool.

One lexical explanation of dread invokes being filled with fear and becoming incapacitated.

Al-Kahf 18:18 itself uses muliʾta … ruʿban.

This combination is noteworthy as linguistic imagery.

The person is so afraid that their whole being appears filled with dread.

This resemblance does not establish that the original historical meaning of ruʿb was necessarily fullness, later developing into fear.

Another analysis records three separate semantic branches.

It is more accurate to say that this verse expresses dread through the image of being filled, without deriving every use from a certain historical origin meaning fullness.

Lawi iṭṭalaʿta: a hypothetical condition

The verse states:

لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ

lawi iṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim

Al-Kahf 18:18

“Had you looked upon them.”

Then:

لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا

la-wallayta minhum firāran

Al-Kahf 18:18

“You would have turned away fleeing from them.”

And:

وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

wa-la-muliʾta minhum ruʿban

Al-Kahf 18:18

“You would have been filled with dread of them.”

This construction presents a hypothetical encounter and its stated consequence. The response is asserted on fulfillment of the condition, rather than offered as an indeterminate possibility.

The Qur’an does not say that a particular person actually entered the cave and fled upon seeing its inhabitants.

It says that if someone had looked upon them in that situation, they would have responded in this way.

The distinction matters: the expression should not be converted into a report of a completed historical event.

Firāran and ruʿban

The verse uses two different words.

Firāran belongs to F R R and concerns fleeing.

Ruʿban belongs to R ʿ B and denotes dread.

Fear can cause flight.

The two are nevertheless distinct.

A person may be afraid without fleeing.

They may also withdraw to avoid a recognized danger without experiencing intense dread.

Here both responses appear together and convey the situation’s intensity.

In a root definition, however, ruʿb should not be translated as fleeing.

What caused the dread?

Commentarial explanations differ over the observer’s fear.

Some connect it with the sleepers’ unusual appearance.

Others mention their sleeping condition, long stay in the cave, or bodily posture.

Further explanations speak of a special dread preventing others from approaching them.

The verse itself does not specify the precise cause.

It describes the sleepers, their turning from side to side, and the dog’s presence, then states the observer’s reaction if they looked upon them.

One should therefore not assert that their eyes were certainly open, their appearance transformed, their bodies arranged in some particular manner, or a specific invisible agent present to frighten observers.

These details are not explicitly stated in the verse.

What is clear is the intensity of dread a hypothetical observer would experience at the sight.

Does dread imply that the sleepers were bad people?

No.

Al-Kahf 18:13 introduces them as young people who believed in their Lord and whose guidance The God increased.

An observer’s fear of them therefore does not mean that they themselves were wicked or threatening.

This matters for understanding the root.

Dread is the state of the person who fears; its existence alone does not judge the moral worth of the person feared.

Someone may fear the unknown or unexpected even when it has no actual intention of causing harm.

Ruʿb here should not be equated with the sleepers’ corruption or hostility.

8. “He cast dread into their hearts”: Al-Aḥzāb

The fourth occurrence reads:

وَأَنْزَلَ الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ صَيَاصِيهِمْ وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ فَرِيقًا تَقْتُلُونَ وَتَأْسِرُونَ فَرِيقًا

wa-anzala alladhīna ẓāharūhum min ahli al-kitābi min ṣayāṣīhim wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba farīqan taqtulūna wa-taʾsirūna farīqan

Al-Aḥzāb 33:26

“He brought down from their fortresses those of the People of the Book who had supported them, and cast dread into their hearts: a group you killed and a group you took captive.”

Al-ruʿba accompanies qadhafa here.

Qadhafa: forceful casting

Qadhafa belongs to Q Dh F.

In Arabic, the verb can concern throwing or casting something.

Here what is cast is not a material object but dread.

Qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba thus describes a powerful arising of dread in their hearts.

In general outcome, this resembles sanulqī … al-ruʿba in Āl ʿImrān.

Alqā and qadhafa are nevertheless distinct verbs.

Qadhafa gives greater prominence to the image of throwing.

Their difference does not establish mild fear in one verse and fear of a precisely measured, multiplied intensity in another.

The verses provide no numerical measurement of dread.

A rigid technical hierarchy of kinds of ruʿb should not be constructed from this difference.

Which group is meant?

The verse says:

الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ

alladhīna ẓāharūhum min ahli al-kitābi

Al-Aḥzāb 33:26

“Those of the People of the Book who had supported them.”

It identifies the group by their conduct.

The statement should therefore not be generalized to all People of the Book in every period.

The preceding verses concern the retreat of attacking groups and the confrontation’s outcome.

Here too, the people meant had supported the opponents in that particular situation.

Historical accounts connect the event with a Jewish group in Medina, but the verse itself does not name that group.

Historical identity must be investigated through those accounts, not incorporated into the lexical meaning of ruʿb.

Ṣayāṣīhim: fortresses and defensive shelters

Ṣayāṣī here denotes fortresses and defensive positions.

It belongs to Ṣ Y Ṣ.

The verse thus describes people brought down from their fortresses with dread cast into their hearts.

Together, these details connect defensive circumstances, collapse of position, and dread.

Ruʿb itself does not mean leaving a fortress or captivity.

Those events are expressed by other verbs.

Nor does the verse establish that everyone frightened in a confrontation deserves harm because of false belief or wrongdoing.

It concerns a particular situation and a group with specific conduct.

9. “He cast dread into their hearts”: Al-Ḥashr

The fifth occurrence is in Al-Ḥashr:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَخْرَجَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ لِأَوَّلِ الْحَشْرِ مَا ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنْ يَخْرُجُوا وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ فَأَتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَمْ يَحْتَسِبُوا وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَيْدِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ فَاعْتَبِرُوا يَا أُولِي الْأَبْصَارِ

huwa alladhī akhraja alladhīna kafarū min ahli al-kitābi min diyārihim li-awwali al-ḥashri mā ẓanantum an yakhrujū wa-ẓannū annahum māniʿatuhum ḥuṣūnuhum mina Allāhi fa-atāhumu Allāhu min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba yukhribūna buyūtahum bi-aydīhim wa-aydī al-muʾminīna fa-iʿtabirū yā ulī al-abṣāri

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

“He is the One who expelled those who disbelieved among the People of the Book from their homes at the first gathering. You did not think they would leave, and they thought their fortresses would protect them against The God. But The God came upon them from where they had not reckoned and cast dread into their hearts. They were destroying their houses with their own hands and the believers’ hands. Take heed, then, people of insight.”

Here too, al-ruʿba accompanies qadhafa and fī qulūbihim.

The context provides further important details.

Confidence in fortresses and the arising of dread

The verse says:

وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ

wa-ẓannū annahum māniʿatuhum ḥuṣūnuhum mina Allāhi

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

“They thought their fortresses would protect them against The God.”

Then:

فَأَتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَمْ يَحْتَسِبُوا

fa-atāhumu Allāhu min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

“But The God came upon them from where they had not reckoned.”

And afterward:

وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ

wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

“He cast dread into their hearts.”

This sequence reveals a substantial difference between the group’s expectations and the actual outcome.

They relied on their fortresses’ strength, but events unfolded unexpectedly.

Dread and the destruction of houses follow in the account.

Ruʿb here is thus associated with lost security and the collapse of previous confidence.

The word itself should not be made to mean a miscalculation.

Mistaken expectation comes from ẓannū and min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū.

Ruʿb is the dread arising in this situation.

Are fortresses and defensive equipment always useless?

The verse establishes no such general rule.

It concerns a particular group who thought their fortresses would prevent the event in question.

It does not declare every defensive tool, plan, or safeguard useless.

Other Qur’anic settings also discuss preparing strength and exercising foresight.

The outcome of one event should not become an absolute law about all fortresses, equipment, and defensive situations.

The subject is the difference between this group’s confidence and their actual outcome.

Yukhribūna buyūtahum

The verse continues:

يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَيْدِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

yukhribūna buyūtahum bi-aydīhim wa-aydī al-muʾminīna

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

“They were destroying their houses with their own hands and the believers’ hands.”

The verb belongs to Kh R B and concerns destruction.

Destroying houses should not be incorporated into the meaning of ruʿb.

Contextually, however, a connection can be seen between dread and the accompanying destructive events.

The verse does not explain every practical motive or detail of each act of destruction.

An identical, certain intention should therefore not be assigned to every individual or group without independent evidence.

Is dread attributed to all People of the Book here?

No.

The verse says:

الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ

alladhīna kafarū min ahli al-kitābi

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

This means those among the People of the Book who disbelieved and were involved in the particular event under discussion.

It does not establish that all People of the Book experience this situation in every period.

Nor can their dread be treated as an inherent characteristic of an ethnic or religious group.

The Qur’an describes a specific event here.

Lexical and contextual inquiry should therefore avoid unsupported ethnic or religious generalizations.

10. Different Qur’anic constructions with ruʿb

All five occurrences are nouns, but they appear in three constructions.

Sanulqī and sa-ulqī

In Āl ʿImrān 3:151 and Al-Anfāl 8:12, dread accompanies alqā.

In the former:

سَنُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ

sanulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba

Āl ʿImrān 3:151

“We shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved.”

In the latter:

سَأُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ

sa-ulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba

Al-Anfāl 8:12

“I shall cast dread into the hearts of those who disbelieved.”

Both constructions concern casting dread into a group’s hearts.

Translation should preserve the verbal person and number, but the root meaning remains the same in both.

Wa-qadhafa

Al-Aḥzāb 33:26 and Al-Ḥashr 59:2 state:

وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ

wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba

Al-Aḥzāb 33:26; Al-Ḥashr 59:2

“He cast dread into their hearts.”

This also describes dread arising in hearts.

Qadhafa belongs to a separate root and emphasizes casting or throwing.

Wa-la-muliʾta

Al-Kahf 18:18 states:

وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

wa-la-muliʾta minhum ruʿban

Al-Kahf 18:18

“You would have been filled with dread of them.”

Unlike the other four verses, this does not describe casting dread into a particular group’s hearts. It describes fear filling an observer.

It also occurs in a conditional construction.

This difference shows that Qur’anic ruʿb can be expressed both as arising in human hearts and as a pervasive state experienced by someone encountering a frightening situation.

None of these constructions changes the noun’s basic meaning from dread into something else.

11. Ruʿb and related roots

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and Kh W F (khawf)

Khawf has a wide range of Qur’anic uses.

People may fear danger, harm, enemies, the consequences of deeds, or future events.

Ruʿb is also fear, but its intensity and pervasiveness are prominent in its Qur’anic uses.

In Al-Kahf 18:18, for example, the person is filled with dread and flees.

This image is stronger than mere awareness of possible danger.

It does not establish an absolute rule that khawf is always mild or ruʿb always the most intense conceivable fear.

Khawf can also be very intense in some verses.

The distinction is principally a tendency of usage: ruʿb often emphasizes overwhelming, unsettling dread.

Study of Kh W F

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and F Z ʿ (fazaʿ)

Fazaʿ concerns sudden fear and agitation in Arabic.

The Qur’an states:

وَيَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَفَزِعَ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ

wa-yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri fa-faziʿa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi

Al-Naml 27:87

“On the day the Trumpet is blown, those in the heavens and those on earth are stricken with fear.”

Fazaʿ and ruʿb are close in their sense of fear.

Some uses of fazaʿ emphasize a sudden response and agitation caused by an event.

Ruʿb can also be sudden, but its Qur’anic usage emphasizes dread taking hold of a person.

This distinction should not become an absolute boundary.

Arabic lexical explanations themselves use fazaʿ to explain ruʿb; their overlap is real.

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and R H B (rahbah)

Rahbah and rahab concern fear or apprehension that can accompany a sense of the feared subject’s greatness and power.

The Qur’an states:

وَإِيَّايَ فَارْهَبُونِ

wa-iyyāya fa-irhabūni

Al-Baqarah 2:40

“Fear Me alone.”

This use concerns fear within the human relationship with The God.

The five Qur’anic uses of ruʿb instead concern opposing groups or a hypothetical observer of the sleepers.

Ruʿb should therefore not lexically be restricted to religious humility or respectful fear.

Rahbah, conversely, is not always calm or respectful fear; it can also describe intense fear.

The roots’ differences must be understood through actual usage.

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and Kh Sh Y (khashyah)

Khashyah also concerns fear and accompanies knowledge or awareness in some Qur’anic uses.

Fāṭir states:

إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى اللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ الْعُلَمَاءُ

innamā yakhshā Allāha min ʿibādihi al-ʿulamāʾu

Fāṭir 35:28

“Among His servants, it is those with knowledge who fear The God.”

Here khashyah is connected with knowledge.

Ruʿb in Al-Kahf 18:18 instead concerns an observer’s response upon encountering an unexpected situation.

The two contexts are therefore distinct.

This does not mean that every khashyah necessarily arises from complete knowledge or that every ruʿb always arises from ignorance.

The roots themselves establish no such absolute division.

Study of Kh Sh Y

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and W J L (wajal)

Wajal appears in the Qur’an for a person’s inward state.

For example:

إِنَّمَا الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ

innamā al-muʾminūna alladhīna idhā dhukira Allāhu wajilat qulūbuhum

Al-Anfāl 8:2

“The believers are those whose hearts tremble when The God is mentioned.”

Wajal here concerns remembrance of The God and the believers’ inward response.

Ruʿb can also arise in a heart, but its Qur’anic uses emphasize intense fear and dread.

Sharing an association with qalb does not make them one word with an identical meaning.

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and Dh ʿ R (dhuʿr)

Dhuʿr concerns fear and sudden terror in Arabic.

Its family is semantically close to fazaʿ and ruʿb.

The root Dh ʿ R does not itself occur in the Qur’an.

It may help explain intense dread lexically, but should not be presented as direct Qur’anic evidence.

These related meanings likewise do not establish a numerical, certain hierarchy of fear’s intensity.

R ʿ B (ruʿb) and F R R (firār)

Firār concerns fleeing and moving away from a situation.

It appears alongside ruʿb in Al-Kahf 18:18.

Their combination clarifies the difference.

Ruʿb is an inward state of dread.

Firār is a behavioral response.

Fear may lead to flight, but this relationship is not necessary in every situation.

Ruʿb should therefore not simply be translated as flight or retreat.

12. Dread, courage, and moral responsibility

The five Qur’anic uses yield an important distinction: experiencing fear and acting rightly or wrongly are separate matters.

Al-Kahf 18:18 describes dread upon seeing the sleepers, although they are presented as faithful, guided young people.

Āl ʿImrān 3:151, Al-Anfāl 8:12, Al-Aḥzāb 33:26, and Al-Ḥashr 59:2 describe dread in opposing groups’ hearts during confrontations.

Here fear is connected with wartime circumstances and the conduct of particular groups.

Together, these verses do not establish that every fear signifies disbelief or punishment.

Nor do they establish that everyone without fear is necessarily right.

People may experience natural fear in the face of real danger.

Such fear can encourage caution and prevent harm.

Recklessness without understanding or assessment can, conversely, cause harm.

Dread should therefore not lexically be equated with cowardice or moral corruption.

Nor should frightening others be considered permissible or commendable for people merely because several verses attribute the arising of ruʿb to The God.

Those verses describe specific events.

An ethical or legal judgment about threatening others requires independent examination of passages concerning justice, fighting, prohibited aggression, and human rights.

The lexical meaning of ruʿb alone supplies no such judgment.

13. Translation recommendations for Forqan

This family requires distinctions among dread, frightening, becoming frightened, filling, and cutting.

Ruʿb: dread; intense fear; terror taking hold of a person.

Ruʿub: another vocalization of ruʿb, with the same sense of dread and fear.

Raʿb: frightening or fear in the relevant branch; elsewhere filling or cutting, depending on structure and context.

Raʿabahu: he frightened or terrified him.

Raʿaba / raʿuba / ruʿiba: became afraid or was frightened, according to the verb’s intransitive, transitive, or passive form.

Raʿʿabahu: he frightened him or caused him dread; in another use, he cut something such as a hump into pieces.

Tarʿīb: frightening or causing dread; in another use, cutting a hump into pieces.

Tarʿāb: frightening, in a recorded lexical use.

Irtaʿaba: became frightened or experienced intense fear.

Irtiʿāb: becoming frightened; experiencing dread.

Marʿūb: frightened or terrified; someone overcome by dread.

Murtaʿib: someone who has become frightened.

Raʿīb: a frightened person in the fear branch; in another use, something dripping with fat.

Rāʿib: frightening in the relevant use; in saylun rāʿibun, a flood filling a valley.

Raʿʿāb: in early usage, an incantation reciter; one explanation describes someone causing fear through frightening speech. This designation does not establish that incantations are effective.

Tirʿābah: very timid; easily frightened.

Ruʿbūbah: in one use, a piece of a hump; in another descriptive use, a tall or well-proportioned woman.

Muraʿʿab: cut into pieces in the cutting branch; frightened or terrified in other uses, depending on vocalization and structure.

The five Qur’anic occurrences

VerseQur’anic formSuitable meaning

Al-Anfāl 8:12الرُّعْبَDread; I shall cast dread into their hearts
Al-Kahf 18:18رُعْبًاDread; you would have been filled with fear of them
Al-Aḥzāb 33:26الرُّعْبَDread; He cast dread into their hearts

“Dread” is a natural, suitable equivalent in all five verses.

“Terror” can also convey intensity in some contexts, but should not imply that the word itself establishes a monstrous being or a certain physical danger.

“You would have been filled with fear” suits Al-Kahf 18:18 because muliʾta accompanies ruʿban.

In the other four verses, casting dread into hearts closely follows the Arabic construction.

The readings ruʿb and ruʿub

The familiar Ḥafṣ reading uses ruʿb with a vowelless ʿayn.

The recognized readings of Ibn ʿĀmir, al-Kisāʾī, Abū Jaʿfar, and Yaʿqūb also transmit ruʿub with ḍammah on ʿayn.

This difference is reported for both definite and indefinite Qur’anic forms.

Lexical explanations treat them as two Arabic vocalizations for fear and dread.

The different vowel should therefore not generate two wholly independent meanings.

Nor does it change the root’s Qur’anic count.

Both forms represent the same five occurrences of R ʿ B.

14. Conclusion

Arabic R ʿ B has three recognized semantic branches: fear, filling, and cutting.

Lexical analysis records these branches separately.

In the fear branch, ruʿb denotes dread that can overwhelm a person and disrupt their sense of security.

In the filling branch, raʿaba al-ḥawḍa means filling a pool, and saylun rāʿibun describes a flood filling a valley.

In the cutting branch, raʿʿaba al-sanāma concerns dividing a hump into pieces.

Their images may be related, but sufficient historical evidence is needed before deriving them all from one certain original meaning.

Early poetic witnesses likewise show that the family was not confined to dread.

Mulayḥ ibn Ḥakam’s poem describes valleys filled by rain.

The witness attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb uses the same branch for food dishes being filled.

The verse attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ uses a particular derivative to describe a camel.

The Qur’an, however, uses only the sense of dread.

The root occurs five times in five verses, always as the noun ruʿb.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:151, The God speaks of casting dread into a group of disbelievers’ hearts during confrontation, connecting it with their associating partners with Him.

Al-Anfāl 8:12 combines dread in opponents’ hearts with making believers stand firm during warfare.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:26 describes dread in a group from the People of the Book who had supported the opponents.

Al-Ḥashr 59:2 connects dread with a group’s collapsed confidence in their fortresses and their departure from home.

Al-Kahf 18:18 has a different subject: someone looking upon the sleepers would flee and be filled with dread.

This shows that ruʿb need not concern hostility, disbelief, or fighting.

Dread may arise from an unexpected sight without the people feared being wicked.

In four verses, alqā and qadhafa express dread arising in hearts.

In the Cave verse, muliʾta emphasizes dread filling the person.

In these Qur’anic uses, the noun ruʿb itself does not mean casting, filling, or fleeing; those features come from accompanying verbs and expressions.

Ruʿb should also not be equated with every natural fear, informed fear, religious reverence, or lasting cowardice.

Despite proximity to khawf, fazaʿ, rahbah, khashyah, and wajal, each family requires investigation within its own uses.

Fear itself and a person’s moral worth remain separate matters.

Becoming frightened does not alone prove someone wrong; fearlessness does not alone prove them right.

Final definition: In its Qur’anic usage, R ʿ B denotes intense, pervasive dread that disrupts a person’s sense of security. Ruʿb names this state and may accompany flight, hesitation, or retreat, but those consequences are not necessary parts of its meaning. Arabic also records separate branches concerning filling and cutting, which should not be imposed on the Qur’anic sense. Across the five Qur’anic occurrences, the kind of dread, its cause, its experiencers, and its consequences must be identified through accompanying verbs and each verse’s context, rather than through one fixed physical image or moral judgment for every case.
