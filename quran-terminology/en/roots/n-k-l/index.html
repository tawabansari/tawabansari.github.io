Short Introduction to the Root

The root N K L moves in the semantic field of holding back, restraining, pushing back, and making someone retreat.

In Arabic uses of this family, one important direction is that a person draws back from an affair, does not dare to continue, or turns away from it; and another direction is that something or someone holds another back from continuing the path and pushes him back.

In the Qur’an, this field appears in three main forms:

أَنكَال: restraining bonds and fetters.

نَكَال: a consequence or encounter that has a restraining quality.

تَنكِيل: the carrying out of an encounter whose restraining and pushing-back quality is prominent.

Therefore, “punishment” alone is not the core of the root. In some verses, nakal stands in a punitive context and can be translated as “a restraining punishment”; but the shared element across the whole family is holding back from advancing or repeating.

Short root meaning:

holding back, restraining, pushing back, making one retreat; and in punitive contexts, a restraining punishment or consequence.

Qur'anic Forms and Derivatives

The root N K L appears five times in the Qur’an in three main forms:

أَنكَال

Once in Al-Muzzammil 12.

Ankal is the plural of nikl, and in the verse it refers to restraining bonds or fetters.

نَكَال

Three times in:

  • Al-Baqarah 2:66
  • Al-Ma’idah 5:38
  • An-Nazi’at 79:25

In these uses, nakal is connected with a consequence or encounter that takes on a restraining role.

تَنكِيل

Once in An-Nisa 4:84.

Tankil is the verbal noun of Form II and appears in a context of a severe, restraining encounter.

The Semantic Core of the Root

The defensible core of N K L may be stated as follows:

holding someone back from continuing or entering a path, and creating a state that makes the person retreat and refrain.

This core has two directions.

In one direction, the person himself nakala ʿan al-amr; that is, he drew back from an affair, did not dare to continue, or turned away from it.

In the other direction, something holds him back from advancing; like a fetter that restrains movement or a consequence that reduces the daring to repeat.

Therefore, “deterrence” in this root is not merely a later legal reading; the lexical family itself has the capacity for the meaning of retreating and holding back.

The Material Image of the Root: Fetter and Bridle

In Arabic, an-nikl has been used for a fetter and also for a kind of bridle or iron piece of a bridle and rein.

This image is very clear for understanding the root.

A fetter limits movement.

A bridle directs and restrains the movement of an animal and prevents uncontrolled advancing.

In the Qur’an, the plural form of this word appears in Al-Muzzammil 12:

إِنَّ لَدَيْنَا أَنكَالًا وَجَحِيمًا

“Indeed, with Us are restraining bonds and a blazing Fire.”

Al-Muzzammil, 12

In this verse, ankal is itself the tool of restraint.

But one should not conclude from this use that “the horse’s bridle” is necessarily the historical origin and first meaning of the whole family N K L. The lexical data only shows that the material image of restraining and limiting truly exists in this family.

Retreating in Arabic Usage

In Arabic one says:

نَكَلَ عن الأمر

meaning that he drew back from the affair, refrained from continuing it, did not dare, or turned away from it.

Likewise, one can hold someone back and keep him away ʿan ash-shay’.

This use shows that the root is not only about a physical obstacle; restraint can also appear at the level of decision and will.

Even so, it is better not to say that the meaning of the root is “breaking the will.” That expression goes beyond the lexical data. What is defensible is that nukul can be connected with drawing back, reduced daring, and refraining from continuing.

Ankal: The Material Form of Restraint

إِنَّ لَدَيْنَا أَنكَالًا وَجَحِيمًا

“Indeed, with Us are restraining bonds and a blazing Fire.”

Al-Muzzammil, 12

This verse shows the clearest material form of the root.

Ankal is not something that merely causes pain; its function is binding and limiting movement.

For this reason, this derivative allows us to take seriously, in other uses of the root too, the element of “restraining the possibility of advancing.”

But ankal and nakal are not synonyms. One is the material tool of restraint, and the other in the Qur’an is usually a restraining consequence or encounter.

Al-Baqarah 2:66: Nakal and Admonition

فَجَعَلْنَاهَا نَكَالًا لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهَا وَمَا خَلْفَهَا وَمَوْعِظَةً لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ

“So We made it a nakal for what was before it and what was behind it, and an admonition for the mindful.”

Al-Baqarah, 66

This verse is very important for understanding nakal, because the Qur’an places two concepts separately side by side:

نَكَالًا

and

مَوْعِظَةً

If nakal were merely “admonition” or “lesson,” this linguistic separation would become faint.

Mawʿizah is admonition and reminding.

Nakal highlights the restraining and pushing-back face of the event.

The phrase لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهَا وَمَا خَلْفَهَا also shows that the effect of nakal is not limited to the event itself, although the exact referent of the pronoun and the range of “before it and behind it” should be examined in an independent study of the verse.

Nakal and Ibrah Are Not the Same

In Al-Baqarah 66, the Qur’an separates nakal from mawʿizah.

In An-Nazi’at too, after mentioning nakal in verse 25, the next verse speaks of ibrah:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً لِّمَن يَخْشَىٰ

“Indeed in that there is a lesson for one who has khashyah.”

An-Nazi’at, 26

This pairing shows that “nakal” and “ibrah” should not be treated as complete synonyms.

An event can be nakal, meaning that it creates restraint, and at the same time it can also be ibrah, meaning that for the observer it provides a lesson and a mental crossing into another understanding.

Al-Ma’idah 5:38: Jaza and Nakal

وَالسَّارِقُ وَالسَّارِقَةُ فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا نَكَالًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

“And the male thief and the female thief, cut their hands, as a recompense for what they earned, and a nakal from The God; and The God is Mighty, Wise.”

Al-Ma’idah, 38

This verse is one of the clearest witnesses for distinguishing jaza and nakal.

جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا

expresses the relation of the consequence to what was done.

نَكَالًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ

highlights the restraining face of that same encounter.

Therefore, translating nakal only as “punishment” removes part of the lexical difference in the verse.

In this context, a translation such as:

“a restraining punishment”

can be natural; but one must know that “punishment” comes from the punitive context of the verse, and the special core of nakal is that restraining quality.

Must Nakal Always Be Public Deterrence?

In some verses, restraint goes beyond the individual. Al-Baqarah 66 explicitly speaks of an effect connected with ma bayna yadayha wa-ma khalfaha.

But one should not make from this a fixed rule that every nakal is necessarily “a public punishment to frighten society”.

The core of the root is holding back and pushing back. Whether this restraint is directed at the individual himself, observers, society, or several levels at the same time must be made clear from the context of that verse.

An-Nazi’at 79:25: Nakal of the Latter and the First

فَأَخَذَهُ اللَّهُ نَكَالَ الْآخِرَةِ وَالْأُولَىٰ

“So The God took him with the nakal of the latter and the first.”

An-Nazi’at, 25

This verse has deliberately been left somewhat open in translation, because the construction al-akhirah wa-al-ula has capacity for different interpretations, and the root article should not place one of them inside the meaning of nakal.

What is clear for this entry is that akhadhahu expresses taking and seizing, and nakal shows the quality or kind of that seizing from the angle of restraint and exemplary consequence.

The next verse also says:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً لِّمَن يَخْشَىٰ

“Indeed in that there is a lesson for one who has khashyah.”

An-Nazi’at, 26

So the Qur’an itself places “nakal” and “ibrah” in two separate expressions.

An-Nisa 4:84: Tankil Beside Kaff

فَقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا تُكَلَّفُ إِلَّا نَفْسَكَ وَحَرِّضِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ ۖ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَن يَكُفَّ بَأْسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا ۚ وَاللَّهُ أَشَدُّ بَأْسًا وَأَشَدُّ تَنكِيلًا

“So fight in the path of The God; you are not charged except with yourself; and urge the believers. Perhaps The God will hold back the force of those who covered truth; and The God is more severe in force and more severe in tankil.”

An-Nisa, 84

This verse is interesting from the angle of semantic field.

Before ashaddu tankilan, the verb:

أَن يَكُفَّ بَأْسَ

appears; meaning holding back or restraining their ba’s.

This pairing fits the field of restraint in N K L, but the two roots should not be made one.

Kaff is an independent verb for holding back.

Tankil is an encounter whose pushing-back and restraining effect is present.

Does Form II Alone Create Intensity?

Tankil is the verbal noun of Form II, but one should not conclude from the morphological pattern alone that its meaning is “several-times-more-severe punishment.”

In An-Nisa 84, the Qur’an itself says:

أَشَدُّ تَنكِيلًا

So comparative severity explicitly enters the structure through ashadd as well.

Form II can build the caused or applied form of the root, but the details of intensity must be understood from the sentence itself.

The Relation of Nakal and Tankil

In Qur’anic usage, a useful difference can be seen:

Nakal is more the name of the consequence, state, or restraining encounter.

Tankil is closer to the applying and actualizing of such an encounter.

This distinction is useful for translation, but it should not be turned into an absolute rule for every historical use of Arabic.

The Relation of Ankal and Nakal

Ankal and nakal are from one root, but they do not have one meaning.

Ankal

actual bonds and fetters.

Nakal

a consequence or encounter with a restraining function.

The shared point may be stated as follows:

limiting the possibility of continuation and advancing.

In one case, this limitation is material; in the other, the consequence of behavior becomes the agent of retreat.

This semantic relation is observable, but by itself it does not prove that one historically derives from the other.

The Psychological Dimension of Retreat

The use nakala ʿan al-amr shows that this root can also relate to a person drawing back from doing something; dictionaries explain it with cowardice, holding back, and retreating.

This data is useful for understanding the restraining function of nakal: a restraining factor can stop not only physical movement, but also the decision to continue an act.

But it is better to avoid expressions such as “breaking the will” as a definition of the root. Nakal may reduce the daring to repeat or cause retreat, but its psychological intensity and quality are understood separately from each context.

Comparison With Nearby Roots

N K L Compared With M N A

Manʿ focuses on preventing occurrence, access, or continuation.

Nakal, in uses of this family, in addition to prevention, highlights the element of retreating before an obstacle or consequence.

N K L Compared With K F F

Kaff is close to holding back and keeping from continuation.

In An-Nisa 84, yakuffa ba’s and tankil appear in one verse; this pairing shows that they are not complete synonyms.

Kaff expresses the act of holding back itself.

Tankil is an encounter that has a restraining and pushing-back quality.

N K L Compared With A Q B / Iqab

Iqab belongs to the field of consequence and punishment after an act.

Nakal, in a punitive context, highlights the restraining face of that consequence.

Therefore, nakal can in a context be a punishment, but the root is not limited to “punishment”; ankal is a clear witness to this point.

N K L Compared With Q Y D

Qayd is the general name of a bond and limitation.

Ankal also refers to fetters in Al-Muzzammil, but it belongs to a family that shows the wider field of restraint and holding back.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

فَجَعَلْنَاهَا نَكَالًا

In Al-Baqarah 66, nakalan is what “it” was made as.

So nakal is not necessarily the name of the punishment-act itself; an event or condition can be made nakal, meaning it takes on a restraining role.

جَزَاءً ... نَكَالًا

In Al-Ma’idah 38, the placement of jaza’an and nakalan beside each other does not allow both to be collapsed into one Persian word without explanation.

نَكَالَ الْآخِرَةِ وَالْأُولَىٰ

In An-Nazi’at 25, nakal appears in an accusative construction and is annexed to al-akhirah wa-al-ula. The precise meaning of these two depends on the context of the surah, not on the root N K L itself.

أَشَدُّ تَنكِيلًا

In An-Nisa 84, tankilan specifies the field of severity in the construction ashadd.

So comparative severity comes from the whole structure.

Challenges of Understanding and Rendering

First, nakal should not always be translated only as “punishment”; the element of restraint becomes hidden.

Second, nakal should not be made identical with “lesson” or “admonition.” The Qur’an uses these concepts separately.

Third, ankal should not be ignored; this derivative is a direct witness to the material field of restraint.

Fourth, the image of the bridle and its iron piece is a lexical witness, not proof of the historical origin of the root.

Fifth, nakala ʿan al-amr shows that retreat and reduced daring exist in the family’s field; but “breaking the will” should not become the dictionary definition of the root.

Sixth, absolute intensity should not be extracted from Form II alone; in An-Nisa 84, ashadd itself expresses this intensity.

Seventh, restraint should not always be limited to “publicly frightening society.” Its range is determined from the context of each verse.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For أَنكَال:

“fetters”

or

“restraining bonds”

For nakal in Al-Baqarah 66:

“a means of restraint”

or

“a restraining consequence”

For نَكَالًا مِنَ اللَّهِ in Al-Ma’idah 38:

“a nakal from The God”

and in a more explanatory translation:

“a restraining punishment from The God”

For نَكَالَ الْآخِرَةِ وَالْأُولَىٰ:

It is better in the base translation to preserve the word itself:

“the nakal of the latter and the first”

and examine the meaning of the construction in a separate explanation.

For تَنكِيلًا:

“tankil / restraining encounter”

or in explanatory English:

“a severe restraining punishment and encounter”

Short Root Meaning for Forqan

Short root meaning:

holding back, restraining, pushing back, making one retreat.

In punitive context:

a restraining punishment or consequence.

“Restraining punishment” is one important use of the root, not the definition of the entire family N K L.

Conclusion

The root N K L in the Qur’an moves around the axis of restraining the possibility of continuation and creating restraint.

In ankal, this core is seen materially: bonds and fetters that restrain movement.

In nakal, an event or consequence takes on a restraining role.

In tankil, an encounter is carried out whose pushing-back and restraining quality is prominent.

The Arabic use nakala ʿan al-amr also shows that this same field can appear within the person as retreat, refraining, or losing the daring to continue.

Therefore, the final definition of this root may be stated as follows:

N K L expresses holding back, restraining, or making one retreat. In the punitive uses of the Qur’an, this field appears as a consequence or encounter that, beyond recompense, has a restraining quality; and in “ankal,” the same field is seen as material bonds and restraints.
