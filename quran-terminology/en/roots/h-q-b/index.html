The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Q B

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(ḥuquban)

أَحْقَابًا
(aḥqāban)

From fastening and withholding to long periods of time

Ḥ Q B has several familiar Arabic uses. Ḥaqab denotes a strap fastened around a camel to secure its load. Ḥaqiba sometimes concerns urine or rain being held back. In early usage, ḥaqībah concerns a part or pad at the rear of the saddle, or something a rider carries behind them. In another branch, ḥuqub and ḥiqbah denote a period of time which may be long, without having one fixed duration in every use.

Only the temporal meaning occurs in the Qur’an. In Al-Kahf 18:60, Moses speaks of continuing his journey even if it takes a long time. In Al-Nabaʾ 78:23, aḥqāban concerns a group remaining in Hell.

Both occurrences concern an extended duration. The important question is whether the word itself specifies a period such as eighty years or merely denotes a long time. It is also necessary to distinguish whether the plural aḥqāb itself determines that these periods end or continue without end.

Qur’anic forms: «حُقُبًا» in Al-Kahf 18:60 and «أَحْقَابًا» in Al-Nabaʾ 78:23.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the two temporal occurrences of Ḥ Q B

Arabic records forms such as «حَقَبَ», «حَقِبَ», «حَقَب», «حُقْب», «حِقْبَة», «حَقِيبَة», «حِقَاب», «أَحْقَب», «حَقْبَاء», «حَاقِب», «أَحْقَبَ», «احْتَقَبَ», and «اسْتَحْقَبَ», but these forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic principle and material uses in early Arabic

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for Ḥ Q B: «الْحَبْس», withholding or restraining the movement or flow of something.

He illustrates it through rain being withheld, a camel’s urine being obstructed, and a strap securing its load.

This explanation illuminates part of the family’s usage, but Ibn Fāris also records its temporal meaning. This definition alone does not establish the precise historical relationship between withholding and naming periods of time.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: withholding and the temporal use

Ḥaqab: a strap securing a camel’s load

Al-ʿAyn describes ḥaqab as a strap fastening the camel’s saddle or load to the underside of its body to prevent it shifting.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise distinguishes ḥaqab from other saddle straps.

Camels were important for transporting people and goods in Arab life. During travel, a load had to be secured so that it would not slide forward or backward as the animal moved.

Ḥaqab was one of the straps used for this purpose.

Its principal feature is securing the load and preventing unwanted movement. It was not the only means of fastening a camel’s load: different saddle parts and straps had other names.

«أَحْقَبَ الْبَعِيرَ» means putting this strap on a camel.

The strap itself should therefore be distinguished from fastening it. Ḥaqab names the implement; aḥqaba describes the action of fastening it and fitting out the camel.

Ḥaqiba: a flow being withheld

Arabic uses «حَقِبَ الْبَعِيرُ» of a camel whose urine is obstructed or becomes difficult to pass because of pressure from the saddle strap.

Early dictionaries explain this with details about the strap’s position on the animal.

Within this field, «حَقِبَ الْمَطَرُ» denotes rain being withheld or delayed.

«حَقِبَ الْعَامُ» likewise describes a year whose rain has been withheld or diminished.

In these uses, withholding is clearer than the idea of a long duration. A flow or expected event is stopped or delayed rather than continuing or occurring as expected.

Ḥaqiba in these constructions does not itself mean an eighty-year delay or another specified period.

Nor does the rain-related use make Qur’anic ḥuqub mean drought. Their forms and contexts differ.

Ḥaqībah: the saddle’s rear and a load behind the rider

Early sources record ḥaqībah for padding or a part at the rear of a camel’s saddle, and also for what a rider carries behind them. It should therefore not be defined exclusively as a bag or load in every early use.

Al-ʿAyn explains iḥtaqaba and istaḥqaba through fastening a ḥaqībah behind oneself or carrying something behind the rider.

These uses have a tangible relationship with the saddle’s rear and securing a load. “Behind the rider,” however, is not identical to the general expression “on the animal’s back.”

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: saddle straps and ḥaqībah

Iḥtaqaba al-shayʾ can also mean carrying or taking something along even when no actual camel or saddle is mentioned.

Figurative carrying is likewise recorded in this family. The semantic closeness does not establish the historical sequence in which all these uses developed.

«احْتَقَبَ إِثْمًا» describes someone taking on or carrying a sin.

Sin is pictured as a burden carried by a person.

That image should not become the direct meaning of every derivative. In Al-Kahf 18:60, Moses uses ḥuquban for a period of time, rather than a physical load or sin he carries.

A poetic witness: carrying a burden and responsibility

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

فَالْيَوْمَ فَاشْرَبْ غَيْرَ مُسْتَحْقِبٍ
إِثْمًا مِنَ اللَّهِ وَلَا وَاغِلِ

fa-l-yawma fa-shrab ghayra mustaḥqibin / ithman mina allāhi wa-lā wāghili

Meaning: “So drink today, bearing no sin before The God, and not as an uninvited intruder at the gathering.”

Mustaḥqib describes someone carrying something, but its object here is ithman, sin, rather than a material load.

The expression thus moves from carrying an actual load to carrying responsibility or sin.

Lexical sources attribute this verse to Imruʾ al-Qays, but its opening varies. Fa-l-yawma fa-shrab is transmitted; Sībawayh quotes fa-l-yawma ashrab while discussing the unvowelled bāʾ, and grammatical commentaries also report fa-l-yawma usqā. This article quotes fa-l-yawma fa-shrab as one transmitted version.

The variation should remain visible. The witness matters because figurative carrying through mustaḥqib is recorded in early literary Arabic.

Sharḥ Kitāb Sībawayh: fa-shrab, ashrab, and usqā in Imruʾ al-Qays’s witness

2. Ḥuqub and ḥiqbah: a period of time

In the temporal branch, ḥuqub denotes a period of time, and ḥiqbah a portion of an age or a duration.

Al-ʿAyn explains ḥiqbah as a period without a fixed specified length.

The same dictionary explains ḥuqub, with u on both ḥāʾ and qāf, as eighty years.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: ḥiqbah, ḥuqub, and carrying behind oneself

Lisān al-ʿArab records differing uses and accounts: ḥuqub may denote an age or long duration, a year or years in some reports, and eighty years or more in others.

The lexical sources therefore do not offer one universally agreed measure.

Different vocalisations

Arabic vowel differences matter when studying this family.

Ḥaqab, with a on ḥāʾ and qāf, names the load-securing strap in the camel-saddle use.

Ḥuqub, with u on both consonants, occurs in Al-Kahf 18:60 for a period of time.

Ḥuqb, with u on ḥāʾ and an unvowelled qāf, is likewise recorded for a temporal period.

Ḥiqbah, with i on ḥāʾ, denotes a portion of an age or a period.

These forms overlap in some uses, but their vocalisation should not be ignored.

Aḥqāb in Al-Nabaʾ 78:23 is plural. Al-Ṣiḥāḥ treats it as the plural of ḥuqub with two u vowels, giving ḥiqāb for ḥuqb. Other sources report aḥqāb and aḥqub as plurals of both vocalisations.

The plural aḥqāb alone therefore does not establish that each unit has a fixed number of days or years.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Tāj al-ʿArūs: vocalisations and plurals of ḥuqub and ḥuqb

Is every ḥuqub eighty years?

Some early dictionaries give eighty years as a measure for ḥuqb.

Ibn Fāris also records this measure alongside the temporal use.

Other sources, however, disagree about its length. Thaʿlab transmits a year or years, and commentarial accounts give widely differing figures for one ḥuqb.

Eighty years is therefore one measure recorded in lexical tradition, rather than the fixed, exclusive meaning of every use of the family.

For example, saying that someone accompanied another for ḥiqbatan mina al-dahr may mean a long period without requiring a listener to calculate exactly eighty years.

Conversely, an indefinite use does not mean that ḥuqb has never had a specified measure in any convention or terminology.

Measures are reported in some sources and uses. For Qur’anic translation, however, it is necessary to ask whether the verse or sound evidence specifies such a measure.

Neither Al-Kahf 18:60 nor Al-Nabaʾ 78:23 states an explicit figure.

A poetic witness: a ḥiqbah of time

Mutammim ibn Nuwayrah says in an elegy for his brother Mālik:

وَكُنَّا كَنَدْمَانَيْ جَذِيمَةَ حِقْبَةً
مِنَ الدَّهْرِ حَتَّى قِيلَ لَنْ يَتَصَدَّعَا

wa-kunnā ka-nadmānay jadhīmata ḥiqbatan / mina al-dahri ḥattā qīla lan yataṣaddaʿā

Meaning: “For a span of time we were like Jadhīmah’s two inseparable companions, until people said we would never part.”

Ḥiqbatan mina al-dahr denotes a period of time here.

The verse emphasises the length and closeness of the companionship, rather than its precise number of years.

Mutammim ibn Nuwayrah was a mukhaḍram poet who experienced both pre-Islamic and early Islamic times. This elegy concerns his brother, killed in the early Islamic period; it cannot therefore be treated as certainly composed before Qur’anic revelation.

Its use nevertheless helps illuminate ḥiqbah in Arabic associated with the period of revelation.

This witness shows that a period can be expressed relatively and without a particular number. The construction emphasises duration rather than a calendar measure with a fixed number of years.

Al-Aghānī: Mutammim’s elegy for Mālik and the ḥiqbah witness

Is the temporal meaning related to withholding?

Ibn Fāris treats the entire family under the principle of ḥabs, withholding.

One might explain a temporal period as enclosing part of the flow of time within bounds, just as a camel’s strap holds something in place.

This is only a possible explanation of the relationship, however. It does not establish the history of ḥiqbah.

Early sources also record the temporal meaning in its own right. “Holding time back” should therefore not be imported into ḥuqub to defend an apparently unified core meaning.

In the Qur’an, the word concerns time passing and extending, rather than stopping.

3. «أَوْ أَمْضِيَ حُقُبًا»: Al-Kahf 18:60

وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِفَتَىٰهُ لَا أَبْرَحُ حَتَّىٰ أَبْلُغَ مَجْمَعَ الْبَحْرَيْنِ أَوْ أَمْضِيَ حُقُبًا

wa-idh qāla mūsā li-fatāhu lā abraḥu ḥattā ablugha majmaʿa al-baḥrayni aw amḍiya ḥuquban

Translation: And when Moses said to his young companion: “I shall not give up until I reach the meeting place of the two seas, even if I travel for a long time.”

Al-Kahf 18:60

Ḥuquban is an indefinite accusative noun. In amḍiya ḥuquban it expresses the time Moses is prepared to spend reaching his destination.

Amḍiya comes from M Ḍ Y and here concerns continuing to move and time passing.

Ḥuquban therefore does not itself mean walking or travelling; it describes how long that movement may continue.

A destination and an indefinite duration

Moses says he will not give up his effort before reaching majmaʿ al-baḥrayn, the meeting place of the two seas.

The destination is specified, but the time needed to reach it is not.

Aw amḍiya ḥuquban indicates readiness to continue travelling for a long time to reach that destination.

It does not establish that he intended to walk for precisely eighty years. No number of years is specified.

Nor does it necessarily mean that Moses knew beforehand that his journey would be very long. His statement expresses readiness to continue even if reaching the destination takes considerable time.

Al-Kahf 18:61 and Al-Kahf 18:62 describe subsequent events, but do not allow the entire journey’s duration to be calculated in years and months.

The role of aw

Aw connects two expressions: reaching the meeting place of the seas and travelling for a long period.

Reading it merely as a choice between independent destinations could suggest that Moses intends either to reach his destination or to spend a specified period travelling.

The sentence’s sense, however, presents prolonged travel in pursuit of that same destination.

The Persian rendering “even if I travel for a long time” can therefore express the relationship.

This naturally explains the clauses, but ḥuqub itself does not mean perseverance or determination. Those ideas come from Moses’ statement and the complete construction.

Does ḥuqub mean endless?

Here ḥuquban denotes a long duration but does not express infinite time.

Moses speaks of a journey and a destination. An indefinite expression for a long time does not mean that he intends to travel for an infinity of time.

Conversely, a destination does not establish precisely how long he is prepared to spend reaching it.

This use clearly distinguishes an unspecified duration from an endless one.

Ḥuqb can be a long time whose numerical limit is unspecified, without the word itself deciding whether it is endless.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Kahf 18:60: continuing the journey, time, and differing measures

4. «لَابِثِينَ فِيهَا أَحْقَابًا»: Al-Nabaʾ 78:23

لَابِثِينَ فِيهَا أَحْقَابًا

lābithīna fīhā aḥqāban

Translation: Remaining there for long ages.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:23

Aḥqāban is plural and accusative here, expressing the duration of remaining.

Lābithīn comes from L B Th and concerns remaining in a place or condition. Fīhā refers to Hell, mentioned in Al-Nabaʾ 78:21.

Aḥqāban therefore does not itself mean remaining; it denotes the periods during which remaining takes place.

The plural aḥqāb

Aḥqāb is plural, indicating multiple periods rather than only one.

Lexical and commentarial sources sometimes explain these periods as successive: another ḥuqb follows whenever one passes.

This fits the image of remaining over multiple periods.

The plural form itself, however, specifies neither their exact number nor that another must follow each one without end.

For example, saying someone remained somewhere for many years does not specify the exact end of the stay. Nor does the plural “years” alone establish that the stay must be endless.

Aḥqāban therefore concerns long periods, while deciding whether the remaining ends requires broader investigation.

Reports of seventy and eighty years

Different measures for one ḥuqb are reported in explaining Al-Nabaʾ 78:23.

They include eighty years, seventy years, and much longer durations in some accounts.

Al-Qurṭubī: reports of seventy, eighty, and other measures

Some reports even reckon each day of these years as a thousand worldly years.

These figures do not appear in the verse itself.

The substantial disagreement also prevents one figure being treated as the word’s certain, sole meaning without independent evidence.

Lexical method distinguishes three matters: the recorded Arabic meaning of ḥuqb, a measure assigned to it in a historical or commentarial report, and the actual duration intended by the verse.

A number in a dictionary or commentary does not by itself establish that the Qur’an intends that number; its basis must be examined.

“Long periods” or “long ages” is more suitable for the base translation than adding a particular number of years.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Nabaʾ 78:23: reported measures and periods of punishment

Does aḥqāban determine that punishment ends?

An important question is whether remaining for aḥqāb means punishment ends once those periods have passed.

The lexical meaning should first be distinguished from a theological conclusion.

Aḥqāb is the plural of temporal periods. Here the word itself states no final count of them.

It therefore does not directly establish that remaining in Hell ends after a specified number of years.

Conversely, the word alone does not mean unlimited eternity.

A report from Al-Farrāʾ in lexical sources compares unnumbered aḥqāb with “five aḥqāb” or “ten aḥqāb.” In interpreting this verse, he envisages one period following whenever another passes. His interpretation is therefore continued remaining, rather than suspending judgment about it.

His distinction between a specified and unspecified count matters. His interpretation of this verse should nevertheless remain separate from claiming that aḥqāb inherently means infinity in every Arabic sentence.

Al-Basīṭ: Al-Farrāʾ’s interpretation of successive aḥqāb

It should not be concluded that aḥqāb means infinity in all its uses.

Just as mentioning a long duration does not by itself establish that it ends, omitting a final count does not alone prove endlessness.

Judging the ultimate outcome of punishment requires the verses on enduring residence, abad, divine will, and other descriptions of the hereafter to be considered together.

The context in Al-Nabaʾ

Al-Nabaʾ 78:21 introduces Hell, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:22 mentions those who transgress.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:23 expresses their duration through aḥqāban.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:24 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:25 describe their condition and drink there.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:26 then calls this jazāʾan wifāqan, a proportionate recompense.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:27 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:28 describe their conduct and stance toward reckoning and the divine signs.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:23 therefore belongs to a sequence describing both the people concerned and their condition.

This context prevents aḥqāb being isolated from everything around it and made the sole basis of a complete theory of punishment in the hereafter.

The relationship to verses on khulūd

Elsewhere, the Qur’an describes remaining in the Fire through expressions from Kh L D.

Al-Baqarah 2:39, for example, speaks of khālidūn in the Fire. Al-Jinn 72:23 has khālidīna fīhā abadan.

Hūd 11:107 also describes remaining in the Fire through the duration of the heavens and earth and an exception concerning the Lord’s will.

These verses matter for a comprehensive study of punishment’s duration and outcome.

Ḥuqb, khulūd, and abad nevertheless have different semantic fields.

Ḥuqb denotes a period; khulūd concerns enduring residence in a place or condition; and abad in some constructions makes the temporal horizon more explicit.

Sharing a context of the hereafter does not make them exact synonyms.

Conversely, different roots do not mean their descriptions must conflict.

A condition may be described through several temporal expressions without their meanings being precisely identical.

Whether punishment in the hereafter ends therefore goes beyond the lexical definition of Ḥ Q B and requires an independent study of the relevant verses together.

5. Distinctions from related roots and words

Ḥ Q B (ḥuqub) and D H R (dahr)

Dahr concerns time and the passing of an age, and may encompass a long period.

The temporal use of ḥuqb likewise concerns a period within time.

Some dictionaries explain ḥuqub through dahr.

They should not, however, be treated as exact synonyms. Ḥuqb can denote a particular period or portion, while dahr often has a broader field encompassing the passing of time.

In Al-Kahf 18:60, the subject is time potentially spent travelling, rather than necessarily the whole of an age or all time.

Ḥ Q B (ḥuqub) and ʾ M D (amad)

In the Qur’an, amad concerns extended time and a limit or horizon envisaged for something.

Al-Jinn 72:25 uses amadan concerning the time of the promise.

Ḥuqb can likewise denote a long period but does not itself establish its final limit.

Amad can foreground a condition’s endpoint or temporal horizon, whereas ḥuqb foregrounds the period itself.

This is a tendency that must be assessed against each context.

Study of ʾ M D

Ḥ Q B (ḥuqub) and W Q T (waqt)

Waqt concerns a specified time or a time appointed for an event.

Ḥuqb is not necessarily an exact, appointed time.

For example, saying an activity will occur at a particular hour specifies its time; saying someone will travel for a long period leaves that period’s exact length unknown.

Ḥuquban in Al-Kahf 18:60 therefore cannot simply be translated as “an appointed time.”

Study of W Q T

Ḥ Q B (ḥuqub) and L B Th (labitha)

Labth concerns remaining in a place or condition for a period.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:23 contains both lābithīn and aḥqāban.

Lābithīn is an accusative plural active participle from labitha, expressing remaining. Aḥqāban is an accusative adverbial of time describing its duration.

Aḥqāban therefore does not itself mean being imprisoned, remaining, or residing. That comes from lābithīn.

Labth also occurs for both short and long stays in the Qur’an, so the root does not invariably imply a long duration.

Al-Basīṭ: the active participle lābithīn and the temporal adverbial

Ḥ Q B (ḥuqub) and Kh L D (khuld)

Kh L D concerns enduring residence and has broad Qur’anic uses concerning the hereafter.

In both its Qur’anic occurrences, ḥuqb concerns a temporal period.

Aḥqāb accompanies remaining in Hell in Al-Nabaʾ 78:23, but this does not make ḥuqb itself mean khulūd.

Nor does the difference between the roots establish that every aḥqāb must end, or that every Arabic use of Kh L D necessarily denotes mathematical infinity.

The construction and the relevant verses must be examined in both cases.

Study of Kh L D

Ḥ Q B (ḥaqab) and Ḥ B S (ḥabs)

Ibn Fāris relates Ḥ Q B to ḥabs, withholding.

Ḥ B S is nevertheless a separate root concerning holding something and preventing movement or departure.

Ḥaqiba al-maṭar is close to ḥabs in denoting withheld rain, but this similarity does not make them one root.

Nor should ḥabs replace ḥuqub or aḥqāb in the Qur’anic temporal uses: the verses concern periods passing and extending.

Study of Ḥ B S

Ḥ Q B (ḥaqab) and Ḥ Q Q (ḥaqq)

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

Qur’anic Ḥ Q B concerns temporal periods, while Ḥ Q Q concerns right, truth, realisation, stability, and firmness.

Ḥuqb should therefore not be understood as right or truth merely because of its similar appearance.

Nor does calling a historical period a ḥiqbah imply anything necessarily right or wrong about it.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ḥuquban in Al-Kahf 18:60 denotes a long period of time.

The following rendering is suitable:

“And when Moses said to his young companion: ‘I shall not give up until I reach the meeting place of the two seas, even if I travel for a long time.’”

This preserves the long duration without adding a particular number of years.

“Long years” is also possible in Persian, but is narrower in wording: ḥuqb more generally denotes a period.

Aḥqāban in Al-Nabaʾ 78:23 is plural, which the translation should preserve.

“Remaining there for long ages” is defensible.

“They remain there for ages” is likewise natural and fluent.

Neither eighty years nor another numerical measure should replace aḥqāban without sound evidence.

Adding “forever” or “until the end of a specified period” to the word itself likewise goes beyond its direct meaning.

Any conclusion about the duration of remaining drawn from other verses should be explained in the relevant study of the hereafter, rather than inserted in place of aḥqāb in the base translation.

Outside the Qur’an, ḥaqab can be rendered a particular camel-saddle strap; ḥaqiba al-maṭar, rain being withheld or delayed; ḥaqībah, according to usage, the saddle’s rear padding or a load carried behind the rider; iḥtaqaba, fastening or carrying something behind oneself; and ḥiqbah, a period of time.

Aḥqāb can likewise be rendered “periods” or “ages,” whose exact length and extent must be understood through context.

Conclusion

Ḥ Q B has several related Arabic uses: a strap securing a camel’s load, a flow or event such as rain being withheld, a rear saddle part or load carried behind the rider, and a period of time.

Ibn Fāris explains the family through ḥabs, withholding. This is clear in uses such as the camel’s strap and withheld rain, but its precise historical relationship to naming temporal periods needs further evidence.

Only the temporal branch occurs in the Qur’an.

In Al-Kahf 18:60, ḥuquban denotes the long time Moses is prepared to spend reaching his destination.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:23, aḥqāban concerns multiple long periods of remaining in Hell.

Some dictionaries give eighty years for one ḥuqb, while differing reports also exist. Neither Qur’anic verse itself specifies a number of years.

Brief root meaning: In Qur’anic usage, a long period without the verse specifying a number of years; aḥqāb is the plural of periods and does not alone determine their final count.

Aḥqāb therefore neither establishes a definite end to punishment nor, by that word alone, proves endlessness.

This issue requires the relevant verses on enduring residence, khulūd, abad, and divine will to be considered together.

For Forqan, “a long time” suits ḥuquban, while “long periods” or “long ages” suits aḥqāban.

These equivalents preserve the linguistic meaning and prevent a reported number or theological conclusion from replacing the Qur’anic word before its evidence is examined.
