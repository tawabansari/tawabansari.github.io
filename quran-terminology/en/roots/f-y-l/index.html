Qur’anic root F Y L (fīl)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْفِيلِ
(al-fīli)
Elephant; one Qur’anic occurrence

أَفْيَال، فُيُول، فِيَلَة
(afyāl; fuyūl; fiyalah)
Lexical plurals; not Qur’anic forms

فَيَّال / اِسْتَفْيَلَ
(fayyāl / istafyala)
Elephant keeper; becoming elephant-like; non-Qur’anic

The elephant: the word’s history in Arabic and Persian, the People of the Elephant, and the distinction between an animal’s name and its historical story

1. The word in early Arabic

In Arabic, «فِيل» names the large animal with a trunk also called fīl in Persian. Elephants have large bodies, broad ears, and long trunks used to gather food, take up drinking water, and move objects.

Classical Arabic dictionaries use «فِيل» for this familiar animal and record the plurals «أَفْيَال», «فُيُول», and «فِيَلَة». «فِيلَة» also denotes a female elephant.

Unlike many verbal roots, the dictionary definition of «فِيل» does not begin with an action or an abstract state. It names a particular animal.

This distinction matters. When the Qur’an mentions «الْفِيل», we should not first assign it a meaning such as greatness, power, heaviness, or defeat and then treat the animal as an instance of that abstraction.

Its direct meaning is elephant. Large size, the ability to carry loads, and military employment may be characteristics or uses of the animal, but they are not all components of the noun’s lexical meaning.

The word’s history and possible borrowing from Persian

An important distinction concerns the historical origin of «فِيل».

Middle and New Persian have pīl for the animal; Old Persian has a related form attested for ivory. Related words also occur in Aramaic and Syriac.

Arabic fīl is commonly explained as a borrowing related to Iranian pīl, possibly through an intermediary language. This proposed route into Arabic must be distinguished from the word’s more remote origin, which has also been traced to Mesopotamian languages.

The correspondence in form and meaning supports the proposed connection. Standard Arabic lacks the Persian sound p; adaptation as f occurs in some loanwords.

Similar words in several languages do not alone establish an exact route of transmission. Trade, migration, commercial contact, or military encounters may carry a word between languages, but identifying a particular route requires further historical evidence.

Thus, a historical connection between Arabic fīl and Persian pīl is well supported; neither its precise route into Arabic nor an ultimately Iranian origin should be asserted without further evidence.

Borrowing does not mean that a word remains foreign to the language that adopts it.

Once speakers use a borrowed word, it becomes part of their language’s vocabulary. New plurals, descriptions, and derivatives may then be formed from it.

«فِيل» was intelligible in the Arabic of the revelation period, and the Qur’an uses it without explaining the animal’s name.

An Arabic Qur’anic usage is therefore compatible with a possibly non-Arabic historical origin.

A poetic example attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī reads:

تَرْعَى مَذَانِبَ وَسْمِيٍّ أَطَاعَ لَهُ
بِالْجَزْعِ حَيْثُ عَصَى أَصْحَابَهُ الْفِيلُ

Tarʿā madhāniba wasmiyyin aṭāʿa lahu / bi-l-jazʿi ḥaythu ʿaṣā aṣḥābahu l-fīlu.

“She grazes in the channels watered by the early seasonal rain, in the valley bend where the elephant disobeyed its companions.”

Here «الْفِيل» refers to the familiar animal.

The poet describes a place associated with the elephant’s story and its refusal to obey its companions.

The example matters in two ways: it uses the animal’s name in Arabic poetry, and its wording associates the elephant with companions or handlers.

Early literary sources attribute the verse to Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī. However, it survives through compilations made after Islam, and the date of this particular verse has not been independently established.

It is useful evidence for the linguistic tradition and early accounts of the elephant, but cannot by itself establish every historical detail of the People of the Elephant or the precise date of composition.

The Qur’an itself provides direct evidence for the name’s use in Arabic during the revelation period.

2. Forms and distinct lexical branches

Qur’anic morphological indexes place «فِيل» under F Y L. This classification does not necessarily mean that the animal’s name derives from an inherited Arabic verb with these three consonants.

Arabic records the following forms:

«فِيل»: the animal.

«أَفْيَال», «فُيُول», and «فِيَلَة»: its plurals.

«فَيَّال»: someone responsible for tending, guiding, or caring for an elephant.

«اِسْتَفْيَلَ»: used of a camel becoming elephant-like in size.

In some usages, «تَفَيَّلَ» likewise concerns becoming stout or large-bodied.

These forms show that an animal’s name can serve as a basis for new words. Comparing a large camel to an elephant draws on the familiar animal’s meaning.

Not every form listed under F Y L in dictionaries necessarily derives from the animal’s name.

Weakness or error in judgment

Arabic dictionaries record «فَالَ رَأْيُهُ» for someone’s judgment becoming weak or unsound.

«رَجُلٌ فِيلُ الرَّأْيِ» describes a man with weak judgment or discernment.

«فَيَّلَ رَأْيَهُ» can mean judging someone’s opinion weak or mistaken.

Some lexical analyses identify weakness of judgment as the core of this branch.

This does not establish that the elephant’s name originally meant foolishness.

Several semantic families may share a dictionary entry because their consonants coincide, without a common historical origin having been demonstrated.

Especially given the possible borrowing of the animal’s name, «فِيل» must be distinguished from expressions such as «فَالَ رَأْيُهُ».

The animal name does not in itself mean an unintelligent animal. Deriving it from supposed weak judgment in elephants lacks sufficient historical support.

«مُفَايَلَة»: a game

«مُفَايَلَة» also named an early Arabic game.

A small object was hidden beneath soil or sand, which was then divided into two parts; another player had to guess which part concealed it.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd includes:

يَشُقُّ حَبَابَ الْمَاءِ حَيْزُومُهَا بِهَا
كَمَا قَسَمَ التُّرْبَ الْمُفَايِلُ بِالْيَدِ

Yashuqqu ḥabāba l-māʾi ḥayzūmuhā bihā / kamā qasama t-turba l-mufāyilu bi-l-yadi.

“Her bow cleaves the water’s waves, as the player divides the soil with his hand.”

The poet compares a ship cutting through water to a player dividing soil in this game.

Lexical explanations connect «مُفَايَلَة» with weak judgment or a mistaken guess. Some sources also record a form with hamzah and associate it with another family.

Its appearance in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition does not prove that the elephant’s name derives from the same meaning.

Classification under a set of consonants must remain distinct from establishing historical derivation.

3. Qur’anic usage: the People of the Elephant

F Y L occurs only once in the Qur’an:

أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْفِيلِ

A-lam tara kayfa faʿala rabbuka bi-aṣḥābi l-fīl.

“Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the People of the Elephant?”

Al-Fīl 105:1

«الْفِيلِ» is the family’s only Qur’anic form.

Grammatically, it is a definite singular noun in the genitive case.

«أَصْحَابِ» refers to a group of people; «الْفِيلِ» is the second term of its genitive construction.

The expression identifies people associated with an elephant and known by that association.

«أَصْحَاب» belongs to Ṣ Ḥ B, whose Arabic usages include companionship, association, affiliation, or a relationship with a person or thing.

Here it does not exclusively denote legal ownership. People may accompany an animal, use it, or become known through its presence in an event.

The basic sense here is people identified by their association with the elephant.

Does the expression mean one elephant or several?

«فِيل» is singular in form and appears with the definite article «ال».

A definite singular noun can refer to a particular animal. It can also name a kind or a familiar characteristic within a group’s designation.

The expression does not provide a count of all the elephants involved in the event.

The singular wording identifies “the elephant”; by itself, it neither rules out other elephants nor establishes that several were present.

Later historical accounts differ about the number of elephants and some of their names. Such details are not part of the word’s lexical meaning.

The Qur’an names the animal, but does not give a total count, its sex, color, size, or individual name.

Were these people necessarily the elephant’s owners?

The Persian rendering “owners of the elephant” may suggest legal ownership.

Arabic «أَصْحَاب» has a wider range.

The Qur’anic people of the cave are associated with the cave, not necessarily its legal owners.

Likewise, depending on context, the people of the garden may be inhabitants of Paradise or owners of a garden.

The subject and context establish the relationship.

In Al-Fīl, the designation shows the animal’s importance in identifying the group. But «أَصْحَاب» alone does not identify its owner, its handler, or how it was employed in the event.

“The People of the Elephant” is suitable as the basic translation. A lexical explanation may clarify it as “the group accompanying or associated with the elephant.”

4. The animal and the story of the people associated with it

After introducing the People of the Elephant, the surah describes their outcome.

Al-Fīl 105:2 states that their scheme was brought to failure.

Al-Fīl 105:3 describes birds sent against them in flocks.

Al-Fīl 105:4 mentions stones cast at them.

Al-Fīl 105:5 compares their resulting condition to eaten vegetation.

The surah is therefore not merely an introduction to an animal. It concerns a group identified through the elephant whose scheme failed.

Each word nevertheless makes its own contribution.

«فِيل» names the animal.

«أَصْحَاب» identifies the associated group.

«كَيْد» concerns their scheme or planning.

«طَيْر» denotes birds.

«أَبَابِيل» concerns their grouping in that context.

«حِجَارَة» names stones; the image of eaten vegetation describes the group’s outcome.

Their occurrence together does not combine all their meanings within the name of the elephant.

Was the elephant itself responsible for their scheme?

The second verse attributes the scheme to the People of the Elephant.

This does not establish that the animal shared the intentions or moral responsibility of its human companions.

Humans may use animals for carrying loads, travel, work, or warfare. The political or military aims of owners and handlers do not change an animal name’s lexical meaning.

The distinction matters here: the Qur’an identifies a human group through the elephant and then speaks of that group’s scheme.

The designation should not be turned into a claim that the animal planned the attack or independently decided to undertake it.

Nor does the animal’s name alone establish size or physical power as the central meaning and definitive message of every word in the surah.

A reflection may connect the failure of people associated with such a large animal to the limits of human power. That insight comes from the story as a whole, not from the noun’s lexical meaning.

5. The designation and historical accounts

Familiar historical narratives associate the People of the Elephant with Abraha, the Abyssinian ruler in Yemen, and his expedition toward Mecca.

These accounts describe an attack intended to destroy the Kaaba, with one or more elephants accompanying the army.

Some give a name for the principal elephant and details about its stopping or movement.

The surah itself does not name Abraha, count the troops or elephants, name the animal, or specify the event’s exact date.

Neither Mecca nor the Kaaba is named in these five verses.

This does not automatically invalidate all historical accounts. They may be examined to understand the background, but each detail requires assessment independently of the word’s meaning.

A root study should not substitute the historical narrative for the definition of elephant.

Even if a particular account of the commander or the animal’s behavior is accepted, the lexical meaning remains the animal’s name.

The Qur’an uses the designation without defining the animal or extensively introducing the group. This is compatible with allusion to an event familiar to its audience, but does not prove that every later narrative detail was known or accepted in precisely that form at the time of revelation.

Forqan should keep Qur’anic statements, historical evidence, and exegetical narratives distinct.

6. Comparison with nearby words

F Y L and J M L: elephant and camel

«جَمَل» denotes a male camel in Arabic and is also used for that animal in the Qur’an.

«فِيل» names a different animal.

They can be compared in size, load-carrying ability, and human use, but their lexical meanings are not interchangeable.

Camels were familiar in Arabian life and economy. The Qur’an mentions them and other livestock in contexts of food, transport, and the benefits of creation.

The elephant appears only in the designation of a group in Al-Fīl.

This difference does not mean that fīl names only a war animal or jamal only a pack animal. Each has its own name; human uses are established historically and contextually.

F Y L and Kh Y L: elephant and horses

«خَيْل» denotes horses, mentioned in various Qur’anic contexts including transport and military preparation.

Both elephants and horses have been used in human warfare.

They nevertheless name different animals.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:6, horses occur in the context of riding to obtain property. In Al-Fīl, the elephant helps identify people whose scheme failed.

This contrast belongs to the verses’ subjects; it does not make F Y L inherently mean defeat or Kh Y L victory.

F Y L and F Sh L: elephant and loss of resolve

«فَشِلَ» concerns weakness, failing resolve, and loss of steadfastness.

Dictionaries also list a branch concerning weak or erroneous judgment under F Y L.

Similarity in meaning does not make the roots identical.

F Sh L contains fāʾ, shīn, and lām; F Y L contains fāʾ, yāʾ, and lām.

The Qur’anic «الْفِيل» does not invoke the weak-judgment branch.

The group’s defeat or failure therefore does not prove that the animal’s name derives from defeat or weakness.

F Y L and F N D: elephant and unsound judgment

F N D concerns error, weak judgment, and attributing unsoundness to another’s assessment.

«تُفَنِّدُونِ» in Yūsuf 12:94 belongs to that family.

«فَالَ رَأْيُهُ» likewise concerns weak or mistaken judgment.

This semantic proximity does not establish a shared historical identity.

Nor does it make the elephant’s name mean foolishness.

In the Qur’an, fīl names an animal, while tufannidūni concerns attributing weak judgment to a person.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For «فِيل», Persian and Dari use fīl; the direct English equivalent is “elephant.”

Arabic and Persian name the same familiar animal, so no new equivalent or interpretive gloss is needed in the basic translation.

Its non-Qur’anic plurals can be translated “elephants.”

«فَيَّال»: “elephant keeper” or “elephant handler.”

In the bodily-size usage, «اِسْتَفْيَلَ» can mean “became large and stout like an elephant.”

«فَالَ رَأْيُهُ»: “his judgment became weak or unsound.” This usage should remain distinct from the animal name.

For the sole Qur’anic occurrence:

أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْفِيلِ

A-lam tara kayfa faʿala rabbuka bi-aṣḥābi l-fīl.

“Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the People of the Elephant?”

Al-Fīl 105:1

This preserves the familiar Qur’anic designation “the People of the Elephant.”

An explanation may add: “people who were identified by their association with the elephant.”

“Abraha’s army” reflects a familiar historical narrative but is not the direct meaning of the Arabic expression: Abraha is not named in the verse.

“Owners of several war elephants” adds a number and use that the phrase does not specify.

“Powerful people” or “mighty forces” changes an animal’s name into a symbolic interpretation.

Retaining “elephant” and explaining the group’s relationship to it separately is therefore more precise for Forqan.

Summary

«فِيل» is Arabic for the large animal with a trunk, also called fīl in Persian.

It is historically related to Iranian pīl; direct borrowing or transmission through an intermediary is possible. This does not settle the word’s more remote origin.

Possible borrowing does not make it foreign to revelation-period Arabic. The Qur’an itself attests its use at that time.

Dictionaries also list «فَالَ رَأْيُهُ» and «مُفَايَلَة» under F Y L. These concern weak judgment and an early game, but sharing a dictionary entry does not prove common historical derivation.

The family occurs only once in the Qur’an, as «الْفِيلِ» in Al-Fīl 105:1.

The People of the Elephant are identified through the animal. The designation does not provide individual identities, a count of animals, or full details of the event.

The following verses describe the failure of their scheme and their outcome. Failure is not the animal name’s lexical meaning.

Nor should human users’ intentions and responsibility be attributed to the animal without evidence.

Final definition for Forqan:

In Qur’anic usage, F Y L is a nominal family centered on «فِيل», elephant, a large animal with a trunk. The name is historically related to Iranian pīl, without this establishing its ultimate origin.

«فِيل» is singular; recorded plurals include «أَفْيَال», «فُيُول», and «فِيَلَة».

Other branches under these consonants concern weak judgment and early games, but their historical connection to the animal name has not been established.

In Al-Fīl 105:1, «الْفِيلِ» is the elephant, and the accompanying designation identifies people associated with it. Their story, scheme, and outcome emerge from the surah’s verses, not from the noun itself.
