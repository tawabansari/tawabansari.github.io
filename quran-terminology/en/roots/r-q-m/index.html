Marking, inscribing, and written recording

Brief root meaning: Making a line, design, or mark on something; writing and recording through recognizable signs. In extended uses, this family also concerns textile patterns, marked objects, recorded writings, and numbering.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root R Q M concerns drawing lines, creating patterns, marking, and writing. رَقَمَ (raqama) describes making a line or mark on a surface; مَرْقُوم (marqūm) describes something bearing a design or inscription.

This root is not limited to writing letters. Textile patterns, identifying marks, colored markings on some animals, and signs used to identify things or record information also fall within its range.

In modern Arabic, رَقْم (raqm) is especially familiar as “number.” Yet this common modern sense should not be the starting point for defining the root in the Qur’an. A number is one kind of sign, whereas the older range of raqm also includes lines, writing, patterns, and marks.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The root R Q M occurs three times in the Qur’an, in two forms:

  • الرَّقِيمِ: In Al-Kahf 18:9, in أَصْحَابَ الْكَهْفِ وَالرَّقِيمِ.
  • مَرْقُومٌ: In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9, in كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ.
  • مَرْقُومٌ: In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20, in the same expression, كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ.

Other recognized Arabic forms include رَقَمَ, يَرْقُمُ, رَقْم, تَرْقِيم, رَاقِم, مِرْقَم, مُرَقَّم, رَقْمَة, and أَرْقَم, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

رَقِيم (raqīm) follows the faʿīl pattern and can lexically denote writing or something written upon. مَرْقُوم (marqūm) is the passive participle of رَقَمَ and denotes something bearing a mark, design, or inscription.

The Qur’an thus uses raqīm in the account of the Companions of the Cave and twice describes a book as marqūm in Al-Muṭaffifīn.

2. Early Arabic meanings and linguistic evidence

Lines, patterns, and marks: the semantic core

One principal axis of R Q M is making lines and writing, together with closely related uses.

Within this field, رَقْم can denote a line or mark made on something.

One distinctive feature of this root, compared with writing in a general sense, is its emphasis in various uses on making a recognizable sign. That sign may be a letter, line, pattern, mark, or complete inscription.

A design made on cloth can be called raqm. A book that has been written or marked can be described as marqūm. A merchant’s marking of goods to identify them or indicate their price is also expressed through this root in Arabic usage.

These uses show that the root’s range is broader than number alone.

Raqm in textiles and decorative patterns

In early Arabic usage, رَقَمَ الثَّوْبَ means to decorate cloth or make lines and marks on it.

Cloth bearing decorative designs and lines can be called ثَوْبٌ مَرْقُومٌ.

رَقْم also denotes certain patterned fabrics and textiles with colored stripes.

In such uses, the purpose is not necessarily to convey a written sentence. A textile pattern may be a shape, color, or arrangement visible on its surface.

Restricting raqm to writing letters would therefore overlook part of its actual range.

The same verb is also attested for marking goods and indicating their price. This has an evident connection with modern numbering, but it does not establish that raqm originally denoted numbers alone.

Raqm in writing and recording

رَقَمَ الْكِتَابَ is attested for writing or marking a book.

One established usage also explains raqm through تَعْجِيمُ الْكِتَابِ: making letters clear through distinguishing marks.

This belongs to a specialized branch of marking written texts.

The general meaning of raqm, however, should not be restricted to adding distinguishing dots to letters. كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ is also attested with the meaning “a written book.”

These two levels matter. A text may be distinguished by special signs, but marqūm in the Qur’an does not itself identify the script, dotting system, or technical details of writing.

Within the same family, مِرْقَم denotes a pen, while مُرَقِّم denotes someone who makes designs or writing.

These forms show that the root’s association with writing and marking was established in Arabic.

Natural and applied markings on animals

أَرْقَم is used for a kind of snake, or to describe a snake with distinctive patterns and colors.

Lexical descriptions of arqam mention black, white, or other colored markings on the snake’s body.

مَرْقُوم is also attested for an animal with small brands on its lower legs. These applied marks should be distinguished from natural coloration.

These uses matter because they show that the family includes both natural markings and marks made by people, without limiting marking to the use of a pen.

Natural markings on an animal can resemble designs and marks made on objects.

That resemblance does not establish that people first observed a snake’s markings and subsequently derived the concept of writing from them. The evidence is insufficient for such a historical sequence.

What is clear is the shared field of recognizable lines and signs in these uses.

Raqm and features of the land

رَقْمَة is attested for certain tracts of land, meadows, and parts of a valley.

رَقْمَةُ الْوَادِي is explained in some usages as the side of a valley or the place where its water collects. It does not invariably imply a location visibly distinguished from every other side.

مَرْقُومَة is also attested for land with scattered plants or traces of growth.

Such naming has sometimes been explained through the image of scattered markings on the ground.

That connection is conceivable, but a visual explanation should not become a claim of certain historical derivation. Place names in particular may have independent histories, and similarity in spelling alone cannot establish why they were named.

Early poetic evidence

The transmitted Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes:

وَدَارٌ لَهَا بِالرَّقْمَتَيْنِ كَأَنَّهَا
مَرَاجِيعُ وَشْمٍ فِي نَوَاشِرِ مِعْصَمِ

wa-dārun lahā bi-al-raqmatayni ka-annahā / marājīʿu washmin fī nawāshiri miʿṣami

Translation: “And a dwelling of hers at Al-Raqmatayn, whose remaining traces resemble renewed tattoo marks on the skin near a wrist.”

الرَّقْمَتَيْن is a place name in this line. Early geographical accounts differ about its exact location.

The witness attests this place name in poetry transmitted from the pre-Islamic period; it does not by itself settle the general meaning of the verb raqama or the historical reason for the place name.

At the same time, comparing a dwelling’s traces to tattoo lines shows that markings remaining on a surface were a recognizable poetic image.

Another witness is transmitted in lexical material without a secure historical attribution to a pre-Islamic poet:

سَأَرْقُمُ فِي الْمَاءِ الْقَرَاحِ إِلَيْكُمُ
عَلَى نَأْيِكُمْ إِنْ كَانَ فِي الْمَاءِ رَاقِمُ

sa-arqumu fī al-māʾi al-qarāḥi ilaykumu / ʿalā naʾyikum in kāna fī al-māʾi rāqimu

Translation: “Despite your distance, I shall write to you upon clear water—if there is anyone who can write upon water.”

Here, أَرْقُم means to make a design or inscription.

The poetic image rests on the difficulty of making writing remain on water. Unlike writing on stone, skin, or cloth, a mark on water does not endure.

The Arabic expression يَرْقُمُ فِي الْمَاءِ also describes exceptional skill and resourcefulness: as though someone could make a mark even on something that cannot retain it.

This usage places patterns and writing within the root’s central field, but does not establish that all its derivatives imply permanent endurance or immutability.

3. Raqīm in the account of the Companions of the Cave

Al-Kahf 18:9

Verse text:

أَمْ حَسِبْتَ أَنَّ أَصْحَابَ الْكَهْفِ وَالرَّقِيمِ كَانُوا مِنْ آيَاتِنَا عَجَبًا

am ḥasibta anna aṣḥāba al-kahfi wa-al-raqīmi kānū min āyātinā ʿajaban

Al-Kahf 18:9

Translation:

“Did you suppose that the companions of the Cave and the Inscription were among Our wondrous signs?”

Al-Kahf 18:9

The lexical meaning of raqīm

الرَّقِيم follows the faʿīl morphological pattern.

In Arabic, this pattern can sometimes carry a passive-participial sense, as جَرِيح denotes someone wounded.

On this analysis, raqīm can mean something upon which writing or a mark has been placed.

This is the linguistic basis for the early explanations of raqīm as a book, writing, tablet, or inscribed surface.

The analysis has linguistic support, but it does not yet determine precisely which object or place raqīm refers to in this verse.

Raqīm: writing, a tablet, or a place name?

Several identifications of raqīm have been proposed since early times.

On one reading, it was writing or a tablet recording the names or account of the Companions of the Cave.

That possibility is linguistically defensible because raqīm can belong to the field of writing and inscribed objects.

Another account identifies it as the name of the valley, settlement, or mountain where the cave was situated.

On that reading, the word functions as a proper place name, like many names that may initially have been descriptive but later became the name of a particular location.

Accounts differ about the tablet’s material, where it was placed, the names written on it, and other details. Some mention stone or metal; others describe its being kept in a particular place.

The Qur’an itself states none of these details.

Three questions must therefore remain distinct: the lexical meaning of raqīm, the possibility that it is a proper name, and the historical identification of the object or place so named.

Lexically, “writing” or “something marked” has clear support. But the verse itself does not conclusively identify raqīm with a particular tablet, cave entrance, settlement, or mountain.

Are the companions of the Cave and of Raqīm two groups?

The construction أَصْحَابَ الْكَهْفِ وَالرَّقِيمِ contains one occurrence of أَصْحَاب followed by two coordinated nouns.

It can identify one group through two associations: people known in connection with the Cave and Raqīm.

The conjunction wāw alone does not establish two entirely separate groups.

The continuation also describes a group of young people who took refuge in the cave. Al-Kahf 18:10 states:

Verse text:

إِذْ أَوَى الْفِتْيَةُ إِلَى الْكَهْفِ فَقَالُوا رَبَّنَا آتِنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً وَهَيِّئْ لَنَا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا رَشَدًا

idh awā al-fityatu ilā al-kahfi fa-qālū rabbanā ātinā min ladunka raḥmatan wa-hayyiʾ lanā min amrinā rashadan

Al-Kahf 18:10

Translation:

“When the young people took refuge in the cave and said: Our Lord, grant us mercy from Yourself and prepare for us a right course in our affair.”

Al-Kahf 18:10

This continuity supports the reading of one group with two associations.

After introducing raqīm, however, the account does not explain it further under that name. Determining whether the second association is specifically with writing, a place, or another sign still requires independent evidence.

Is raqīm the same as kitāb marqūm?

Raqīm and marqūm belong to the same root family and can both concern writing and recording.

In Al-Kahf 18:9, however, raqīm accompanies the name of the Cave; in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20, marqūm describes kitāb.

A shared root therefore does not imply an identical referent.

If raqīm in the Cave account denotes writing or a tablet, that writing could semantically be described as marqūm. This lexical connection does not establish that raqīm is the same record of human deeds discussed in Al-Muṭaffifīn.

Translating raqīm in Forqan

Forqan’s current Persian translation renders الرَّقِيم as “writing” or “inscription.”

That choice is linguistically defensible and follows one established reading of the word.

Choosing “inscription” does not, however, conclusively establish the historical identity of raqīm.

If the base translation is to retain more uncertainty about the referent, “the companions of the Cave and Raqīm” is also possible, with the lexical meaning explained in the word box or root study.

If “inscription” is retained, the explanation should make clear that it follows an established lexical sense rather than a historically demonstrated tablet of a particular material, location, and content.

4. Marqūm in Al-Muṭaffifīn

The other two occurrences of R Q M are in Al-Muṭaffifīn.

The expression has the same structure in both:

كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ

Their contexts, however, differ.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

Verse text:

كَلَّا إِنَّ كِتَابَ الْفُجَّارِ لَفِي سِجِّينٍ

kallā inna kitāba al-fujjāri la-fī sijjīnin

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

Translation:

“No indeed; the record of the wicked is surely in Sijjīn.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8

Verse text:

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا سِجِّينٌ

wa-mā adrāka mā sijjīnun

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8

Translation:

“And what has made you know what Sijjīn is?”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

Verse text:

كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ

kitābun marqūmun

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

Translation:

“An inscribed record.”

This sequence mentions the record of the wicked and then a kitāb marqūm.

مَرْقُوم is a passive participle: something upon which marking or inscription has been performed.

Alongside kitāb, the description concerns being recorded and written.

In its direct sense, kitāb marqūm can be understood as “a recorded document,” “a written book,” or “a marked inscription.”

Marqūm itself does not yet tell us what has been recorded, who wrote it, what instrument was used, or on what material it was written.

The subject must be understood from the context.

The record of the wicked and the recording of deeds

The opening of Al-Muṭaffifīn concerns those who give short measure: they take their own due in full but give less when measuring or weighing for others.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:4 asks about their being raised, while Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:6 speaks of people standing before the Lord of all worlds.

The record of the wicked is then introduced.

This connection places the passage within the recording and accounting of human deeds, rather than an ordinary document unrelated to conduct.

The idea of recording deeds, however, comes from the context as a whole. R Q M does not itself mean sin, crime, or punishment.

Marqūm describes the record’s inscribed state; its association with the wicked is conveyed by الْفُجَّار.

Does marqūm mean sealed?

Some lexical and interpretive explanations render marqūm as written, designated, or even sealed.

Yet raqm and khatm are two distinct Arabic root families.

A document may be both recorded and sealed, but these are different acts.

“A sealed book” is therefore appropriate for kitāb marqūm only where the context or a particular lexical witness provides sufficient support. The more direct and transparent meaning is written or recorded.

Interpretive explanations also say that this writing is not forgotten or erased. That conclusion can be considered in a broader discussion of accountability, but neither the mafʿūl pattern nor R Q M alone makes permanent immutability part of the lexical definition.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

Verse text:

كَلَّا إِنَّ كِتَابَ الْأَبْرَارِ لَفِي عِلِّيِّينَ

kallā inna kitāba al-abrāri la-fī ʿilliyyīna

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

Translation:

“No indeed; the record of the righteous is surely in ʿIlliyyīn.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

Verse text:

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا عِلِّيُّونَ

wa-mā adrāka mā ʿilliyyūna

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

Translation:

“And what has made you know what ʿIlliyyūn is?”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

Verse text:

كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ

kitābun marqūmun

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

Translation:

“An inscribed record,”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21

Verse text:

يَشْهَدُهُ الْمُقَرَّبُونَ

yashhaduhu al-muqarrabūna

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21

Translation:

“which those brought near witness.”

Here the same expression, kitāb marqūm, concerns the record of the righteous.

The important difference is that the first passage associates the record with الْفُجَّار and Sijjīn, while the second associates it with الْأَبْرَار and ʿIlliyyīn.

Lexically, marqūm retains the same sense of recorded or written in both cases.

The root therefore has no inherently positive or negative moral value. Both the wicked’s record and the righteous’s record are described as marqūm.

This parallel shows that being marqūm concerns recording, not the moral value of the recorded content.

The syntax of kitāb marqūm

In both verses, مَرْقُومٌ is an adjective modifying كِتَابٌ, agreeing with it in case and indefiniteness.

In both passages, kitāb marqūm follows the question “And what has made you know?”

Different grammatical and interpretive analyses take kitāb marqūm either as explaining Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyīn or as referring back to the record mentioned before the question.

Either way, marqūm denotes being written and recorded.

This disagreement should not become a claim that R Q M itself specifies the physical location of the records, a level of the heavens, or a particular place where deeds are stored.

Such details cannot be extracted from the lexical meaning of marqūm.

Does marqūm mean numbered?

In contemporary Persian, raqam commonly concerns digits and numbers. Kitāb marqūm might therefore readily be understood as “a numbered book.”

That rendering, however, restricts the word’s meaning without justification.

Arabic raqm encompasses lines, patterns, marks, and writing. Numbering can be a kind of marking, but the verses in Al-Muṭaffifīn make no separate mention of page numbers, file numbers, numerical sequences, or numerical encoding.

“A recorded document” or “an inscribed record” is therefore closer to the verse’s semantic field than “a numbered book.”

Nor should this word alone be used to derive a theory about hidden numerical systems, special encoding, or mathematical calculations in the records of deeds.

This Qur’anic use concerns written recording; any further claim requires independent evidence.

The two records and human responsibility

Elsewhere, Al-Kahf 18:49 describes the record being set out and the offenders’ reaction to it.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:29 likewise speaks of a record that addresses people truthfully and is connected with the recording of their deeds.

These verses matter for understanding the Qur’anic network of recording and accountability.

Nevertheless, the full concepts of accounting, recompense, testimony, and responsibility should not be placed inside the lexical meaning of marqūm.

R Q M concerns recording and marking in these scenes; moral responsibility and human outcomes are conveyed through other statements and the wider set of accountability passages.

5. Comparing R Q M with related roots

R Q M (raqm) and R Q Q (riqqah)

These roots resemble one another in writing but have different meanings.

R Q Q primarily concerns thinness and softness. رَقّ in Al-Ṭūr 52:3 denotes thin skin prepared for writing.

R Q M, by contrast, concerns making lines, patterns, and records on a surface.

Raqq can therefore be the writing material, while raqm denotes the act or result of making marks on it.

This distinction matters in Qur’anic vocabulary: writing skin and a recorded inscription may occur together, but the material’s name is not identical to the name of the act of recording.

R Q M (raqm) and K T B (katb)

The Arabic root K T B concerns writing and bringing parts together.

Kitāb can denote writing or a body of written material, and has further uses in the Qur’an.

In kitāb marqūm, the two root families appear together.

Kitāb identifies the writing or document itself; marqūm describes its being recorded or marked.

The combination shows that two words can occupy a shared semantic field without being completely synonymous.

It should not yield an absolute rule that every kitāb is merely a collection without recording, or that every marqūm must have been produced by a method different from writing.

R Q M (raqm) and S Ṭ R (saṭr)

Saṭr concerns a line or row of writing.

Al-Ṭūr 52:2 uses كِتَابٍ مَسْطُورٍ: a book written in lines.

Masṭūr relates to writing in lines, while marqūm belongs to marking and recording. This association does not establish a mandatory technical distinction between two writing methods.

Both descriptions can apply to one document while highlighting different aspects of it.

In each case, the adjective must be understood through its verse and sentence structure; neither should be treated as invariably a more advanced stage than the other.

R Q M (raqm) and R Q Sh (raqsh)

R Q Sh concerns varied designs and lines in Arabic.

Raqsh is associated with decorative patterns, and رَقْشَاء is used for an animal with colored spots or markings.

This field overlaps with some uses of raqm.

Semantic similarity does not make the two roots or their histories identical.

Raqsh also concerns embellishing or manipulating speech in some usages; that feature should not be transferred without evidence to all uses of raqm.

R Q M (raqm) and Kh T M (khatm)

Khatm belongs to the field of sealing, bringing to an end, and closing.

Sealing a document may concern its authentication, closure, or protection, but such results do not follow from raqm itself.

Kitāb marqūm in Al-Muṭaffifīn concerns the written record, not necessarily its sealing.

This distinction prevents “sealed” from replacing “recorded” without evidence.

6. Interpretive challenges and translation proposals for Forqan

Three important limits should be observed when understanding this root.

First, raqm should not be restricted to number. Digits and numbers are prominent in modern usage, but Qur’anic raqīm and marqūm concern writing and recording.

Second, the lexical meaning of raqīm should not be equated with conclusive identification of its referent. Writing or a tablet has linguistic support, while a proper place name also appears in early accounts. The Qur’an itself does not establish the historical details of these possibilities.

Third, marqūm should not be restricted to an eternal record, a sealed or numbered document, or a record with a specific numerical structure. Such features require separate evidence.

Translation proposals

الرَّقِيمِ — Al-Kahf 18:9

Possible renderings include “Raqīm,” “the Inscription,” or “the inscribed tablet.”

“Raqīm” preserves uncertainty about the referent. “The Inscription” is also defensible when emphasizing the possible lexical sense.

Forqan’s current Persian rendering says “the companions of the Cave and the Inscription.” It may be retained provided the lexical explanation does not confuse choosing “inscription” with conclusively identifying Raqīm historically.

مَرْقُومٌ — Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

Suitable renderings include “recorded,” “written,” and “inscribed.”

“An inscribed record” is a natural and clear rendering of kitāb marqūm.

مَرْقُومٌ — Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

The same equivalents are valid here.

Because the Arabic construction is identical in both places, the base translation should preferably remain consistent unless there is a specific linguistic reason for a difference.

Proposals for Forqan word boxes

Root: R Q M

Word: وَالرَّقِيمِ — Al-Kahf 18:9

Meaning in this verse: And Raqīm; possibly an inscription or tablet associated with the Companions of the Cave, whose historical identity is uncertain.

Word: مَرْقُومٌ — Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

Meaning in this verse: Written or recorded; describing the record of the wicked.

Word: مَرْقُومٌ — Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

Meaning in this verse: Written or recorded; describing the record of the righteous.

These brief explanations concern the particular Qur’anic uses. Details about patterned cloth, animal markings, the history of numbering, and disagreements over Raqīm’s identity remain in the root study.

7. Synthesis and final definition

R Q M forms an Arabic family of meanings concerning lines, designs, marks, and writing.

In early usage, raqm concerns patterned cloth, writing and marking books, identifying goods, and describing natural markings on some animals. This breadth shows that the root’s central meaning extends beyond numbers and digits.

The family occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Raqīm in Al-Kahf 18:9 belongs to the Cave account. Writing or something inscribed has lexical support, but the verse itself does not determine Raqīm’s precise identity.

Marqūm describes kitāb in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20. Both instances concern recording and writing; the distinction between the wicked’s record and the righteous’s record comes from context and other descriptions, not from the root itself.

Final definition of R Q M:

Its principal field is making a line, design, or mark on something and recording through recognizable signs. The sign may be an image, decorative pattern, identifying mark, letter, inscription, or number.

In Qur’anic usage, raqīm is lexically associated with writing or an inscribed object, although its referent in the Cave account remains uncertain; marqūm describes something bearing writing or a recorded mark.

Understanding and translating the root therefore requires distinguishing a pattern or sign, the act of recording, the resulting inscription, and a number. All can belong to one semantic family, but none can replace the others in every use.

In the Qur’anic passages, marqūm most transparently means recorded or written; for raqīm, the lexical sense of writing should be retained with caution about its historical referent.
