1. Root Meaning and Word Structure

Arabic sarmad (سَرْمَد) describes something that continues without a break or interruption during the period in question. It is especially used of time, night, and day: a night continuing without interruption, or a day that does not give way to night.

Al-ʿAyn explains sarmad through the duration and continuity of time. In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, al-Jawharī defines it as al-dāʾim (الدَّائِمُ), “lasting” or “continuing.”

Ibn Manẓūr writes in Lisān al-ʿArab: “السَّرْمَدُ: دَوَامُ الزَّمَانِ مِنْ لَيْلٍ أَوْ نَهَارٍ”—the continuance of time, whether night or day.

He also adds: “وَلَيْلٌ سَرْمَدٌ: طَوِيلٌ”—a night described as sarmad is a long night.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: continuous time and a long night

These explanations establish an important point. Sarmad concerns duration and continuation, but Arabic usage does not invariably mean absolute infinity. Even a night experienced as very long can receive this description.

The word’s meaning should therefore be distinguished from philosophical claims about endless time.

In ordinary usage, sarmad emphasizes the continuity of a condition that does not undergo the usual interruption or give way to another condition. How long that continuance lasts is clarified by the word’s use or its accompanying qualifications.

In the Qur’an, sarmadan (سَرْمَدًا) describes night once and day once. Both occurrences include ilā yawmi al-qiyāmah (إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ), “until the Day of Resurrection,” specifying the imagined duration.

These Qur’anic occurrences thus describe a hypothetical continuance up to the temporal limit stated in the sentence, rather than beginninglessness or necessary absolute endlessness. The expression alone also gives no account of the condition of night and day after the Resurrection.

2. Morphological Origin and the Proposed Connection with S R D

Qur’anic morphological indexes list sarmad under the quadriliteral entry S R M D (س ر م د). Early lexicographers and grammarians nevertheless disagreed about whether its mīm is an original radical or an added letter.

Ibn Fāris: Connection with Sard

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris connects sarmad with the triliteral root S R D (س ر د).

He writes:

السَّرْمَدُ: الدَّائِمُ، وَالْمِيمُ فِيهِ زَائِدَةٌ، وَهُوَ مِنْ سَرَدَ، إِذَا وَصَلَ، فَكَأَنَّهُ زَمَانٌ مُتَّصِلٌ بَعْضُهُ بِبَعْضٍ

al-sarmadu: al-dāʾimu, wa-l-mīmu fīhi zāʾidatun, wa-huwa min sarada, idhā waṣala, fa-ka-annahu zamānun muttaṣilun baʿḍuhu bi-baʿḍin

That is: sarmad means “lasting”; its mīm is additional, and it comes from sarada, “to join,” as though it were time whose parts are connected to one another.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, vol.3, p.160: the analysis of an added mīm

This account rests on an attested meaning of S R D: joining and following consecutively.

In Arabic, sarada can mean bringing something forth in succession. Sard al-ḥadīth (سَرْدُ الْحَدِيثِ) can concern continuous narration, while sard in armor making concerns the ordered connection of its components.

The Qur’an likewise uses al-sard in Sabaʾ 34:11 in the context of making armor.

Ibn Fāris’s explanation therefore has a clear semantic connection: as armor rings or parts of speech follow one another, time described as sarmad continues without a gap.

This semantic connection does not, however, by itself establish that sarmad historically derives from sarada.

Another View: An Independent Quadriliteral

Other grammarians instead regard mīm as an original letter of the word.

On this reading, sarmad follows the pattern faʿlal (فَعْلَل), rather than faʿmal (فَعْمَل).

In Al-Durr al-Maṣūn, al-Samīn al-Ḥalabī considers an original mīm morphologically more apparent, because inserting mīm in the middle of such a word is not a regular, productive pattern.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn, vol.8, p.692: the two morphological analyses

Accordingly, the resemblance between sarmad and sard can represent a semantic relationship without establishing direct derivation.

The disagreement should be preserved. Two distinct questions are involved: the meaning of sarmad, which the lexicons explain clearly, and the historical formation of its structure, which remains disputed.

Continuity and uninterrupted duration do not depend on proving that mīm is added. Whether sarmad derives from sard or is an independent quadriliteral, its attested use still denotes duration and continuity.

Linguistic Forms

The basic form is sarmad, used as a noun or description.

سَرْمَد — sarmad: lasting, continuous, and ongoing, especially of time and conditions extending without interruption.

سَرْمَدًا — sarmadan: the accusative form and the only form used in the Qur’an.

سَرْمَدِيّ — sarmadī: a relational adjective formed from sarmad, associated with duration or enduring continuity.

سَرْمَدِيَّة — sarmadiyyah: an abstract noun also used in philosophical and theological discussion.

Later Arabic also records the verb sarmada for persisting in and continuing an activity without interruption. Dozy cites yusarmidu al-ṣawma (يُسَرْمِدُ الصَّوْمَ), concerning continuous fasting. This witness alone establishes neither the general meaning “make anything permanent” nor the verb’s currency in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

Dozy’s Supplément: the witness concerning continuous fasting

Only sarmadan occurs in the Qur’an. No verb, plural, or relational adjective from this family appears there.

3. Early Arabic Evidence and Continuance

An important pre-Islamic poetic witness is a verse from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s Muʿallaqah:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا أَمْرِي عَلَيَّ بِغُمَّةٍ
نَهَارِي وَلَا لَيْلِي عَلَيَّ بِسَرْمَدِ

la-ʿamruka mā amrī ʿalayya bi-ghummatin / nahārī wa-lā laylī ʿalayya bi-sarmadi

“By your life, my affairs are not obscure to me by day, nor does my night become unending and exceedingly long for me.”

The poet speaks of decisiveness and emerging from difficulty. He presents himself as someone who does not remain trapped in prolonged uncertainty and bewilderment when facing problems.

Wa-lā laylī ʿalayya bi-sarmadi, “nor is my night unending for me,” evokes a night experienced as very long through worry, suffering, or uncertainty.

Early commentaries on the Muʿallaqah explain sarmad here as lasting and uninterrupted, without taking the poet to mean that a person’s night actually continues to infinity.

Al-Zawzanī’s Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Sabʿ: explanation of Ṭarafah’s verse

He describes the experience of a lengthened night and a continuing difficult condition.

The witness matters because it places sarmad in pre-Qur’anic Arabic poetry and associates it with a condition’s continuing and becoming prolonged.

Continuity is clear in this use, but the actual duration is not specified by the word alone.

Lexical explanations also record laylun sarmadun (لَيْلٌ سَرْمَدٌ) for a very long night.

Some sources also record abadan samadan sarmadan (أَبَدًا سَمَدًا سَرْمَدًا) as an emphatic expression of duration and continuance. The proximity of samad and sarmad is noteworthy, but their juxtaposition does not establish the historical derivation of one from the other.

Al-Mughrib: the emphatic expression abadan samadan sarmadan

The entry S M D has other uses as well; they should not all be transferred into sarmad.

Ṭarafah’s verse and the lexical accounts thus agree on a central point: sarmad describes a condition continuing without interruption during the period in question.

Is Sarmad Used Only of Time?

The best-known lexical uses concern time, night, and day.

Al-Ṭabarī nevertheless notes that Arabic can also apply the description to continuous conditions such as ease, affliction, or blessing.

Al-Ṭabarī: continuous ease, affliction, and blessing

This extension is understandable: being sarmad expresses a condition’s continuity rather than the kind of thing it is.

For example, when ease or hardship continues without interruption, its continuity can be described in this way.

That does not mean sarmad intrinsically names ease, suffering, or blessing.

The word expresses continuation; the noun it describes identifies the condition continuing.

Its temporal use likewise does not establish that anything called sarmad must have existed since the beginning of the world or must survive its end.

Such claims depend on the time span, the subject, and the sentence’s context.

4. Qur’anic Usage: Continuous Night and Day

S R M D occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times as sarmadan in consecutive verses of Al-Qaṣaṣ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences of sarmadan

The verses have almost identical structures, but one supposes continuous night and the other continuous day.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71: Continuous Night without Daylight

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ جَعَلَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمُ اللَّيْلَ سَرْمَدًا إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ مَنْ إِلَٰهٌ غَيْرُ اللَّهِ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِضِيَاءٍ أَفَلَا تَسْمَعُونَ

qul araʾaytum in jaʿala Allāhu ʿalaykumu al-layla sarmadan ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati man ilāhun ghayru Allāhi yaʾtīkum bi-ḍiyāʾin a-fa-lā tasmaʿūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Say: Have you considered—if The God made night continuous over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than The God would bring you light? Will you not hear?”

Sarmadan describes the condition of night in this verse.

Jaʿala Allāhu ʿalaykumu al-layla sarmadan describes a hypothetical situation in which night remains continuously present.

If jaʿala means “make into a condition,” al-layla, “night,” is its first object and sarmadan its second: to make night continuous.

In another grammatical analysis, if jaʿala means “create,” sarmadan can be a circumstantial qualifier of al-layla: creating night in a continuous condition.

The principal meaning remains the same in both readings: a night continuing without interruption.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: sarmadan as second object or circumstantial qualifier

The Importance of “Until the Day of Resurrection”

Sarmadan is followed by ilā yawmi al-qiyāmah, “until the Day of Resurrection.”

This phrase specifies the temporal span of the hypothetical situation.

The qualification presents the continuance within the hypothetical frame “until the Day of Resurrection.” It does not establish necessary absolute endlessness; equally, merely stating this limit does not independently report the ending of night or day after that point.

Understanding sarmad as unbroken continuance makes the sentence clear: suppose night continued from the time in question until the Day of Resurrection without giving way to day.

Being sarmad thus concerns uninterrupted presence during the stated period, rather than denying every beginning and ending.

Ilā, “until,” has a real function here. It expresses extension up to a stated temporal limit and should not be ignored in translation or analysis. Expressing extension up to a limit does not, by itself, independently describe events beyond that limit.

Light in Contrast with Continuous Night

The verse then asks what god other than The God could bring people ḍiyāʾ, “light.”

Ḍiyāʾ (ضِيَاء) concerns light and illumination. Here the light people need is set against continuous night.

Sarmad itself does not mean darkness. It expresses continuation; al-layl, “night,” supplies the subject of that continuation.

Were day made sarmad, the same word would describe daylight and the uninterrupted presence of day.

Sarmad is therefore intrinsically neither dark nor light: it denotes the continuation of the condition named in the sentence.

Here that condition is night. The question about providing light brings out the significance of day replacing night.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72: Continuous Day without Night for Rest

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ جَعَلَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمُ النَّهَارَ سَرْمَدًا إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ مَنْ إِلَٰهٌ غَيْرُ اللَّهِ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِلَيْلٍ تَسْكُنُونَ فِيهِ أَفَلَا تُبْصِرُونَ

qul araʾaytum in jaʿala Allāhu ʿalaykumu al-nahāra sarmadan ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati man ilāhun ghayru Allāhi yaʾtīkum bi-laylin taskunūna fīhi a-fa-lā tubṣirūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Say: Have you considered—if The God made day continuous over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than The God would bring you a night in which to rest? Will you not see?”

The same word and construction recur, but al-nahāra, “day,” replaces al-layla, “night.”

This contrast makes sarmad’s meaning clearer.

The first verse supposes night without day; the second supposes day without night.

Both concern the absence of the usual alternation between the two conditions.

If night is sarmad, day does not replace it. If day is sarmad, night does not replace it.

In this context, sarmad can therefore be explained as the continuation of a temporal condition without its opposite normally succeeding it.

This is more precise than simply “very long.” The verse concerns not merely length of time but the uninterrupted presence of night or day until the Day of Resurrection.

Stillness and Rest

The second hypothetical situation ends with a question about a night in which people can rest: bi-laylin taskunūna fīhi (بِلَيْلٍ تَسْكُنُونَ فِيهِ).

This phrase identifies an important function of night in human life.

Continuous day provides light, but does not replace every function of night.

Likewise, continuous night may provide a setting for rest, but does not replace daylight and the opportunities of day.

The verses concern more than the beauty of alternating night and day: human life depends on differing conditions with distinct functions.

They should nevertheless not be taken to require every person to sleep throughout every night or conduct all earning activities only in daytime.

The verses describe the general functions of night and day, rather than a uniform, unchangeable schedule for every person and every region of the earth.

The Continuing Context in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:73

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:73

وَمِنْ رَحْمَتِهِ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ لِتَسْكُنُوا فِيهِ وَلِتَبْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-min raḥmatihi jaʿala lakumu al-layla wa-l-nahāra li-taskunū fīhi wa-li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“And out of His mercy He made night and day for you, so that you may rest therein, seek of His bounty, and perhaps give thanks.”

This verse gives the outcome of the preceding two questions.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72 present two hypothetical situations: continuous night and continuous day. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:73 speaks of the presence of both and their differing functions.

The uninterrupted presence of night alone or day alone is thus contrasted with an arrangement containing both, from each of which human life benefits.

This should not be taken to mean that continuity itself is always harmful.

Being sarmad has no intrinsically negative meaning. What matters in these hypothetical situations is the absence of the opposite condition and the loss of its functions for people.

Sarmad here names neither affliction nor mercy; it describes continuance. Its consequences depend on what continues and on the human needs identified by the context.

5. Comparison with Abad, Khulūd, Dawām, and Sard

Sarmad and Abad

Abad (أَبَد) concerns temporal continuation and the scope of the future.

The Qur’an uses abadan (أَبَدًا), particularly in affirmative and negative sentences, to express the continuation or denial of a condition throughout the future in question.

Sarmad, however, describes night and day in its two Qur’anic uses, emphasizing their uninterrupted presence.

The important difference is the focus of emphasis.

Abadan often expresses a future time span for an action or judgment. Sarmadan in Al-Qaṣaṣ expresses how a condition continues without interruption or replacement.

Both can concern long or uninterrupted duration; that proximity does not make them interchangeable in every construction.

The qualifications accompanying abadan must also be recognized. Just as abadan mā dāmū fīhā (أَبَدًا مَا دَامُوا فِيهَا) in Al-Māʾidah 5:24 qualifies continuation by a group’s presence in a particular place, “until the Day of Resurrection” in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72 specifies the span of the hypothetical continuance.

In both cases the qualification contributes to the sentence’s full meaning and should not be omitted because a word of duration is present.

Study of ʾ B D

Sarmad and Khulūd

Khulūd (خُلُود) concerns remaining and not leaving a condition or place in Arabic.

In the Qur’an, this family is especially used of people remaining in the Garden or the Fire.

Khulūd and sarmad overlap in that both can concern continuing and remaining.

Khulūd more often emphasizes something or someone remaining in a condition, whereas Qur’anic sarmad highlights a temporal condition’s continuity.

This should not become an absolute rule restricting khulūd to people and sarmad to time. Arabic evidence does not support such exclusivity.

More precisely, the Qur’anic uses of the two families differ; the definition of one should not be imposed on the other without evidence.

Study of KH L D

Sarmad and Dawām

Dawām (دوام), “continuance,” is the closest recorded lexical equivalent of sarmad.

Lexicographers such as al-Jawharī and al-Zajjāj define sarmad as al-dāʾim, “lasting.”

Dawām can, however, describe the continuation of an action, condition, or thing in various settings.

Qur’anic sarmad accompanies night and day with an emphasis on absence of interruption.

Persian equivalents include peyvasteh, “continuous,” hamīshegī, “permanent,” or bedūn-e vaqfeh, “without interruption”; the choice should convey the complete sentence accurately.

Sarmad and Sard

Attested Arabic uses of sard concern continuity and components following one another.

Ibn Fāris uses this meaning to explain the formation of sarmad.

His proposed connection is understandable: time whose moments follow without gaps can be imagined as a chain of connected parts.

Qur’anic sard itself, however, occurs in the context of armor and connected components, rather than continuous night and day.

Even if derivation of sarmad from sard is accepted, each word’s meaning in its own sentence must be examined independently.

The connection of armor rings and the continuity of time are distinct applications. A possible shared conception of connection does not make their referents identical.

Sarmad and the Philosophical Concept of Sarmadiyyah

In later philosophy and theology, sarmad and sarmadiyyah sometimes describe existence without beginning or end, or existence conceived as outside time altogether.

Lane citing Al-Taʿrīfāt: the technical meaning of sarmadī

This technical meaning belongs to discussions of beginninglessness, endlessness, and the relationship between existence and time.

The philosophical meaning of sarmadiyyah should not be imposed on Qur’anic sarmadan.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72, night and day are created conditions whose hypothetical continuation until the Day of Resurrection is considered.

Neither verse describes a night existing from eternity or a day outside time.

These occurrences therefore do not, by themselves, establish the philosophical definition “existence without beginning or end.”

Their direct meaning is continuance and continuity, whose span the sentence specifies.

6. Conclusion and Suggested Translation

Arabic sarmad denotes continuous duration, especially of night, day, and temporal conditions extending without interruption.

Early sources explain it both as lasting and unbroken and as describing a very long night.

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetic witness places the word in pre-Qur’anic Arabic, where it described a night’s prolongation and continuation.

There are two morphological analyses. Ibn Fāris treats mīm as additional and connects the word with sard, “joining.” Other grammarians regard mīm as original and sarmad as an independent quadriliteral.

The disagreement does not fundamentally alter the word’s clear Qur’anic meaning.

S R M D occurs twice in the Qur’an and only as sarmadan.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71 supposes continuous night, which does not give way to daylight until the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72 supposes continuous day, which night does not replace until the Day of Resurrection.

The two uses emphasize a condition’s uninterrupted presence and the absence of its usual alternation with its opposite.

“Until the Day of Resurrection” in both verses also makes clear that being sarmad does not itself entail beginninglessness or absolute endlessness.

Brief root meaning: Continuous duration and unbroken continuance; a condition continuing without a break or interruption during the period in question.

Qur’anic forms and suggested translation:

سَرْمَدًا — sarmadan, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:71: A night continuing uninterrupted until the Day of Resurrection, without day replacing it. Suggested translation: “continuous.”

سَرْمَدًا — sarmadan, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:72: A day continuing uninterrupted until the Day of Resurrection, without night replacing it. Suggested translation: “continuous.”

For both verses, Persian peyvasteh gardānad, “make continuous,” can naturally convey jaʿala together with sarmadan.

“Permanent” is also lexically close. Since both verses explicitly say “until the Day of Resurrection,” however, “continuous” avoids implying absolute infinity without weakening the word’s force or genuine continuity.

This study’s central finding is that sarmad denotes unbroken duration, rather than necessarily an existence without beginning or end.

What is made sarmad continues and retains its uninterrupted condition during the stated period. Its beginning, ending, and whether its continuation is desirable must be established from context.

Continuous night and continuous day in Al-Qaṣaṣ are contrasting hypothetical situations revealing the importance of both conditions in human life. The next verse identifies their presence as an expression of divine mercy.

The root’s meaning is therefore not identical with darkness, light, harm, or mercy. Its core is continuity and continuance; the sentence identifies what continues and its consequences.
