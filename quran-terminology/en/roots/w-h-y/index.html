The Arabic root و ح ي concerns conveying a message, meaning, or command from one party to another. This may take place through speech, gesture, writing, sound, or an imparted meaning. In many uses, the mode of transmission is not apparent to others or differs from ordinary conversation.

The Quran uses this root across a broad semantic field. The God reveals to Prophets; revelation is given to Moses’ mother and Jesus’ disciples; Zechariah signals to his people; The God communicates to bees and the earth; and even devils communicate to one another or their allies.

This breadth establishes a basic distinction: waḥy does not in itself mean a divine message or heavenly scripture. The sender, recipient, content, and setting of each verse determine whether the communication is divine revelation, a human gesture, guidance within creation, or a deceptive message.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing its linguistic meaning from its particular use for prophetic revelation, while recognizing the reality and distinctive standing of divine revelation in the Quran.

1. The root in early Arabic

Early Arabic evidence shows at least two clear uses: conveying meaning through signs or sounds, and writing or marks that record a message.

Waḥy as writing and inscription

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, a poet who lived before and after the advent of Islam, includes:

فَمَدَافِعُ الرَّيَّانِ عُرِّيَ رَسْمُهَا

fa-madāfiʿu al-rayyāni ʿurriya rasmuhā

خَلَقًا كَمَا ضَمِنَ الوُحِيَّ سِلامُهَا

khalaqan ka-mā ḍamina al-wuḥiyya silāmuhā

“The worn traces at the watercourses of al-Rayyān lie exposed, like inscriptions held within their stones.”

The poet likens the remaining traces of former encampments to inscriptions carved in stone. Time has worn the sites’ marks, yet they remain visible, like writing preserved on stone.

Here الْوُحِيّ refers to writings or recorded marks, while سِلَام refers to stones.

This witness shows that the family was not confined in poetry associated with the pre-Islamic period to receiving heavenly messages or inward impressions. Writing that records and conveys meaning also belonged to it.

Poetry attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā likewise includes:

لِمَنِ الدِّيَارُ غَشِيتُهَا بِالفَدْفَدِ

li-mani al-diyāru ghashītuhā bi-al-fadfadi

كَالوَحْيِ فِي حَجَرِ المَسِيلِ المُخْلِدِ

ka-al-waḥyi fī ḥajari al-masīli al-mukhlidi

“Whose dwellings are these that I visited on the high, open ground, their traces like writing on the enduring stone of a watercourse?”

Here too, the traces of an encampment are compared to writing on stone.

Both witnesses support the use of waḥy for writing and recorded marks in the poetic tradition attributed to pre-Islamic times. Their transmission in old collections alone does not establish the precise date of each verse’s composition.

Waḥy as conveying a sign or message

Poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah, a pre-Islamic poet, includes:

يُوحِي إِلَيْهَا بِإِنْقَاضٍ وَنَقْنَقَةٍ

yūḥī ilayhā bi-inqāḍin wa-naqnaqatin

كَمَا تَرَاطَنُ فِي أَفْدَانِهَا الرُّومُ

ka-mā tarāṭanu fī afdānihā al-rūmu

“He communicates to her with distinctive calls and cries, as the Romans speak their unfamiliar tongue in their palaces.”

This passage describes a male ostrich’s behavior and communication with its mate.

إِنْقَاض and نَقْنَقَة concern the bird’s distinctive sounds. The poet compares them to people speaking a language unfamiliar to the listener.

Here يُوحِي describes conveying a message through sound.

Communication need not involve human words and sentences. The bird communicates with its mate through calls, and the poet describes this with يُوحِي.

The comparison does not establish that birds possess spoken language structured identically to human language. What matters for the root is the poet’s use of يُوحِي for communication through acoustic signs.

What the historical evidence establishes

These witnesses show two recognizable uses:

First, writing or marks that record and convey meaning; second, communicating meaning through sounds or signs.

Arabic lexicons also record pointing, sending messages, speaking in a way concealed from others, and imparting meaning.

Some lexical explanations identify concealment and speed as features of waḥy. These should not be made definitive requirements of every historical usage without examination.

An inscription on stone is not necessarily hidden. A gesture addressed to a group may also be made in others’ sight.

Thus, a defensible historical description connects waḥy with conveying or recording meaning and messages through various means, including gestures, sounds, writing, and concealed communication.

These witnesses do not establish speed or concealment as the first and sole historical meaning of every branch.

2. Forms and the semantic core

Important Arabic forms include:

وَحَى: To communicate, signal, or write; a verb recorded in early Arabic.

وَحْي: Communication or imparting meaning; in some historical uses, writing or a recorded text. In its particular religious use, it also expresses divine revelation.

أَوْحَى: To convey or impart a message, meaning, or command. Depending on context, it can describe divine revelation, a human gesture, or another kind of communication.

إِيحَاء: Conveying or imparting meaning; the verbal noun of أَوْحَى.

يُوحِي: “Communicates” or “reveals”; an active imperfect form.

يُوحَى: “Is revealed” or “is conveyed”; a passive imperfect form.

أُوحِيَ: “Was revealed”; a passive perfect form.

وَاحٍ: An active participle recorded for one who communicates or writes in lexical usage.

مُوحًى: Something revealed or conveyed.

These are recognized Arabic forms, but they do not all occur in the Quran.

Forms actually found in the Quran

The Quran uses this root in two main formations: the form IV verb and the noun وَحْي. There are 78 occurrences: 72 verbal forms and six nouns.

Verbal forms include أَوْحَى, أَوْحَيْنَا, أَوْحَيْتُ, يُوحِي, نُوحِي, يُوحَى, and أُوحِيَ.

The noun occurs in forms such as وَحْيٌ, وَحْيًا, وَحْيِنَا, وَحْيُهُ, and بِالْوَحْيِ.

Differences in prefixes, attached pronouns, tense, and voice do not necessarily create independent lexical derivatives.

The Quran uses this family’s verb in form IV. This does not mean that the basic form did not exist in Arabic or had an entirely different meaning.

The semantic core

Taken together, the historical witnesses and Quranic uses support the following account:

Conveying or imparting a message, meaning, or command to a recipient, through speech, gesture, writing, signs, or a mode other than ordinary spoken communication, depending on the setting.

The transmission of meaning is central to this description.

The sender may be human or, in Quranic usage, The God or another agent.

The recipient may be a human, angel, animal, or another being.

The content may be information, a command, guidance, or deceptive speech.

Neither a divine sender nor the truth of the message is therefore an invariant part of the root’s lexical meaning.

3. Divine revelation to Prophets

One of the root’s principal Quranic uses is The God’s revelation to Prophets.

Here The God is the sender and the Prophet receives divine knowledge; in the role of Messenger, the recipient is commissioned to convey the message.

Revelation to a human being in the prophetic role

Fuṣṣilat 41:6 states:

قُلْ إِنَّمَا أَنَا بَشَرٌ مِثْلُكُمْ يُوحَىٰ إِلَيَّ أَنَّمَا إِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ فَاسْتَقِيمُوا إِلَيْهِ وَاسْتَغْفِرُوهُ وَوَيْلٌ لِلْمُشْرِكِينَ

qul innamā anā basharun mithlukum yūḥā ilayya annamā ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥidun fa-istaqīmū ilayhi wa-istaghfirūhu wa-waylun li-al-mushrikīn

Fuṣṣilat 41:6

“Say: I am only a human being like you, to whom it is revealed that your deity is one deity. So be upright toward Him and seek His forgiveness. And woe to those who associate partners.”

Muhammad is instructed to identify himself as a human being like other people.

The distinction emphasized is his reception of revelation.

يُوحَى is passive: the message is revealed to him. Its content follows in the sentence: the oneness of the deity.

This use presents revelation in the Messenger’s mission as receiving a message originating from The God, rather than the recipient’s own imagination or wishes.

Al-Najm 53:4 states:

إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا وَحْيٌ يُوحَىٰ

in huwa illā waḥyun yūḥā

Al-Najm 53:4

“It is nothing but revelation being revealed.”

This follows the passage about Muhammad and the relation of his speech to personal desire.

وَحْيٌ is a noun, and يُوحَى a passive verb from the same root.

The noun and verb together emphasize the revealed character of what is under discussion.

The verse must nevertheless be read in context. It does not establish that every statement later attributed to Muhammad is divine revelation without historical and Quranic examination. Establishing a message’s revealed character and establishing a historical report’s authentic attribution are different questions.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:163 compares revelation to Muhammad with revelation to Noah and other Prophets. Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and others are named there.

This continuity shows that divine revelation to Prophets is not unique to Muhammad.

Revelation and the Messenger’s mission

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:25 states that earlier Messengers also received the message of the deity’s oneness.

Al-Shūrā 42:7 identifies an Arabic Quran as what is revealed to Muhammad and describes the purpose of warning people.

In Al-Anʿām 6:19, Muhammad likewise says that this Quran was revealed to him so that he might warn his audience and whomever it reaches.

In the Messenger’s mission, these verses present revelation as more than a private experience: the received message is to reach its audience.

Revelation and delivery nevertheless do not mean the same thing.

Revelation concerns a message’s reception or transmission from its source. Delivery concerns conveying it to the audience of the mission.

The Messenger receives revelation and then delivers it. These acts may form one sequence, but they should not be treated as lexically identical.

Revelation and The God’s communication with human beings

A foundational verse is Al-Shūrā 42:51:

وَمَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُكَلِّمَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَّا وَحْيًا أَوْ مِنْ وَرَاءِ حِجَابٍ أَوْ يُرْسِلَ رَسُولًا فَيُوحِيَ بِإِذْنِهِ مَا يَشَاءُ إِنَّهُ عَلِيٌّ حَكِيمٌ

wa-mā kāna li-basharin an yukallimahu allāhu illā waḥyan aw min warāʾi ḥijābin aw yursila rasūlan fa-yūḥiya bi-idhnihi mā yashāʾu innahu ʿaliyyun ḥakīm

Al-Shūrā 42:51

“It is not for any human that The God should speak to him except by revelation, or from behind a veil, or by sending a Messenger who then reveals, by His permission, what He wills. Indeed, He is Exalted, Wise.”

Three modes are described: revelation, speaking from behind a veil, and sending a Messenger to convey the message by divine permission.

Here وَحْيًا stands alongside two other modes.

In this construction, waḥy can denote a particular mode, although the Quran also uses أَوْحَى more broadly for transmitting a message through a Messenger.

The broader root meaning should therefore be distinguished from a narrower use within a particular classification.

In فَيُوحِيَ بِإِذْنِهِ, the verb is an imperfect in the subjunctive. On the immediate syntactic reading, its subject is the Messenger sent by The God, who communicates by divine permission.

Mediation is thus compatible with revelation’s divine origin.

The verse does not give a detailed account of this communication’s physical form, the manner of hearing or seeing, or the transmission of a message in the unseen realm.

The lexical meaning of revelation should therefore not be used to deduce a definite material mechanism for divine communication with humans.

4. Other recipients of revelation and human signaling

A central feature of Quranic usage is that receiving waḥy does not in itself establish a prophetic role. The following examples also distinguish the sender, recipient, and kind of communication.

Revelation to Moses’ mother

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7 states:

وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ أُمِّ مُوسَىٰ أَنْ أَرْضِعِيهِ فَإِذَا خِفْتِ عَلَيْهِ فَأَلْقِيهِ فِي الْيَمِّ وَلَا تَخَافِي وَلَا تَحْزَنِي إِنَّا رَادُّوهُ إِلَيْكِ وَجَاعِلُوهُ مِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

wa-awḥaynā ilā ummi mūsā an arḍiʿīhi fa-idhā khifti ʿalayhi fa-alqīhi fī al-yammi wa-lā takhāfī wa-lā taḥzanī innā rāddūhu ilayki wa-jāʿilūhu mina al-mursalīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7

“And We revealed to Moses’ mother: Nurse him. Then, when you fear for him, cast him into the water, and do not fear or grieve. We shall return him to you and make him one of the Messengers.”

The God communicates a message to Moses’ mother.

Its content is more than a vague feeling. It contains specific commands and news of the future: nursing the child, entrusting him to the water when danger arises, not fearing or grieving, and the promise of his return and future mission as a Messenger.

Ṭā Hā 20:38 also refers to this revelation.

These verses, however, do not identify Moses’ mother as a Prophet.

Applying أَوْحَيْنَا to a person does not by itself establish that they held the office of prophethood or were commissioned to proclaim a religious law to people.

Conversely, the clear message should not be reduced to a maternal guess or ordinary feeling: the Quran attributes it to The God and specifies its content.

Its exact mode of reception is not explained. Identifying it definitively as a dream, an angel, an audible voice, or a direct inward communication requires separate evidence.

Revelation to Jesus’ disciples

Al-Māʾidah 5:111 states:

وَإِذْ أَوْحَيْتُ إِلَى الْحَوَارِيِّينَ أَنْ آمِنُوا بِي وَبِرَسُولِي قَالُوا آمَنَّا وَاشْهَدْ بِأَنَّنَا مُسْلِمُونَ

wa-idh awḥaytu ilā al-ḥawāriyyīna an āminū bī wa-bi-rasūlī qālū āmannā wa-ishhad bi-annanā muslimūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:111

“And when I revealed to the disciples: Believe in Me and My Messenger, they said: We believe; bear witness that we are submitters.”

The God uses أَوْحَيْتُ concerning the disciples.

The message commands faith in The God and His Messenger.

The disciples respond by declaring their faith and submission.

This too shows that waḥy is not confined to people identified by the title Prophet or Messenger.

The verse does not specify how they received the message. The verb alone cannot establish inward communication, an angel, or another means.

Revelation to Joseph

In Yūsuf 12:15, as Joseph’s brothers agree to place him in the well, The God says that He revealed to Joseph that he would tell them of this deed while they were unaware. Understanding their unawareness as failure to recognize Joseph is a contextual interpretation, rather than the sole literal meaning of the phrase.

This revelation occurs in a difficult and dangerous situation in Joseph’s life.

The message concerns the future and the outcome of an event.

Divine revelation is therefore not always described as a general religious command or a revealed book. News and reassurance about the future may also be its content.

This occurrence alone does not settle every detail of Joseph’s status or the stages of his life. The verse explicitly states that this particular message was conveyed to him.

Zechariah’s communication to his people

Maryam 19:11 states:

فَخَرَجَ عَلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ مِنَ الْمِحْرَابِ فَأَوْحَىٰ إِلَيْهِمْ أَنْ سَبِّحُوا بُكْرَةً وَعَشِيًّا

fa-kharaja ʿalā qawmihi mina al-miḥrābi fa-awḥā ilayhim an sabbiḥū bukratan wa-ʿashiyyā

Maryam 19:11

“Then he came out to his people from the sanctuary and signaled to them to glorify morning and evening.”

Zechariah is the subject of أَوْحَى here.

This differs from The God’s revelation to Prophets or Moses’ mother: a human communicates to other people.

Maryam 19:10 and Āl ʿImrān 3:41 help explain how.

Zechariah is unable to speak normally to people; Āl ʿImrān 3:41 says إِلَّا رَمْزًا, indicating communication through signs or gestures.

“Signaled” is thus clearer and more appropriate to the context of Maryam 19:11 than “revealed.”

This is important evidence within the Quran itself: أَوْحَى can describe conveying a message through a human gesture.

The verb cannot therefore be assumed always to mean heavenly revelation.

Revelation to angels

In Al-Anfāl 8:12, The God reveals to the angels that He is with them and that they are to strengthen the believers.

The sender is again The God, but the recipients are not human.

The Quran’s recipients of divine revelation therefore extend beyond humanity.

The way angels receive a message cannot be determined from the structure of human language or human communication.

5. Revelation to animals and elements of creation

The Quran also uses this root for beings not ordinarily described as recipients of scripture or religious messages in the human sense.

Revelation to bees

Al-Naḥl 16:68 states:

وَأَوْحَىٰ رَبُّكَ إِلَى النَّحْلِ أَنِ اتَّخِذِي مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا وَمِنَ الشَّجَرِ وَمِمَّا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥli ani ittakhidhī mina al-jibāli buyūtan wa-mina al-shajari wa-mimmā yaʿrishūn

Al-Naḥl 16:68

“And your Lord revealed to the bees: Take homes in the mountains, in the trees, and in what they erect.”

The sender is The God, and the recipient is النَّحْل, the bees.

The content is specified: taking homes in the places named.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 then speaks of eating from fruits and following the Lord’s paths.

These verses relate the bees’ behavior to the Lord’s guidance and command.

Is this revelation identical to that received by Moses or Muhammad?

The Quran does not state such an equivalence.

The bee is not presented as a Prophet receiving scripture for human society. The subject is the behavior and guidance of one of The God’s creatures.

A contemporary understanding may relate this use to living creatures’ capacities, instinctive behavior, and patterns of guidance.

That explanation must not become a definitive lexical claim that waḥy originally means instinct or biological programming.

Instinct may help explain this instance, but the root’s semantic field is broader.

The verse does not describe the bee’s nervous system, genetics, or information transmission in detail. Assigning a specific scientific mechanism to أَوْحَى requires independent evidence.

Revelation within the heavens

Fuṣṣilat 41:12 states:

فَقَضَاهُنَّ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ فِي يَوْمَيْنِ وَأَوْحَىٰ فِي كُلِّ سَمَاءٍ أَمْرَهَا وَزَيَّنَّا السَّمَاءَ الدُّنْيَا بِمَصَابِيحَ وَحِفْظًا ذَٰلِكَ تَقْدِيرُ الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ

fa-qaḍāhunna sabʿa samāwātin fī yawmayni wa-awḥā fī kulli samāʾin amrahā wa-zayyannā al-samāʾa al-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa-ḥifẓan dhālika taqdīru al-ʿazīzi al-ʿalīm

Fuṣṣilat 41:12

“Then He completed them as seven heavens in two days and revealed in each heaven its affair. And We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps, and for protection. That is the determination of the Mighty, the Knowing.”

Here revelation is associated with أَمْرَهَا, the affair of each heaven.

فِي كُلِّ سَمَاءٍ also differs in construction from expressions such as إِلَى النَّحْلِ.

The Quran describes communicating the affair within each heaven, rather than delivering scripture or a religious command to humans.

This use connects the root with the guidance and ordering of creation.

Yet أَوْحَى is not the name of a particular physical law.

This verse alone does not disclose the number of fundamental forces, the precise structure of the heavens, or a scientific mechanism governing them.

It relates the affair and ordering of each heaven to The God’s will and determination.

Revelation to the earth

Al-Zalzalah 99:5 states:

بِأَنَّ رَبَّكَ أَوْحَىٰ لَهَا

bi-anna rabbaka awḥā lahā

Al-Zalzalah 99:5

“Because your Lord has revealed to it.”

The preceding verses describe the earth’s great shaking, its bringing forth its burdens, and its recounting its news.

أَوْحَى لَهَا belongs to that setting.

The preposition here is لَـ rather than إِلَى.

This distinction should be recognized. Arabic allows أَوْحَى with either preposition, but their difference alone does not establish an absolute rule separating two entirely distinct kinds of revelation.

In Al-Zalzalah, revelation to the earth relates to the command and enablement of what the preceding verses describe.

The verse does not explain how the earth receives the message or recounts its news.

The Quranic image should neither be denied nor turned without evidence into a definitive theory of the earth’s biological consciousness or a physical mechanism of speech.

6. Deceptive communication and the word’s ethical scope

The use of this root concerning devils is particularly important for understanding its semantic core.

Devils communicating to one another

Al-Anʿām 6:112 states:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ جَعَلْنَا لِكُلِّ نَبِيٍّ عَدُوًّا شَيَاطِينَ الْإِنْسِ وَالْجِنِّ يُوحِي بَعْضُهُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ زُخْرُفَ الْقَوْلِ غُرُورًا وَلَوْ شَاءَ رَبُّكَ مَا فَعَلُوهُ فَذَرْهُمْ وَمَا يَفْتَرُونَ

wa-kadhālika jaʿalnā li-kulli nabiyyin ʿaduwwan shayāṭīna al-insi wa-al-jinni yūḥī baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin zukhrufa al-qawli ghurūran wa-law shāʾa rabbuka mā faʿalūhu fa-dharhum wa-mā yaftarūn

Al-Anʿām 6:112

“And thus We appointed for every Prophet an enemy: devils among humans and jinn, some communicating to others adorned speech as deception. Had your Lord willed, they would not have done it. So leave them and what they fabricate.”

يُوحِي here concerns devils among humans and jinn.

The message’s content is also specified:

زُخْرُفَ الْقَوْلِ غُرُورًا

zukhrufa al-qawli ghurūrā

Al-Anʿām 6:112

That is, adorned speech used to deceive.

يُوحِي alone therefore makes no judgment about a message’s truth.

The word family used for divine revelation to Prophets is here used for deceptive communication.

The difference lies in the sender, content, and purpose.

Al-Anʿām 6:121 likewise states that devils communicate to their allies so that they may dispute with the believers.

In its broad linguistic use, waḥy can thus convey thought, speech, or a command intended to influence its recipient.

This does not make every negative thought, disagreement, or opposing conversation satanic revelation.

Al-Anʿām 6:112 specifically concerns devils and speech adorned to deceive. Those contextual qualifications cannot be removed to apply the same judgment to every differing statement.

A false claim to revelation

Al-Anʿām 6:93 describes someone claiming to have received revelation when nothing has been revealed to them.

Claiming revelation and actually receiving it are distinct.

A person may claim a divine message while making a false claim.

The word waḥy does not thereby acquire a different meaning; what differs is the truth or falsehood of attributing it to that person.

This matters in studying Quranic language: a word’s occurrence within a human falsehood does not mean the Quran endorses the claim.

Is every waḥy sacred?

The verses give a clear answer.

When The God reveals to Prophets, the message’s source is divine.

When Zechariah signals to his people, the communication is human.

When devils impart deceptive speech to one another, يُوحِي describes communicating falsehood.

The sanctity of divine revelation derives from The God and the truth of His message, rather than from supposing that the root is inherently sacred with every agent and in every use.

This distinction avoids two opposite errors: restricting the whole root to prophetic revelation, and equating divine revelation with every message, inspiration, or thought that comes to mind.

7. Related roots and translation proposals

W Ḥ Y (وَحْي) compared with N Z L (نُزُول)

The root ن ز ل concerns descending and bringing down.

The Quran describes divine scripture using both revelation and descent.

The roots nevertheless do not mean the same thing.

Waḥy emphasizes communicating a message or command; nuzūl presents its descent or being sent down.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:192, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:193, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:194 describe the message’s descent from the Lord of the worlds, its being brought down by the Trustworthy Spirit, and its reception upon Muhammad’s heart.

These verses clarify its route and source within prophetic revelation.

Descent language for the Quran does not mean every waḥy in Arabic is necessarily a book descending from heaven: Zechariah’s gesture and the devils’ communication also use this root.

W Ḥ Y (وَحْي) compared with L H M (إِلْهَام)

Ilhām concerns imparting meaning within a person. Its root is ل ه م; the initial hamzah in أَلْهَمَ belongs to form IV, not the root.

Al-Shams 91:8 uses أَلْهَمَهَا concerning the human self.

This may overlap in meaning with some kinds of waḥy, especially inward reception of meaning.

They should not, however, be assumed completely synonymous.

Not all Quranic waḥy is described as inward inspiration. Zechariah’s signaling and communication through a Messenger illustrate its wider scope.

Conversely, an inward feeling or thought is not divine revelation merely because it is inward.

W Ḥ Y (وَحْي) compared with W Ṣ Y (وَصِيَّة)

The root و ص ي concerns enjoining, entrusting instructions, and giving counsel.

Al-Shūrā 42:13 places وَصَّى, concerning what was enjoined upon Noah, alongside أَوْحَيْنَا, concerning what was revealed to Muhammad.

Enjoining and revelation may thus overlap in communicating an instruction or message.

Waṣiyyah tends to emphasize the entrusted instruction or obligation, while waḥy concerns communicating the message.

This is not an absolute distinction. Al-Nisāʾ 4:163 explicitly describes revelation to Noah. The contrasting expressions in Al-Shūrā 42:13 cannot establish that Noah received only injunctions and Muhammad only revelation.

W Ḥ Y (وَحْي) compared with R S L (رِسَالَة)

The root ر س ل concerns sending and conveying messages.

A Messenger is one who is sent and, in that mission, commissioned to deliver a message.

Waḥy is not the Messenger; it concerns receiving or communicating a message or command.

In Al-Shūrā 42:51, the commissioned Messenger may mediate revelation.

“Messenger” designates a role and mission, whereas waḥy concerns a mode of communication or transmission.

W Ḥ Y (وَحْي) compared with W S W S (وَسْوَسَة)

Waswasah concerns concealed or recurring suggestions of thought and speech, and occurs in Quranic contexts of deception and temptation.

In some settings, the devils’ waḥy and waswasah may overlap as concealed influences on a person.

Waḥy has a wider scope, encompassing divine messages, human gestures, and commands concerning creation.

It should therefore not be confined to hidden inward whispering.

Translation proposals for Forqan

One equivalent does not suffice for every verse.

For divine revelation to Prophets: “We revealed,” “is revealed,” and “was revealed to him” are suitable. The established Persian term for revelation is natural in this recognized Quranic use.

For example, Fuṣṣilat 41:6:

“I am only a human being like you, to whom it is revealed…”

For Moses’ mother and the disciples: “We revealed” or “I revealed,” matching the verse’s actual grammatical person, remains suitable because the Quran attributes the communication to The God. Commentary can clarify that this alone does not establish prophethood.

In Maryam 19:11: “Signaled” is more precise than “revealed,” since the context supports Zechariah’s communication by signs.

In Al-Naḥl 16:68: “Revealed” or “communicated His guidance and command to the bees” may be considered. The first is a more direct translation; the second belongs more naturally in an explanation.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:12 and Al-Zalzalah 99:5: Retain revelation language in the basic translation, explaining its relation to divine command separately.

In Al-Anʿām 6:112: “Impart to one another” or “communicate to one another” suits the context. “Divine revelation” would misrepresent it because the senders are devils.

In Al-Anʿām 6:121: “The devils impart to their allies” is clear and avoids restricting the word to prophetic revelation.

The governing principle is to preserve each sentence’s meaning, rather than mechanically impose one equivalent throughout. These are editorial proposals, not replacements for existing verse word boxes.

8. Conclusion

The root و ح ي concerns conveying and imparting messages, meanings, and commands. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period uses it both for writing and recorded marks and for communication through sounds.

Its Quranic uses are broad and varied.

The God reveals to Prophets, who receive divine knowledge and, in their mission as Messengers, convey it. Revelation is also attributed to Moses’ mother and the disciples. Zechariah signals to his people. The God communicates to bees and the earth, and reveals each heaven’s affair within it. Devils also impart deceptive speech to one another.

Lexically, waḥy is therefore not confined to prophethood, just as not every message or inward feeling is automatically divine revelation.

Concealment, speed, writing, and lack of mediation cannot be made absolute requirements of every use. Such features may be present in particular cases, determined by their constructions and contexts.

The final definition is conveying or imparting a message, meaning, or command through various modes of communication, which may be apparent or concealed, spoken or unspoken, divine or nondivine.

In prophetic revelation, The God is the message’s source, and its truth and authority derive from that source and content. Elsewhere, the sender, recipient, and kind of message are established by context.

Forqan’s translation and research should distinguish three matters: the linguistic meaning of waḥy, the communication described in each verse, and divine revelation’s distinctive place in human guidance.

This distinction preserves the root’s breadth while preventing divine revelation from being equated with every gesture, thought, inspiration, or communication.
