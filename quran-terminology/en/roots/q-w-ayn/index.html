Qāʿ and qīʿah: open, level ground without conspicuous elevations or irregularities

1. Meaning and background in early Arabic

In its principal Quranic use, Q W ʿ concerns open, extensive, level ground. Qāʿ denotes an expanse with a relatively even surface and no conspicuous rises or hollows.

In familiar Arabic usage, it can be a plain lying between mountains and hills, lower than its surroundings. Such ground may collect rainwater or become an open, level expanse during dry seasons.

For example, a broad plain between two mountain ranges may have a relatively even surface compared with the surrounding rocky slopes. This expanse may be called qāʿ.

Early Arabic sources define al-qāʿ as broad, smooth, level ground without conspicuous elevations or depressions. Some definitions also mention its being lower than the surrounding land.

Descriptions of its soil and vegetation differ.

Some describe it as hard ground without vegetation.

Others describe clay ground that retains rainwater and supports plant growth.

This variation shows that the central feature is the ground's form and levelness; it need not always be dry, waterless, or entirely without vegetation.

Qāʿ should therefore not always be restricted to “dry desert.”

A witness from Imruʾ al-Qays

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

تَرَى بَعَرَ الْآرَامِ فِي عَرَصَاتِهَا

tarā baʿara al-ārāmi fī ʿaraṣātihā

وَقِيعَانِهَا كَأَنَّهُ حَبُّ فُلْفُلِ

wa-qīʿānihā ka-annahu ḥabbu fulfuli

“You see the gazelles' droppings in its open spaces and level plains, as though they were peppercorns.”

The poet describes places once inhabited and frequented by people; their residents have departed, and animals now move through them.

Al-ārām is the plural of riʾm, a name for white gazelles.

ʿAraṣāt concerns open spaces and grounds.

Qīʿān is the plural of qāʿ and denotes open, level expanses.

The poet likens the gazelles' droppings on their surface to peppercorns.

Here the open surface makes scattered objects on the ground visible.

The use shows that qīʿān in early Arabic poetry denotes level ground, rather than a deep pit or a desert necessarily devoid of animals.

The verse appears in the familiar transmission of the Muʿallaqah, although some early commentators regarded it as an addition to the transmitted poem. Its meaning is clear, but its precise date and attribution should not be treated as certain.

A witness from Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī reads:

كَأَنَّ سَدَى قُطْنِ النَّوَادِفِ خَلْفَهَا

ka-anna sadā quṭni al-nawādifi khalfahā

إِذَا اسْتَوْدَعَتْهُ كُلَّ قَاعٍ وَمِذْنَبِ

idhā istawdaʿathu kulla qāʿin wa-midhnabi

“Behind the horses, it is as though cotton fabric stretches out when they leave their dust upon every level plain and watercourse.”

The poet describes horses moving and raising dust from the ground.

In the early commentary, sadā, the warp of fabric, evokes cotton cloth: the dust behind the horses appears as a sheet spread over the plain.

Qāʿ appears here beside midhnab.

Midhnab in this use concerns a watercourse or channel through which water passes.

Qāʿ denotes the level expanse crossed by the horses and covered by their dust.

The distinction shows that it names a recognizable landform.

Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī was a pre-Islamic poet, and the verse occurs in poetry attributed to him. Although its precise date is unknown, it provides a separate early poetic witness for qāʿ as level ground.

What the historical witnesses establish

The two witnesses clarify the family's use in describing terrain.

Qāʿ is an open, level expanse whose surface appears more even than the surrounding rough terrain.

It may be dry or moist, sparsely vegetated or covered with plants. These features depend on environment and context, not on the word alone.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • Qāʿ: a plain or open, level ground.
  • Qāʿah: an open, level space; in a familiar use, the open area of a house or building.
  • Qīʿ: a recorded form for level ground.
  • Qīʿah: open, level ground; in some analyses, the plural of qāʿ.
  • Qīʿān: plural of qāʿ, level plains.
  • Aqwāʿ: another plural of qāʿ.
  • Aqwuʿ: a recorded plural form.
  • Quwayʿ: the diminutive of qāʿ.
  • Qawʿ: in one lexical use, a surface on which dates or grain are spread.
  • Qāʿatu al-dār: the grounds or open space of a house.

Some dictionaries also record qāʿa al-faḥlu al-nāqata for animal mating and taqawwaʿa al-ḥirbāʾu al-shajarata for a chameleon climbing a tree.

There is insufficient evidence to derive these historically from qāʿ as level ground. Some early philologists regarded the mating verb as a metathesized form of Q ʿ W.

Their written similarity should not transfer mating or tree climbing into the Quranic definition of qāʿ.

Why is the root Q W ʿ?

In qāʿ, the middle root letter appears as alif.

Traditional morphological analysis identifies its underlying letter as wāw.

One indication is the diminutive quwayʿ, where the wāw becomes visible.

The family is therefore studied under Q W ʿ, rather than Q A ʿ.

In qīʿah, the middle letter changes its form and appears as yāʾ.

Such sound and morphological changes are familiar in Arabic roots containing a weak letter.

Is qīʿah singular or plural?

Early Arabic sources offer two analyses.

Some treat it as singular, denoting a plain or level expanse.

Others, including al-Farrāʾ, count it as the plural of qāʿ.

Under that analysis, it denotes a collection of level expanses.

The disagreement should be acknowledged because it matters morphologically.

In Al-Nūr 24:39, however, both analyses lead to a similar image: a mirage on open, level ground.

“On a plain” is therefore a suitable fluent rendering, with the number distinction explained in the lexical study.

The semantic core of the Quranic branch

Uses concerning terrain display three main features.

First is the openness of the ground.

Second is its relative levelness and lack of prominent irregularities.

Third, in many examples, is its position among higher surrounding land.

That third feature is not essential to every use.

A house's open grounds, for example, may be called qāʿah without lying in a depression between mountains.

A defensible semantic core is therefore:

An open, level expanse of ground whose surface lacks conspicuous irregularities.

This is broader than “desert” and more specific than “any ground.”

3. First occurrence: Ṭā Hā 20:106

The ground after the mountains are scattered

Ṭā Hā 20:106

فَيَذَرُهَا قَاعًا صَفْصَفًا

fa-yadharuhā qāʿan ṣafṣafan

Ṭā Hā 20:106

“and leave it a level plain,”

Qāʿan here denotes a level expanse of ground.

The preceding verse helps explain the scene.

In Ṭā Hā 20:105, Muhammad is asked about the mountains and instructed to say that his Lord will scatter them completely.

Ṭā Hā 20:106 then describes a level expanse remaining.

The passage thus concerns the transformation of the mountains and the resulting condition of the ground.

The structure of qāʿan

Qāʿan is an indefinite singular noun in the accusative.

Syntactically, it describes the condition in which what remains is left after the event.

Fa-yadharuhā belongs to W DH R and concerns leaving something behind.

Qāʿan specifies the condition of what is left.

The sentence therefore describes the ground remaining smooth and level after the mountains are scattered.

Two analyses have been proposed for the pronoun hā in fa-yadharuhā.

In one, it refers to the ground, understood from the discussion of the mountains and their condition.

In the other, it concerns the mountains themselves and the sites remaining after their dispersal.

Either way, the final image is the same: mountainous elevations disappear and the ground becomes level.

The distinction between qāʿ and ṣafṣaf

The verse places two words together:

Qāʿan and ṣafṣafan.

They are close in meaning but do not share a root.

Qāʿ names open, level ground.

Ṣafṣaf, when describing terrain, emphasizes uniformity and the absence of elevations and irregularities.

Together, qāʿan ṣafṣafan depicts entirely even, level ground.

A plain may, for example, be generally level while still containing small hills or hollows.

Emphasizing its complete evenness produces a more specific image.

Ṣafṣaf highlights that feature here.

Connection with Ṭā Hā 20:107

Ṭā Hā 20:107 explains that no crookedness or elevation will be seen there.

It completes the preceding image.

ʿIwaj concerns crookedness and unevenness.

Amt here concerns a rise or elevation.

Three expressions thus work together:

Qāʿ introduces the level expanse.

Ṣafṣaf emphasizes its evenness.

The next verse explains the absence of crookedness and elevation.

The combined expressions clarify qāʿ in this passage.

The entire meanings of ṣafṣaf, ʿiwaj, and amt should not, however, be assigned to Q W ʿ itself.

Each word contributes separately to the scene.

Is the verse about a dry desert?

Qāʿ itself does not mean a dry, waterless desert.

Arabic sources describe some such plains as collecting rainwater and supporting vegetation.

The mention of level ground here therefore does not establish from qāʿ alone that it must lack water or all life.

Conversely, Ṭā Hā 20:105 and Ṭā Hā 20:106 concern a profound transformation of mountains and terrain, not an ordinary agricultural plain.

The basic meaning is level ground; further details require context or independent verses.

Does qāʿ mean the destruction of the earth?

No. It names a form of the earth's surface.

The mountains are scattered, and a level expanse remains.

The image describes a change in terrain, but qāʿ itself does not mean the absolute annihilation of matter.

Nor does it specify the precise physical mechanism, the remaining surface's composition, or its geological characteristics.

The verse presents a clear image of elevations disappearing and even ground remaining.

4. Second occurrence: Al-Nūr 24:39

A mirage on a level expanse

Al-Nūr 24:39

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَسَرَابٍ بِقِيعَةٍ يَحْسَبُهُ الظَّمْآنُ مَاءً حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَهُ لَمْ يَجِدْهُ شَيْئًا وَوَجَدَ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ فَوَفَّاهُ حِسَابَهُ وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ

wa-alladhīna kafarū aʿmāluhum ka-sarābin bi-qīʿatin yaḥsabuhu al-ẓamʾānu māʾan ḥattā idhā jāʾahu lam yajidhu shayʾan wa-wajada Allāha ʿindahu fa-waffāhu ḥisābahu wa-Allāhu sarīʿu al-ḥisāb

Al-Nūr 24:39

“Those who disbelieved—their deeds are like a mirage on a plain, which the thirsty person thinks is water, until he reaches it and finds it nothing, but finds The God there, who settles his account in full. The God is swift in reckoning.”

Bi-qīʿatin denotes the open, level ground where the mirage is seen.

Qīʿah belongs to the same family as qāʿ, here preceded by the preposition bi-.

Grammatically, the prepositional phrase specifies the location where the mirage appears.

Qīʿah itself therefore does not mean mirage.

Sarāb belongs to another lexical family and denotes an optical phenomenon in which something resembling water appears where actual water is absent.

Qīʿah identifies the setting of that appearance.

Why is a mirage seen on a plain?

On open, level expanses, especially when sunlight has heated the surface, an observer may see something resembling water in the distance.

This can result from light refracting through layers of air at different temperatures.

For example, someone looking along a road on a hot day may see its distant surface as though it were wet.

Approaching that point, however, they find no water.

This familiar experience helps explain the verse's imagery.

An extended surface and the absence of large visual obstructions can make such a sight more apparent in open country.

Qīʿah itself nevertheless means neither extremely hot ground nor a site of light refraction.

Those are conditions that may contribute to a mirage.

Its lexical meaning remains open, level ground.

Yaḥsabuhu al-ẓamʾānu māʾan

A thirsty person then enters the scene.

Al-ẓamʾān belongs to Ẓ M ʾ and denotes a thirsty person.

Yaḥsabuhu states that he takes the mirage for water.

This sentence plays an important role in the comparison.

He does not merely see a distant image; driven by thirst, he takes what he sees to be water.

His real need gives the mirage significance for him.

Imagine someone who has walked for a long time in the heat without carrying water.

Seeing something resembling water in the distance may give him hope of soon quenching his thirst.

On reaching it, he discovers that the appearance was not actual water.

Three things must be distinguished:

His thirst is real.

The appearance is also a real visual experience.

The water he imagined at that location, however, does not exist.

The distinction matters for understanding the comparison.

Lam yajidhu shayʾan

The Quran continues that when the person reaches the mirage, he finds it to be nothing.

This does not mean that the ground or everything around him has disappeared.

The ground still exists.

What he fails to find is the expected water.

The absence of an expected result must therefore be distinguished from the destruction of all material reality.

He sought water, but on arrival what he had taken for water offered nothing to quench his thirst.

At this point, the natural image connects with the comparison concerning human deeds.

The mirage and human deeds

The verse begins by comparing the deeds of those who disbelieve to a mirage.

A person may thus imagine value and results for their deeds that do not meet their expectations at the final reckoning.

For example, someone may rely on power, wealth, or social standing, believing these achievements have secured safety and salvation.

Before divine judgment, however, what seemed valuable in earthly life does not necessarily yield the expected result.

Just as the thirsty person sees apparent water but finds none on arrival, someone facing the reckoning of their deeds may discover that certain hopes and sources of reliance were unfounded.

This comparison does not establish that human deeds have no real worldly effects.

An action may genuinely benefit or harm others and have real social consequences, while its value and outcome in divine judgment remain a separate question.

The central issue in Al-Nūr 24:39 is the accounting and outcome of deeds before God.

Connection with the surrounding verses

Al-Nūr 24:35 speaks of God's light.

Al-Nūr 24:36 and Al-Nūr 24:37 describe people whom trade and buying and selling do not distract from remembering God and establishing prayer.

Al-Nūr 24:38 presents the reward for their deeds.

Al-Nūr 24:39 then introduces the mirage as an image for the deeds of those who disbelieve.

Al-Nūr 24:40 offers another image: layers of darkness in a deep sea.

The sequence brings together light and guidance, deeds and reward, a mirage and unfounded hope, and darkness and inability to see.

The mirage is a distinct image within that sequence.

The full meanings of disbelief, reckoning, or light should not be derived from Q W ʿ.

Qīʿah is simply the expanse where the mirage is seen.

The comparison's theological and ethical significance comes from the complete sentence and its context.

Must qīʿah here be a waterless desert?

In the image, the thirsty person seeks water absent from the mirage's location.

This does not mean that qīʿah names ground that never contains water or plants.

Some plains called qāʿ in Arabic collect rainwater.

Such ground may contain water in one season and dry out in another.

The absence of water at the mirage comes from the comparison and lam yajidhu shayʾan, not from a fixed definition of qīʿah.

The distinction prevents a feature of one Quranic scene from becoming the root's permanent meaning.

5. Comparisons with related roots and words

Q W ʿ (qāʿ) and Ṣ F Ṣ F (ṣafṣaf): a level expanse and an even surface

They occur together in Ṭā Hā 20:106.

Qāʿ names open, level ground.

Ṣafṣaf emphasizes its evenness and lack of irregularities.

In this combination, qāʿ identifies the terrain and ṣafṣaf further specifies its complete levelness.

Their closeness does not establish a shared root or complete synonymy in every use.

Q W ʿ (qāʿ) and Q ʿ R (qaʿr): a level plain and the bottom of a hollow

Qaʿr concerns the bottom or depth of something.

The qaʿr of a well, for example, is its lowest interior part.

Qāʿ, however, denotes open, level ground.

In some uses it lies below the surrounding terrain, bringing the two words close in the feature of lowness.

Qaʿr need not be open or level, however.

A well's bottom may be narrow, rocky, or uneven.

Qāʿ in its principal sense concerns the ground's extent and levelness.

Being low is therefore a possible shared feature, not the full definition of either root.

Q W ʿ (qāʿ) and Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿīd): a plain and the ground's surface

Ṣaʿīd is used in the Quran for the surface of the ground.

Al-Kahf 18:8 uses ṣaʿīdan juruzan.

Al-Kahf 18:40 describes land becoming ṣaʿīdan zalaqan.

Familiar uses of ṣaʿīd concern the ground's surface.

Qāʿ is more specific: an open, level expanse.

Every qāʿ is part of the ground's surface, but not every surface is necessarily qāʿ.

A rocky slope is also part of the surface, yet pronounced irregularities distinguish it from the usual image of qāʿ.

Q W ʿ (qāʿ) and S R B (sarāb): real ground and an optical appearance

They appear together in Al-Nūr 24:39.

Qīʿah is the real expanse of ground.

Sarāb is an optical phenomenon that may resemble water.

The ground and the appearance in a person's field of vision are different things.

Seeing a mirage on a plain does not make the plain imaginary.

The verse concerns someone taking an appearance for actual water and failing to find that water on arrival.

Qīʿah should therefore not be translated as imagination, illusion, or deception.

Those ideas concern the mirage and the person's response to it.

Q W ʿ (qāʿ) and M H D (mihād): level ground and a resting bed

Mihād belongs to M H D and is used in the Quran for a bed or something prepared for settlement and rest.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6 compares the earth to a bed.

Ṭā Hā 20:53 likewise describes the earth being made a mahd.

Qāʿ in Ṭā Hā 20:106 concerns the ground's level form.

Level ground may suit movement or settlement, but qāʿ alone does not mean prepared for ease and life.

Mihād, by contrast, emphasizes the image of a bed and a prepared resting place.

Both concern the earth while offering different images and semantic emphases.

Q W ʿ (qāʿ) and Q ʿ W (qaʿā): level ground and sitting

Arabic sources relate qaʿā and nearby forms to sitting postures and certain animal movements.

Quranic qāʿ belongs to Q W ʿ and denotes level ground.

Early sources also identify some verbs listed under Q W ʿ as metathesized forms of Q ʿ W.

Letter similarity alone cannot establish sitting, animal mating, and level ground as one historical meaning.

6. Challenges in interpretation and translation

Is qāʿ always lower than surrounding land?

Many early definitions describe ground with higher land around it.

Others emphasize levelness and openness themselves.

Relative lowness is thus a familiar feature, but should not become a necessary condition of every use.

In Ṭā Hā 20:106, the context clearly concerns ground becoming level after the mountains are scattered.

Is qāʿ always waterless and barren?

No. Arabic sources describe some such plains as collecting rainwater and supporting vegetation.

Others are dry and bare.

Dryness or plant growth depends on ground and water conditions.

Adding “dry,” “bare,” or “barren” to a direct Quranic translation requires support from the context.

Does qāʿ mean a sandy desert?

No. It may lie in a desert region, but not all sandy terrain is qāʿ.

Sand dunes can have many rises and hollows.

The principal sense of qāʿ concerns an open, level surface.

Some such plains also have clay soil and are not necessarily composed of sand.

Does turning mountains into qāʿ describe a specific scientific process?

Ṭā Hā 20:105 and Ṭā Hā 20:106 describe mountains being scattered and ground becoming level.

The image is clear, but qāʿ itself does not explain the physical process.

It cannot establish the remaining particles' composition, the event's speed, or its precise geological mechanism.

The word means a level expanse.

Does qīʿah in Al-Nūr 24:39 mean mirage?

No. Qīʿah specifies where the mirage is seen.

Sarāb names the optical phenomenon.

If the words meant the same thing, ka-sarābin bi-qīʿatin would lose the distinction between the phenomenon and its setting.

Both must therefore remain distinct in translation.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for familiar forms include:

Qāʿ: a level plain; open, even ground.

Qāʿah: an open, level space; in building-related usage, open grounds or a hall.

Qīʿ: open, level ground.

Qīʿah: a level plain or open expanse; under the plural analysis, level plains.

Qīʿān: plains and level expanses.

Aqwāʿ: level plains.

Aqwuʿ: a recorded plural of qāʿ.

Quwayʿ: a small plain or level expanse.

Qawʿ: in a specific use, a surface for spreading and drying dates or grain.

The two Quranic occurrences

Ṭā Hā 20:106: qāʿan

Principal equivalent: “plain” or “level expanse.”

The existing Forqan translation:

“and leave it a level plain,”

This appropriately combines the meanings of qāʿ and its accompanying adjective ṣafṣaf in a natural rendering.

Al-Nūr 24:39: bi-qīʿatin

Principal equivalent: “on a plain” or “on a level expanse.”

The existing Forqan translation:

“Those who disbelieved—their deeds are like a mirage on a plain, which the thirsty person thinks is water, until he reaches it and finds it nothing, but finds The God there, who settles his account in full. The God is swift in reckoning.”

“On a plain” is appropriate here as well.

“In a dry, waterless desert” adds details not supplied by qīʿah alone.

“In a mirage” would also be incorrect, merging the location with the phenomenon itself.

On this lexical point, the current Forqan translations of both occurrences need no change.

8. Conclusion

Quranic Q W ʿ denotes open, level ground.

Qāʿ is a relatively smooth, even expanse, often lower than its surroundings.

Early poetic witnesses, including qīʿān in the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and qāʿ in Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī, illustrate this terrain-related meaning.

Dictionaries also record qāʿah, qīʿah, qīʿān, and aqwāʿ.

Whether qīʿah is singular or plural is disputed, without changing its central meaning in Al-Nūr 24:39.

This family occurs twice in the Quran.

In Ṭā Hā 20:106, qāʿan describes the level ground after the mountains are scattered.

In Al-Nūr 24:39, bi-qīʿatin identifies the location of a mirage taken for water by a thirsty person who finds no expected water on arrival.

Both preserve the meaning of open, level ground.

Their contexts differ: one depicts the earth's transformation, while the other uses a natural setting in a comparison concerning expectations and the outcome of deeds.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its principal Quranic branch, Q W ʿ denotes an open, extensive, level expanse without conspicuous surface irregularities. It may lie below its surroundings, but dryness, lack of water, and absence of vegetation are not essential to its meaning.

Qāʿ names this expanse, and qīʿah is another form of the family denoting a plain or level ground in Quranic usage.

In Ṭā Hā 20:106 it describes the ground after the mountains are scattered; in Al-Nūr 24:39 it identifies where the mirage appears. The mirage's figurative significance comes from the complete verse, not from Q W ʿ itself.
