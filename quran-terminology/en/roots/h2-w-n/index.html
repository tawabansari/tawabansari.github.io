The Qur’anic Root H W N

Ease, gentleness, and the lowering of status

The root ه و ن (H W N) moves between two neighboring fields in the Qur’an. In one branch, something is light, easy, or free of severity, and behavior can be calm and gentle. In the other, someone or something is regarded as insignificant or of little worth, leading to abasement, humiliation, and dishonoring.

Early Arabic itself preserves this proximity. Hāna ʿalayhi sh-shayʾ (هان عليه الشيء) means that something became light and easy for someone; hayyin (هَيِّن) describes what is easy and not burdensome. Within the same family, al-hawān (الهوان) and al-mahānah (المهانة) express a lowering of status and being held in contempt. This proximity between being light and being treated as insignificant helps us understand the root’s semantic development, but should not become a definitive theory about the derivational history of all its forms.

The Qur’an also preserves a subtle distinction between different vocalizations of this root. The sole occurrence of hawn (هَوْن), with fatḥah on the hāʾ, in Al-Furqān 25:63 concerns calmness and gentleness, whereas the four instances of hūn (هُون), with ḍammah on the hāʾ, concern humiliation and diminished standing. Arabic sources do not regard this distinction as absolute across every dialect, but the Qur’anic pattern is clear and significant.

H W N occurs 26 times in the Qur’an: twice as the form IV verb ahāna (أَهَانَ), once as ahwan (أَهْوَن), once as hawn (هَوْن), four times as hūn (هُون), three times as hayyin (هَيِّن), fourteen times as muhīn (مُهِين), and once as muhān (مُهَان).

Early language: from lightness and ease to abasement

The use of hawān for humiliation was established before the Qur’an. The pre-Islamic poet Al-Mutalammis aḍ-Ḍubaʿī says:

إِنَّ الهَوَانَ حِمَارُ القَوْمِ يَعْرِفُهُ
وَالْحُرُّ يُنْكِرُهُ وَالرَّسْلَةُ الأُجُدُ

Inna l-hawāna ḥimāru l-qawmi yaʿrifuhu / wa-l-ḥurru yunkiruhu wa-r-raslatu l-ujud.

“The people’s donkey knows humiliation; the free person rejects it, as does the strong, easy-paced she-camel.”

In this verse, al-hawān stands against freedom and the refusal of abasement. Hawān is a condition in which someone is diminished in standing, humiliated, or made subordinate.

On the other side of the family, Arabic uses hawn (هَوْن) for gentleness, dignity, deliberateness, and calm movement. It also says hāna ʿalayhi sh-shayʾ (هان عليه الشيء): something became light, khaffa (خفّ), for someone—that is, easy and unburdensome. This usage matters for understanding hayyin and ahwan.

An intelligible semantic relationship emerges here: what has less weight and difficulty is easier, while something judged “light” may be regarded as unimportant and of little worth. An-Nūr 24:15 shows this bridge within the Qur’an itself: “وَتَحْسَبُونَهُ هَيِّنًا وَهُوَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَظِيمٌ” (wa-taḥsabūnahu hayyinan wa-huwa ʿinda llāhi ʿaẓīm): you considered it insignificant, although it was tremendous in the sight of The God.

Here hayyin (هَيِّن) stands directly against ʿaẓīm (عظيم), “tremendous.” The branch of lightness is therefore not confined to ease of execution; it can also extend to regarding something as slight in importance.

Nevertheless, this opposition does not establish that every H W N word arose from a physical metaphor of weight. The linguistic evidence shows the historical proximity of lightness, ease, and diminished worth; the exact order in which they developed is unclear.

In neighboring Semitic languages, forms of this family are likewise associated with ease and treating something as light. This comparative evidence supports the antiquity of the branch of ease and lightness, but Arabic evidence itself is clearer for explaining the full range of abasement and humiliation.

Hawn, hayyin, and ahwan: what is not heavy or difficult

Al-Furqān 25:63 contains the only occurrence of hawn (هَوْن): “وَعِبَادُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الَّذِينَ يَمْشُونَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ هَوْنًا” (wa-ʿibādu r-raḥmāni lladhīna yamshūna ʿalā l-arḍi hawnā). Hawn here does not mean abasement. The servants of Ar-Raḥmān do not walk in humiliation or disgrace; their manner of movement is described through calmness, dignity, gentleness, and an absence of harshness.

“Humbleness” is a suitable translation only if understood as calmness and an unassuming manner, rather than self-degradation and abasement. In this verse, the root describes how someone moves more than it judges their social worth.

The three occurrences of hayyin (هَيِّن) have two related but distinct uses. In Maryam 19:9 and 19:21, the response concerning the births of John and Jesus is “هُوَ عَلَيَّ هَيِّنٌ” (huwa ʿalayya hayyin): this is easy for Me. What appears difficult and burdensome to human beings is hayyin in relation to the power of The God.

In An-Nūr 24:15, the same word enters the field of evaluation: “وَتَحْسَبُونَهُ هَيِّنًا وَهُوَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَظِيمٌ” (wa-taḥsabūnahu hayyinan wa-huwa ʿinda llāhi ʿaẓīm). The issue here is not the ability to do something; a person has considered a grave matter unimportant and slight.

These uses reveal a common field: hayyin is something whose weight, difficulty, or importance is considered small. Context determines which kind of lightness is intended.

Ahwan (أَهْوَن) in Ar-Rūm 30:27 is the comparative form of this branch: “وَهُوَ الَّذِي يَبْدَأُ الْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُ وَهُوَ أَهْوَنُ عَلَيْهِ” (wa-huwa lladhī yabdaʾu l-khalqa thumma yuʿīduhu wa-huwa ahwanu ʿalayh). The root’s contribution is “easier.” How this expression should be understood theologically alongside unlimited divine power is a question beyond the root itself; its morphological meaning should not be changed to resolve that question.

Hūn and ihānah: when someone is treated as insignificant and of low standing

The four Qur’anic occurrences of hūn (هُون) belong to the field of abasement.

An-Naḥl 16:58–59 shows the word’s social setting with particular clarity. On receiving news of a daughter’s birth, a man’s face darkens, he contains his anger and hides from people, then considers: “أَيُمْسِكُهُ عَلَىٰ هُونٍ أَمْ يَدُسُّهُ فِي التُّرَابِ” (a-yumsikuhu ʿalā hūnin am yadussuhu fī t-turāb): should he keep the child in a condition of hūn, or conceal her in the earth?

In ʿalā hūnin (عَلَىٰ هُونٍ), hūn concerns humiliation, but the relation of that humiliation within the sentence has been understood in two ways: the humiliation the father imagines for himself if he keeps his daughter, or the degraded condition in which he keeps her. The expression alone does not conclusively establish either reading. In both, the subject is a human judgment about a daughter’s birth, not proof of her inherent inferiority. The verse ends: “أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَحْكُمُونَ” (a-lā sāʾa mā yaḥkumūn): how badly they judge.

This point prevents us from reducing H W N to an inner feeling of “shame.” Hawān can also have a social form: someone regards another person as of low standing and treats them accordingly.

The other three occurrences of hūn accompany punishment: ʿadhāba l-hūn (عَذَابَ الْهُونِ) in Al-Anʿām 6:93 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:20, and al-ʿadhābi l-hūn (الْعَذَابِ الْهُونِ) in Fuṣṣilat 41:17. Such punishment is not merely painful; it also humiliates and lowers standing.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:20 connects this directly with arrogance: “فَالْيَوْمَ تُجْزَوْنَ عَذَابَ الْهُونِ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَسْتَكْبِرُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ” (fa-l-yawma tujzawna ʿadhāba l-hūni bi-mā kuntum tastakbirūna fī l-arḍi bi-ghayri l-ḥaqq). In this particular verse, hūn stands against conduct in which someone unjustly elevates themselves. A person who acted arrogantly in the world encounters the punishment of abasement.

This opposition is explicit in Al-Aḥqāf, but it should not be generalized to every use of hūn or muhīn. The root does not itself mean “breaking arrogance”; the context of that verse establishes the relationship.

Honor and abasement: what determines a person’s worth?

Al-Fajr 89:15–17 presents another aspect of the issue. When people see abundant provision and blessings, they say “رَبِّي أَكْرَمَنِ” (rabbī akraman), “My Lord has honored me”; when their provision is restricted, they say “رَبِّي أَهَانَنِ” (rabbī ahānan), “My Lord has humiliated me.” The response then comes:

كَلَّا

Kallā.

“No indeed.”

And immediately afterward:

بَلْ لَا تُكْرِمُونَ الْيَتِيمَ

Bal lā tukrimūna l-yatīm.

“Rather, you do not honor the orphan.”

This context offers one of the Qur’an’s most important corrections concerning honoring and humiliating. People draw conclusions about their worth and standing from their material circumstances: they read abundance as a sign of honor and restriction as a sign of abasement. The Qur’an rejects this simple equation and immediately turns to how people treat the orphan and the needy.

A shortage of wealth or restricted provision is therefore not, in itself, “divine humiliation,” just as possessing wealth alone does not indicate greater human worth.

This point shares a general direction with An-Naḥl 16:59 without making the two contexts identical. In An-Naḥl, a daughter’s birth is associated with imagined humiliation or degrading treatment; in Al-Fajr, people make poverty and wealth the measure of honor and humiliation. In both, the Qur’an subjects human judgments about standing and worth to criticism.

It is better, however, not to construct a general theory of the entire social structure of pre-Islamic Arabia from these two passages. What we can say more confidently is that the Qur’an challenges two specific examples of human valuation.

Muhīn and muhān: humiliating and humiliated

Muhīn (مُهِين) occurs fourteen times in the Qur’an, describing punishment in every instance. Morphologically, muhīn is the active participle of ahāna (أهان): something that humiliates and places a person in a condition of abasement.

This construction differs from ʿadhāb alīm (عذاب أليم), “painful punishment.” Alīm (أليم) emphasizes the punishment’s painfulness; muhīn emphasizes its humiliating effect.

ʿAdhāb muhīn (عذاب مهين) is therefore not merely punishment experienced as painful. Through its grammatical description, the punishment itself is presented as that which brings about humiliation. This gives the expression a dynamic quality, but we should not infer more than the word’s structure establishes. The active participle says that this punishment causes abasement; it does not by itself establish that its function in every verse is to “break the structure of pride.”

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:20, the connection with arrogance is explicit because the verse itself says that the punishment of abasement is bi-mā kuntum tastakbirūn (بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَسْتَكْبِرُونَ), “because you acted arrogantly.” Elsewhere, the cause and context may differ.

By contrast, muhān (مُهَان) occurs only once, in Al-Furqān 25:69: “وَيَخْلُدْ فِيهِ مُهَانًا” (wa-yakhlud fīhi muhānā): he remains therein humiliated. Muhān is a passive participle: someone upon whom humiliation has been inflicted and who has come into a condition of abasement.

The distinction is simple but important: muhīn is humiliating; muhān is humiliated.

Form IV also expresses this field through the verb. Al-Ḥajj 22:18 says: “وَمَنْ يُهِنِ اللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُ مِنْ مُكْرِمٍ” (wa-man yuhini llāhu fa-mā lahu min mukrim): whomever The God humiliates has no one to honor them. Here ihānah (إهانة), humiliating, stands directly against ikrām (إكرام), honoring. The root has moved from ease and lightness into the field of standing and status: someone who is humiliated is brought down from a position of honor.

This family overlaps with KH Z Y and DH L L, but they are not identical. Khizy emphasizes disgrace and exposed dishonor more strongly; hawān focuses more on lowered standing and diminished esteem. Dhull has its own independent field, and its precise boundaries should be determined in the study of that root.

Translation and conclusion

H W N is a root whose vowel markings and morphological structure are decisive for translation.

Hawn (هَوْن) in Al-Furqān 25:63 expresses calmness, gentleness, and dignity; hūn (هُون) in An-Naḥl and in ʿadhāb al-hūn (عذاب الهون) means abasement and lowered standing; hayyin (هَيِّن) can mean easy or regarded as slight and unimportant; ahwan (أَهْوَن) is the comparative “easier”; ahāna (أَهَانَ) means to humiliate and lower someone’s standing; muhīn (مُهِين) is humiliating, and muhān (مُهَان) is humiliated.

Behind these branches lies an observable semantic relationship. Arabic itself explains hāna ʿalayhi sh-shayʾ (هان عليه الشيء) through khaffa (خف), becoming light and easy. This field can move from a matter being light to its being treated as insignificant, and then to a person being regarded as of little worth. An-Nūr 24:15, with its opposition between hayyin and ʿaẓīm (هَيِّن / عظيم), offers one of the clearest Qur’anic bridges between lightness and importance.

This relationship, however, should not be extended beyond the evidence into a theory of “spiritual weight.” The Qur’an does not formulate such a term. What we observe is a network of ease, lightness, treating something as unimportant, and abasement.

The Qur’an also criticizes human measures of honor and humiliation in several contexts. A daughter’s birth is associated with humiliation in her father’s judgment; that judgment does not establish her inherent inferiority. A person who describes restricted provision as rabbī ahānan (ربي أهانن), “My Lord has humiliated me,” is likewise answered with kallā (كلا), “No indeed.” Wealth, sex, or social position are not, in themselves, the measure of honor before The God.

In punishment, too, the Qur’an distinguishes pain from abasement. ʿAdhāb muhīn (عذاب مهين) is punishment that, in addition to any other suffering, humiliates. In Al-Aḥqāf, this abasement stands directly against unjust arrogance, but that opposition belongs to that particular verse and should not become the definition of the whole root.

A cautious synthesis of the root is as follows:

H W N: a family that, in one branch, expresses ease, lightness, calmness, and reduced intensity, and in another extends to treating something as insignificant, low standing, abasement, and humiliating. The Qur’an keeps these branches distinct: hawn is calmness; hayyin is easy or regarded as slight; hūn is abasement; ahāna is to humiliate; muhīn is humiliating; and muhān is humiliated. The connection between lightness and diminished worth is real, but sentence structure and context determine the field in which each derivative operates.
