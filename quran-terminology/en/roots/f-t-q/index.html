The Root ف ت ق — Splitting, Opening, and Separating What Is Closed or Joined

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا
(fa-fataqnāhumā)
Then We opened the two; al-Anbiya 21:30

فَتْق
(fatq)

The three-consonant root ف ت ق in Arabic means splitting, opening, or making a breach in something previously closed, sewn, or joined together. This opening may completely separate two parts, or simply create a passage or gap in something that was closed.

Its established opposite is ر ت ق, which concerns closing, joining, and bringing separated parts together.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in al-Anbiya 21:30, as فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا. The same verse also uses رَتْقًا. The direct contrast between these roots is central to understanding their meaning.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Early Arabic

In established early Arabic usage, فَتَقَ الشَّيْءَ means splitting something, opening its point of connection, or creating an opening in it.

For example, فَتَقَ الثَّوْبَ describes tearing open a garment or undoing its seam. Conversely, رَتَقَ الثَّوْبَ means sewing and joining its torn or separated parts.

This contrast shows that fatq is not merely moving two things away from each other. It concerns opening a previous closure or connection.

If two pieces of fabric lie beside each other, moving them apart is not necessarily fatq. If they have been sewn together and their seam is undone, however, the meaning is clearly present.

The same distinction applies to splitting a closed covering. Its parts need not all separate; creating an opening in it can also be fatq.

Evidence from Pre-Islamic Poetry

Al-A‘sha, the well-known poet of pre-Islamic Arabia, says in a poem attributed to him:

وَرَادِعَةٍ بِالْمِسْكِ صَفْرَاءَ عِنْدَنَا

Wa-rādiʿatin bi-l-miski ṣafrāʾa ʿindanā

لِجَسِّ النَّدَامَى فِي يَدِ الدِّرْعِ مَفْتَقُ

Li-jassi n-nadāmā fī yadi d-dirʿi maftaqu.

Translation: “A woman scented with musk and adorned with yellow was with us; her sleeve had an opening through which the drinking companions could touch her.”

Here مَفْتَق denotes a slit or opening in part of a garment. الدِّرْع in this context is a woman’s garment, not battle armour.

The verse is recorded in the surviving collection of al-A‘sha’s poetry and cited in lexical sources, although some transmissions vary, for example using بِالطِّيب instead of بِالْمِسْك.

Its importance lies in a tangible, everyday use of fatq: an opening in previously continuous fabric.

This supports the sense of splitting and opening, but does not by itself prove that every later use of the root developed directly from sewing.

Other Arabic Uses

Classical dictionaries record several further uses.

فَتْقُ الصُّبْح refers to morning light appearing amid darkness. The image is associated with the horizon opening and light emerging.

In أَفْتَقَ الْقَمَرُ, the moon appears through an opening in clouds. Here fatq concerns a gap in the cloud cover, not the moon itself splitting.

فَتْقُ عَصَا الْجَمَاعَةِ describes breaking a community’s unity and creating division among its members. Social cohesion is likened to the connection between parts of an object.

أَعْوَامُ الْفَتَقِ also denotes years of abundance and fertility in lexical accounts. This use has been associated with the earth opening to growth and abundance, but its development cannot be treated as historically certain without independent evidence.

These recorded uses show the root extending beyond physical splitting to natural and social phenomena. Recording a usage in a later dictionary does not, by itself, establish that the same expression was current before Islam.

2. Lexical Forms and the Semantic Core

The principal recognized forms include:

  • فَتَقَ: split, opened, opened apart.
  • فَتْق: splitting, an opening, or a resulting breach.
  • فَتَّقَ: split or opened; in some uses, with repetition or extension of the action.
  • تَفَتَّقَ: became split or opened.
  • اِنْفَتَقَ: opened, developed a breach.
  • مَفْتُوق: something split or opened.
  • فَتِيق: in some lexical uses, an animal whose flesh and fat have expanded through fatness.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Its only form from this root is فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا.

A defensible semantic core can be expressed as follows:

Opening or splitting a closure or connection, so that previously closed or connected parts open apart, or a passage or breach appears in them.

This definition preserves two features: a preceding closed or joined condition, and the emergence of an opening or separation.

Fatq does not necessarily mean destruction, fragmentation, or the complete scattering of all parts. The object and context determine the nature and extent of the opening.

3. Qur’anic Usage: The Heavens and Earth in a State of Ratq

Surah al-Anbiya 21:30

أَوَلَمْ يَرَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَنَّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا ۖ وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ ۖ أَفَلَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

A-wa-lam yara lladhīna kafarū anna s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa kānatā ratqan fa-fataqnāhumā wa-jaʿalnā mina l-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayyin a-fa-lā yuʾminūn.

Translation: “Have those who rejected the truth not seen that the heavens and the earth were joined, then We opened the two, and made every living thing from water? Will they not believe?”

The verse places two opposing roots together:

رَتْقًا — a condition of closure or connection; being closed.

فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا — then We opened the two, or brought them out of their closed condition.

The verse thus describes a change brought about by the will of The God: the heavens and earth were in a closed or joined state, and that condition was then opened.

Were the Heavens and Earth Attached to Each Other?

One natural reading of كَانَتَا رَتْقًا is that the heavens and earth were joined to one another and The God separated them.

This reading is compatible with the lexical meanings of ratq and fatq.

Nevertheless, رَتْقًا can also refer to both being closed, rather than necessarily describing one material mass whose parts were all attached to each other.

Two situations help clarify the distinction.

In the first, two parts are joined together and become separated through fatq.

In the second, each part is closed in itself and is opened through fatq: for example, a sky opened for rainfall and earth split open for vegetation to grow.

Both images fall within the semantic field of ratq and fatq. Preferring one requires examination of the verse’s context and related evidence.

Fatq, Water, and the Emergence of Life

Immediately after فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا, the Qur’an says:

«وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ»

Wa-jaʿalnā mina l-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy.

This connection makes the reading concerning the sky opening for rain and the earth splitting for growth worthy of consideration.

Al-Tariq 86:11 and al-Tariq 86:12 likewise place the sky characterized by returning alongside the earth characterized by splitting. These verses can assist an examination of rainfall and growth in relation to al-Anbiya 21:30.

Yet “We made every living thing from water” is not limited to plant growth. It concerns the fundamental connection between water and life and can have a wider horizon than rainfall and vegetation.

Thus, just as ratq and fatq should not be restricted without evidence to a particular cosmological event, they should not be reduced exclusively to rain and emerging plants, ruling out in advance a connection with the broader creation of the heavens and earth.

4. Morphology and Syntax of فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا

فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا consists of four components:

فَـ: a connective expressing sequence, here marking the transition from the preceding condition to the following action.

فَتَقْ: the perfect stem of a form I verb.

نَا: the first-person plural subject suffix, referring here to The God.

هُمَا: the dual object suffix, meaning “the two of them.”

Together, the form means: “Then We opened or split the two.”

هُمَا refers to the heavens and earth.

Although السَّمَاوَاتِ is plural and الْأَرْضَ singular, together they constitute the two groups or sides under discussion. Accordingly, the Qur’an uses كَانَتَا and هُمَا.

رَتْقًا is singular because it is a verbal noun and can describe the condition of both sides. Its singular form does not by itself prove that the heavens and earth were materially a single body.

This point helps prevent conclusions extending beyond what the syntax can establish.

فَتَقَ is transitive here: the action of opening affects an object. The construction attributes the opening to The God, but does not specify its instrument, duration, speed, or physical mechanism.

5. Fatq and Readings Concerning the Universe’s Origins

Al-Anbiya 21:30 can also be considered in research into the universe’s origins and development.

In contemporary cosmology, the observable universe has expanded from a very hot, dense earlier condition. Understanding this development offers a fresh perspective for reflection on the Qur’anic image of a transition from closure to opening.

One may ask whether رَتْق and فَتْق in this verse, beyond their clear linguistic imagery, also refer to a stage in the emergence and expansion of the universe’s structure.

This is a question that can be examined interpretively, since the verse concerns the heavens and earth and a transformation of their previous condition.

The word فَتْق itself, however, is not equivalent to the scientific term “Big Bang.” The root does not specifically express an explosion, the rate of expansion, the time of the universe’s beginning, or how matter came into being.

The scientific model of cosmic expansion does not mean that an object exploded within a pre-existing empty space. Presenting fatq as an ordinary explosion and a definitive explanation of the verse can therefore both narrow the lexical meaning and create a scientifically misleading picture.

Conversely, the absence of a scientific theory’s name from the verse does not mean that readers cannot reflect on the scope of its imagery in light of new knowledge.

What the wording clearly conveys is a preceding condition of ratq and the occurrence of fatq by the will of The God. Identifying the physical nature of that transformation is a separate stage of research requiring independent evidence.

6. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ت ق (فَتَقَ) and ر ت ق (رَتَقَ)

These roots stand in direct opposition in al-Anbiya 21:30.

رَتَقَ concerns closing, joining, and bringing parts together.

فَتَقَ concerns opening or splitting that closure or connection.

In the example of sewing a garment, ratq joins two torn parts, while fatq can undo the seam.

The verse uses this contrast to express a change in the condition of the heavens and earth.

ف ت ق (فَتَقَ) and ف ل ق (فَلَقَ)

ف ل ق is also associated with splitting and the separation of parts.

In al-An‘am 6:95, فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ concerns splitting grain and fruit stones.

In al-An‘am 6:96, فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ conveys the image of bringing forth daybreak.

Fatq and falq overlap in the field of splitting, but the Qur’anic use of ف ت ق is marked by its opposition to ratq and highlights a preceding closed or joined condition.

Falq highlights splitting and the emergence of parts, or something appearing from within a preceding condition.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute boundary covering every Arabic use.

ف ت ق (فَتَقَ) and ف ر ق (فَرَقَ)

ف ر ق concerns separating and distinguishing things.

In al-Baqarah 2:50, فَفَرَقْنَا بِكُمُ الْبَحْرَ concerns the sea being parted and a separation appearing within it.

An important distinction is that farq can emphasize separation and distinction between two sides, whereas fatq highlights opening a closed or joined condition.

Two things may be separated without having previously been sewn or closed together. Not every separation is therefore fatq, although fatq can result in separation.

ف ت ق (فَتَقَ) and ف ت ح (فَتَحَ)

ف ت ح concerns opening something closed or obstructing passage, as well as openings and relief in various affairs.

For example, al-Zumar 39:73 describes the gates of the Garden being opened.

Opening a door does not necessarily split or separate the door’s own parts. It may simply turn on its hinges and allow passage.

Fatq, by contrast, is more closely associated with splitting, opening a point of connection, or creating a breach in something.

Both roots can be translated as “opening,” but their material setting and the kind of action are not always identical.

ف ت ق (فَتَقَ) and ش ق ق (شَقَّ)

ش ق ق concerns splitting, dividing into two, or creating a fissure.

‘Abasa 80:26 says:

«ثُمَّ شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا»

Thumma shaqaqnā l-arḍa shaqqā.

This describes the earth splitting in connection with plant growth.

Shaqq and fatq can both express splitting something. In the Qur’anic use of ف ت ق, however, the opposition to ratq is particularly important, bringing the preceding closed or joined condition into focus.

This overlap does not justify treating the two roots as completely identical.

7. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

One major difficulty is treating fatq as “complete separation” in every use.

Splitting a covering can merely create an opening without separating all its parts.

Another difficulty is equating fatq with an explosion. An explosion may split something, but its intensity, pressure, and mechanism are not inherent in the lexical meaning of fatq.

Nor should the contrast between ratq and fatq become a rule that any two separated things must previously have been one material body. Such a conclusion is warranted only where the sentence and relevant evidence support it.

Conversely, restricting fatq to a superficial slit is also mistaken. An opening can lead to complete separation, and the lexical meaning does not exclude this possibility.

Al-Anbiya 21:30 concerns the condition of the heavens and earth, not a garment, door, or ordinary covering. Material examples help explain the root, but should not restrict the nature of the world’s opening to the way fabric is split or a door opens.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for فَتَقَ include “split,” “opened,” “opened apart,” and, where context warrants, “separated.”

فَتْق can be rendered as “splitting,” “opening,” “a breach,” or “an opening.”

In al-Anbiya 21:30, فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا can be rendered:

“Then We opened the two.”

Or:

“Then We split the two apart.”

The first is shorter and can preserve both the opening of a closed condition and the separation of joined parts.

The second makes splitting and separation more explicit, but may lead readers more strongly toward imagining a single joined material mass.

For Forqan’s base translation, “then We opened the two” is suitable: it preserves the verb’s core meaning without adding unspecified physical details.

The explanation can clarify that this opening brings the heavens and earth out of ratq: a condition that linguistically defensible readings can understand as their being joined together or each being closed.

9. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ت ق concerns splitting and opening something previously closed or joined. The verse attributed to al-A‘sha illustrates an opening in a garment; other recorded uses show the root’s extension to natural and social phenomena.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only in al-Anbiya 21:30, as فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا. Its opposition to رَتْقًا indicates bringing the heavens and earth out of a closed or joined condition.

The root’s semantic core is splitting and opening a preceding closure or connection, whether this produces complete separation or merely a passage or breach.

This definition explains the root’s material use while allowing the verse to be understood across the actual scope of its language, without reducing it in advance to a narrow natural image or a particular cosmological theory.
