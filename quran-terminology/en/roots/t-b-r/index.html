Short Introduction to the Root

The root moves in the field of breaking, shattering, tearing apart, and destroying. In this root, a person, thing, system, or body is damaged so severely that it loses its coherent form, its ability to remain, or the possibility of continuing its previous function.

Tatbir is not merely suffering loss or failing. Something may suffer loss while still preserving its structure and identity. In tatbir, the body itself—or the order that joined its parts into a continuous whole—is exposed to breaking and destruction.

Important Forms and Derivatives

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an in four morphological forms.

تَبَّرَ (tabbara)

is a form II verb indicating breaking apart, reducing to pieces, destroying, or eliminating.

In the Qur’an, this verb occurs in two forms:

وَلِيُتَبِّرُوا مَا عَلَوْا تَتْبِيرًا

“And so that they may completely break apart whatever they overpowered.”

Al-Isra 17:7

And:

وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

“And each We completely broke apart and destroyed.”

Al-Furqan 25:39

تَتْبِير (tatbir)

is the verbal noun of form II. It indicates the act of breaking apart, destroying, or reducing something to scattered pieces.

In both Qur’anic uses, the verbal noun follows a verb from the same root:

وَلِيُتَبِّرُوا مَا عَلَوْا تَتْبِيرًا

Al-Isra 17:7

وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

Al-Furqan 25:39

This construction highlights the intensity and comprehensiveness of the act.

تَبَار (tabar)

is a noun or form I verbal noun indicating destruction, perishing, and a ruined outcome:

وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا

“And increase the wrongdoers in nothing except destruction.”

Nuh 71:28

Here, tabar places greater emphasis on the state or outcome of destruction than on the manner of breaking.

مُتَبَّر (mutabbar)

is the passive participle of form II: something broken apart, destroyed, or exposed to a certain destructive end:

إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ مُتَبَّرٌ مَا هُمْ فِيهِ وَبَاطِلٌ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

“What these people are in is surely bound to be broken apart, and what they were doing is batil.”

Al-A’raf 7:139

Non-Qur’anic Forms

In Arabic, the form I verb is also recorded with the meaning of perishing or passing away, but this verb does not occur in this form in the Qur’an.

The word is also used for unworked gold, broken gold, or metal that has not yet been coined or fashioned. This word does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be imposed directly upon the ayat.

Semantic Core of the Root

The central meaning of is breaking apart and eliminating the body or order that holds the parts of something together as a durable whole.

In tatbir, the issue is not merely that something is damaged or loses part of its function. The breaking is severe enough that its previous form, internal order, or capacity to continue is lost.

This meaning can appear materially: buildings, centers, and things destroyed after being overpowered. It can also be used at a social, religious, or historical level: a system that appears to remain standing but whose structure and possibility of continuation are eventually broken apart.

Yet not every political change, social weakness, or loss of credibility of an idea should automatically be called tatbir. This root is more intense and emphasizes the real destruction or collapse of the body itself.

Material Image of the Root

The material image of this root is a coherent object broken and scattered so thoroughly that it can no longer preserve its previous form and function.

One may picture an earthen vessel that has not merely cracked but has become pieces whose sum is no longer a usable vessel. Its material may still remain, but its body, coherence, and function have disappeared.

This image also accords with one lexical use of . Tibr is used of raw, broken, or unworked gold; the valuable material exists, but it is not—or is no longer—within a fashioned and ordered structure.

This connection can help explain the root’s imaginal field, but it should not be presented as the certain origin of all its meanings. The clearer lexical evidence is that means to break, reduce to pieces, and destroy something.

At the social level, people, buildings, or material elements may remain while the bond and system that turned them into an active body disappears. Such a use is a natural extension of the root’s material image, provided that the text and context support it.

Use of the Root in Early Arabic

In Arabic lexical sources, the verb is used for breaking something, breaking it into pieces, crushing it, and destroying it. The form I also indicates perishing and passing away.

is recorded with the meaning of destruction and perishing, and is used of something broken and destroyed.

The word was commonly used of unworked gold, broken gold, particles of gold, and at times silver or metal before being fashioned or coined. In some reports, it is also used for pieces of broken glass.

The exact relation between the meaning of unworked metal and the meaning of destroying requires caution. One may say that both are connected with a state of being in pieces, unfashioned, or without a complete form, but a definite historical account of the root’s semantic development should not be built from this resemblance.

The Qur’anic use of the root clearly stands in the field of destruction, breaking apart, and a ruined outcome, not in the meaning of unworked metal.

History of Understanding, Misuse, or Semantic Restriction

Some translations render the derivatives of only as “destroy.” This is correct with respect to the result, but it weakens the image of breaking and shattering present in the root’s lexical structure.

At the other extreme, translations such as “We caused their structure to collapse” may clarify the social and systemic dimension of the ayat, but when used in direct translation they may replace the Qur’anic wording with an analytical meaning. can include structural collapse, but the word itself is not merely a political or sociological term.

The phrase is also sometimes translated as though it meant only that their “belief” or “religion” was wrong. The ayah then says separately:

وَبَاطِلٌ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

For this reason, and should not be translated as entirely identical. Batil highlights lack of truth and validity, while mutabbar indicates that what they are in is bound for destruction.

Nor should the historical use in Al-Isra 17:7 be turned into a general ruling to destroy cities, buildings, or places of worship. The ayah describes a punitive event in its own context, not an unrestricted and permanent command for destruction.

Methodological Note

When examining this root, lexical meaning, contextual use, and structural reflection must be distinguished.

The recorded lexical meaning includes breaking, reducing to pieces, destroying, and perishing.

Contextual use may concern material devastation after domination, the destruction of peoples who rejected, the outcome of a system of worship, or Noah’s supplication concerning the wrongdoers.

Structural reflection draws from these uses the thought that a system may preserve its material parts for a time while losing its coherence and ability to reproduce itself. This reflection can be useful, but it should not be presented as the direct dictionary meaning of .

Likewise, not every break or injury should be called tatbir. Tatbir indicates severe and destructive breaking. A crack, weakness, or limited loss is not necessarily tatbir so long as it has not eliminated the primary body and function.

Qur’anic Uses of the Root

What Is Batil and Has a Destructible Outcome

إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ مُتَبَّرٌ مَا هُمْ فِيهِ وَبَاطِلٌ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

“What these people are in is surely bound to be broken apart, and what they were doing is batil.”

Al-A’raf 7:139

This ayah comes after the people who remained devoted to their idols were observed. Moses states two separate judgments about their condition.

First, : what they are in is destroyed or bound for destruction.

Second, : what they were doing is batil.

The two statements are related, but they are not complete synonyms. Being batil judges the truth and validity of the action. Being mutabbar concerns the durability and outcome of what they are in.

An action or system may be batil with respect to truth while continuing in a society for some time. shows that its outward continuation does not mean true permanence.

Tatbir After Domination

وَلِيَدْخُلُوا الْمَسْجِدَ كَمَا دَخَلُوهُ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ وَلِيُتَبِّرُوا مَا عَلَوْا تَتْبِيرًا

“And so that they may enter the masjid as they entered it the first time, and completely break apart whatever they overpowered.”

Al-Isra 17:7

The subject of is those who enter, prevail, and devastate what they have brought under their control.

The phrase refers to what they have overpowered or risen over. In this context, ‘uluw is directly the action of the attacking agents, not a moral description of the things destroyed.

Tatbir in this ayah has an outward and historical manifestation. Entry, domination, and destruction stand beside one another. The translation should therefore preserve the image of severe devastation.

Yet the ayah should not be turned into a general permission to destroy places of worship or cities. Reporting a historical and punitive outcome is not the same as establishing a general ethical rule for every time and group.

The Final Tatbir of Peoples Who Rejected

وَكُلًّا ضَرَبْنَا لَهُ الْأَمْثَالَ وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

“And for each We set forth examples, and each We completely broke apart and destroyed.”

Al-Furqan 25:39

This ayah follows the mention of several peoples and figures who rejected. Its two parts are connected:

وَكُلًّا ضَرَبْنَا لَهُ الْأَمْثَالَ

“And for each We set forth examples.”

And then:

وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

“And each We completely destroyed.”

Tatbir is not introduced here without a preceding process. Before the outcome there were explanation, warning, and the setting forth of examples.

The emphatic verbal noun highlights the intensity and completeness of the destruction. “We completely broke them apart” conveys this feature better than “We harmed them.”

Tabar in Noah’s Supplication

وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا

“And increase the wrongdoers in nothing except destruction.”

Nuh 71:28

This supplication comes at the end of the long account of Noah’s calling. Before it, Noah says:

إِنَّكَ إِنْ تَذَرْهُمْ يُضِلُّوا عِبَادَكَ وَلَا يَلِدُوا إِلَّا فَاجِرًا كَفَّارًا

“If You leave them, they will lead Your servants astray and will give birth to and raise none but a corrupt, deeply ungrateful one.”

Nuh 71:27

in this context indicates destruction and perishing. The supplication does not speak only of the failure of a plan or limited loss; it asks for a destructive outcome for wrongdoers who persisted in leading others astray.

The context can show that the matter also concerns the continuation and reproduction of wrongdoing, but the direct meaning of should not be reduced only to political or cultural reproduction. The ayah still speaks of giving birth to and raising human beings.

This supplication should not be made into a general model for cursing every opponent. Its setting is the conclusion of a particular and prolonged situation explained by the surah itself.

Important Qur’anic Derivatives

مُتَبَّر (mutabbar)

is the passive participle of form II. The passive participle indicates that the subject has been exposed to an act or that its outcome is described as the product of that act.

In Al-A’raf 7:139, the sentence does not say that only the people are bound for destruction. It says:

مُتَبَّرٌ مَا هُمْ فِيهِ

The subject of the judgment is . This phrase can include the condition, system of worship, or way in which they are immersed.

“What they are in is bound for destruction” preserves the structure of the ayah more closely. “Their religion is false” merges the second part of the ayah into the first and removes the difference between and .

يُتَبِّرُوا (yutabbiru)

is a subjunctive masculine plural imperfect verb in form II. The lam before it can express aim or result:

وَلِيُتَبِّرُوا

“And so that they may break apart.”

Its subject is the same people who enter the masjid. This syntactic continuity is important.

تَبَّرْنَا (tabbarna)

is a form II past verb. The pronoun identifies the subject.

تَتْبِيرًا (tatbiran)

In both ayat in which the form II verb occurs, a verbal noun from the same root follows it:

يُتَبِّرُوا … تَتْبِيرًا

تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

This is an absolute or emphatic verbal noun and highlights the intensity or completeness of the act.

In English, it can be reflected through expressions such as “completely,” “entirely,” or “thoroughly,” without requiring a separate and heavy translation of the Arabic verbal noun.

تَبَارًا (tabaran)

comes after and restricts the result of the increase:

لَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا

Nothing except tabar is to be added to them.

In this construction, “nothing except destruction” is more natural than “nothing except breaking apart,” because tabar expresses the state or outcome of destruction more than the transitive act of breaking.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

The Emphatic Verbal Noun

In the following two expressions, a verbal noun follows a verb from the same root:

وَلِيُتَبِّرُوا مَا عَلَوْا تَتْبِيرًا

وَكُلًّا تَبَّرْنَا تَتْبِيرًا

This construction highlights the intensity, comprehensiveness, or certainty of the act. Translating only “destroy” may convey the main meaning but loses part of the construction’s emphasis.

Suitable renderings include “destroy completely,” “break apart entirely,” or “shatter thoroughly.”

The Construction مَا عَلَوْا (ma ‘alaw)

In the expression:

مَا عَلَوْا

refers to what the agents have overpowered. is the action of those agents.

Possible translations include “whatever they overpowered,” “what they brought under their control,” or “whatever they gained mastery over.”

A wording such as “whatever they became superior over” may be confused with moral or evaluative superiority; “overpowered” is clearer.

The Construction مُتَبَّرٌ مَا هُمْ فِيهِ

In this sentence, appears first and highlights the judgment. identifies what the judgment concerns.

A close translation is:

“What they are in is bound for destruction.”

“What they insist upon” is explanatory and may be inferred from context, but it is not the exact syntactic structure of the ayah.

The Construction لَا تَزِدْ … إِلَّا

In the expression:

وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا

the construction restricts the result of the increase: nothing except destruction is to be added to them.

“And increase the wrongdoers in nothing except destruction” preserves this structure closely.

Comparison With Nearby Roots

T B R compared with T B B: tatbir and tabab

The root indicates loss, failure, and a harmful outcome. In tabab, a person, effort, plan, or support fails to reach success and beneficial result.

The root emphasizes breaking apart and destroying the body or order itself.

The difference may be pictured in this way: in tabab, result and benefit reach a dead end; in tatbir, the vessel and structure that should continue are themselves broken apart.

This difference is not absolute, because destruction of structure is also loss and tabab can lead to destruction. Yet the dominant images of the two roots differ.

T B R compared with K S R: tatbir and breaking

The root carries the general meaning of breaking. Something may break while retaining part of its form or function.

indicates a more severe and destructive breaking, one that leads to the loss of the body or its continuity.

Every tatbir is a kind of breaking, but not every kasr is necessarily tatbir.

T B R compared with F T T: tatbir and crumbling

The root emphasizes crushing and reducing to small pieces. Its image is material becoming small fragments.

can include fragmentation, but its field extends to total destruction and the loss of structure.

Fatt emphasizes the state of the pieces; tatbir emphasizes the destructive outcome of the body.

T B R compared with D M R: tatbir and damar

The root indicates severe devastation, extensive destruction, and elimination.

preserves more clearly the image of breaking and shattering a body, while damar can indicate comprehensive devastation without equal emphasis on the fragmentation of parts.

The boundary between them is not completely separate in every Arabic usage and should not be enlarged beyond the linguistic evidence.

T B R compared with H L K: tatbir and halak

The root indicates passing away and the ending of existence or the ability to remain.

Halak more often expresses the result, while tatbir can show the manner or process of reaching that result through the image of breaking and shattering.

is very close to halak in result, while more strongly highlights the act of destruction.

T B R compared with B T L: tatbir and butlan

The root indicates lacking truth, being invalid, or being ineffective.

The root emphasizes destruction and the loss of the body or its continuity.

In Al-A’raf 7:139, the Qur’an places the two beside one another: what they are in is , and what they do is . Their placement together shows that they should not be treated as complete synonyms.

T B R compared with Q T ʿ: tatbir and cutting

The root indicates cutting, separating, or ending a connection.

In tatbir, the bonds among parts may be cut, but the action is not limited to severing one connection; the whole body or order is exposed to being broken apart.

Cutting can be part of tatbir, but it is not its complete equivalent.

T B R compared with S D ʿ: tatbir and splitting

The root indicates splitting, cracking, or creating separation in a surface or body.

A split may begin collapse, but it does not necessarily destroy the whole structure. Tatbir goes beyond a crack and conveys severe destruction of the body.

T B R compared with H T M: tatbir and hatm

The root is also close to severe breaking and crushing and preserves a clear image of impact, pressure, and fragmentation.

and overlap closely. In the Qur’anic uses under discussion, however, tatbir includes not only fragmentation but also the destructive outcome of a condition, people, or structure.

No absolute boundary should be constructed between them; the difference lies more in their dominant image and contexts of use.

T B R compared with N Q D: tatbir and naqḍ

The root indicates undoing something previously tied, woven, or made firm. It can be used of a covenant, construction, or bond.

In naqḍ, connection and firmness are undone. In tatbir, the body reaches destruction and brokenness.

Naqḍ may weaken or open a structure; tatbir deprives it of the possibility of continuing in its previous form.

T B R compared with ʿ L W: tatbir and ʿuluw

The root indicates rising, being above, overpowering, or seeking superiority.

In Al-Isra 17:7, refers to the agents’ domination, and is the action performed after this domination.

In other contexts, ‘uluw can concern arrogance and seeking superiority, but this ethical meaning should not be imposed directly on in this ayah.

Exceptional Section: The Relation of Tibr to the Root’s Semantic Field

The word is used in Arabic for unworked gold, broken gold, particles of gold, or metal that has not yet been fashioned and coined.

This word does not occur in the Qur’an, but etymologically it belongs to the family of .

An imaginal connection can be seen between this use and the meaning of breaking or losing form: the material exists, but it is not within a fashioned, unified, or final form. In some definitions, tibr refers to particles or broken pieces of gold and silver.

Yet one should not conclude that every Qur’anic use of was directly formed from the image of gold or unworked metal. Lexical sources record several uses beside one another, but their historical order and exact relation have not been clearly established.

is therefore useful for clarifying part of the root’s imaginal field, but it should not become the main axis for interpreting the ayat.

Summary

The root in the Qur’an indicates destruction, breaking apart, and the loss of a body or order that previously possessed the ability to remain and continue. Its dominant image is something broken and scattered so thoroughly that it can no longer preserve its previous form and function.

This root occurs six times in the Qur’an. is used of a condition bound for destruction; is used of agents who devastate after domination; states the outcome of peoples who rejected; and in Noah’s supplication points to the destruction of the wrongdoers.

In Al-A’raf 7:139, being mutabbar and being batil appear beside one another, but they do not carry the same meaning. Being batil concerns lack of truth and validity; being mutabbar concerns an outcome of destruction and the instability of the structure.

In Al-Isra 17:7, tatbir occurs after entry and domination. This ayah should not be turned into a general command for destruction.

In Al-Furqan 25:39, the emphatic verbal noun highlights the intensity and completeness of the destructive outcome. In Nuh 71:28, likewise expresses a state or outcome of destruction.

The root overlaps with roots such as , and , but it is not completely identical with any of them.

The distinctive feature of is that it does not show loss or weakness only in the result; the body, coherence, or capacity of the structure itself to continue is exposed to breaking and destruction.
