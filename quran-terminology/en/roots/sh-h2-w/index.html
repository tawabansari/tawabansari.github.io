The soul’s desire for something it finds appealing: natural wants, following desires, and enjoying the blessings of Paradise

In early Arabic, SH H W concerns wanting, desiring, and being drawn toward something considered pleasant, enjoyable, or desirable. Shahwah names this inward desire; ishtahā describes wanting something and wishing to enjoy it.

Such desire may concern food, drink, sleep, sexual relations, wealth, children, comfort, or other desirable things. Restricting shahwah to sexual desire captures only part of its semantic range.

In contemporary Persian, shahwat commonly suggests sexual desire and sometimes carries a negative moral connotation. In Quranic Arabic, the word is neither confined to one kind of desire nor condemned in every occurrence.

The Quran warns against following desires that lead away from responsibility and guidance. It also describes the people of Paradise enjoying what their souls desire.

This contrast shows that the presence of desire is not, by itself, either a sin or a virtue. Its value depends on its object, the means of pursuing it, and the resulting conduct.

1. Meaning in early Arabic and poetic evidence

Wanting something desirable

Early Arabic uses shahiya and shahā for liking or wanting something. Shahiya al-shayʾ means that someone liked and desired it.

Shahwah likewise denotes the soul’s inclination toward something desirable.

The object need not be sexual. A hungry person may desire food, a tired person sleep, or someone may pursue another pleasurable object.

Shahiyy describes something pleasant and desirable. Ṭaʿāmun shahiyy is appetizing food that encourages a wish to eat.

Shahwān and shahwāniyy describe someone with a strong appetite or desire, including for food or other pleasures.

The family thus includes both desire itself and what arouses it.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: wanting sleep

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward reads:

أَقِلِّي عَلَيَّ اللَّوْمَ يَا بِنْتَ مُنْذِرٍ

aqillī ʿalayya al-lawma yā binta mundhir

وَنَامِي فَإِنْ لَمْ تَشْتَهِي النَّوْمَ فَاسْهَرِي

wa-nāmī fa-in lam tashtahī al-nawma fa-s-harī

Translation:

“Reproach me less, daughter of Mundhir; sleep, and if you do not wish to sleep, stay awake.”

Here tashtahī concerns sleep.

The poet asks his addressee to stop reproaching him and sleep, or remain awake if she does not want sleep.

The witness matters for early usage because it shows that ishtihāʾ concerned wanting and was not restricted to sexual desire.

Sleep is something a person may or may not desire at a particular moment.

The line occurs in a poem attributed to ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward. Its general attribution is established in the poetic tradition; the precise date of this individual line has not been independently established here.

It is therefore an important witness from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, but does not establish when ishtahā first entered the language.

Arabic close to the period of revelation

A line attributed to al-Ḥuṭayʾah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, reads:

وَأَشْعَثَ يَشْهَى النَّوْمَ قُلْتُ لَهُ ارْتَحِلْ

wa-ashʿatha yashhā al-nawma qultu lahu irtaḥil

إِذَا مَا النُّجُومُ أَعْرَضَتْ وَاسْبَكَرَّتِ

idhā mā al-nujūmu aʿraḍat wa-sbakarrati

Translation:

“To a disheveled man who wanted sleep I said: Depart, when the stars had spread out across the sky.”

This line also concerns wanting sleep, but uses yashhā.

The earlier witness uses tashtahī, Form VIII; this one uses yashhā from the basic verb.

Both express wanting or desiring.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived in both periods. This line’s date has not been independently established here, so it cannot be treated as certainly pre-Quranic.

Together, the witnesses show that the basic verb and Form VIII were used in early Arabic for wanting something such as sleep.

Appetite: wanting something pleasant

Recorded Arabic usage relates ishtahā to wanting something the soul finds desirable.

The desire may arise from bodily need, as with food when hungry or sleep when tired.

It may also concern enjoyment without an immediate necessity, such as wanting tasty food despite not being hungry.

Need and desire are related, but are not identical.

Need may concern conditions necessary for life or health; shahwah concerns a person’s felt attraction toward something.

Someone may need something without presently wanting it, or want something strongly without needing it.

Shahwah should therefore not be equated with all human needs.

Shahwah and tashahhī: intensity and repetition

Tashahhā is also recorded for wanting or requesting something.

In some usages, tashahhī concerns successive requests for desired things, one wish following another.

Shahhā is used for arousing desire. Depending on construction, ash-hā can express greater desirability or occur in an exclamatory expression; these constructions should not all be treated as one causative verb.

For example, the smell of food may stimulate appetite, creating or increasing a wish to eat.

Morphology alone does not establish that ishtahā always expresses stronger desire than shahiya, or that tashahhā necessarily expresses blameworthy desire.

Each form must be understood through attested usage and context.

Is shahwah inherently excessive desire?

Some lexical definitions describe shahwah as intense desire.

That description fits some contexts, but not all.

In ʿUrwah’s line, tashtahī al-nawm means wanting sleep; it does not necessarily indicate an uncontrollable or excessive appetite.

The Quran likewise uses mā yashtahūn for what the people of Paradise desire. Excess and sin are not part of the word’s meaning there.

The semantic core is therefore desire for something appealing. Intensity, repetition, or excess should be added only where supported by context.

2. Quranic forms and semantic core

The root occurs thirteen times in thirteen Quranic verses, in three morphological groups:

The singular shahwah, the plural shahawāt, and various forms of the Form VIII verb ishtahā.

The singular shahwatan appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:81 and Al-Naml 27:55.

The plural al-shahawāt occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:14, Al-Nisāʾ 4:27, and Maryam 19:59.

Form VIII appears as yashtahūn, ishtahat, tashtahī, and tashtahīhi.

Other forms, including shahiya, shahā, shahiyy, shahwān, shahhā, and tashahhā, are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Quran.

In tashtahīhi, the suffix -hi refers to the desired object. It is neither part of the root nor a separate derivative.

In yashtahūn, the plural wāw is the subject pronoun, while the final nūn marks the indicative in this construction. Neither adds a lexical meaning to the root.

The family’s Quranic semantic core is:

The soul’s inclination toward something it finds desirable, pleasant, or enjoyable, and wishes to obtain or experience.

In some verses shahwah denotes desire itself. Elsewhere, shahawāt can denote wishes or desirable things.

Translation should preserve this distinction between desire and its object.

3. Desirable things in worldly life: natural affection and excessive attachment

Loving desirable things

Āl ʿImrān 3:14 reads:

زُيِّنَ لِلنَّاسِ حُبُّ الشَّهَوَاتِ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ وَالْبَنِينَ وَالْقَنَاطِيرِ الْمُقَنْطَرَةِ مِنَ الذَّهَبِ وَالْفِضَّةِ وَالْخَيْلِ الْمُسَوَّمَةِ وَالْأَنْعَامِ وَالْحَرْثِ ذَٰلِكَ مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاللَّهُ عِنْدَهُ حُسْنُ الْمَآبِ

zuyyina li-l-nāsi ḥubbu al-shahawāti mina al-nisāʾi wa-l-banīna wa-l-qanāṭīri al-muqanṭarati mina al-dhahabi wa-l-fiḍḍati wa-l-khayli al-musawwamati wa-l-anʿāmi wa-l-ḥarthi dhālika matāʿu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-llāhu ʿindahu ḥusnu al-maʾāb

Āl ʿImrān 3:14

Translation:

“Love of desirable things has been made appealing to people: women, sons, amassed treasures of gold and silver, marked horses, livestock, and cultivated land. These are the enjoyment of worldly life; with God is the good destination.”

Al-shahawāt here is not restricted to sexual desire.

The list includes human and family relationships, children, wealth, possessions, animals, and cultivated land.

It shows the breadth of wanting: people may desire a family, children, wealth, property, or the means of living.

Al-shahawāt can here refer to desires and desirable things: the name for desire is also applied to its objects.

This fits the root’s core: something is considered desirable, and the soul is drawn toward it.

Is loving these things sinful?

The verse does not say that women, children, wealth, animals, or cultivated land are inherently bad or forbidden.

The Quran also presents children and possessions as blessings. Al-Naḥl 16:72 mentions spouses, children, and wholesome provisions; Al-Aʿrāf 7:32 challenges the prohibition of God’s adornments and wholesome provisions.

Loving a blessing is therefore distinct from misusing it.

People may love their children, provide for their families, acquire wealth, and enjoy wholesome blessings without those actions being inherently wrong.

Those same attachments may facilitate injustice when personal wishes take precedence over truth and justice.

A father may lawfully provide for his children; another person may invoke the same affection to justify bribery or taking others’ property.

Both involve affection for family, but their moral value differs.

The issue is the resulting conduct and the place desire occupies in decision-making, rather than affection itself.

The meaning of zuyyina

Zuyyina at the beginning is passive: love of these desirable things has been made appealing to people.

The construction does not explicitly name the agent of embellishment.

Its entire effect should not be attributed to Satan or another specific cause without examining related verses.

Something’s appearing attractive does not, by itself, establish whether it is permitted or forbidden.

The verse ends by calling these things “the enjoyment of worldly life,” directing attention to the better destination with God.

Āl ʿImrān 3:15 contrasts the blessings of the Hereafter with worldly enjoyment. Enjoyment itself is not denied; its relative worth is considered in light of the human destination.

Women and sons among desirable things

Āl ʿImrān 3:14 mentions women and sons before possessions and cultivated land.

The list can be read in relation to the social setting and familiar aspirations of its audience.

It does not make “a desire” the lexical definition of woman, or reduce her value to being an object of male desire.

Mentioning sons likewise does not imply that daughters lack value.

Ḥubb al-shahawāt concerns attraction to desirable things. The identity and value of a beloved person or thing are distinct from someone else’s affection for it.

The social or cultural features of a list should not become a fixed definition of women, children, or family.

4. Following desires: when wishes govern conduct

Al-Nisāʾ 4:27 reads:

وَاللَّهُ يُرِيدُ أَنْ يَتُوبَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَيُرِيدُ الَّذِينَ يَتَّبِعُونَ الشَّهَوَاتِ أَنْ تَمِيلُوا مَيْلًا عَظِيمًا

wa-llāhu yurīdu an yatūba ʿalaykum wa-yurīdu alladhīna yattabiʿūna al-shahawāti an tamīlū maylan ʿaẓīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:27

Translation:

“God wishes to turn toward you and accept your repentance; those who follow desires wish you to incline into a great deviation.”

Here shahawāt accompanies yattabiʿūn.

Ittibāʿ, from T B ʿ, means following.

The verse concerns people who follow desires and want others to deviate, beyond the mere existence of desire.

Having a wish and following it

Someone may want something but decide against acting after considering consequences and the limits of right conduct.

For example, a wish for more wealth need not lead to taking others’ property when the person recognizes that means as wrong.

Desire is present, but has not become the final authority over conduct.

Another person may make desire the criterion of decision, disregarding truth and justice to satisfy it.

An inward inclination then becomes the determining factor in behavior.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:27 addresses this issue.

Tamīlū maylan ʿaẓīman describes what the followers of desires want for others. Great deviation is expressed by this construction, not by SH H W alone.

Shahwah itself should therefore not be translated as “deviation.”

Following desires and neglecting prayer

Maryam 19:59 reads:

فَخَلَفَ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ خَلْفٌ أَضَاعُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَاتَّبَعُوا الشَّهَوَاتِ فَسَوْفَ يَلْقَوْنَ غَيًّا

fa-khalafa min baʿdihim khalfun aḍāʿū al-ṣalāta wa-ttabaʿū al-shahawāti fa-sawfa yalqawna ghayyā

Maryam 19:59

Translation:

“Then successors came after them who neglected prayer and followed desires; they will encounter error and its consequences.”

Here too the criticism concerns following desires, ittabaʿū al-shahawāt.

It is preceded by aḍāʿū al-ṣalāh: they neglected or squandered prayer.

Two actions appear together: neglecting a religious responsibility and following desires.

Shahawāt does not itself mean abandoning prayer; the actions are separately named.

Maryam 19:60 makes an exception for those who repent, believe, and act rightly.

The passage therefore warns of following desires while leaving open a path of return and reform.

Does restraining desire mean destroying it?

These verses do not require people to eliminate every natural inclination and wish.

Desire for food, sleep, affection, companionship, and comfort belongs to human life.

Eliminating desire differs from regulating conduct and preventing its misuse.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:31 commands eating and drinking while prohibiting excess. Al-Aʿrāf 7:32 affirms access to adornment and wholesome provisions.

The response is observance of limits and avoidance of wrongdoing, rather than destruction of human wants.

Enjoying wholesome provisions

This discussion relates to Forqan’s principles on governing one’s desires and enjoying wholesome provisions.

5. Shahwah in the account of Lot’s people: sexual desire in a particular context

Al-Aʿrāf 7:81 reads:

إِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ الرِّجَالَ شَهْوَةً مِنْ دُونِ النِّسَاءِ بَلْ أَنْتُمْ قَوْمٌ مُسْرِفُونَ

innakum la-taʾtūna al-rijāla shahwatan min dūni al-nisāʾi bal antum qawmun musrifūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:81

Translation:

“You approach men with desire instead of women; rather, you are a people exceeding bounds.”

Al-Naml 27:55 likewise reads:

أَئِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ الرِّجَالَ شَهْوَةً مِنْ دُونِ النِّسَاءِ بَلْ أَنْتُمْ قَوْمٌ تَجْهَلُونَ

a-innakum la-taʾtūna al-rijāla shahwatan min dūni al-nisāʾi bal antum qawmun tajhalūn

Al-Naml 27:55

Translation:

“Do you approach men with desire instead of women? Rather, you are a people acting ignorantly.”

In these two verses, shahwatan concerns sexual desire.

This sense comes from taʾtūn, its object al-rijāl, and min dūni al-nisāʾ, rather than the word alone.

The specific context therefore identifies sexual attraction.

The syntax of shahwah

Shahwatan is accusative in both verses.

It may explain the motive or purpose of the act—acting out of desire—or be examined as describing the condition in which the act occurs.

Either way, it identifies the role of sexual desire in the conduct under discussion.

Shahwah itself is not the name of a particular sexual act. The verb and its object identify the conduct.

The word alone does not establish the relationship’s nature, consent, the presence or absence of assault, or other particulars. Each requires examination of the relevant verses and wider narrative.

Desire and conduct

These verses describe conduct attributed to Lot’s people.

Taʾtūn is the verb; shahwatan concerns its motive or accompanying condition.

The subject is therefore an act performed with desire, beyond an inward feeling alone.

Other verses criticize their conduct from additional angles. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29 mentions cutting off the road and wrongdoing in their assembly.

These witnesses require joint consideration in a separate study of the narrative.

The lexical finding here is narrower: shahwah denotes sexual desire in these two verses, without restricting the root to sexuality throughout the Quran.

Why this meaning cannot be generalized

Considering these verses alone might suggest that Quranic shahwah always means sexual attraction.

But Āl ʿImrān 3:14 includes wealth, children, and cultivated land. The Paradise passages use the same root for food and other blessings.

Defining a root from one or two occurrences without examining the others can therefore produce an incorrect conclusion.

Shahwah names desire for something appealing. Its sexual or nonsexual character is determined by its object and context.

6. Ishtihāʾ: wanting in this world and the Hereafter

What people choose for themselves

Al-Naḥl 16:57 reads:

وَيَجْعَلُونَ لِلَّهِ الْبَنَاتِ سُبْحَانَهُ وَلَهُمْ مَا يَشْتَهُونَ

wa-yajʿalūna li-llāhi al-banāti subḥānahu wa-lahum mā yashtahūn

Al-Naḥl 16:57

Translation:

“They assign daughters to God—glory be to Him—and for themselves what they desire.”

Yashtahūn concerns what this group considers desirable for themselves.

Al-Naḥl 16:58 and Al-Naḥl 16:59 describe their adverse reaction to news of a daughter’s birth.

This context reveals their preference and double standard.

They attribute what their own judgments devalue to God, while reserving what they desire for themselves.

Ishtihāʾ here means wanting what is desirable, rather than sexual desire.

Yashtahūn does not itself mean discrimination or contempt for daughters; those emerge from the group’s statements and reactions.

Being denied what one desires

Sabaʾ 34:54 reads:

وَحِيلَ بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ مَا يَشْتَهُونَ كَمَا فُعِلَ بِأَشْيَاعِهِمْ مِنْ قَبْلُ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا فِي شَكٍّ مُرِيبٍ

wa-ḥīla baynahum wa-bayna mā yashtahūna kamā fuʿila bi-ashyāʿihim min qablu innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb

Sabaʾ 34:54

Translation:

“A barrier was placed between them and what they desire, as was done to their counterparts before; they were in disquieting doubt.”

The verse describes people no longer able to reach what they desire.

The preceding verses present them facing the consequences of denial after their earlier opportunities and choices have passed.

In light of Sabaʾ 34:52, their wish may concern belief and its acceptance at this late stage; returning to worldly life or escaping their predicament are also interpretive possibilities. The precise object must be assessed through the passage.

Yashtahūn itself expresses wanting without requiring food, sexual pleasure, or wealth as its object.

The root’s range therefore extends beyond bodily pleasures.

What the people of Paradise desire

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102 reads:

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ حَسِيسَهَا وَهُمْ فِي مَا اشْتَهَتْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ خَالِدُونَ

lā yasmaʿūna ḥasīsahā wa-hum fī mā ishtahat anfusuhum khālidūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102

Translation:

“They will not hear its faint sound, and will abide in what their souls have desired.”

Ishtahat is a feminine singular past verb; anfusuhum is its subject.

Desire is attributed to the souls of the people of Paradise.

The verb appears in a description of enjoyment and ease, rather than a blameworthy setting.

Fuṣṣilat 41:31 also reads:

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِي أَنْفُسُكُمْ وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَا تَدَّعُونَ

wa-lakum fīhā mā tashtahī anfusukum wa-lakum fīhā mā taddaʿūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:31

Translation:

“There you will have whatever your souls desire, and there you will have whatever you request.”

Tashtahī is present tense here.

The verse places two related expressions together: what souls desire and what people request.

Wanting and requesting are related, but their entire semantic distinction cannot be derived from this pairing alone. Taddaʿūn is from D ʿ W and here concerns asking or calling for something; it need not mean making an unsupported claim.

Al-Zukhruf 43:71 reads, in the Ḥafṣ reading used here:

وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ وَتَلَذُّ الْأَعْيُنُ وَأَنْتُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

wa-fīhā mā tashtahīhi al-anfusu wa-taladhdhu al-aʿyunu wa-antum fīhā khālidūn

Al-Zukhruf 43:71

Translation:

“There is whatever souls desire and eyes delight in; you will abide there.”

This passage is especially useful for understanding ishtihāʾ.

Tashtahīhi al-anfus concerns what souls want.

Taladhdhu al-aʿyun concerns delight experienced by the eyes.

Wanting and enjoying appear together, but are not the same verb.

Someone may desire something before obtaining it and enjoy it afterward.

Ishtihāʾ emphasizes inclination and wanting; pleasure emphasizes the agreeable experience gained from something.

Appetite for food in Paradise

Al-Ṭūr 52:22 reads:

وَأَمْدَدْنَاهُمْ بِفَاكِهَةٍ وَلَحْمٍ مِمَّا يَشْتَهُونَ

wa-amdadnāhum bi-fākihatin wa-laḥmin mimmā yashtahūn

Al-Ṭūr 52:22

Translation:

“We supply them with fruit and meat of what they desire.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:21 likewise reads:

وَلَحْمِ طَيْرٍ مِمَّا يَشْتَهُونَ

wa-laḥmi ṭayrin mimmā yashtahūn

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:21

Translation:

“And bird meat of what they desire.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:42 reads:

وَفَوَاكِهَ مِمَّا يَشْتَهُونَ

wa-fawākiha mimmā yashtahūn

Al-Mursalāt 77:42

Translation:

“And fruits of what they desire.”

In all three, yashtahūn concerns wanting food.

The sense is clear because the objects are food and edible blessings.

The same root used for blameworthy following of desires in worldly life describes the foods desired by the people of Paradise.

Desire for pleasant food cannot therefore be treated as inherently sinful or a moral defect.

Permissible enjoyment does not make every means of obtaining or consuming food lawful in this world. Lawfulness, wholesomeness, others’ rights, and excess require separate consideration.

Ishtihāʾ itself remains wanting something desirable.

Are heavenly desires identical to earthly desires?

These verses give the soul’s desires a place in the description of Paradise.

Shared vocabulary does not establish that every quality, limitation, and need of earthly life remains identical in the Hereafter.

Earthly appetite may arise from hunger, bodily need, or pleasure. The Paradise passages mention desirable fruit and meat without fully describing the biology of need or the manner of otherworldly enjoyment.

The meaning of wanting is clear; the nature of the Hereafter is a separate question.

Mā tashtahī al-anfus should not, without examining related verses, be taken to mean that every imaginable human wish, regardless of its object or consequences, is fulfilled unchanged there.

The verse describes the people of Paradise in the stated circumstances. Its scope and character must be understood through the Quran’s wider descriptions of the Hereafter.

7. Related roots and distinctions

SH H W (shahwah) and H W Y (hawā)

Shahwah concerns desire for something pleasant and appealing.

In its branch concerning desire, hawā denotes inward inclination across a broad range, from personal affection to judgment, belief, and intellectual preference.

Shahawāt sometimes concerns desirable blessings. Hawā and ahwāʾ appear in warnings when made authorities for conduct and judgment.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 links following inclination with departure from justice. Ṣād 38:26 forbids David from following inclination in judgment.

These passages need not concern bodily or sexual pleasure; they address treating personal preference as the standard of judgment.

By contrast, mā yashtahūn in Paradise concerns what people wish to enjoy.

This is not an absolute division: both roots overlap in the field of desire.

Forqan’s study of H W Y separately examines its semantic branches.

Study of H W Y

SH H W (shahwah) and R GH B (raghbah)

Raghbah also concerns wanting, but the direction of desire matters in its various constructions.

Raghiba fī concerns wanting something; raghiba ʿan turns away from it; raghiba ilā directs desire and hope toward someone.

Quranic ishtihāʾ chiefly concerns the soul’s attraction to what it finds desirable.

Someone may turn eagerly toward God and request guidance. Such a wish need not be called shahwah in the sense of desiring pleasure.

Wanting pleasant food, by contrast, can be expressed through ishtihāʾ.

These are tendencies, not absolute rules. The object and construction must be examined individually.

SH H W (shahwah) and Ḥ B B (love)

Maḥabbah concerns love and affection.

Shahwah concerns attraction toward something the soul finds desirable.

They may coexist without being identical.

Someone may love another person without presently seeking a particular pleasure.

Someone may also strongly desire a food without deep or lasting affection for it.

Āl ʿImrān 3:14 uses ḥubb al-shahawāt: love of desirable things.

Their pairing shows a relationship without complete identity.

SH H W (ishtihāʾ) and L DH DH (pleasure)

Pleasure concerns the agreeable experience a person gains from something.

Ishtihāʾ concerns wanting it.

Someone may desire a particular food before eating it: appetite is present, but the pleasure of eating has not yet occurred.

Al-Zukhruf 43:71 places tashtahīhi al-anfus beside taladhdhu al-aʿyun.

Wanting and enjoying remain distinct expressions despite their close relationship.

SH H W (shahwah) and M Y L (inclination)

Mayl concerns inclination or movement from one direction toward another, and also describes psychological inclination.

Shahwah more specifically concerns the soul’s attraction toward a desirable object.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:27 combines the two:

“يَتَّبِعُونَ الشَّهَوَاتِ” and “أَنْ تَمِيلُوا مَيْلًا عَظِيمًا”.

Following desires is associated here with wanting to lead others into serious deviation.

Great deviation is what this group wants for others; it is not shahwah’s own lexical meaning.

SH H W (shahwah) and SH W Q (longing)

Shawq concerns longing or attraction toward something loved or sought.

Its uses can involve separation from a beloved and a wish to meet or reach them.

Shahwah likewise involves wanting, with the object’s desirability or pleasantness prominent in its attested uses.

A person might long to see a distant friend or have an appetite for a favored food.

These experiences share features without being identical.

Shahwah should not invariably be classified as bodily and shawq as spiritual; the roots themselves do not establish that absolute division.

8. Interpretive challenges and social applications

Is every pleasurable wish wrong?

One restrictive reading treats human pleasure and desire as inherently suspect or blameworthy.

The Quran, however, describes blessings people may enjoy. Food, spouses, children, possessions, and other resources can be blessings in their proper settings.

Enjoying desirable things is also prominent in the descriptions of Paradise.

The root therefore cannot ground a universal rule that everything the soul desires must be rejected.

Such a rule would fail to account for the positive uses of ishtihāʾ in Paradise passages.

When desire becomes the criterion of decision

A person may desire something while knowing that the means of obtaining it are wrong.

For instance, wanting substantial wealth is not inherently blameworthy if someone works for it while respecting others’ rights.

Lying, taking bribes, or misusing public resources to obtain the same wealth introduces a different issue.

Desire has then led the person beyond the limits of right and justice.

Strong affection for a spouse, child, community, or group may likewise lead someone to defend wrongdoing and disregard others’ rights.

The issue in these cases is not simply having affection or desire, but making it a standard above truth and justice.

This distinction must be preserved when examining the following of desires.

Does controlling desire mean depriving people of life?

Self-restraint and respect for limits differ from artificial deprivation and unjustified severity.

People need food, rest, healthy relationships, affection, and material resources. Alongside these needs, they have wishes and interests that make life more enjoyable.

The Quran does not deny enjoyment of wholesome things, but prohibits excess, injustice, corruption, and transgression.

Restraining desire therefore does not mean destroying every wish; it means preventing desire from overriding rights and responsibility.

Someone who pursues a wish within the bounds of right and justice is not in the same position as someone who violates others’ rights to satisfy it.

A blanket judgment about shahwah should not erase this distinction.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Persian translations of shahwah, suitable choices include “inclination,” “the soul’s attraction,” “desire,” and, where appropriate, the familiar Persian term shahwat.

Shahwat is familiar in Persian, but commonly suggests sexual desire. It should not be used throughout without considering what is desired.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:81 and Al-Naml 27:55, shahwat itself is appropriate because the context concerns sexual conduct.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:14, al-shahawāt can be rendered “desirable things” or “desired objects,” since the list names things people want.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:27 and Maryam 19:59, “desires” or “the soul’s desires” is appropriate, especially because the passages concern following them.

Natural equivalents for ishtahā include “want,” “desire,” “wish for,” and, in food contexts, “have an appetite for.”

In Al-Naḥl 16:57, wa-lahum mā yashtahūn may be rendered:

“And for themselves they assign what they desire.”

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102:

“And they will abide in what their souls have desired.”

In Fuṣṣilat 41:31:

“There you will have whatever your souls desire.”

In Al-Zukhruf 43:71:

“There is whatever souls desire and eyes delight in.”

In food passages such as Al-Ṭūr 52:22, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:21, and Al-Mursalāt 77:42, “what they desire” or “what they have an appetite for” may suit the sentence.

In Sabaʾ 34:54, “what they desire” is also preferable to “sexual pleasures” or “bodily appetites,” since the verse does not restrict the object to those things.

Shahwah should not automatically become “sin,” “corruption,” “licentiousness,” or “illicit desire” in translation. Such judgments must come from the verse’s construction and context.

Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH H W concerns the soul’s inclination toward something it finds desirable and pleasant.

Poetic witnesses attributed to ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward and al-Ḥuṭayʾah use this family for wanting sleep. Its early meaning was therefore not confined to sexual desire.

The Quran uses shahwah, shahawāt, and forms of the verb ishtahā.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:14, shahawāt concerns desirable things in worldly life, from relationships and children to wealth and cultivated land.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:27 and Maryam 19:59, following desires is condemned in contexts where wishes lead to deviation and neglected responsibility.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:81 and Al-Naml 27:55, shahwah occurs in the account of sexual conduct by Lot’s people. Its sexual sense comes from the conduct and accompanying expressions, rather than an exclusive meaning of the root.

In Al-Naḥl 16:57, ishtihāʾ concerns what the group desires for itself. Sabaʾ 34:54 describes people being denied what they desire.

The Paradise passages use the same root for enjoying desired blessings: the soul’s wishes, fruit, meat, and other pleasant things.

This range shows that Quranic shahwah does not name an inherently blameworthy inclination.

Having a desire, following it, and attaining its object are three different things. Someone may want something but refrain from acting because the means are wrong. Someone may also have a sound wish and fulfill it through lawful means.

Its ethical value therefore comes from the object desired, the person’s decision, and their conduct toward truth and justice, rather than the root alone.

The resulting definition is:

SH H W denotes the soul’s desire for something it finds appealing, pleasant, or enjoyable and wishes to obtain or experience. Shahwah names this desire, shahawāt is its plural, and ishtahā expresses wanting something desirable. Such desire may be natural, commendable, or, in another context, contribute to wrongdoing; its ethical value is not determined by the root itself.

In Forqan’s Persian translation, equivalents of “inclination” and “wanting” clarify the semantic core. Shahwat should be understood specifically as sexual or blameworthy only where the verse warrants that sense.
