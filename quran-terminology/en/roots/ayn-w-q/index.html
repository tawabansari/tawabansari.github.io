Hindering, obstructing, and dissuading others from acting

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ʿ W Q concerns preventing someone from acting, hindering progress toward a destination, placing obstacles in the way, or turning someone from an intended decision or action.

ʿĀqa yaʿūqu is used when someone or something prevents another from reaching a goal. The obstacle may be physical, such as a blocked road, or nonphysical, such as fear, trouble, preoccupation, or words that deter a decision.

Someone may intend to visit a friend but be prevented by illness or other difficulties. Arabic can describe that difficulty as having hindered the visit. Someone may also discourage others from acting through their words. Both belong to this field, although their obstacles and effects differ.

Early Arabic uses ʿawq for hindrance or difficulty, ʿāʾiq for something obstructing, and ʿawāʾiq for multiple obstacles or troubles. The root extends beyond stopping physical movement to factors standing between a person and an intended action.

A witness from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to Abu Dhuayb al-Hudhali says:

أَلَا هَلْ أَتَى أُمَّ الْحُوَيْرِثِ مُرْسَلٌ
نَعَمْ خَالِدٌ إِنْ لَمْ تَعُقْهُ الْعَوَائِقُ

alā hal atā umma al-ḥuwayrithi mursalun / naʿam khālidun in lam taʿuqhu al-ʿawāʾiq

Translation: Has a messenger reached Umm al-Huwayrith? Yes, Khalid will, unless obstacles hold him back.

Taʿuqhu comes from ʿāqa; al-ʿawāʾiq is the plural of ʿāʾiqah.

The messenger intends to reach a destination, but difficulties or obstacles may intervene.

The root concerns obstructing an action's realization, not necessarily destroying someone's ability or making that action permanently impossible.

Abu Dhuayb lived through both pre-Islamic and Islamic times. The verse is recorded among poetry attributed to him and in dictionaries, but its composition date is not independently established. It witnesses early Arabic close to the revelation period, not a securely dated pre-Quranic composition.

Another transmitted poetic phrase, hammat bi-khayrin ʿāqat ʿawāʾiquhā, means “she intended something good, but her obstacles held her back.” It is sometimes attributed to Umayyah ibn Abi al-Salt, but the attribution is disputed and a pre-Islamic date cannot be assumed.

Despite that dating limitation, early linguistic witnesses and Quranic usage support hindering and placing obstacles before an action as the principal meaning discussed here.

The semantic core

The core of ʿ W Q can be defined as:

An obstacle standing or being placed before someone's movement, decision, or action, stopping or slowing progress or diverting it from its intended course.

This includes two situations:

First, an obstacle arising in someone's way, such as illness, difficulty, or an event preventing arrival.

Second, a person deliberately or through their conduct hindering others, for example by frightening, discouraging, or inviting them to withdraw.

Both concern hindrance. Whether it is justified or wrongful, helpful or harmful, depends on the action, purpose, and circumstances rather than the root alone.

2. Forms and derivatives

Arabic records several forms in this family.

ʿĀqa yaʿūqu: hindered, obstructed, or dissuaded someone from something.

ʿAwq: hindrance, an obstacle, or a difficulty.

ʿĀʾiq: a person or thing obstructing an action.

ʿAwāʾiq: obstacles and difficulties making a goal harder or impossible to reach.

ʿAwwaqa yuʿawwiqu: hindered or obstructed; depending on use, creating barriers, holding others back, or discouraging them.

Taʿwīq: hindering, obstructing, delaying, or stopping progress.

Muʿawwiq: someone who obstructs or prevents others from acting.

Iʿtāqa and taʿawwaqa: other forms recorded for hindering or being held back. In transitive usage, iʿtāqahu means “he hindered him”; in intransitive usage, taʿawwaqa can describe being delayed or held back.

Some lexical uses describe a person who holds others back as ʿuwaqah. Expressions such as ʿawāʾiq al-dahr refer to life's troubles and obstacles.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran.

The Quran's derivative expressing hindering occurs once: the plural active participle al-muʿawwiqīn in Al-Aḥzāb 33:18. It is a nominal derivative, not a finite verb.

Nūḥ 71:23 also contains the proper name Yaʿūq, which requires separate treatment: a proper name's occurrence does not constitute verbal usage.

3. The Quranic use of al-muʿawwiqīn

Holding others back from confrontation and responsibility

قَدْ يَعْلَمُ اللَّهُ الْمُعَوِّقِينَ مِنْكُمْ وَالْقَائِلِينَ لِإِخْوَانِهِمْ هَلُمَّ إِلَيْنَا وَلا يَأْتُونَ الْبَأْسَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

qad yaʿlamu allāhu al-muʿawwiqīna minkum wa-l-qāʾilīna li-ikhwānihim halumma ilaynā wa-lā yaʾtūna al-baʾsa illā qalīlā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18

The God certainly knows those among you who hinder, and those who tell their brothers, “Come to us”; they enter the battle only a little.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18

The surrounding verses describe people confronting attacking groups in difficult wartime conditions.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:9 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 describe the armies' arrival and the intense fear people faced.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:12 mentions hypocrites and those with disease in their hearts. Al-Aḥzāb 33:13 describes people calling others to return and some seeking permission to leave on the pretext of exposed houses.

In that setting, Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 speaks of al-muʿawwiqīn.

These are not merely people afraid to participate themselves: the described conduct also calls others toward them and discourages collective participation.

Halumma ilaynā means “come to us.” In context, it invites withdrawal from the confrontation and joining those avoiding it.

Al-muʿawwiqīn and al-qāʾilīn are coordinated accusative descriptions. They may characterize people engaged in related conduct, but coordination alone establishes neither two entirely separate groups nor complete identity between them.

The clear connection is between hindering others and inviting withdrawal.

Being unable to act versus hindering others

This use distinguishes personal inability or refusal from attempts to prevent others from acting.

Illness, disability, or real-life circumstances may prevent participation. That alone does not make someone a muʿawwiq in the verse's sense.

A person who holds others back through words or conduct plays a different role.

Personally avoiding a difficult situation through fear differs from repeatedly frightening others or inviting them to abandon their responsibilities.

The verse emphasizes the latter behavior.

Not every warning about danger or an action's consequences is blameworthy obstruction. Preventing a dangerous decision can sometimes be right and necessary.

The negative judgment in Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 comes from the confrontation, the invitation to withdraw, and the surrounding descriptions, not from the root being inherently negative in every context.

The following contextual description

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 describes these people's behavior during fear and after it passes.

Danger overwhelms them with fear; once it passes, they appear with sharp tongues and changed conduct.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 also describes their wish to be far from the confrontation.

Taʿwīq here is therefore more than a simple delay. It belongs to a wider pattern of avoiding hardship while discouraging others from joining.

Fear itself is not taʿwīq. It may be a human feeling, whereas hindering others is conduct assessed through its purpose, circumstances, and consequences.

4. The morphology of muʿawwiqīn and the passive participle

Al-muʿawwiqīn is the sound masculine plural active participle of Form II ʿawwaqa.

Its singular is muʿawwiq.

The person performs the act of hindering: creating an obstacle or holding others back.

Form II can express intensity, repetition, or causation in certain uses, but none of those nuances should be mechanically assumed from the pattern alone.

Here the active role emerges from the participle, the meaning of hindering, and the context of inviting others to withdraw.

Muʿawwiq and muʿawwaq

The vowel on the doubled wāw is significant:

Muʿawwiq is active: one who hinders.

Muʿawwaq is passive: someone or something hindered or facing an obstacle.

They may look identical without vowel marks, but differ morphologically and semantically.

Some modern Arabic uses apply the passive form to people with disabilities. That usage must not be confused with Quranic al-muʿawwiqīn.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 concerns those hindering others, not people unable to act because of physical disability or personal limitations.

Accurate translation requires this distinction.

5. Yaʿūq in the Quran: a proper name, not a verb

وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

wa-qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa-lā tadharunna waddan wa-lā suwāʿan wa-lā yaghūtha wa-yaʿūqa wa-nasrā

Nūḥ 71:23

They said: Do not abandon your deities; do not abandon Wadd, Suwa, Yaghuth, Yaʿūq, or Nasr.

Nūḥ 71:23

Yaʿūq names one of the deities Noah's people were urged not to abandon.

Its spelling resembles the imperfect verb yaʿūqu from ʿ W Q, but it is not a verb here.

Alongside Wadd, Suwa, Yaghuth, and Nasr, it names a deity mentioned in the people's speech.

Translation should therefore preserve the proper name Yaʿūq.

Is Yaʿūq derived from ʿ W Q?

Arabic dictionaries commonly place Yaʿūq under ʿ W Q. Its form warrants examination, but that placement alone does not establish the name's historical origin.

Even if originally formed from hindering, this would not establish that Noah's people necessarily understood the deity as “the hinderer” or attributed that characteristic to it.

The verse records several deity names without explaining their formation, historical meanings, or supposed powers.

Accounts outside the Quran relate stories about these deities and the rise of their worship. Without independent evidence, such accounts do not establish Yaʿūq's lexical meaning or a certain naming history.

Root study should therefore distinguish:

Al-muʿawwiqīn, a clear derivative of ʿ W Q carrying the meaning of hindering.

Yaʿūq, a proper name whose historical lexical meaning is not supplied by the sentence.

This prevents unsupported historical or interpretive conclusions from a name's resemblance to an Arabic verb.

6. Neighboring roots

ʿ W Q (ʿawq) and M N ʿ (manʿ)

M N ʿ denotes preventing, withholding, or blocking access.

ʿ W Q shares this field, emphasizing obstruction of an intended action or its realization in familiar uses.

Closing a door to prevent entry can be described as manʿ.

Discouraging someone from traveling or fulfilling a responsibility clearly illustrates taʿwīq.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary: both can concern preventing an action.

ʿ W Q (ʿawq) and Ṣ D D (ṣadd)

Quranic Ṣ D D concerns turning away, barring, or diverting from a path.

In expressions about barring The God's way, it can emphasize preventing others from following that course.

ʿ W Q also concerns hindering action; Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 specifically describes people discouraging participation in confrontation.

Both can concern social behavior and influence on decisions, but this overlap does not make them synonymous in every construction.

ʿ W Q (ʿawq) and TH B Ṭ (tathbīṭ)

TH B Ṭ concerns holding back, slowing, and discouraging.

Al-Tawbah 9:46 uses fa-thabbaṭahum for a group being held back from setting out and joining.

Taʿwīq and tathbīṭ can overlap in stopping movement or reducing readiness to act.

The Quranic ʿ W Q passage emphasizes people hindering others, while Al-Tawbah 9:46 concerns the group itself being held back.

This difference depends partly on context and should not become an absolute rule governing the roots.

ʿ W Q (ʿawq) and ʿ Ṭ L (ʿaṭal)

ʿ Ṭ L concerns being deprived of something normally accompanying or sustaining it; one branch describes disuse or abandonment.

Al-Ḥajj 22:45, for example, describes an abandoned, unused well as biʾrin muʿaṭṭalah.

ʿ Ṭ L may describe the state of disuse itself, whereas ʿ W Q emphasizes an obstacle preventing action or attainment.

An unused or broken device is not automatically an instance of taʿwīq, unless the construction presents it as preventing someone from acting.

Similar-looking roots

ʿ W Q is distinct from ʿ Q Q. ʿUqūq belongs to a different family and should not be equated with taʿwīq because of similar letters.

It also differs from W Q ʿ: changing the order produces another root.

Al-ʿAyyūq names a star in Arabic. Lexical discussions relate its formation to ʿ W Q or ʿ Y Q, so it cannot establish this root's historical origin without separate examination. The name does not occur in the Quran.

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

Equivalents should reflect the kind of obstacle and the word's role in the sentence.

ʿĀqa can mean “hindered,” “prevented,” or “dissuaded from acting.”

ʿĀʾiq naturally corresponds to “obstacle” or Persian māneʿ.

ʿAwāʾiq can be “obstacles” or “hindering difficulties.”

ʿAwwaqa can contextually mean “hindered,” “obstructed,” “delayed,” or “discouraged,” but the last two do not cover its full range.

Taʿwīq can be “hindering,” “obstruction,” or “delay,” according to context.

For al-muʿawwiqīn in Al-Aḥzāb 33:18, the proposed short Persian equivalent is bāzdārandegān, “those who hinder.”

It preserves active agency without confusing it with those left behind, unable to act, or living with limitations.

The Persian māneʿ-tarāshān, “obstructionists,” can clarify context but carries stronger negative coloring. Bāzdārandegān is preferable for the basic rendering, with the conduct explained separately.

Yaʿūq in Nūḥ 71:23 should remain a proper name. Replacing it with “the hinderer” or another attribute inserts a meaning the verse does not state.

8. Conclusion

The principal branch of ʿ W Q concerns hindering and obstructing an action. The obstacle may be physical, social, or related to decisions and behavior.

Early Arabic witnesses use ʿāʾiq and ʿawāʾiq for difficulties preventing arrival or action. Some poetic dates remain uncertain and should not be assumed pre-Quranic without examination.

The clear Quranic derivative is al-muʿawwiqīn in Al-Aḥzāb 33:18: people holding others back from participation during a difficult confrontation and calling them toward themselves.

Condemnation arises from the context and their conduct, not from every warning or act of prevention being blameworthy.

Active muʿawwiq must remain distinct from passive muʿawwaq, “hindered,” preventing the Quranic word from being misapplied to personal inability or physical disability.

Yaʿūq in Nūḥ 71:23 names a deity. Resemblance to yaʿūqu does not suffice to determine its historical meaning or attributed qualities.

Final definition of ʿ W Q:

ʿ W Q denotes hindering movement, a decision, or an action so that the person or thing is stopped, slowed, or diverted from a destination, course, or intended act. Muʿawwiq attributes this to an active hinderer. Whether the hindrance is good or bad depends on its purpose, circumstances, and consequences, not the root alone.
