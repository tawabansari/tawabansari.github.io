Q T R has two prominent branches in early Arabic. The first concerns straitened livelihood, limited resources, and restrictive spending; the second concerns dust, smoke, darkness, and the smell rising from burning or roasting something.

A verse attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, a pre-Islamic poet, describes his people’s hospitality:

حِينَ قَالَ النَّاسُ فِي مَجْلِسِهِمْ
أَقُتَارٌ ذَاكَ أَمْ رِيحُ قُطُرْ

Ḥīna qāla l-nāsu fī majlisihim / a-qutārun dhāka am rīḥu quṭur.

When the people in their gathering said: “Is that the smell of roasting meat, or the fragrance of aloeswood?”

Qutār here denotes the smell rising from roasting meat. In the surrounding passage, the poet compares the pleasant smell of food during winter’s cold with the fragrance of aloeswood.

This transmitted poetic witness illustrates the smell-and-smoke branch in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, without independently fixing its precise date. Straitened livelihood and restrictive spending are also recorded in classical Arabic, with the Qur’an clearly attesting them in the period of revelation. Their lexical documentation alone does not establish a securely dated, independent pre-Islamic witness for this financial branch.

Forms examined: qatara (قَتَرَ), qattara (قَتَّرَ), aqtara (أَقْتَرَ), qatr (قَتْر), qatar (قَتَر), qatarah (قَتَرَة), qutār (قُتَار), qatūr (قَتُور), muqtir (مُقْتِر), taqtīr (تَقْتِير), and iqtār (إِقْتَار).

Qur’anic forms: yaqturū, muqtir, qatūr, qatar, and qatarah. Each occurs once, giving five occurrences in total.

1. Core meaning and semantic branches

Qatara ʿalā ahlihi means restricting one’s family’s living expenses. Aqtara al-rajulu can mean that a man became poor or his resources diminished.

Another material usage is qutrah, a small, confined shelter in which a hunter hides.

These uses relate to narrowness, restriction, and confinement.

In the other branch, qatar and qatarah denote dust and smoke-like gloom, while qutār names the smell rising from roasted meat and similar things.

Arabic lexicographers have tried to relate all these uses to gathering and restriction, but their historical relationship is not definitively established. The definition should therefore retain both principal branches.

2. Limited means and stinginess: two different conditions

Muqtir: a person with limited resources

لَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِنْ طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ مَا لَمْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ أَوْ تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً ۚ وَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ عَلَى الْمُوسِعِ قَدَرُهُ وَعَلَى الْمُقْتِرِ قَدَرُهُ مَتَاعًا بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۖ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُحْسِنِينَ

Lā junāḥa ʿalaykum in ṭallaqtumu l-nisāʾa mā lam tamassūhunna aw tafriḍū lahunna farīḍatan. Wa-mattiʿūhunna ʿalā l-mūsiʿi qadaruhu wa-ʿalā l-muqtiri qadaruhu matāʿan bi-l-maʿrūfi ḥaqqan ʿalā l-muḥsinīn.

There is no blame upon you if you divorce women when you have not yet touched them or prescribed an obligation for them; and provide for them—the affluent according to his means, and the one of limited means according to his means—an honorable provision, a duty upon those who do good.

Al-Baqarah 2:236

Muqtir is the active participle of aqtara. Here it describes someone with limited financial resources.

The Qur’an contrasts it with mūsiʿ, someone whose livelihood affords financial scope and ability.

Muqtir here does not mean stingy or unwilling to pay another’s due. Poverty is an economic condition, not a morally blameworthy character trait.

The verse relates each person’s financial responsibility to their capacity. The person of limited means is not asked for what exceeds their resources; the affluent cannot evade their responsibility by giving a negligible amount.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:7 expresses the same principle: a person of ample means should spend from that abundance, and someone whose provision is restricted should spend from what The God has given them.

Qatūr: one who restricts spending out of fear

قُلْ لَوْ أَنْتُمْ تَمْلِكُونَ خَزَائِنَ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّي إِذًا لَأَمْسَكْتُمْ خَشْيَةَ الْإِنْفَاقِ ۚ وَكَانَ الْإِنْسَانُ قَتُورًا

Qul law antum tamlikūna khazāʾina raḥmati rabbī idhan la-amsaktum khashyata l-infāqi wa-kāna l-insānu qatūrā.

Say, “If you possessed the treasuries of my Lord’s mercy, you would hold them back for fear of spending.” The human is exceedingly tight-fisted.

Al-Isra 17:100

Qatūr follows the pattern faʿūl, describing an intense tendency toward withholding in this context.

Khashyata al-infāq identifies the motive: fear of spending and losing what one possesses.

The Qur’an presents a hypothetical situation: even if people possessed the treasuries of the Lord’s mercy, they would retain them for fear of spending.

Such a person’s problem is not a lack of wealth, but their attitude toward it.

Someone with few resources may give from that little, while a wealthy person may continually fear poverty and refuse to give.

The former can be muqtir without being qatūr; the latter can be qatūr without being muqtir.

3. Restrictive spending and excess: the appropriate measure

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا أَنْفَقُوا لَمْ يُسْرِفُوا وَلَمْ يَقْتُرُوا وَكَانَ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ قَوَامًا

Wa-lladhīna idhā anfaqū lam yusrifū wa-lam yaqturū wa-kāna bayna dhālika qawāmā.

Those who, when they spend, are neither excessive nor restrictive, and whose spending stands between the two in a balanced measure.

Al-Furqan 25:67

In describing the servants of the Entirely Merciful, the Qur’an contrasts two unsuitable behaviors: isrāf and taqtīr.

Isrāf exceeds the appropriate limit; taqtīr here restricts or reduces expenditure that is fitting or necessary.

The transmitted readings include yaqturū, yaqtirū, and yuqtirū. The first two belong to Form I, and the third to Form IV. All express restrictive spending in this setting.

Infāq here is not confined to financial help for the poor. It also includes expenditure in one’s own life and toward others.

For example, someone who has sufficient means but deprives their family of suitable food, education, or necessary care is not economical merely because they spend little. Such behavior can exemplify taqtīr.

Conversely, someone with a limited income who adjusts expenditure to their resources is not blamed merely for spending little.

Qawāman denotes a measure and manner that sustain life and responsibility. This balance is not necessarily the arithmetic midpoint between two sums; it is understood in relation to ability, need, rights, and obligations.

Avoiding excess must not become an excuse for restrictive spending, nor should avoiding restriction lead to excess.

4. Qatar and qatarah: gloom on the face

The Qur’an’s second branch describes people’s faces in the Hereafter.

The faces of those who do good

لِلَّذِينَ أَحْسَنُوا الْحُسْنَىٰ وَزِيَادَةٌ ۖ وَلَا يَرْهَقُ وُجُوهَهُمْ قَتَرٌ وَلَا ذِلَّةٌ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ ۖ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

Li-lladhīna aḥsanū l-ḥusnā wa-ziyādatun wa-lā yarhaqu wujūhahum qatarun wa-lā dhillatun. Ulāʾika aṣḥābu l-jannati hum fīhā khālidūn.

For those who do good is the finest reward and an increase. Neither gloom nor humiliation will cover their faces. They are the inhabitants of the Garden, remaining there.

Yunus 10:26

Qatar here denotes gloom or a dust-like darkening covering the face.

Yarhaqu, from R H Q, expresses covering or overtaking. Qatar and humiliation are the two things denied any covering of the faces of those who do good.

Qatar denotes gloom on the face; dhillah denotes humiliation and abasement. They can accompany one another without having the same lexical meaning.

Dust-covered and gloomy faces

وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ عَلَيْهَا غَبَرَةٌ

Wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin ʿalayhā ghabarah.

And faces that day will have dust upon them.

'Abasa 80:40

تَرْهَقُهَا قَتَرَةٌ

Tarhaquhā qatarah.

Gloom will cover them.

'Abasa 80:41

أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْكَفَرَةُ الْفَجَرَةُ

Ulāʾika humu l-kafaratu l-fajarah.

Those are the disbelieving, the wicked.

'Abasa 80:42

These verses place ghabarah and qatarah alongside one another.

Ghabarah pictures dust upon the face; qatarah emphasizes its covering gloom. Arabic lexicons also describe qatarah as dust accompanied by smoke-like darkness.

Yūnus 10:26 denies that qatar will cover the faces of those who do good, while ʿAbasa 80:41 describes qatarah covering the faces of the contrasting group.

Both passages also use a verb from R H Q, reinforcing the image of a covering that overtakes the face.

This gloom belongs to the resurrection setting and should not be confused with natural skin color. The passage concerns the condition of faces on the day of reckoning, not racial features or people’s appearance in this world.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q T R (taqtīr) and B KH L (bukhl)

Bukhl concerns withholding something one could give to another. Taqtīr places more emphasis on restricting expenditure or giving.

Both can characterize someone who has sufficient means but refuses necessary expenditure.

Muqtir in Al-Baqarah 2:236, however, means a person of limited means. Limited resources alone do not make someone stingy.

Q T R (taqtīr) and S R F (isrāf)

Al-Furqān 25:67 explicitly contrasts the two.

Isrāf exceeds the appropriate measure; taqtīr in this contrast unduly restricts expenditure.

The amount spent alone does not determine its value. A large expenditure can be appropriate to need and responsibility, while a small one can, in some circumstances, still be excessive.

Q T R (qatr) and Q D R (qadr)

In livelihood contexts, qadr can express measuring out and restricting provision.

In Al-Baqarah 2:236, muqtir describes a person of limited resources, while qadaruhu denotes that person’s capacity.

Together, the words show that limited resources do not eliminate responsibility, but help determine its extent.

Q T R (taqtīr) and M S K (imsāk)

Imsāk means retaining and refraining from release. In financial contexts, taqtīr denotes restrictive spending.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:100, imsāk describes holding back resources, while qatūr describes the disposition from which this behavior arises.

Not every act of retention is taqtīr. Keeping part of one’s income for future needs can be prudent; withholding someone’s due or necessary expenditure despite having the means is different.

Q T R (qatarah) and GH B R (ghabarah)

Ghabarah denotes dust or dustiness; qatarah in Sūrat ʿAbasa emphasizes the gloom covering the face.

Although the words overlap in lexical usage, their occurrence in consecutive verses makes it useful to preserve the distinction between dustiness and gloom in translation.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

  • Al-muqtir, Al-Baqarah 2:236: the person of limited means.
  • Qatūran, Al-Isrāʾ 17:100: exceedingly tight-fisted.
  • Lam yaqturū, Al-Furqān 25:67: do not restrict spending.
  • Qatar, Yūnus 10:26: gloom.
  • Qatarah, ʿAbasa 80:41: gloom.

Brief root meaning: Straitened livelihood, limited resources, and restrictive expenditure or giving; in another branch, dust, smoke-like gloom, and the smell of burning or roasting.

Conclusion

Q T R has two Qur’anic branches: financial straits and restrictive spending; and gloom or dust-like darkness that covers the face.

In the first, muqtir describes economic circumstances, qatūr a tendency to withhold, and yaqturū behavior in spending.

In the second, qatar and qatarah describe faces darkened in the resurrection scene.

An important distinction is that the Qur’an does not condemn limited means. It condemns undue restriction and withholding expenditure where needs, rights, and responsibilities call for it.

Poverty is a shortage of resources, whereas stinginess can exist amid abundant resources.
