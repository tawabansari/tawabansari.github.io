Smallness in size, age, and quantity: from natural littleness to subordination, abasement, and loss of standing

The Arabic root Ṣ GH R concerns smallness, scantness, and a lower position in comparison. An object may be small in size, a person young in age, a sum of money little compared with another, or someone may lose standing and become abased and subordinate.

This family includes ṣaghīr, small or young; aṣghar, smaller; ṣighar, smallness; ṣaghār, abasement and subordination; and ṣāghir, someone brought into an abased, subordinate condition.

The root occurs thirteen times in thirteen Qur’anic verses, in five lexical families: ṣaghīr three times, ṣaghīrah twice, aṣghar twice, ṣaghār once, and plural forms of ṣāghir five times.

Its Qur’anic uses have two main domains: smallness in size, age, and quantity, and low standing in the sense of abasement and subordination.

These domains are related but must not be equated. A young child is not necessarily abased, nor is an abased person necessarily physically small. Social and moral uses also require a distinction between voluntary humility, defeat and subordination to power, and being humiliated.

1. The semantic core: smallness in comparison

Ṣighar versus kibar

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes about this family:

يَدُلُّ عَلَى قِلَّةٍ وَحَقَارَةٍ

yadullu ʿalā qillatin wa-ḥaqāratin

That is, the root denotes scantness and low estimation or standing.

He then contrasts ṣighar with kibar and ṣaghīr with kabīr.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise contrasts ṣighar and ṣaghārah with greatness; some lexicographers, however, distinguish bodily smallness, ṣighar, from low estimation and rank, ṣaghārah.

This distinction matters when understanding the word family.

Physically, one stone may be small compared with another.

In age, a person is young compared with an adult.

In quantity, one expenditure is little compared with another.

In standing, however, the issue is not bodily size or an amount of money, but someone’s rank, reputation, capacity, or position in relation to others.

Smallness is relative

An important feature of ṣaghīr is that smallness is often understood by comparison.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī explains that something may be small compared with one thing and large compared with another.

A stone small beside a mountain may, for example, be very large beside a seed.

Likewise, a sum of money little to one person may be valuable to another.

In uses concerning age, childhood and adulthood relate to a person’s stage of life.

Ṣaghīr alone therefore does not determine a fixed number, specified size, or definitive value.

The subject and context establish the comparison’s criterion.

Does smallness mean worthlessness?

No.

This is one of the root’s most important distinctions.

Something may be small yet very valuable.

A small seed can matter greatly to a large plant’s growth. A little money may be valuable to someone in need. A child may be physically small and young, but neither feature determines that child’s human worth.

Ṣaghār and ṣāghir, by contrast, denote subordination and abasement in some Arabic uses.

The root’s meanings must therefore not all be reduced to worthlessness.

Small size, little quantity, and lowered standing are three related uses, not one identical judgment about someone’s or something’s value.

2. Uses in early Arabic language and poetry

Actual smallness: a witness from Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

The well-known pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

صِغَارِ النَّوَى مَكْنُوزَةٍ لَيْسَ قِشْرُهَا
إِذَا طَارَ قِشْرُ التَّمْرِ عَنْهَا بِطَائِرِ

ṣighāri al-nawā maknūzatin laysa qishruhā / idhā ṭāra qishru al-tamri ʿanhā bi-ṭāʾiri

A rendering close to the sense: “Plump, compact dates with small stones, whose skins do not come away when the skins of other dates peel off.”

Ṣighār al-nawā here refers to the dates’ small stones.

Ṣighār is the plural of ṣaghīr and has a physical, dimensional meaning.

The use contains no idea of abasement, humiliation, or low rank.

It shows that pre-Islamic Arabic used forms of smallness to describe objects’ actual size.

The vowels also matter: ṣighār here means small ones, whereas ṣaghār, with a fatḥah on ṣād, denotes abasement and subordination in another use.

Vocalization and context prevent the meanings from being confused.

Nihāyat al-Arab: Al-Nābighah’s witness about dates

Small versus large: Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī’s witness

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī says:

يُطِيفُ بِنُصْبِهِمْ حُجُنٌ صِغَارٌ
فَقَدْ كَادَتْ حَوَاجِبُهُمْ تَشِيبُ

yuṭīfu bi-nuṣbihim ḥujunun ṣighārun / fa-qad kādat ḥawājibuhum tashību

A cautious rendering: “Small, bent figures circle their cult-stone; their eyebrows have almost turned gray.” Precisely identifying ḥujun requires commentary.

Ṣighār here qualifies ḥujun and expresses smallness; this single line does not by itself determine exactly what is being described or all its implications.

Identifying ḥujun and judging whether the line also carries satire or contempt requires the fuller poem and commentary; it should not be treated as an entirely neutral image solely because ṣighār occurs.

The witness establishes the presence of the word for smallness in early Arabic, while any additional judgment must be examined separately.

Contempt therefore cannot be extracted merely from the presence of Ṣ GH R in an expression.

Ṣaghār: abasement and subordination

In the family’s social branch, ṣaghār denotes abasement, subordination, and loss of standing.

Ibn Fāris explains ṣāghir as:

الرَّاضِي بِالضَّيْمِ

al-rāḍī bi-al-ḍaymi

That is, someone who has entered a condition of abasement or oppression and accepted it.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ṣaghār through dhull and ḍaym, abasement and oppressive injury.

“Accepting abasement” should not, however, always be understood as heartfelt, voluntary satisfaction.

Someone may become subordinate through defeat, threats, or another’s power without inwardly welcoming that condition.

Ṣāghir can therefore more broadly describe someone in an abased, defeated, or subordinate position. Inward acceptance or resistance must be established from context.

Ṣaghghara and istaṣghara

Ṣaghghara al-shayʾa in Arabic means making something small.

Istaṣgharahu means considering it small.

The constructions differ.

Making small may concern an actual change of size.

Considering small concerns judgment and evaluation: something may actually be small, or someone may underestimate its true worth.

Socially, ṣaghgharahu fī aʿyuni al-nāsi means making someone appear insignificant and discredited in people’s eyes.

This can involve humiliation, but considering small need not concern a person’s character; a matter’s amount or importance may also be underestimated.

Quiet and loud sounds: iṣghār and ikbār

A less common branch describes an animal’s voice.

Arabic records aṣgharat al-nāqatu for a female camel’s low moan or cry, and akbarat for its loud cry.

Al-Khansāʾ says:

حَنِينَ وَالِهَةٍ ضَلَّتْ أَلِيفَتَهَا
لَهَا حَنِينَانِ إِصْغَارٌ وَإِكْبَارُ

ḥanīna wālihatin ḍallat alīfatahā / lahā ḥanīnāni iṣghārun wa-ikbāru

Translation: “Like the moaning of a distraught female animal that has lost its companion; she has two cries, one low and one loud.”

Smallness and greatness here concern neither bodily size nor social standing, but differences in vocal intensity.

Al-Khansāʾ lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without examining this line’s precise date, it cannot be treated as a definitely pre-Qur’anic witness.

Its appearance in early sources nevertheless shows that small and large could describe degrees of sound.

This branch must not be confused with iṣghāʾ, listening attentively, which belongs to the separate Ṣ GH W family.

3. Smallness of age, size, and quantity in the Qur’an

Al-Baqarah 2:282: small or large debts

Al-Baqarah 2:282 commands recording debts contracted for a fixed term.

One part says not to become weary of writing the debt down, whether small or large.

Ṣaghīran here concerns the amount of the debt or right to be recorded, not the debtor’s age, social standing, or personal worth.

Kabīran, large, appears in contrast.

The two qualifiers therefore express the range of financial amounts.

A small debt does not independently make its owner’s right unimportant.

In context, recording small and large amounts relates to justice, stronger testimony, and avoiding doubt.

These features follow from the verse’s separate commands, not from ṣaghīr’s lexical meaning.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:24: young age and childhood

وَاخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ وَقُلْ رَبِّ ارْحَمْهُمَا كَمَا رَبَّيَانِي صَغِيرًا

wa-ikhfiḍ lahumā janāḥa al-dhulli mina al-raḥmati wa-qul rabbi irḥamhumā ka-mā rabbayānī ṣaghīran

Translation: Lower for them the wing of humility out of mercy, and say: “My Lord, have mercy upon them as they raised me when I was small.”

Ṣaghīran here refers to the speaker’s age while being raised by the parents.

It is accusative and describes the speaker’s condition during that upbringing.

“In childhood” or “while I was a young child” is therefore appropriate.

Ṣaghīran itself does not mean powerless, worthless, or abased.

A child may depend on adult care and help for many needs, but that dependence is not an independent component of the root’s meaning.

Ṣaghīran versus al-dhull in the same verse

This verse matters because it places two related but distinct concepts together.

Ṣaghīran refers to childhood.

Al-dhull belongs to DH L L and, in janāḥa al-dhulli mina al-raḥmati, concerns humility and gentleness toward one’s parents.

Young age and behavioral humility are not identical here.

Humility out of mercy also differs from abasement through humiliation and force.

Shared ideas of low position or lowering therefore do not make childhood, humility, and abasement the same condition.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, childhood recalls a period of upbringing; humility is the conduct requested of the child toward the parents.

Al-Ṭabarī: childhood and humility toward parents

Study of KH F Ḍ

Al-Tawbah 9:121: small and large expenditures

This verse concerns expenditures made along the way under discussion and recorded for those who make them.

Nafaqatan ṣaghīratan contrasts with kabīratan.

Ṣaghīrah here qualifies an expenditure.

Smallness therefore concerns the amount of money, not the spender’s age or social standing.

The context indicates that a little expenditure does not make the action disregarded.

This follows from the complete verse and its account of deeds being recorded, not because ṣaghīr lexically means deserving a reward.

Al-Kahf 18:49: small and large in the record of deeds

Al-Kahf 18:49 describes the record of deeds and the criminals’ reaction.

They are astonished at a record leaving nothing small or large uncounted.

Ṣaghīratan denotes a small thing or act identified by the context of recording deeds.

Kabīratan appears in contrast.

Smallness and greatness here can concern deeds’ scale and significance.

The smallness of an action must nevertheless be distinguished from its moral value.

Some legal and moral writings use ṣaghīrah and kabīrah as technical categories of sins.

This technical usage must not be imposed on the verse’s translation without evidence.

The verse concerns recording and counting small and great matters; ṣaghīrah alone does not mean the technical category “minor sin.”

Al-Qamar 54:53: small and large in the record of events

وَكُلُّ صَغِيرٍ وَكَبِيرٍ مُسْتَطَرٌ

wa-kullu ṣaghīrin wa-kabīrin mustaṭarun

Translation: Every small and great thing is written and recorded.

The preceding verse, Al-Qamar 54:52, mentions recording what they did.

Al-Qamar 54:53 then uses the general expression kullu ṣaghīrin wa-kabīrin.

Ṣaghīr and kabīr contrast, showing the record’s comprehensive scope.

The words alone do not determine each act’s precise kind or numerical amount.

Nor should every small thing be restricted to a minor sin and every great thing to a major sin unless context and subject require that limitation.

The important feature is comprehensive recording, not a fixed table defining sins’ size or degree.

4. Aṣghar: smaller by comparison

Aṣghar is the family’s comparative form and means smaller.

It occurs twice in the Qur’an, in Yūnus 10:61 and Sabaʾ 34:3.

Both verses speak of something smaller than a dharrah and something larger.

Yūnus 10:61: something smaller than a dharrah

The verse concerns the reach of God’s knowledge and nothing being hidden from Him.

After mithqāli dharratin, it says wa-lā aṣghara min dhālika wa-lā akbara.

Aṣghara expresses smallness relative to the preceding measure.

Dharrah is therefore not presented as an ultimate boundary or the smallest possible size: the verse itself speaks of something smaller.

The word dharrah alone thus cannot establish that the Qur’an considers it the world’s smallest indivisible component.

Conversely, the verse does not give a scientific definition of the smallest particles’ size or the structure of matter.

The clear point is that no amount, small or great, is hidden from God’s knowledge.

Sabaʾ 34:3: the same comparison in the context of resurrection

Sabaʾ 34:3 likewise says wa-lā aṣgharu min dhālika wa-lā akbaru.

Some deny the Hour’s coming; the Qur’anic response emphasizes its coming and the Lord’s comprehensive knowledge.

Aṣghar retains the meaning smaller.

In the Ḥafṣ reading, however, its grammatical ending differs: aṣghara with fatḥah in Yūnus 10:61, aṣgharu with ḍammah in Sabaʾ 34:3.

This concerns sentence structure, not two independent words with different meanings.

Both verses compare size, not abasement, humiliation, or low moral standing.

5. Ṣaghār and ṣāghir: abasement and subordination

The second branch of Qur’anic uses concerns neither bodily smallness nor quantity, but lowered standing, defeat, and abasement.

Ṣaghār is a noun or verbal noun for this condition.

Ṣāghir describes someone in that condition.

They occupy the same semantic field but have different grammatical roles.

Al-Anʿām 6:124: abasement before God

وَإِذَا جَاءَتْهُمْ آيَةٌ قَالُوا لَنْ نُؤْمِنَ حَتَّىٰ نُؤْتَىٰ مِثْلَ مَا أُوتِيَ رُسُلُ اللَّهِ اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ حَيْثُ يَجْعَلُ رِسَالَتَهُ سَيُصِيبُ الَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا صَغَارٌ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَعَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ بِمَا كَانُوا يَمْكُرُونَ

wa-idhā jāʾathum āyatun qālū lan nuʾmina ḥattā nuʾtā mithla mā ūtiya rusulu allāhi allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatahu sa-yuṣību alladhīna ajramū ṣaghārun ʿinda allāhi wa-ʿadhābun shadīdun bi-mā kānū yamkurūna

Translation: When a sign comes to them, they say: “We will never believe until we are given the like of what God’s messengers were given.” God knows best where to place His message. Those who committed crimes will be struck by abasement before God and severe punishment because of their scheming.

Ṣaghārun here means abasement and loss of standing.

Grammatically, ṣaghārun is the delayed subject of sa-yuṣību, and alladhīna ajramū is its object.

The sentence thus means that abasement will befall the criminals.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains it through humiliation and abasement.

Why is abasement mentioned here?

The opening describes people demanding a special gift like that given to God’s messengers, making belief conditional upon receiving that position.

The Qur’an then emphasizes that God knows best where to place His message.

Abasement and punishment are subsequently assigned to the criminals.

The desire for a special position, criminal behavior, and an abased outcome therefore appear together in this context.

Ṣaghār’s meaning should not, however, be restricted to being denied prophethood.

Abasement is the stated outcome for the group; their crime and scheming are established by other parts of the sentence.

Ṣaghārun ʿinda allāhi concerns their standing in divine judgment, not ṣaghār necessarily naming a specified bodily punishment.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣaghār and the criminals’ abasement

Al-Aʿrāf 7:13: subordination contrasted with conceit

قَالَ فَاهْبِطْ مِنْهَا فَمَا يَكُونُ لَكَ أَنْ تَتَكَبَّرَ فِيهَا فَاخْرُجْ إِنَّكَ مِنَ الصَّاغِرِينَ

qāla fa-ihbiṭ minhā fa-mā yakūnu laka an tatakabbara fīhā fa-ukhruj innaka mina al-ṣāghirīna

Translation: He said: “Then descend from it. It is not for you to act arrogantly there. Depart; surely you are among the abased.”

The verse belongs to Iblīs’s story and refusal to prostrate.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:12, Iblīs claims superiority over Adam on the basis of their different materials of creation.

Verse 13 condemns his arrogance and commands departure.

It then says:

إِنَّكَ مِنَ الصَّاغِرِينَ

innaka mina al-ṣāghirīna

Ṣāghirīn here denotes the abased or those who have lost their standing.

The context clearly contrasts Iblīs’s claim to greatness with his conduct’s outcome of subordination and abasement.

Ṣāghir itself nevertheless does not mean arrogant.

Arrogance follows from tatakabbara, abasement from al-ṣāghirīna.

They relate as cause and consequence here rather than sharing one lexical meaning.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:119: defeat and an abased return

فَغُلِبُوا هُنَالِكَ وَانْقَلَبُوا صَاغِرِينَ

fa-ghulibū hunālika wa-inqalabū ṣāghirīna

Translation: They were defeated there and returned abased and subordinate.

This verse belongs to Moses’s encounter with the magicians.

Fa-ghulibū expresses being defeated.

Wa-inqalabū denotes their return or changed condition.

Ṣāghirīna specifies their condition in that situation.

Defeat and abasement are therefore related but separate concepts.

Those who came seeking victory are defeated and lose their former position.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:120, however, describes the magicians prostrating, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:121 describes their belief.

Ṣāghirīn in verse 119 should therefore not be treated as permanent moral condemnation of every individual in the group.

The word describes their condition in that encounter. Their later response and new choice appear in the following verses.

Yūsuf 12:32: the threat of prison and abasement

قَالَتْ فَذَٰلِكُنَّ الَّذِي لُمْتُنَّنِي فِيهِ وَلَقَدْ رَاوَدْتُهُ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ فَاسْتَعْصَمَ وَلَئِنْ لَمْ يَفْعَلْ مَا آمُرُهُ لَيُسْجَنَنَّ وَلَيَكُونًا مِنَ الصَّاغِرِينَ

qālat fa-dhālikunna alladhī lumtunnanī fīhi wa-la-qad rāwadtuhu ʿan nafsihi fa-istaʿṣama wa-la-in lam yafʿal mā āmuruhu la-yusjananna wa-la-yakūnan mina al-ṣāghirīna

Translation: She said: “This is the one about whom you blamed me. I sought to draw him to myself, but he held himself back. If he does not do what I command, he will surely be imprisoned and become one of the abased.”

Al-ṣāghirīna here occurs in the speech of a woman holding power.

She threatens Joseph with imprisonment and abasement if he refuses her demand.

Two separate results are stated: imprisonment and entering an abased condition.

The combination shows that socially, ṣāghir can concern losing standing and becoming subordinate to another’s power.

The speaker must nevertheless be identified.

The threat comes from a woman attempting to force Joseph to comply. The Qur’an does not present her demand as its own approved moral command; it recounts the story and pressure imposed upon him.

Ṣāghir lexically means abased, while the moral assessment of imposing that abasement follows from the narrative context.

This verse also shows that abasement before social power does not necessarily make the abased person guilty.

Al-Ṭabarī: the threat of prison and abasement against Joseph

Al-Naml 27:37: defeat and subordination in a political confrontation

ارْجِعْ إِلَيْهِمْ فَلَنَأْتِيَنَّهُمْ بِجُنُودٍ لَا قِبَلَ لَهُمْ بِهَا وَلَنُخْرِجَنَّهُمْ مِنْهَا أَذِلَّةً وَهُمْ صَاغِرُونَ

irjiʿ ilayhim fa-la-naʾtiyannahum bi-junūdin lā qibala lahum bihā wa-la-nukhrijannahum minhā adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūna

Translation: “Return to them. We will surely come against them with forces they cannot withstand, and expel them from it abased, while they have become subordinate.”

The verse belongs to the story of Solomon and the ruler of Sabaʾ.

Ṣāghirūna occurs in speech about military confrontation and expelling the opposing group.

Their inability to withstand the forces is mentioned first.

Adhillatan and ṣāghirūna then appear together.

The difference between adhillatan and ṣāghirūna

Both words can concern abasement and subordination.

Adhillah comes from DH L L and here denotes being abased and defeated.

Ṣāghirūna belongs to Ṣ GH R and foregrounds lowered rank and entering a subordinate condition.

The descriptions reinforce each other without requiring a separate, definitive stage of defeat for each.

Their combination also shows that ṣāghirūna is not merely bodily smallness or fewness of soldiers.

The issue is the opposing side’s defeat and subordination in a confrontation.

Al-Tawbah 9:29: wa-hum ṣāghirūna

Ṣāghirūna in Al-Tawbah 9:29 occurs in the expression concerning payment of jizyah.

Grammatically, ṣāghirūna is the predicate of hum, and wa-hum ṣāghirūna describes the group’s condition when paying jizyah.

Lexical and exegetical sources generally explain it through abasement, defeat, and subordination.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise understands ṣāghirūna as those who are defeated and subdued.

“While they are subordinate and subdued” therefore fits the word’s semantic field.

Does ṣāghirūna mean voluntary humility?

Not in this context.

The expression concerning jizyah describes a condition at the end of a confrontation and acceptance of a financial payment.

Ṣāghirūna here therefore does not mean moral, voluntary humility toward others.

Nor does the word establish that the payer inwardly believes in the other side’s religious or moral rightness.

People may accept another’s authority because of defeat or political circumstances without changing their inward beliefs.

Acceptance of subordination, heartfelt satisfaction, and religious belief must therefore be distinguished.

Does the verse prescribe a particular humiliating procedure for paying jizyah?

Some later legal and exegetical accounts propose details to display ṣaghār during payment, such as the payer standing before a seated recipient, or other expressions of subordination.

Al-Ṭabarī also transmits differing explanations.

Wa-hum ṣāghirūna itself, however, contains no separate instruction about standing, sitting, clothing, bodily movements, or verbally abusing the payer.

The lexical meaning of subordination must therefore be distinguished from procedures proposed in later sources.

This does not erase the expression’s unpleasant force: it does concern abasement and being subdued.

A word’s meaning of abasement does not independently authorize deriving any desired form of bodily or social humiliation from it.

The scope of jizyah, conditions of fighting, and human rights belong in an independent study of the relevant verses. The root article should first clarify ṣāghirūna itself rather than replace it with the ruling’s details.

6. Important forms and morphological distinctions

Ṣaghura and ṣighar

In uses concerning size and amount, ṣaghura means being or becoming small.

Ṣighar is this branch’s verbal noun.

It can concern body, age, quantity, or a subject considered smaller in comparison.

Ṣaghira and ṣaghār

Some Arabic vocalizations use ṣaghira for losing standing and becoming abased.

Ṣaghār is a verbal noun or noun for that condition.

Lexicographers distinguish this from ṣaghura in the sense of small size, although some forms and vocalizations overlap.

The vowel of one verb should therefore not turn the branches into a historically proven, complete separation.

Ṣaghīr and ṣaghīrah

Ṣaghīr is the masculine adjective, ṣaghīrah its feminine form.

The Qur’an uses them for smallness of age, quantity, and things recorded among deeds.

Known plurals of ṣaghīr include ṣighār and other forms recorded in lexical sources.

Poetry also uses ṣugharāʾ for small ones.

Aṣghar and ṣughrā

Aṣghar is the comparative form and means smaller in comparisons.

Ṣughrā is its feminine form.

Only aṣghar occurs in the Qur’an, in two verses.

Ṣughrā does not occur independently as a form of this root in the Qur’an.

This must be distinguished from words’ similarity in meaning.

Ṣāghir and ṣāghirūn

Ṣāghir is an active participle of the branch concerning becoming subordinate and abased.

The Qur’an uses the plurals ṣāghirūna and ṣāghirīna.

The endings -ūn and -īn depend on grammatical role and case, not two different lexical families.

Ṣāghir in this use cannot mean a young child.

Ṣaghghara and taṣghīr

Ṣaghghara is Form II and can mean making something small.

Taṣghīr is its verbal noun.

In Arabic morphology, taṣghīr names a diminutive formation, with uses such as smallness, affection, or disparagement in certain words.

The morphological term must not become the meaning of every Qur’anic derivative of Ṣ GH R.

Istaṣghara and taṣāghara

Istaṣghara means considering someone or something small.

Taṣāghara can denote making oneself appear small, outward humility, or entering a state of lowliness.

Neither form occurs in the Qur’an.

In behavioral uses, distinguishing actual humility from merely presenting oneself as small depends on context and conduct.

Early lexicons: morphology and being small versus being considered small

7. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura) and K B R (kabura)

The families contrast in size and quantity.

Ṣaghīr is small; kabīr is large.

The contrast also appears in Al-Baqarah 2:282, Al-Tawbah 9:121, Al-Kahf 18:49, and Al-Qamar 54:53.

The behavioral branch of K B R, however, also concerns arrogance, while the social branch of Ṣ GH R concerns abasement and subordination.

These contrasts must not establish that being small always means humility or being large always means arrogance.

Bodily size, social standing, and moral conduct have separate criteria.

Study of K B R

Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura) and Q L L (qalla)

Qalla in Arabic concerns scantness and being little.

Ṣaghura can likewise express a small quantity.

Qillah often foregrounds little quantity or fewness, while ṣighar may concern bodily size, age, degree, or quantity.

For example, nafaqatan ṣaghīratan in Al-Tawbah 9:121 denotes a small expenditure.

Qalīl could also relate to its little amount in that context, but ṣaghīr’s uses are not all restricted to number and quantity.

Study of Q L L

Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura) and DH L L (dhalla)

The families can be close in social and moral uses.

Ṣāghir describes someone abased and subordinate.

Dhalīl also concerns abasement, defeat, and powerlessness before another’s power in some uses.

DH L L nevertheless has other branches, such as becoming soft, tractable, or humble.

Adhillatan and ṣāghirūna occur together in Al-Naml 27:37.

Their combination shows semantic proximity without proving an absolute, nonoverlapping boundary.

Study of DH L L

Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura) and H W N (hāna)

Hāna concerns becoming light or insignificant, ease, or loss of estimation and standing.

Ṣaghār can likewise denote abasement and lowered standing.

Small versus large is nevertheless a basic branch of Ṣ GH R, while H W N has a different range involving lightness, gentleness, and abasement.

They are therefore close in some uses, not complete synonyms.

Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura) and Ḥ Q R (ḥaqara)

Ḥaqara means considering small or contemptible.

Istaṣghara can likewise concern considering something small.

Such consideration may be a correct or incorrect judgment of a matter’s size or importance.

Humiliating a person, by contrast, usually involves lowering their worth and standing.

Considering contemptible and being small should therefore not be equated without evidence.

Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura) and Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā)

Despite their similar letters, these are separate roots.

Ṣ GH R concerns smallness, scantness, and subordination.

Ṣ GH W concerns inclination, with aṣghā known for listening attentively and turning one’s attention toward something.

Some lexical uses record iṣghār from Ṣ GH R for an animal’s quiet voice; it must not be confused with iṣghāʾ, listening attentively.

Two shared letters and similar pronunciation do not establish historical derivation or identical meanings.

8. Challenges of understanding and translation

The main difficulty is that Persian kūchak, small, can express size and age, while some combinations express humiliation and lowered standing.

Qur’anic translation must identify the intended meaning.

Ṣaghīran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:24 denotes childhood.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282 it concerns the debt’s amount.

Al-Kahf 18:49 concerns smallness of the recorded matter or act.

Ṣaghār in Al-Anʿām 6:124 and ṣāghirīna in Al-Aʿrāf 7:13, by contrast, concern abasement and subordination.

These forms must therefore not all be restricted to one fixed equivalent.

Does ṣāghir mean humble?

Abasement and subordination are clearer in this form’s Qur’anic uses.

Humility may be a conscious moral choice; abasement may result from defeat, threats, or pressure.

In Yūsuf 12:32, abasement is part of the threat directed at Joseph.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:119 it concerns defeat in the encounter.

In Al-Tawbah 9:29 it concerns subordination when paying jizyah.

These cannot all be translated as commendable humility.

Is smallness always relative?

Many uses understand smallness through comparison.

This does not mean the word has no established meaning.

For example, ṣaghīr for a person can directly mean a child; ṣāghir in standing can describe someone abased.

The relativity of size must therefore not obscure each use’s clear, known meaning.

Does being abased always show someone’s guilt?

No.

Some verses present abasement as the consequence of criminal conduct, as in Al-Anʿām 6:124.

In Yūsuf 12:32, however, someone resisting an improper demand is threatened with abasement.

The subordinate condition alone therefore cannot establish guilt.

Power or high standing likewise does not independently prove rightness or moral worth.

Comparison of the Qur’anic uses makes this distinction clear and prevents every defeat or abasement from becoming a definitive judgment on someone’s character and deeds.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ṣaghīr in dimensional uses is appropriately rendered small.

For age, young or in childhood is more natural.

Smaller is a clear, direct equivalent for aṣghar.

Ṣaghār and ṣāghir, however, require retaining abasement and subordination.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ GH R concerns smallness and scantness by comparison, whether of body, age, money, a matter’s importance, or someone’s standing.

In the dimensional branch, ṣaghīr is small and aṣghar smaller. For age, ṣaghīr can denote a child. In the social branch, however, ṣaghār and ṣāghir concern abasement, subordination, and lowered standing.

Early Arabic witnesses show both actual objects’ size and people’s social condition. Lexical sources also record narrower uses, such as a camel’s low cry.

The Qur’an contains both main branches.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, Al-Tawbah 9:121, Al-Kahf 18:49, and Al-Qamar 54:53, small and great express quantity or the scope of recording.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, smallness concerns childhood.

In Yūnus 10:61 and Sabaʾ 34:3, aṣghar denotes comparative smallness.

Al-Anʿām 6:124, Al-Aʿrāf 7:13, Al-Aʿrāf 7:119, Al-Tawbah 9:29, Yūsuf 12:32, and Al-Naml 27:37, however, concern subordination and abasement.

Concise root definition: Ṣ GH R denotes smallness and scantness in size, age, or quantity; in its branch concerning standing, it denotes lowered rank, subordination, and abasement. Brief root meaning: Smallness in size, age, or quantity; in ṣaghār and ṣāghir, abasement and subordination

The family’s central distinction is that being small, being considered small, and being abased are three different conditions.

Small size or young age independently determines nothing about human and moral worth. Abasement may likewise follow someone’s wrongdoing or be imposed by another’s power and pressure.

Each derivative’s precise meaning must therefore be established from its form, the subject of comparison, and the verse’s context, rather than reducing every use to vague general smallness or a moral judgment of contemptibility.
