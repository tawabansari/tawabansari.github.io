Becoming calm, settling, and finding reassurance: from settled ground and hearts to social security, steadfast faith, and a soul at peace

The Arabic family ṭ m ʾ n concerns calm, settledness, and coming to rest. It can describe a bodily or spatial state, such as something settling after movement or land lying below its surroundings; an inward state, such as worry and uncertainty subsiding; or living conditions, such as a community enjoying security and stability. Its descriptions do not always require a preceding disturbance.

The Quran uses this family in different situations. Abraham asks The God to show him how He gives life to the dead so that his heart may be reassured. Believers’ hearts find rest in remembering The God. People return to calm and security after danger and threat. A town is described as secure and settled. Conversely, some become so attached to worldly life that they rest content in it and neglect the Hereafter.

This variety makes a basic point clear: reassurance alone proves neither truth nor misguidance. People may find reassurance in truth, but may also rely on an unstable situation, an erroneous belief, or temporary prosperity. Its value and validity depend on what reassures them and on the understanding and conduct accompanying it.

This root occurs 13 times in 12 Quranic verses. Its principal forms are inflections of اطْمَأَنَّ and the descriptions مُطْمَئِنّ, مُطْمَئِنَّة, and مُطْمَئِنِّين. The verbal nouns اطْمِئْنَان and طُمَأْنِينَة are known in Arabic, but neither noun itself occurs in the Quran.

Alongside calm, this family helps distinguish security from reassurance, faith from reassurance, certainty from reassurance, and enduring peace from temporary satisfaction.

1. Historical meanings in early Arabic

Stillness, settledness, and peace of mind

In early Arabic, اطْمَأَنَّ describes someone becoming settled, calm, and at ease after disturbance.

اطْمَأَنَّ الرَّجُلُ means “the man became calm” or “found reassurance.”

اطْمَأَنَّ قَلْبُهُ means “his heart became calm.”

اطْمَأَنَّتْ نَفْسُهُ means “his soul found peace and rest.”

In other uses, اطْمَأَنَّ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ means finding reassurance in something or coming to trust it.

Here, stillness is not simply the cessation of bodily movement. A person may be moving, working, or making decisions while the heart has emerged from anxiety and instability.

Conversely, someone may be physically motionless yet inwardly troubled and worried. Bodily stillness and inward reassurance belong to a related semantic field but are not the same state.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

A poem in the collected verse of al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, the renowned pre-Islamic poet, includes:

لَدَى جَرْعَاءَ لَيْسَ بِهَا أَنِيسٌ
وَلَيْسَ بِهَا الدَّلِيلُ بِمُطْمَئِنِّ

ladā jarʿāʾa laysa bihā anīsun / wa-laysa bihā al-dalīlu bi-muṭmaʾinn

“Beside open sandy ground with no companion, where even the guide is not at ease.”

Here مُطْمَئِنّ describes the guide, someone expected to know the way and lead others through it.

Yet the poet describes a place so remote and lonely that even the guide feels no settled ease.

This matters because مُطْمَئِنّ concerns peace of mind and a sense of settledness, rather than bodily lowness or stopped movement.

The guide may still be travelling while inwardly lacking reassurance.

The poem is recorded in al-Nābighah’s collected verse and attributed to the pre-Islamic period. As with other poetry transmitted from that period, however, the poet’s early date does not by itself establish the precise date of composition and transmission of every word. What the witness clearly supports is an early use of مُطْمَئِنّ for inward ease and settledness.

Describing land

Older Arabic dictionaries record أَرْضٌ مُطْمَئِنَّة for land lying lower than its surroundings.

تَطَامَنَتِ الْأَرْضُ likewise concerns land subsiding or lying low.

طَأْمَنَ ظَهْرَهُ and طَامَنَ ظَهْرَهُ describe bending or lowering one’s back.

These expressions concern physical position and elevation, not a person’s heart or soul.

In this lexical sense, مُطْمَئِنَّة land is low-lying or sunken ground. The description does not itself say that it is safer, more fertile, or better suited to habitation.

The conceptual connection between settling low and coming to rest is understandable: something moving from a raised or mobile state to a lowered, settled position can provide a tangible image of stability.

That image must not, however, become a supposedly certain history of how all the family’s meanings developed.

“Low-lying land” alone does not establish that early Arabic speakers necessarily conceived inward peace as “the heart descending.” Nor does psychological reassurance imply personal weakness or abasement.

Both spatial and inward meanings are recorded in Arabic, but establishing which came first requires independent evidence.

Disagreement about the word’s historical structure

Arabic grammarians and lexicographers have disagreed over the structure of اطْمَأَنَّ.

Sībawayh regarded اطمأن as involving a rearrangement of letters and connected it with طَأْمَنَ. Other scholars proposed the reverse direction or considered طمأن and طأمن separately.

Accordingly, some older dictionaries place this family under ط م ن, whereas Quranic morphological indexes place its forms under ط م أ ن.

The disagreement concerns the historical analysis of the word’s structure, rather than the clear meanings of its Quranic uses.

The Quranic form اطمأن means becoming calm or settled. Its spelling alone cannot establish that all four letters had the same order and derivational role throughout the word’s history.

Nor should اطمأن be assigned uncritically to an ordinary triliteral verb pattern and given an invented meaning on that basis. This family’s morphology, including the doubled final consonant of اطْمَأَنَّ, requires its own appropriate analysis.

Semantic core

Taken together, Arabic usage supports the following shared core:

Becoming calm or settled, or being in a state of rest and stability, whether physically and spatially, inwardly, or in one’s living conditions and residence.

This definition does not judge the cause or validity of the calm.

A person may find reassurance through clear evidence, rest content after gaining wealth and prosperity, or feel secure when danger ends. These are different routes to reassurance, not mutually contradictory root meanings.

2. Reassuring the heart: understanding, calm, and steadfast faith

In many Quranic uses, reassurance is attributed to the heart.

These uses show that it is not merely a pleasant feeling: it can relate to understanding, observation, faith, remembrance of The God, and reassuring signs.

Abraham’s request for reassurance

Al-Baqarah 2:260 states:

وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ رَبِّ أَرِنِي كَيْفَ تُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ قَالَ أَوَلَمْ تُؤْمِنْ قَالَ بَلَىٰ وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَطْمَئِنَّ قَلْبِي

wa-idh qāla ibrāhīmu rabbi arinī kayfa tuḥyī al-mawtā qāla a-wa-lam tuʾmin qāla balā wa-lākin li-yaṭmaʾinna qalbī

Al-Baqarah 2:260

“And when Abraham said, ‘My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead.’ He said, ‘Have you not believed?’ He said, ‘Yes, but so that my heart may be reassured.’”

Al-Baqarah 2:260

This verse is particularly clear evidence of the distinction between faith and reassurance.

Abraham asks The God to show him how He brings the dead to life. In answer to أَوَلَمْ تُؤْمِنْ, he expressly says بَلَىٰ: yes, he has believed.

He then explains his request:

وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَطْمَئِنَّ قَلْبِي

wa-lākin li-yaṭmaʾinna qalbī

Al-Baqarah 2:260

“But so that my heart may be reassured.”

The wording distinguishes an affirmation of faith from a request for further reassurance; the two expressions do not perform the same role in this sentence.

He affirms his faith while seeking an experience or observation that will bring his heart greater calm.

His question is not framed as whether bringing the dead to life is possible. He asks كَيْفَ تُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ: how do You give life to the dead?

The verse thus distinguishes believing something to be true from knowing how it takes place.

Someone may believe that something happens while still wanting to understand its manner more fully. That desire does not itself negate existing faith.

Conversely, not every request to see something or question about how it happens necessarily comes from complete faith. Here Abraham’s faith is made explicit in his own words.

For ṭ m ʾ n, the relevant point is that reassurance can be a state of inward rest arising through a clearer understanding of how something true occurs.

But اطمأن itself does not mean observing or attaining scientific certainty. Observation and the request for understanding come from other parts of the verse; reassurance is the inward state Abraham seeks.

The disciples’ request for reassurance

In Al-Māʾidah 5:113, the disciples say to Jesus:

قَالُوا نُرِيدُ أَنْ نَأْكُلَ مِنْهَا وَتَطْمَئِنَّ قُلُوبُنَا وَنَعْلَمَ أَنْ قَدْ صَدَقْتَنَا وَنَكُونَ عَلَيْهَا مِنَ الشَّاهِدِينَ

qālū nurīdu an naʾkula minhā wa-taṭmaʾinna qulūbunā wa-naʿlama an qad ṣadaqtanā wa-nakūna ʿalayhā mina al-shāhidīn

Al-Māʾidah 5:113

“They said, ‘We wish to eat from it, for our hearts to be reassured, to know that you have spoken truthfully to us, and to be among its witnesses.’”

Al-Māʾidah 5:113

Here too, inward reassurance relates to receiving a tangible sign.

The disciples ask for a table spread to descend from heaven. They mention eating from it, reassurance, knowing that Jesus has spoken truthfully, and becoming witnesses alongside one another.

تَطْمَئِنَّ قُلُوبُنَا is one of their wishes, but is not identical to نَعْلَمَ or نَكُونَ عَلَيْهَا مِنَ الشَّاهِدِينَ.

Reassurance, knowledge, and testimony are three related but distinct concepts.

In Al-Baqarah 2:260, Abraham likewise seeks to see how the dead are brought to life. In both verses, an additional sign relates to the heart becoming calm.

Their similarity does not establish that Abraham and the disciples were in exactly the same state of faith. Each speaks within a particular context.

Al-Māʾidah 5:111 also speaks of the disciples believing. Their request for reassurance alone therefore cannot conclusively prove unbelief.

Good tidings and reassurance amid danger

Āl ʿImrān 3:126 states:

وَمَا جَعَلَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَّا بُشْرَىٰ لَكُمْ وَلِتَطْمَئِنَّ قُلُوبُكُمْ بِهِ وَمَا النَّصْرُ إِلَّا مِنْ عِنْدِ اللَّهِ الْعَزِيزِ الْحَكِيمِ

wa-mā jaʿalahu allāhu illā bushrā lakum wa-li-taṭmaʾinna qulūbukum bihi wa-mā al-naṣru illā min ʿindi allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥakīm

Āl ʿImrān 3:126

“The God made it only good tidings for you and so that your hearts might be reassured by it; victory comes only from The God, the Almighty, the Wise.”

Al-Anfāl 8:10 also states:

وَمَا جَعَلَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَّا بُشْرَىٰ وَلِتَطْمَئِنَّ بِهِ قُلُوبُكُمْ وَمَا النَّصْرُ إِلَّا مِنْ عِنْدِ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-mā jaʿalahu allāhu illā bushrā wa-li-taṭmaʾinna bihi qulūbukum wa-mā al-naṣru illā min ʿindi allāhi inna allāha ʿazīzun ḥakīm

Al-Anfāl 8:10

“The God made it only good tidings and so that your hearts might be reassured by it; victory comes only from The God. Indeed, The God is Almighty, Wise.”

In both verses, reassurance relates to good tidings and divine support during confrontation.

The purpose particle in لِتَطْمَئِنَّ identifies calming hearts as one purpose of the good tidings.

That calm must not be equated with victory itself.

The verse expressly says that victory comes from The God. Good tidings can calm believers and give them hope and perseverance, but reassurance does not lexically mean military victory.

Nor does reassurance here mean a complete absence of danger. Someone may remain in a difficult, dangerous situation while anxiety subsides after receiving good news or a reassuring sign.

Inward reassurance can therefore arise in the midst of danger.

“In remembering The God, hearts find rest”

Al-Raʿd 13:28 states:

الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَتَطْمَئِنُّ قُلُوبُهُمْ بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ أَلَا بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ تَطْمَئِنُّ الْقُلُوبُ

alladhīna āmanū wa-taṭmaʾinnu qulūbuhum bi-dhikri allāhi alā bi-dhikri allāhi taṭmaʾinnu al-qulūb

Al-Raʿd 13:28

“Those who believe and whose hearts find rest in remembering The God. Indeed, in remembering The God, hearts find rest.”

The verb تَطْمَئِنُّ occurs twice in this verse.

The first occurrence describes believers’ hearts finding rest. The second highlights the connection with remembrance of The God in a separate statement.

بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ connects inward reassurance in this context with remembering The God.

The root ṭ m ʾ n itself does not mean reciting remembrance or repeating words. ذِكْر belongs to another root, with meanings involving reminding, remembering, and bringing something to awareness.

The calm described here should therefore not be restricted without evidence to mechanically repeating a phrase.

Remembering The God can direct attention to divine truth, sovereignty, knowledge, mercy, justice, and promises. In fear, hardship, uncertainty, or confusion, such remembrance can clarify one’s direction and source of support.

Hearts finding rest in remembering The God does not mean that believers never experience fear, grief, or worry.

Al-Anfāl 8:2 describes believers whose hearts experience وَجَل when The God is mentioned. This need not contradict the reassurance in Al-Raʿd 13:28.

Remembrance may awaken a concerned awareness of responsibility while strengthening trust and settled commitment to divine truth and guidance.

Peace of heart should not be turned into emotional numbness, disregard for danger, or an absence of all concern.

3. Security, settledness, and reassurance in communal life

Quranic reassurance is not attributed only to hearts and souls. It can describe people’s circumstances, a community, or beings inhabiting a place.

فَإِذَا اطْمَأْنَنْتُمْ: returning from danger

Al-Nisāʾ 4:103 states:

فَإِذَا اطْمَأْنَنْتُمْ فَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ

fa-idhā iṭmaʾnantum fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāh

Al-Nisāʾ 4:103

“Then, when you have become secure and settled, establish prayer.”

This passage follows verses discussing prayer amid danger and confrontation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:101 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 address fear of enemy harm and the conduct of prayer in those circumstances.

Here اطْمَأْنَنْتُمْ is not merely a personal feeling of calm: returning to safer, more stable conditions after danger also matters.

Someone may inwardly trust The God while remaining in danger. When the threat ends and ordinary conditions return, prayer can be established under settled circumstances.

The verse distinguishes these two situations.

اطمأننتم does not imply that prayer is performed only with complete psychological calm. The preceding verses themselves concern prayer amid danger.

This use consequently shows that reassurance can concern secure, calm circumstances, not only inward feelings.

قَرْيَةً كَانَتْ آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً

Al-Naḥl 16:112 states:

وَضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا قَرْيَةً كَانَتْ آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً يَأْتِيهَا رِزْقُهَا رَغَدًا مِنْ كُلِّ مَكَانٍ

wa-ḍaraba allāhu mathalan qaryatan kānat āminatan muṭmaʾinnatan yaʾtīhā rizquhā raghadan min kulli makān

Al-Naḥl 16:112

“The God sets forth the example of a town that was secure and settled, receiving its provision abundantly from every place.”

آمِنَة and مُطْمَئِنَّة appear together here.

آمِنَة belongs to ʾ m n and concerns security from fear and threat.

مُطْمَئِنَّة belongs to ṭ m ʾ n and conveys calm, rest, and settledness.

The descriptions are close in meaning, but should not be treated as complete lexical synonyms.

A community may be safe from attack and external threat yet economically or socially unstable. Conversely, people may become accustomed to a situation and feel settled in it while their actual security remains vulnerable.

Here both descriptions accompany abundant provision: the town enjoyed security, calm, and prosperity.

But the verse continues:

فَكَفَرَتْ بِأَنْعُمِ اللَّهِ فَأَذَاقَهَا اللَّهُ لِبَاسَ الْجُوعِ وَالْخَوْفِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

fa-kafarat bi-anʿumi allāhi fa-adhāqahā allāhu libāsa al-jūʿi wa-l-khawfi bi-mā kānū yaṣnaʿūn

Al-Naḥl 16:112

“Then it denied the blessings of The God, so The God made it taste the garment of hunger and fear because of what they used to do.”

The continuation shows that its previous settledness neither guaranteed that situation would last nor established the people’s righteousness.

A community may enjoy security and stability for a time, yet through its conduct create conditions that undo them.

مطمئنة thus describes the town’s situation, rather than delivering a final judgment on every inhabitant’s faith, justice, or destiny.

يَمْشُونَ مُطْمَئِنِّينَ: residing and being settled on earth

Al-Isrāʾ 17:95 states:

قُلْ لَوْ كَانَ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَلَائِكَةٌ يَمْشُونَ مُطْمَئِنِّينَ لَنَزَّلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَلَكًا رَسُولًا

qul law kāna fī al-arḍi malāʾikatun yamshūna muṭmaʾinnīna la-nazzalnā ʿalayhim mina al-samāʾi malakan rasūlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:95

“Say: If there were angels on earth walking about as settled inhabitants, We would send down to them from heaven an angel as a Messenger.”

مُطْمَئِنِّينَ describes the angels walking on earth in the hypothetical situation presented.

The context answers those who objected to a human Messenger being sent.

Here مطمئنين relates to being established and settled in one’s living environment. The expression supposes beings for whom earth is the setting of ordinary life and movement.

“Angels walking calmly” therefore does not fully convey the contextual point. The subject is not merely their manner of walking or emotional state, but their hypothetical residence on earth.

Moreover, لَوْ كَانَ introduces a hypothetical condition.

The verse does not report that angels actually inhabit earth as a settled community. It says that if they did, a Messenger appropriate to them would be sent.

This use demonstrates that the family extends beyond inward calm to being established in an environment.

4. Sound reassurance and misleading reassurance

A significant feature of this root’s Quranic use is that reassurance is not always praised.

The Quran speaks of believers’ reassurance in The God, but also uses reassurance in worldly life and temporary prosperity in a critical context.

The word itself thus does not contain a final moral judgment.

وَرَضُوا بِالْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاطْمَأَنُّوا بِهَا

Yūnus 10:7 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ لِقَاءَنَا وَرَضُوا بِالْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاطْمَأَنُّوا بِهَا وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنْ آيَاتِنَا غَافِلُونَ

inna alladhīna lā yarjūna liqāʾanā wa-raḍū bi-l-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-ṭmaʾannū bihā wa-alladhīna hum ʿan āyātinā ghāfilūn

Yūnus 10:7

“Indeed, those who do not hope to meet Us, who are satisfied with worldly life and rest content in it, and those who are heedless of Our signs…”

The verbs رَضُوا and اطْمَأَنُّوا appear together.

رَضُوا concerns satisfaction and acceptance.

اطْمَأَنُّوا concerns finding rest, reassurance, and a basis for reliance.

Together they show that someone can be pleased with something and also find rest and reliance in it.

But the verse is not simply criticizing people for being happy with worldly life.

It gives other descriptions too: not hoping to meet The God and being heedless of His signs.

In this context, worldly life has become the ultimate horizon of their satisfaction and reassurance.

Enjoying life’s blessings is not inherently blameworthy. The criticism concerns an attachment accompanied by disregard for truth and the Hereafter.

اطمأنوا بها therefore cannot be translated “they became unbelievers.” Unbelief and heedlessness must be understood from the verse and other evidence; the verb still means finding rest and reassurance.

فَإِنْ أَصَابَهُ خَيْرٌ اطْمَأَنَّ بِهِ

Al-Ḥajj 22:11 states:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَعْبُدُ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ فَإِنْ أَصَابَهُ خَيْرٌ اطْمَأَنَّ بِهِ وَإِنْ أَصَابَتْهُ فِتْنَةٌ انْقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yaʿbudu allāha ʿalā ḥarfin fa-in aṣābahu khayrun iṭmaʾanna bihi wa-in aṣābat-hu fitnatun inqalaba ʿalā wajhih

Al-Ḥajj 22:11

“Among people is one who worships The God on an edge: if good comes to him, he rests content in it; but if a trial befalls him, he turns back on his face.”

اطْمَأَنَّ بِهِ describes the person becoming reassured when good comes to him.

Yet this same person turns away when confronted with a trial.

His reassurance depends on prosperity that pleases him.

The verse describes reassurance that is not enduring or steadfast: as circumstances change, so does his conduct.

Feeling calm alone therefore does not establish the soundness of faith or a person’s steadfastness.

Someone may feel satisfied and reassured as long as life goes as desired, yet abandon an earlier position when comfort or advantage is threatened.

بِهِ is significant: its pronoun refers to the good that has reached the person. That benefit is what reassures him.

This state should not be equated with Abraham’s reassurance or believers’ peace in remembering The God.

Nor does the verse establish that receiving good and comfort is undesirable. It criticizes a person whose direction in worship and perseverance depend on temporary advantage.

In the Quran, one instance of reassurance may reflect firmness in truth and another reliance on something unstable. The root meaning remains in both; the object of reassurance and the response to changing circumstances explain the difference.

5. Reassurance under coercion and the soul’s final peace

وَقَلْبُهُ مُطْمَئِنٌّ بِالْإِيمَانِ

Al-Naḥl 16:106 states:

مَنْ كَفَرَ بِاللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ إِيمَانِهِ إِلَّا مَنْ أُكْرِهَ وَقَلْبُهُ مُطْمَئِنٌّ بِالْإِيمَانِ وَلَٰكِنْ مَنْ شَرَحَ بِالْكُفْرِ صَدْرًا

man kafara bi-llāhi min baʿdi īmānihi illā man ukriha wa-qalbuhu muṭmaʾinnun bi-l-īmāni wa-lākin man sharaḥa bi-l-kufri ṣadrā

Al-Naḥl 16:106

“Whoever disbelieves in The God after believing—except one who is compelled while the heart remains assured in faith—but whoever opens the breast to unbelief…”

مُطْمَئِنٌّ describes the heart.

The context distinguishes someone under compulsion from someone who knowingly embraces unbelief inwardly.

Under pressure and threat, a person may utter words that do not reflect a genuine belief, while the heart remains assured in faith.

Reassurance here does not mean an absence of fear or bodily suffering.

A person may fear threats and torture while remaining inwardly steadfast in faith.

مُطْمَئِنٌّ بِالْإِيمَانِ should therefore not be rendered “a person who has no fear.”

The verse distinguishes outward conduct produced under compulsion from a person’s inward position.

This clarifies the connection between reassurance and steadfast faith. But مطمئن is not a synonym for “believer”: faith is the object of reassurance, specified by بِالْإِيمَانِ.

يَا أَيَّتُهَا النَّفْسُ الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ

Al-Fajr 89:27 states:

يَا أَيَّتُهَا النَّفْسُ الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ

yā ayyatuhā al-nafsu al-muṭmaʾinnah

Al-Fajr 89:27

“O soul at peace and reassured.”

The next verse states:

ارْجِعِي إِلَىٰ رَبِّكِ رَاضِيَةً مَرْضِيَّةً

irjiʿī ilā rabbiki rāḍiyatan marḍiyyah

Al-Fajr 89:28

“Return to your Lord, pleased and well-pleasing.”

Al-Fajr 89:28

الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ describes النَّفْسُ.

The expression concerns a soul that has attained reassurance and rest.

Within this context, however, its description connects to what follows: returning to the Lord, being pleased, and being approved.

Its reassurance is therefore not merely an ordinary emotional state like feeling relaxed after resting. Across these verses, it relates to the human relationship with the Lord and its final outcome.

Nevertheless, a basic translation of “the reassured soul” should not become “a person whose every sin has been forgiven” or “a spirit attaining a particular mystical rank.” Those expressions are not the word’s lexical definition.

The Quran uses a clear description here: the soul at rest and reassured.

The following verses describe its place and outcome.

The reassured soul and satisfaction

Al-Fajr 89:28 contains رَاضِيَةً and مَرْضِيَّةً.

راضية describes the soul being pleased.

مرضية describes its being approved or well-pleasing.

مطمئنة belongs to another family and describes settledness and rest.

These three expressions are related but linguistically distinct.

Someone may be satisfied with what they have yet remain troubled about the future. Conversely, someone may be assured of a truth or path while displeased with an unjust situation.

In Al-Fajr, reassurance, being pleased, and being approved appear together in a harmonious picture of the soul’s outcome.

That harmony does not make their roots identical.

6. Comparison with related roots

Ṭ M ʾ N and S K N: stillness and reassurance

S k n is among the families closest in meaning to ṭ m ʾ n.

سَكَنَ can describe dwelling, becoming calm, or the cessation of movement or disturbance. The Quran uses this root for residing in a place, the quiet of night, and human rest.

Al-Fatḥ 48:4 states:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ السَّكِينَةَ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ لِيَزْدَادُوا إِيمَانًا مَعَ إِيمَانِهِمْ

huwa alladhī anzala al-sakīnata fī qulūbi al-muʾminīna li-yazdādū īmānan maʿa īmānihim

Al-Fatḥ 48:4

“He is the One who sent down tranquility into the believers’ hearts so that they might increase in faith alongside their faith.”

السَّكِينَةَ here concerns tranquility arising in believers’ hearts.

Al-Raʿd 13:28 likewise uses تَطْمَئِنُّ قُلُوبُهُمْ for hearts finding rest in remembering The God.

Both families can concern inward calm, though their ranges of use differ.

سكن covers a broad field of stopped movement, residence, settledness, and calm.

In inward uses, اطمأن can convey finding settled assurance in something or as a result of a particular cause.

This distinction must not become an absolute rule that سكينة always concerns bodily calm and اطمئنان only rational certainty. Both can concern inward states.

Ṭ M ʾ N and ʾ M N: security and settled ease

ʾ M n relates to security from fear, trust, entrusted responsibility, and faith.

Ṭ m ʾ n concerns becoming calm, settled, and reassured.

Their closeness is well illustrated by آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً in Al-Naḥl 16:112.

Security can describe external conditions, such as freedom from threat and danger.

Reassurance can describe the settled state experienced or enjoyed by a person or community.

Yet security and reassurance do not invariably accompany one another.

Someone may live in safety yet lack peace because of inward worries. Another may be in danger yet remain inwardly steadfast through trust in The God and understanding.

Al-Naḥl 16:106 importantly illustrates the second situation: a person is compelled, yet the heart remains assured in faith.

مطمئن therefore cannot be translated “safe from danger” in every verse.

External security likewise does not itself prove belief in the truth.

Ṭ M ʾ N and Y Q N: reassurance and certainty

Certainty concerns firm knowledge or conviction that something is true.

Reassurance describes becoming calm and settled.

They can converge, because firm knowledge of a truth may reduce uncertainty and disturbance. But certainty and reassurance are not complete synonyms.

In Al-Baqarah 2:260, Abraham affirms his faith and requests reassurance. The word “certainty” does not occur there, though understanding, observation, and inward rest are involved.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:113, the disciples likewise seek reassurance, knowledge that Jesus has spoken truthfully, and becoming witnesses to a sign.

Conversely, Al-Ḥajj 22:11 shows that someone may feel reassured on receiving good without that reassurance necessarily resting on firm knowledge of truth.

Not every reassurance should therefore be called certainty.

Conversely, someone may be certain an unpleasant event will occur, and that very certainty may cause grief or worry.

Certainty primarily concerns a state of knowledge, whereas reassurance concerns rest and settledness. They can strengthen one another without being interchangeable.

Ṭ M ʾ N and R Ḍ W: calm and satisfaction

R ḍ w concerns liking, acceptance, and satisfaction.

Ṭ m ʾ n concerns becoming calm and reassured.

In Yūnus 10:7, the Quran says:

وَرَضُوا بِالْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاطْمَأَنُّوا بِهَا

wa-raḍū bi-l-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-ṭmaʾannū bihā

Yūnus 10:7

“They were satisfied with worldly life and rested content in it.”

The phrase shows that satisfaction and reassurance can coexist in one situation.

Being pleased with something, however, does not necessarily mean finding lasting reassurance in it.

Al-Fajr 89:27 and Al-Fajr 89:28 likewise place النفس المطمئنة and راضية مرضية in two consecutive verses.

The soul’s reassurance and satisfaction form a harmonious outcome here.

Distinguishing the families therefore does not separate them completely; it shows that the Quran uses distinct words for related human states.

Ṭ M ʾ N and Th B T: settledness and steadfastness

Th b t concerns remaining firm, stable, and unmoved from one’s position.

Reassurance may accompany firmness, but does not itself mean resisting every pressure and change.

In Al-Naḥl 16:106, the heart assured in faith has not abandoned inward belief under compulsion. Reassurance and steadfastness are close here.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:11, however, a person is reassured when good comes and turns away when tested.

The second example shows that reassurance under one set of conditions does not necessarily mean steadfastness when they change.

اطمأن should therefore not always be translated “remained steadfast.”

Firmness may characterize reassurance, but its particular character must be understood from context.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal Persian equivalents convey becoming calm, finding reassurance, becoming settled, and being calm and stable. Choosing among them depends on the subject and on what provides reassurance; English likewise needs contextual equivalents.

اطْمَأَنَّ means “became calm,” “settled,” or “found reassurance.” When a benefit or situation supplies its object, “rested content in it” can also fit.

يَطْمَئِنُّ / تَطْمَئِنُّ means “becomes calm,” “finds reassurance,” or “comes to rest.”

مُطْمَئِنّ means “calm,” “reassured,” or “settled and at ease.”

مُطْمَئِنَّة is the feminine form of the same description. For the soul, “at peace and reassured” fits; for a town, “calm and settled” is more natural.

مُطْمَئِنِّين is the masculine plural. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:95, the context favors “settled on earth” or “residing and established” over purely emotional calm.

اطْمِئْنَان and طُمَأْنِينَة denote calm, settledness, and reassurance in Arabic, but these verbal nouns themselves do not occur in the Quran.

The following renderings fit the Quranic forms:

Al-Baqarah 2:260, لِيَطْمَئِنَّ قَلْبِي: “so that my heart may be reassured.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:126, وَلِتَطْمَئِنَّ قُلُوبُكُمْ بِهِ: “and so that your hearts may be reassured by it.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:103, فَإِذَا اطْمَأْنَنْتُمْ: “then, when you have become secure and settled.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:113, وَتَطْمَئِنَّ قُلُوبُنَا: “and that our hearts may be reassured.”

Al-Anfāl 8:10, وَلِتَطْمَئِنَّ بِهِ قُلُوبُكُمْ: “and so that your hearts may be reassured by it.”

Yūnus 10:7, وَاطْمَأَنُّوا بِهَا: “and they rested content in it” or “found reassurance in it.”

Al-Raʿd 13:28, وَتَطْمَئِنُّ قُلُوبُهُمْ بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ: “and their hearts find rest in remembering The God.”

In the same verse, أَلَا بِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ تَطْمَئِنُّ الْقُلُوبُ: “indeed, in remembering The God, hearts find rest.”

Al-Naḥl 16:106, وَقَلْبُهُ مُطْمَئِنٌّ بِالْإِيمَانِ: “while the heart remains assured in faith.”

Al-Naḥl 16:112, آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً: “secure and settled.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:95, يَمْشُونَ مُطْمَئِنِّينَ: “walking about as settled inhabitants.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:11, اطْمَأَنَّ بِهِ: “he rested content in it” or “felt reassured by it.”

Al-Fajr 89:27, النَّفْسُ الْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ: “the soul at peace and reassured.”

These renderings preserve the shared meaning of settled calm while maintaining differences between inward reassurance, social security, spatial settledness, and attachment to temporary prosperity.

For example, translating مطمئنين in Al-Isrāʾ as “those with no worries” is unsuitable because the context highlights living and being settled on earth.

Likewise, rendering اطمأن به in Al-Ḥajj as “he believed in the truth” is incorrect: the person’s reassurance is tied to the good that reaches him.

In النفس المطمئنة too, a title from later mystical ranks or psychological systems should not replace the Quranic description itself.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root ṭ m ʾ n concerns becoming calm, finding rest, and reaching a state of settledness. This meaning appears both in bodily and spatial conditions and in the human heart and soul.

The witness attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī shows an early use of مطمئن for peace of mind. Arabic dictionaries also use this family for low-lying ground. These usages clarify the field of stillness and settledness without establishing a certain historical origin for every branch.

The Quran contains 13 occurrences in 12 verses, many attributing reassurance to the heart.

In Al-Baqarah 2:260, Abraham affirms his faith while seeking reassurance by seeing how the dead are brought to life. In Al-Māʾidah 5:113, the disciples mention reassurance alongside knowledge and testimony. In Āl ʿImrān 3:126 and Al-Anfāl 8:10, divine good tidings reassure hearts amid danger; in Al-Raʿd 13:28, believers’ hearts find rest in remembering The God.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:103, reassurance relates to returning to secure, settled conditions. Al-Naḥl 16:112 describes a secure and settled town, while مطمئنين in Al-Isrāʾ 17:95 describes beings hypothetically established on earth.

Yet the Quran does not invariably regard reassurance as a virtue. In Yūnus 10:7, some rest content in worldly life and neglect divine signs. In Al-Ḥajj 22:11, a person is reassured when good comes, but turns away when tested.

These examples show that calm and reassurance may rest on an unstable or misleading basis. Feeling reassured alone does not prove a belief or choice correct.

In Al-Naḥl 16:106, the heart assured in faith remains inwardly committed even under compulsion and threat. In Al-Fajr 89:27, the reassured soul is described alongside returning to the Lord, being pleased, and being approved.

A final definition of ṭ m ʾ n is: becoming calm and settled after disturbance or instability, or enjoying a state of rest and settledness, whether in place and living conditions, in the heart and soul, or in trust and reliance on something.

This covers Abraham’s and believers’ reassurance, a community’s stability, beings settled on earth, and human contentment with worldly life.

The main distinction is that reassurance describes a person’s state or the situation being characterized; its truth, value, and duration cannot be inferred from the root alone.

Someone may be reassured by a firm truth and strengthened by it through hardship. Another may rely on temporary prosperity, power, wealth, or position, and lose peace and direction when it disappears.

The Quran therefore does more than mention becoming calm: different verses show what people rest in, why they trust it, and what becomes of that reassurance when circumstances change.

The value of reassurance lies not in a feeling of calm alone, but in the truth and stability of what underlies it.
