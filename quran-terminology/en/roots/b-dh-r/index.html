The Qur’anic root B DH R (badhr and tabdhīr)

From scattering seed on the ground to wasting resources: distinguishing generosity, spending, and wrongful consumption

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تُبَذِّرْ
(tubadhdhir)
Qur’anic form II imperfect, second-person singular, jussive after prohibition; Al-Isrāʾ 17:26

تَبْذِيرًا
(tabdhīran)
Qur’anic accusative verbal noun and cognate accusative; Al-Isrāʾ 17:26

الْمُبَذِّرِينَ
(al-mubadhdhirīn)
Qur’anic active participle, sound masculine plural, accusative; Al-Isrāʾ 17:27

بَذْر
(badhr)
Seed for sowing and other lexical uses; this form is not Qur’anic

بَذَرَ
(badhara)
Lexical base verb; not Qur’anic

بُذُور
(budhūr)
Lexical plural; not Qur’anic

بَذَّرَ
(badhdhara)

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root B DH R concerns scattering and dispersing something. A familiar use is scattering seed on the ground for cultivation. Within the same family, financial tabdhīr denotes wrongfully dissipating and wasting resources.

The uses have an intelligible relationship: something leaves its gathered position and is distributed among different places. Their outcome and value nevertheless differ. Scattering seed on suitable land may yield a valuable crop, whereas dispersing assets aimlessly or toward wrongful ends wastes them.

General dispersal must therefore be distinguished from the blameworthy financial sense of tabdhīr.

B DH R occurs three times in the Qur’an, in two verses of one surah. All three forms belong to form II:

  • تُبَذِّرْ (tubadhdhir), in Al-Isrāʾ 17:26: an imperfect verb, jussive within a prohibition.
  • تَبْذِيرًا (tabdhīran), in the same verse: an accusative verbal noun.
  • الْمُبَذِّرِينَ (al-mubadhdhirīn), in Al-Isrāʾ 17:27: the sound masculine plural of the active participle مُبَذِّر (mubadhdhir).

The Qur’an thus uses a prohibited action, its emphatic verbal noun, and a description of those who engage in it. Forms such as بَذْر (badhr), بَذَرَ (badhara), بُذُور (budhūr), and بَذَّار (badhdhār), though used in Arabic, do not occur in the Qur’an.

All three Qur’anic forms occur in one connected passage. The first verse commands giving the relative, needy person, and stranded traveler their due, and prohibits squandering. The next calls squanderers brothers of the devils and recalls Satan’s ingratitude.

This association matters: the prohibition stands directly alongside a command to give. It cannot be treated as an invitation to retain all one’s assets and withhold help from others.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Scattering and separating

In Maqāyīs al-lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s central sense as:

«نَثْرُ الشَّيْءِ وَتَفْرِيقُهُ»

nathru al-shayʾi wa-tafrīquhu

That is: “Scattering and separating something.”

He illustrates this with two uses: بَذَرْتُ الْبَذْرَ, scattering seed, and بَذَّرْتُ الْمَالَ, dissipating wealth.

Before any moral judgment, the definition describes a tangible action: something gathered in one place is dispersed among different places.

Dispersal does not intrinsically mean destruction. Sowing seed on suitable land forms part of cultivation and growth. When assets disappear without regard for rights, need, or a proper destination, the same image of dispersal can express waste.

The blameworthy financial sense is therefore not supplied by dispersing motion alone; outcome, manner, and destination also matter.

Badhr: seed for cultivation

Lisān al-ʿArab records several uses of بَذْر. It can denote grain set aside for sowing and, in some uses, the first emergence of plants from the ground.

The same source transmits al-Aṣmaʿī’s explanation of بَذَرَتِ الْأَرْضُ for land whose plants appear in scattered growth.

The uses are related but distinct: seed prepared for sowing differs from a plant that emerges afterward.

بَذَرَ الْحَبَّ likewise means scattering or sowing grain. The dispersal can have an entirely beneficial purpose.

Starting with agriculture and treating tabdhīr as every act of scattering or distribution would overlook its distinct financial usage. The Qur’anic occurrences concern wrongful spending, rather than agricultural sowing.

From sowing seed to wasting wealth

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains the relationship more fully in Al-Mufradāt. He connects tabdhīr with dispersal, identifies sowing seed as its underlying image, and describes the expression’s application to someone who wastes wealth.

He adds a subtle point: to someone unaware of cultivation’s outcome, scattering seed may appear to be throwing it away. Its actual outcome can instead be growth and harvest.

The explanation shows why dispersal’s outward appearance alone cannot define squandering.

Seed leaving the farmer’s hand is not necessarily lost. It is placed where it may produce a result.

Likewise, assets merely leaving their owner’s possession do not make an action blameworthy. Spending may provide food, education, treatment, development, or assistance. Its destination and manner must be considered.

Dispersing resources toward harm, corruption, or pointless and misplaced ends, however, fits tabdhīr’s recognized sense.

This semantic relationship is defensible without proving a definite derivational history. Agriculture supplies a tangible explanatory image, but does not establish that all other uses developed from it at an identifiable time.

3. Other early Arabic uses

People and animals dispersing

Lisān al-ʿArab records شَذَرَ بَذَرَ for people or camels dispersing in different directions.

Individuals or animals separate and go their different ways. The central meaning is dispersal, rather than financial waste or sin.

The family also includes بَذْر and بُذَارَة for offspring or lineage. بَذْرُ سُوءٍ can describe offspring with undesirable qualities. A relationship with spreading and extending a lineage is plausible, but the available sources do not establish its precise historical development.

The family is therefore not limited to economic consumption. Its wider Arabic range nevertheless does not mean that every sense is present in Qur’anic tabdhīr.

Speech spread without restraint

Lexical sources record بَذُور (badhūr) and بَذِير (badhīr) for someone who fails to keep secrets and spreads speech among people.

Here speech and information, rather than money or seed, are dispersed.

The image is clear: just as seed is scattered among different places, a talkative person spreads information that should remain protected among different people.

Revealing speech nevertheless differs from wasting wealth. Both can involve dispersal, but their subject and outcome differ.

الْمُبَذِّرِينَ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:27 should therefore not become simply “those who reveal secrets.” The passage concerns rights, giving, and the handling of resources. Speech-related uses attest Arabic’s range, rather than the preferred meaning of this Qur’anic expression.

A poetic witness: dispersing water

A poem attributed to al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī describes a cloud:

مُسْتَبْذِرًا يَزْعَبُ قُدَّامَهُ

mustabdhiran yazʿabu quddāmahu

يَرْمِي بِعُمِّ السَّمُرِ الْأَطْوَلِ

yarmī bi-ʿummi al-samuri al-aṭwali

Sense translation: “The water-scattering cloud drives forward and casts down the tall samur trees with its flood.” The second hemistich makes the trees the object of “casting”; its image is more than rain falling on trees.

The verse is transmitted in lexical sources including Tāj al-ʿArūs. Al-Sukkarī, the commentator on Hudhalī poetry, explains مُسْتَبْذِر here as dispersing water.

The wording varies, including يَزْعَبُ versus يَرْغَبُ. More significantly, the Dīwān al-Hudhaliyyīn edition reads مُسْتَبْدِرًا with dāl, whereas Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab transmit dhāl under B DH R. The Arabic displayed here retains the supplied Tāj wording. Attribution to al-Mutanakhkhil, a poet classified as pre-Islamic, is reported, but the verse’s own composition date is not independently established. Its value for this root depends on accepting the dhāl reading.

Under the dhāl reading and the transmitted explanation of al-Sukkarī, istibdhār concerns dispersing water. The dāl reading belongs for examination under B D R. This witness alone therefore does not establish pre-Qur’anic B DH R usage beyond doubt.

Study of B D R

The cloud spreads water widely, while its flood carries trees along. This powerful natural image is not itself a moral judgment on spending. In other contexts, dispersal can also accompany the watering of land and growth.

On the dhāl reading, the witness fits general dispersal, while its textual uncertainty and natural context must remain visible. Every form in the family should not simply be rendered as extravagance or wrongdoing.

Badhdhar as a place name

In Kuthayyir ʿAzzah’s poetry, بَذَّر (Badhdhar) names a watering place or well in the Meccan area. Lexical sources also record the name.

This is first a proper name. Its resemblance to a root does not authorize a historical explanation of its naming without evidence.

The distinction applies throughout Qur’anic root studies: a proper name does not necessarily carry a related lexical root’s descriptive sense in every sentence.

4. Al-Isrāʾ 17:26: giving what is due and avoiding squandering

Al-Isrāʾ 17:26

وَآتِ ذَا الْقُرْبَىٰ حَقَّهُ وَالْمِسْكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَلَا تُبَذِّرْ تَبْذِيرًا

wa-āti dhā al-qurbā ḥaqqahu wa-l-miskīna wa-bna al-sabīli wa-lā tubadhdhir tabdhīran

Translation: “Give the relative his due, and also the needy person and the stranded traveler; and do not spend wastefully.”

The morphology of tubadhdhir tabdhīran

تُبَذِّرْ is a form II imperfect verb addressed to a singular person. It follows prohibitive لَا and is therefore jussive.

تَبْذِيرًا is the same verb’s accusative verbal noun, functioning as a cognate accusative, mafʿūl muṭlaq. The verb and its cognate verbal noun emphasize the prohibited action itself.

وَلَا تُبَذِّرْ تَبْذِيرًا is thus an explicit prohibition of squandering.

Form II alone does not establish that every act must be repeated or that the sum wasted must be very large. The morphology can express an action and its intensity or realization, but does not determine the expenditure’s precise amount.

The expression also lacks an independently stated object such as الْمَال, “wealth.” Financial spending is understood from tabdhīr’s established usage and the context.

“Do not spend wastefully” conveys the meaning concisely and naturally in English. The lexical explanation should clarify that tabdhīr is neither every act of spending nor every large expenditure.

Why does the prohibition accompany a command to give what is due?

The verse begins by commanding that the relative receive his due, then mentions the needy person and stranded traveler.

The prohibition’s position after this command matters.

A prohibition standing alone might be read merely as advice to reduce personal expenditure. Here, however, giving others what is due precedes it.

Assets are therefore more than a means of satisfying their owner’s wishes. Others may have rights in them that require observance.

Consider someone able to address a struggling relative’s essential need, but who dissipates assets in pointless or harmful spending. The problem is more than the sum spent: the conduct may also leave a due obligation unmet.

Conversely, someone may spend a large sum treating a needy patient or helping a family in difficulty. The amount alone does not turn the expenditure into tabdhīr.

This example does not make every charitable payment appropriate in every amount and circumstance merely because its purpose is good. Actual means, dependents’ rights, and consequences also require consideration.

The continuation of this surah supports that caution.

Rights and the manner of spending

حَقَّهُ, at the verse’s opening, belongs to Ḥ Q Q and concerns what must be observed or delivered for the relative.

تُبَذِّرْ belongs to another root and concerns misplaced spending or waste.

Two distinct but connected matters arise: giving what is due and avoiding the dissipation of resources.

Their juxtaposition does not make tabdhīr lexically mean “withholding a relative’s due.” Someone may give that due while wasting wealth elsewhere, or withhold it without spending large sums.

The Qur’an places the conduct side by side without turning the two into one word.

Is distributing wealth in every direction tabdhīr?

Early exegetical accounts place two main emphases on tabdhīr.

The first is spending toward a wrongful end or on something with no rightful claim. Al-Ṭabarī transmits such explanations from Ibn ʿAbbās and others.

The second concerns spending beyond what is needed, potentially exhausting assets and the capacity to meet essential needs. Lexical sources including Lisān al-ʿArab also record this emphasis.

The emphases can converge, but are not precisely identical.

A small expenditure toward harm may be wrong in purpose. Spending all one’s assets, even with a good intention, may instead undermine the ability to meet necessary responsibilities.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits Mujāhid’s account placing the criterion in the spending’s rightful or wrongful destination, rather than its amount alone. That attests an early interpretation, but should not replace other verses’ discussion of balanced spending and avoiding destitution afterward.

Tabdhīr cannot therefore be reduced to an equation in which every large expense is bad and every small one good. Purpose, amount, capacity, and consequences must be considered separately.

5. Al-Isrāʾ 17:27: squanderers and brotherhood with the devils

Al-Isrāʾ 17:27

إِنَّ الْمُبَذِّرِينَ كَانُوا إِخْوَانَ الشَّيَاطِينِ ۖ وَكَانَ الشَّيْطَانُ لِرَبِّهِ كَفُورًا

inna al-mubadhdhirīna kānū ikhwāna al-shayāṭīni wa-kāna al-shayṭānu li-rabbihi kafūran

Translation: “The squanderers are indeed brothers of the devils; and Satan has been deeply ungrateful to his Lord.”

The meaning of al-mubadhdhirīn

الْمُبَذِّرِينَ is the sound masculine plural of the active participle مُبَذِّر. The participle derives from form II بَذَّرَ and denotes someone who squanders.

The plural here is the noun governed by إِنَّ and is therefore accusative, marked by yāʾ. كَانُوا in this sentence does not establish that the relationship existed only in the past and has now ceased.

The structure does not restrict the verse to people whose entire lives have been devoted to squandering or who act that way constantly. An active participle can identify a person through conduct without independently determining its frequency or permanence.

The passage describes people who engage in tabdhīr, not farmers scattering seed.

إِخْوَانَ الشَّيَاطِينِ: what kind of brotherhood?

إِخْوَان (ikhwān) is a plural of أَخ (akh). Brotherhood in Arabic can be genealogical, but can also concern association, shared allegiance, and similarity of conduct.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:27 does not describe a family relationship or shared physical nature between human beings and Satan. It expresses proximity in conduct and approach.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains the brotherhood through following the devils’ path and way.

This fits the context. People who waste favors and resources associate, in their use of what has been entrusted to them, with a course incompatible with The God’s command.

The comparison does not establish that a squanderer has become essentially demonic or that all the person’s deeds and beliefs are identical to Satan’s.

The verse’s wording does not establish that generalization. It judges particular conduct, condemning it through the forceful image of brotherhood with the devils.

Why does the verse end with Satan’s ingratitude?

The closing says:

«وَكَانَ الشَّيْطَانُ لِرَبِّهِ كَفُورًا»

wa-kāna al-shayṭānu li-rabbihi kafūran

كَفُورًا is from K F R on the pattern faʿūl. It expresses Satan’s intense ingratitude to his Lord in this context.

Following the condemnation of squanderers, it creates a semantic relationship between wasting a favor and being ungrateful to its giver.

That relationship should not be imposed on B DH R’s lexical definition. Tabdhīr does not mean kufr, nor does kufr always mean wasting wealth.

The verse juxtaposes the squanderers’ conduct and Satan’s ingratitude. Wrongful use of resources is thus more than a financial matter: it also concerns a person’s response to a favor and the associated responsibility.

A person who spends wrongly should nevertheless not instantly be judged identical to the devils in every aspect of faith and character. The verse strongly condemns conduct; individual responsibility also involves awareness, choice, action, and actual circumstances.

6. Tabdhīr, excess, spending, and related words

B DH R (tabdhīr) and S R F (isrāf)

Isrāf and tabdhīr are close in financial and spending contexts. Many lexical sources explain tabdhīr through isrāf, and both can describe wrongful consumption.

Their central semantic fields nevertheless differ.

Isrāf concerns exceeding limits and can apply to consumption, conduct, sin, and other settings.

Tabdhīr belongs to the family of dispersal and, financially, concerns wrongfully dissipating and wasting resources.

Some lexical and exegetical analyses distinguish excessive amount in isrāf from expenditure in the wrong place in tabdhīr. Tāj al-ʿArūs transmits that distinction alongside other explanations.

The division helps identify two aspects of conduct but should not become an absolute rule. An expense can both exceed limits and serve a wrongful end, making both descriptions applicable.

The Qur’an uses the two families in related settings but with distinct forms.

For example, Al-Anʿām 6:141 recalls agricultural favors, commands eating the produce and giving its due on harvest day, and prohibits excess.

The root there is S R F, not B DH R.

A study of Al-Anʿām 6:141 should therefore not replace isrāf with tabdhīr without regard for the roots’ distinction.

Study of S R F

Balance in spending

Al-Furqān 25:67

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا أَنْفَقُوا لَمْ يُسْرِفُوا وَلَمْ يَقْتُرُوا وَكَانَ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ قَوَامًا

wa-lladhīna idhā anfaqū lam yusrifū wa-lam yaqturū wa-kāna bayna dhālika qawāman

Translation: “Those who, when they spend, neither exceed limits nor constrict, but maintain a firm, balanced course between the two.”

This verse does not use B DH R, but helps clarify the boundary between appropriate and wrongful spending.

It praises neither excessive spending nor constriction. The criterion is appropriate measure and balance.

Qawām should not simply become a fixed percentage of income. Financial circumstances, essential needs, responsibilities, and types of expenditure differ.

An expense ordinary and manageable for a wealthy person might prevent someone on a low income from meeting a family’s basic needs.

Conversely, a large expense for treatment or saving lives may be necessary and proportionate, while a very small payment toward harm is wrongful in purpose.

Balance is therefore more than the size of the number; the subject and circumstances of spending matter.

This accords with Al-Isrāʾ 17:26, where giving others their due stands beside the prohibition of squandering.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:29: neither withholding nor opening the hand without limit

Al-Isrāʾ 17:29

وَلَا تَجْعَلْ يَدَكَ مَغْلُولَةً إِلَىٰ عُنُقِكَ وَلَا تَبْسُطْهَا كُلَّ الْبَسْطِ فَتَقْعُدَ مَلُومًا مَحْسُورًا

wa-lā tajʿal yadaka maghlūlatan ilā ʿunuqika wa-lā tabsuṭhā kulla al-basṭi fa-taqʿuda malūman maḥsūran

Translation: “Do not keep your hand bound to your neck, nor open it to its fullest extent, lest you sit blameworthy and depleted.”

The verse uses two bodily images: a hand bound to the neck and a hand opened completely.

In the setting of giving and spending, the first suggests severe withholding, the second unrestricted expenditure.

Together they clarify the prohibition of squandering alongside the responsibility to help others.

Retaining everything for oneself while disregarding others’ rights approaches the problem from one side. Spending everything without regard for capacity and responsibility, ending depleted, approaches it from the other.

Opening the hand should nevertheless not be presented as B DH R’s lexical meaning. The image uses B S Ṭ, and its relationship with tabdhīr comes from the passage’s context.

Study of B S Ṭ

B DH R (tabdhīr) and B KH L (bukhl)

Bukhl concerns withholding and refusing to give. Qur’anic tabdhīr concerns waste and misplaced spending.

They can represent two undesirable aspects of financial conduct, but are not simple absolute opposites.

Someone may spend lavishly on personal desires while withholding assistance from others, wasting resources in one area and refusing necessary generosity in another.

Conversely, someone may be very generous while failing to observe appropriate limits in other spending.

Not every squanderer is necessarily generous, and a miser is not necessarily free of squandering.

Each root has its own criterion: withholding in bukhl, and wrongful dissipation and waste in tabdhīr.

Study of B KH L

B DH R (tabdhīr) and N F Q (infāq)

Financial infāq concerns spending and paying out of one’s resources.

Spending itself is neither tabdhīr nor conclusive evidence of commendable generosity. Resources can be spent toward rightful or wrongful ends.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:26 concerns giving the relative, needy person, and stranded traveler their due. That payment accompanies the prohibition of squandering; spending itself is not opposed.

Assets leaving their owner’s possession have not necessarily been squandered.

Even the agricultural image makes this clear: seed sown on suitable land leaves the storehouse but may produce a multiplied harvest.

This image should not become an unconditional economic or religious promise that every good expenditure returns multiplied wealth in this life. The point is only the distinction between parting with something and wasting it.

Study of N F Q

B DH R (tabdhīr) and B DH L (badhl)

Badhl and badhr share their first two root letters but have different third letters.

Badhl concerns giving, making available, or relinquishing exclusive possession of something.

Badhr concerns scattering and dispersing; in tabdhīr it takes the sense of wrongful waste.

Giving wealth can therefore be commendable generosity, while Qur’anic tabdhīr is condemned.

Sound resemblance should not make generosity and wasting resources identical in meaning.

B DH R (badhr) and N TH R (nathr)

Nathr concerns scattering and is thus close to badhr’s general sense.

Ibn Fāris himself uses nathr to explain B DH R’s semantic core.

This proximity does not establish a shared root; N TH R is an independent family.

Nathr can describe scattering various things, whereas agricultural badhr has a recognized relationship with sowing seed, and form II also expresses financial squandering.

Nathr helps explain the semantic core, but cannot replace every Qur’anic B DH R use.

Study of N TH R

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish agricultural usage from the blameworthy financial usage of B DH R.

For بَذَرَ الْحَبَّ, “scattered grain” and “sowed seed” are natural equivalents.

For the noun بَذْر, “seed for sowing” or “seed” is suitable.

For dispersing people, animals, or speech, “scatter,” “disperse,” and “spread” can be appropriate, depending on context.

The two Qur’anic verses, however, use form II expressions concerning squandering and those who engage in it.

Translating وَلَا تُبَذِّرْ تَبْذِيرًا

Several English renderings warrant consideration.

“Do not engage in tabdhīr” retains the Arabic term, but is insufficient for readers unfamiliar with its precise sense.

“Do not be extravagant” is familiar and natural, but can restrict the word too strongly to spending large sums.

“Do not waste your wealth pointlessly” clarifies the intended meaning, but “wealth” is understood from context rather than explicitly stated in the Arabic phrase.

The proposed base rendering is:

“And do not spend wastefully.”

It is concise, adds no new explicit object, and naturally conveys misplaced expenditure in English.

The lexical explanation can clarify that tabdhīr is more than high expenditure: it involves wrongfully spending and dissipating resources.

Translating al-mubadhdhirīn

“Squanderers” corresponds closely to the active participle’s structure.

“Spendthrifts” and “those who waste their resources” also convey the sense, but the former may narrow it and the latter is a longer explanation.

“Squanderers” is therefore appropriate where the accompanying root study supplies further explanation.

إِخْوَانَ الشَّيَاطِينِ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:27 is best retained as “brothers of the devils.” “Devilish people” would remove part of the verse’s image and structure.

“Deeply ungrateful” conveys كَفُورًا’s intensity without adding a new concept.

The proposed translations of the two verses are therefore:

Al-Isrāʾ 17:26: “Give the relative his due, and also the needy person and the stranded traveler; and do not spend wastefully.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:27: “The squanderers are indeed brothers of the devils; and Satan has been deeply ungrateful to his Lord.”

8. Conclusion

B DH R concerns scattering and dispersal in Arabic. One tangible use is sowing seed on the ground; the family also includes dispersing people, spreading speech, and handling resources.

It occurs only three times in the Qur’an, in two verses of Al-Isrāʾ. All are form II uses concerning tabdhīr: waste and wrongful spending.

Sowing seed and financial tabdhīr share an intelligible image of dispersal, but differ in value. Sowing can be beneficial, while dispersing resources toward wrongful ends wastes them.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:26, the prohibition accompanies the command to give the relative, needy person, and stranded traveler their due. Avoiding waste should not become an excuse for withholding generosity or disregarding others’ rights.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:27 calls squanderers brothers of the devils and recalls Satan’s ingratitude. This strongly condemns wrongful consumption without asserting biological kinship between humans and devils.

Comparison with excess, withholding, spending, and balance shows that appropriate consumption cannot be judged by amount alone. Destination, others’ rights, actual capacity, and consequences also matter.

No fixed numerical threshold should define tabdhīr, nor should every large gift be called squandering. Conversely, a small amount does not protect an expenditure from being wrongful.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

B DH R in Arabic concerns scattering and dispersing something; its familiar agricultural use is sowing seed. In the Qur’an the root occurs only in forms related to tabdhīr, denoting misplaced expenditure and waste of wealth and resources. Tabdhīr overlaps with isrāf but is not limited to a large amount spent; nor is every act of scattering, giving, or parting with assets squandering. Its blameworthy sense is understood through the spending’s destination, observance of rights, and the verses’ context.
