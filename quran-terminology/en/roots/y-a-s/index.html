Giving up hope, despair of an outcome, and the distinction between despair, recognizing reality, and hoping for divine relief

In Arabic, Y ʾ S primarily denotes giving up hope or ceasing to expect to obtain something. Yaʾisa can describe someone no longer expecting an event or seeing a desired outcome as beyond reach.

Despair does not invariably mean sadness, depression, weak faith, or surrender to difficulties. Someone may lose hope of a person’s return, obtaining something, succeeding in an undertaking, or a particular event occurring, without every such case carrying the same moral judgment.

The Qur’an makes this distinction clear. Despair of divine mercy and relief is condemned in Yūsuf 12:87; Al-Māʾidah 5:3 mentions disbelievers losing hope concerning the believers’ religion; and Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 uses the root for women no longer expecting menstruation to return.

Al-Raʿd 13:31 also contains yayʾas in an expression some early lexicographers explain as knowing or becoming aware. This disputed use requires separate examination.

One lexical definition records two branches:

إِحْدَاهُمَا الْيَأْسُ: قَطْعُ الرَّجَاءِ

iḥdāhumā al-yaʾsu qaṭʿu al-rajāʾi

“One is despair: the cutting off of hope.”

The same definition gives the second branch as أَلَمْ تَيْأَسْ, “Have you not known?” Thus, explaining yaʾs through knowledge has a real place in Arabic lexical tradition, although its independence and scope remain disputed.

Y ʾ S occurs thirteen times in eleven verses across nine surahs. These fall into three morphological groups: Form I yaʾisa and its inflections, Form X istayʾasa, and the description yaʾūs.

1. The semantic core in early Arabic dictionaries

Giving up hope and ceasing to expect

One lexical definition says:

الْيَأْسُ: الْقُنُوطُ، وَقِيلَ: الْيَأْسُ نَقِيضُ الرَّجَاءِ.

al-yaʾsu al-qunūṭu wa-qīla al-yaʾsu naqīḍu al-rajāʾi

Meaning: “Despair is qunūṭ; it is also said to be the opposite of hope.”

A shorter definition says:

الْيَأْسُ: انْتِفَاءُ الطَّمَعِ.

al-yaʾsu intifāʾu al-ṭamaʿi

Meaning: “Despair is the disappearance of expectation.”

These definitions share a semantic field: a person wants or expects something to happen, but no longer looks forward to its fulfillment.

Having no hope and feeling sad are distinct. Someone may lose hope of something yet remain calm through accepting reality. Another may be deeply sad while retaining hope.

Lexically, yaʾs concerns expectation and hope, not necessarily the extent of suffering or sorrow.

Nor does every instance mean abandoning all effort. Someone may give up on one route while pursuing another. Whether despair ends action depends on context.

Is despair always blameworthy?

No. The lexical meaning alone does not determine moral judgment.

If someone loses hope of committing an injustice and realizes they can no longer harm another person, that loss of hope is not necessarily bad. Nor is losing hope of deceiving others inherently sinful.

By contrast, the Qur’an condemns despair of divine mercy and relief where there is still reason to hope for them.

The object of hope or despair therefore matters to evaluation. The root’s meaning remains, while the value of the behavior depends on what the person has lost hope of.

2. Important Arabic forms and derivatives

Yaʾisa yayʾasu: to lose hope

Yaʾisa is a Form I verb meaning to lose hope of something or cease to expect an outcome.

A familiar construction is:

يَئِسَ مِنَ الشَّيْءِ يَيْأَسُ

yaʾisa mina al-shayʾi yayʾasu

Meaning: he lost hope of something.

Yayʾisu is also recorded alongside yayʾasu. Grammatical and lexical transmissions report this vowel variation.

A report concerning Muḍar dialects records an imperfect with kasrah in some branches and fatḥah in others.

This morphological difference does not change the basic meaning.

Yaʾs, yaʾāsah, and yaʾas

Dictionaries record yaʾs, yaʾāsah, and yaʾas as verbal nouns.

All concern despair and giving up hope.

The verbal noun yaʾs itself does not occur in the Qur’an, which expresses the concept through verbs and derived descriptions.

Yāʾis: someone who has lost hope

Yāʾis is the Form I active participle.

It describes someone who has lost hope of obtaining something.

Generally, yāʾis can describe any person. In menstruation-related usage, āyisah or yāʾisah describes a woman who no longer expects menstruation.

This is a specific application of the broader meaning of ceasing to expect.

Yaʾūs: deeply despairing or prone to despair

Yaʾūs follows the faʿūl pattern, which in many Arabic uses can indicate intensity or recurrence of a feature.

It makes despair prominent in describing someone’s behavior or condition.

This form occurs three times in the Qur’an: Hūd 11:9, Al-Isrāʾ 17:83, and Fuṣṣilat 41:49.

The pattern alone does not establish that a person despairs throughout life and about everything. These verses use it in particular situations, especially hardship or loss of a blessing.

Istayʾasa: to reach despair

Istayʾasa is Form X.

It can express reaching despair, losing hope after effort and expectation, or an intensified loss of hope.

Lexical transmissions also explain istayʾasa as yaʾisa, comparing other Form I and Form X pairs whose meanings overlap in some uses.

Form X therefore does not invariably mean becoming completely hopeless after a very long time.

In both Qur’anic occurrences, however, the context concerns a difficult stage reached after earlier effort or expectation.

Ayʾasa, āyasa, and taʾyīs

In Arabic, أَيْأَسَهُ مِنَ الشَّيْءِ means he caused someone to lose hope of something.

Āyasahu is also recorded with this meaning.

Taʾyīs likewise denotes causing despair, but its radical order connects it with ayyasa and the transposed ʾ Y S family. It is not the direct verbal noun of ayʾasa or āyasa.

Losing hope and causing another to lose hope are distinct. Another person’s words or actions may cause a loss of hope, but that cause should be examined separately from the resulting state.

Ayisa and the transposition of radicals

Dictionaries also record ayisa with the meaning of yaʾisa.

One lexical analysis treats ayisa as metathesis: a rearrangement of radicals in yaʾisa, rather than necessarily a wholly independent root.

Other transmissions likewise record the variation between ʾ Y S and Y ʾ S forms.

This matters to word history, but the Qur’anic forms discussed here are classified under Y ʾ S.

The proper name Ilyās should not be derived from this root. It names a prophet in the Qur’an and is not etymologically identical with yaʾisa.

3. Early Arabic poetry and usage

Causing despair in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says in his Muʿallaqah:

وَأَيْأَسَنِي مِنْ كُلِّ خَيْرٍ طَلَبْتُهُ
كَأَنَّا وَضَعْنَاهُ إِلَى رَمْسٍ مُلْحَدِ

wa-ayʾasanī min kulli khayrin ṭalabtuhu / ka-annā waḍaʿnāhu ilā ramsin mulḥadi

Meaning: “He made me despair of every good I sought from him, as though we had laid that request in a grave.”

Ayʾasanī is transitive: another person causes the poet to give up hope of obtaining good.

Comparing the request to something placed in a grave shows that the poet sees it as beyond reach.

The witness matters because it illustrates giving up hope in a setting outside religious belief.

The poet discusses a human request and another person’s failure to answer it, rather than divine mercy or faith.

Transmissions vary in ayʾasanī and parts of the second hemistich. The quoted wording is nevertheless recorded in commentaries on the poem, with the causative meaning retained in lexical explanations.

A-lam tayʾasū: a witness for knowing

A much-debated witness is a line attributed to Suḥaym ibn Wathīl al-Riyāḥī or one of his sons:

أَقُولُ لَهُمْ بِالشِّعْبِ إِذْ يَأْسِرُونَنِي
أَلَمْ تَيْأَسُوا أَنِّي ابْنُ فَارِسِ زَهْدَمِ

aqūlu lahum bi-al-shiʿbi idh yaʾsirūnanī / a-lam tayʾasū annī ibnu fārisi zahdami

Meaning: “As they were taking me captive in the mountain pass, I said to them: Do you not know that I am the son of Zahdam’s horseman?”

A-lam tayʾasū is explained here as “Have you not known?”

Lexical and interpretive transmissions use this witness in explaining Al-Raʿd 13:31.

Several critical qualifications apply.

First, attribution varies between Suḥaym and his son, because one account identifies Zahdam as Suḥaym’s horse.

Second, yaʾsirūnanī and yaysirūnanī are both transmitted in the first hemistich.

Third, readings in the second include “son of Zahdam’s horseman,” “son of Zahdam’s killer,” and “son of Lāzim’s horseman.”

Examination of the collected poetry attributed to Suḥaym has also raised doubts about whether he composed the line.

The witness should therefore not be assigned uncritically to one person or precise historical period.

Does this establish that yaʾs means knowledge?

It shows that tayʾasū in an early usage was explained through taʿlamū, “you know.”

It does not make yaʾs mean knowledge in every Arabic use.

Lexicographers and grammarians disagree about whether this is an independent meaning.

Some identify it as a particular usage of Nakhaʿ or Hawāzin dialects.

Others connect knowledge and despair semantically: knowing that something is impossible can cause a person to lose hope of it.

Knowing then causes the loss of hope, and one may sometimes be used to explain the other.

This disagreement is particularly relevant to Al-Raʿd 13:31.

5. Ceasing to expect a particular outcome

Al-Māʾidah 5:3: the disbelievers’ loss of hope

الْيَوْمَ يَئِسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ دِينِكُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِ

al-yawma yaʾisa alladhīna kafarū min dīnikum fa-lā takhshawhum wa-ikhshawni

“Today those who disbelieved have lost hope concerning your religion. Do not fear them, but fear Me.”

The object of despair here is “your religion.”

The context concerns disbelievers losing hope of an aim they pursued concerning the believers’ religion.

Yaʾisa alone does not identify their precise aim or why they lost hope. Context and related verses must address these questions.

“Today” is a time expression; identifying its historical referent is a separate inquiry, not part of the root’s meaning.

The loss of hope concerns people’s conduct toward the believers’ religion. As a lexical state, it differs from despair of divine mercy.

Yūsuf 12:80: losing hope of a request being accepted

فَلَمَّا اسْتَيْأَسُوا مِنْهُ خَلَصُوا نَجِيًّا

fa-lammā istayʾasū minhu khalaṣū najiyyan

“When they had lost hope of him, they withdrew to confer privately.”

Joseph’s brothers have tried to secure their brother’s release and even offered that one of them be taken instead.

Their request has been refused.

Istayʾasū minhu thus refers to losing hope of their request being accepted.

They do not cease all activity afterward: they withdraw to consult and decide what to do next.

This is a significant witness: giving up on one route need not end all effort.

A request may no longer offer hope of success, while another way of addressing the problem remains to be sought.

This agrees with the verse’s installed Forqan rendering.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4: no longer expecting menstruation

وَاللَّائِي يَئِسْنَ مِنَ الْمَحِيضِ مِنْ نِسَائِكُمْ إِنِ ارْتَبْتُمْ فَعِدَّتُهُنَّ ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ

wa-allāʾī yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi min nisāʾikum ini irtabtum fa-ʿiddatuhunna thalāthatu ashhurin

“Those among your women who no longer expect menstruation, if you are in doubt, have a waiting period of three months.”

Yaʾisna is a third-person feminine plural past verb.

The object is al-maḥīḍ: menstruation.

This has no necessary connection with psychological despair or losing hope of divine mercy. It refers to women no longer expecting menstruation.

In familiar legal and lexical usage, it concerns the end of menstruation.

The verb alone establishes no numerical age for all women in every society. The timing differs between individuals.

The following phrase, وَاللَّائِي لَمْ يَحِضْنَ, and rules concerning the waiting period belong to a separate study of the verse. Their legal and interpretive conclusions should not enter the root’s definition.

This is a particularly clear witness that yaʾs is not inherently sinful or blameworthy.

6. Despair of divine mercy and relief

Yūsuf 12:87: hope accompanied by effort

يَا بَنِيَّ اذْهَبُوا فَتَحَسَّسُوا مِنْ يُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ إِنَّهُ لَا يَيْأَسُ مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الْكَافِرُونَ

yā baniyya idhhabū fa-taḥassasū min yūsufa wa-akhīhi wa-lā tayʾasū min rawḥi allāhi innahu lā yayʾasu min rawḥi allāhi illā al-qawmu al-kāfirūna

“My sons, go and seek news of Joseph and his brother. Do not despair of The God’s relief; none but disbelieving people despair of The God’s relief.”

After prolonged sorrow and separation from Joseph, Jacob sends his sons to search.

Two commands stand together:

اذْهَبُوا فَتَحَسَّسُوا: go and search.

وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا: do not lose hope.

Jacob does not substitute hope for effort. He calls for action and searching while prohibiting despair.

Study of Ḥ S S

The verse thus shows that hoping for divine relief is compatible with trying to find a way forward.

Rawḥ Allāh requires lexical study independently of yaʾs. Early dictionaries and interpretations explain it here through relief, ease after hardship, and mercy.

Forqan likewise uses the Persian equivalent of relief, which suits the context.

Study of R W Ḥ

Is every loss of hope disbelief?

The final phrase says:

إِنَّهُ لَا يَيْأَسُ مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الْكَافِرُونَ

This concerns despair of rawḥ Allāh, not losing hope of anything whatsoever.

It cannot therefore be generalized to label someone a disbeliever for losing hope of a successful transaction, winning a competition, or fulfilling a personal wish.

Nor should the statement be emptied of meaning: the Qur’an explicitly connects disbelief with giving up hope of divine relief.

Hoping in divine power and mercy differs from being certain that a particular personal wish will be fulfilled.

Someone may not know whether The God will fulfill a wish in its desired form, while still hoping for possible relief and mercy.

The verse does not promise that every wish will necessarily be fulfilled in this world precisely as expected.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:23: despair of mercy

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَلِقَائِهِ أُولَٰئِكَ يَئِسُوا مِنْ رَحْمَتِي

wa-alladhīna kafarū bi-āyāti allāhi wa-liqāʾihi ulāʾika yaʾisū min raḥmatī

“Those who disbelieved in the signs of The God and meeting with Him have lost hope of My mercy.”

Despair again has a specific object: “My mercy.”

The context places it beside disbelief in divine signs and meeting with Him.

The context therefore concerns despair of divine mercy in connection with rejecting His signs and meeting with Him, rather than every disappointment over an everyday wish.

This relates to Yūsuf 12:87, but the two verses should not be equated without considering their audiences and contexts.

7. Yaʾūs: reacting to hardship and loss of blessings

Hūd 11:9: despair and ingratitude

وَلَئِنْ أَذَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنَّا رَحْمَةً ثُمَّ نَزَعْنَاهَا مِنْهُ إِنَّهُ لَيَئُوسٌ كَفُورٌ

wa-la-in adhaqnā al-insāna minnā raḥmatan thumma nazaʿnāhā minhu innahu la-yaʾūsun kafūrun

“If We give the human being a taste of mercy from Us, then take it away, he is indeed deeply despairing and ungrateful.”

Yaʾūs and kafūr stand together.

Despair and ingratitude are two different responses.

Yaʾūs concerns expectation and hope; kafūr concerns ingratitude or denying a blessing.

They should not be treated as complete synonyms.

Hūd 11:10 describes another reaction: tasting ease after hardship, the person turns to joy mixed with boasting and self-importance.

Hūd 11:11 excepts those who remain steadfast and do good deeds.

Together, the verses describe unstable and excessive reactions to changing conditions.

Yaʾūs here concerns a response to losing a blessing, rather than an absolute definition of everyone at all times.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83: despair in hardship

وَإِذَا أَنْعَمْنَا عَلَى الْإِنْسَانِ أَعْرَضَ وَنَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ كَانَ يَئُوسًا

wa-idhā anʿamnā ʿalā al-insāni aʿraḍa wa-naʾā bi-jānibihi wa-idhā massahu al-sharru kāna yaʾūsan

“When We favor the human being, he turns away and draws himself aside; when harm touches him, he becomes despairing.”

Two contrasting reactions appear: turning away during prosperity and despair during adversity.

Yaʾūsan is the predicate of kāna, describing the person’s condition.

This does not say that everyone saddened by hardship is the same person who turns away from The God. It presents a pattern of human behavior to be understood in context.

Yaʾs should not be translated as sadness, fear, or pain. Those may accompany it, but the word specifically concerns hope and expectation.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49: yaʾs and qunūṭ

لَا يَسْأَمُ الْإِنْسَانُ مِنْ دُعَاءِ الْخَيْرِ وَإِنْ مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ فَيَئُوسٌ قَنُوطٌ

lā yasʾamu al-insānu min duʿāʾi al-khayri wa-in massahu al-sharru fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭun

“The human being does not tire of asking for good; if harm touches him, he is despairing and hopeless.”

Yaʾūs and qanūṭ appear together.

Lexicographers often explain qunūṭ through yaʾs, while some distinguish them.

One explanation makes qunūṭ more intense despair; another makes yaʾs the inward loss of hope and qunūṭ its outward effects in conduct.

Such distinctions occur in interpretations and linguistic explanations, but this pairing alone cannot establish them as an exceptionless rule.

Two related descriptions clearly combine to emphasize the reaction to hardship.

A detailed comparison belongs to the separate study of Q N Ṭ.

8. Istayʾasa in Yūsuf 12:110: what did the messengers lose hope of?

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا اسْتَيْأَسَ الرُّسُلُ وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ قَدْ كُذِبُوا جَاءَهُمْ نَصْرُنَا

ḥattā idhā istayʾasa al-rusulu wa-ẓannū annahum qad kudhibū jāʾahum naṣrunā

“When the messengers had lost hope and they thought they had been lied to, Our help came to them.”

Both istayʾasa and the pronouns make this a difficult Qur’anic passage.

The verb states that the messengers reached despair, but no explicit complement such as “of their people believing” or “of divine help” follows it.

The object must therefore be identified through context and the surrounding verses.

First interpretation: losing hope of the people believing

One interpretation understands the messengers to have lost hope of their deniers believing and accepting their call.

Then, when opponents thought the messengers had not spoken truthfully about their promises, divine help arrived.

This fits istayʾasa as losing hope of the call being accepted.

Other interpretations and variant readings

In وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ قَدْ كُذِبُوا, two important readings are kudhibū without gemination of dhāl and kudhdhibū with gemination.

This variation and differing possible referents of ẓannū have produced several interpretations.

Some attribute the thought that promises were false to the messengers’ opponents; others describe the messengers’ fear that even their followers might deny them.

Reports also describe the messengers doubting while awaiting help; these reports themselves have been strongly disputed.

These questions must be examined before identifying the object categorically as despair of The God.

Forqan already has a translation of the verse. This root study does not authorize changing it; variant readings and pronoun referents require separate examination in the verse study.

The lexical conclusion

The established lexical point is reaching a stage of lost hope.

Whether the object is people accepting faith, a difficult situation ending, or something else depends on context and pronoun analysis.

The verb istayʾasa itself should not be made to carry every interpretive disagreement about the verse.

9. Al-Raʿd 13:31: does yaʾisa mean knowing?

Al-Raʿd 13:31

أَفَلَمْ يَيْأَسِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْ لَوْ يَشَاءُ اللَّهُ لَهَدَى النَّاسَ جَمِيعًا

a-fa-lam yayʾasi alladhīna āmanū an law yashāʾu allāhu la-hadā al-nāsa jamīʿan

This is among the study’s most important lexical disputes.

First view: yaʾisa means knowing

On this reading, the phrase means:

“Have those who believed not known that, had The God willed, He would have guided all people?”

One interpretation accepts this meaning and reports that some Arabic speakers used yaʾisa for knowing.

Early transmissions attribute the usage to Nakhaʿ or Hawāzin.

Lexical definitions record it as the root’s second branch.

Some transmitted readings or explanations also give أَفَلَمْ يَتَبَيَّنِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا.

That is not the standard muṣḥaf reading and should not replace the principal verse text without explanation.

Second view: yaʾs remains giving up hope

Some lexicographers reject a direct equation of yaʾisa with knowing.

On this analysis, losing hope of an outcome implies that it has become clear that the outcome will not occur through the expected means.

Another analysis explicitly says that explaining yaʾs through knowledge need not make knowledge the word’s original lexical meaning.

Rather, loss of hope may follow learning a previously unclear reality.

This connects the meanings without making yaʾs directly equivalent to knowledge in every setting.

What does the context show?

Before the disputed phrase, the verse says:

وَلَوْ أَنَّ قُرْآنًا سُيِّرَتْ بِهِ الْجِبَالُ أَوْ قُطِّعَتْ بِهِ الْأَرْضُ أَوْ كُلِّمَ بِهِ الْمَوْتَىٰ بَلْ لِلَّهِ الْأَمْرُ جَمِيعًا

wa-law anna qurʾānan suyyirat bihi al-jibālu aw quṭṭiʿat bihi al-arḍu aw kullima bihi al-mawtā bal li-allāhi al-amru jamīʿan

“Were there a Qur’an by which mountains were moved, the earth split, or the dead addressed… Rather, the entire affair belongs to The God.”

The opening supposes mountains being moved, the earth being split, or the dead being addressed through a recitation. Then بَلْ لِلَّهِ الْأَمْرُ جَمِيعًا states that the entire affair belongs to The God. The opening conditional’s conclusion is not explicit in the quoted wording; identifying it is an interpretive question.

It then asks the believers:

أَفَلَمْ يَيْأَسِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا: “Have those who believed not known…?” on the knowledge reading.

This sequence allows two related interpretations.

The first asks whether believers know that The God could guide everyone if He willed.

The second asks whether believers have ceased to expect everyone to be guided through extraordinary events: guidance belongs to The God and cannot be imposed on Him through demands for astonishing signs.

The second deserves consideration in relation to the root’s usual meaning, but a direct translation must explain the omitted or understood complement of yaʾs.

Assessment

Early evidence shows that the knowledge explanation has a precedent. It should not be wholly rejected merely by appealing to the root’s usual meaning.

Conversely, the famous poetic witness has variants of attribution and wording, and some early lexicographers rejected knowledge as an independent usage.

It cannot therefore be presented as undisputed.

The present preference is to retain knowing or becoming aware in the contextual translation of Al-Raʿd 13:31, while recording the dispute in the root study. This corresponds to Forqan’s installed Persian rendering:

“Have the believers not known that, had The God willed, He would have guided all people?” (Al-Raʿd 13:31)

The general definition should nevertheless give priority to giving up hope, identifying knowing as a reported and disputed use.

10. Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:13: despair of the Hereafter and inhabitants of graves

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَتَوَلَّوْا قَوْمًا غَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ قَدْ يَئِسُوا مِنَ الْآخِرَةِ كَمَا يَئِسَ الْكُفَّارُ مِنْ أَصْحَابِ الْقُبُورِ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tatawallaw qawman ghaḍiba allāhu ʿalayhim qad yaʾisū mina al-ākhirati ka-mā yaʾisa al-kuffāru min aṣḥābi al-qubūri

“You who believe, do not take into allegiance a people with whom The God is angry. They have lost hope of the Hereafter, as disbelievers have lost hope concerning the inhabitants of graves.”

The root occurs twice here.

First:

قَدْ يَئِسُوا مِنَ الْآخِرَةِ: “They have lost hope of the Hereafter.”

Second:

كَمَا يَئِسَ الْكُفَّارُ مِنْ أَصْحَابِ الْقُبُورِ: “As disbelievers have lost hope concerning the inhabitants of graves,” following the first construction directly.

Both are Form I verbs.

Differing interpretations of the inhabitants of graves

Early interpretations present two main readings.

In one, living disbelievers have lost hope of their dead returning: they do not expect those in graves to return to them.

In the other, the disbelievers are themselves in graves and no longer hope to share in the good and mercy of the Hereafter.

Early interpretive accounts record this disagreement.

In the first, min aṣḥābi al-qubūr complements yaʾs and identifies its object. In the second, it can specify or qualify al-kuffār: disbelievers who are themselves among the inhabitants of graves.

A direct translation should therefore preserve the expression’s structure and leave a definitive choice between the readings to the verse’s reflection.

Connection with the root meaning

In both readings, the basic meaning remains giving up hope.

The disagreement concerns who has lost hope and what outcome they no longer expect.

A lexical meaning can thus be identified without resolving every interpretive disagreement.

The verse itself does not name a particular ethnic or religious group for the people described. Historical identifications in interpretive reports should not replace its general wording.

11. Distinctions from related concepts

Y ʾ S and Q N Ṭ: despair and qunūṭ

Qur’anic Q N Ṭ concerns despair, especially of divine mercy.

Al-Ḥijr 15:55 says:

فَلَا تَكُنْ مِنَ الْقَانِطِينَ

fa-lā takun mina al-qāniṭīna

“Do not be among those who despair.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:56 says:

وَمَنْ يَقْنَطُ مِنْ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّهِ إِلَّا الضَّالُّونَ

wa-man yaqnaṭu min raḥmati rabbihi illā al-ḍāllūna

“Who despairs of his Lord’s mercy except those who have gone astray?”

These verses concern a subject close to Yūsuf 12:87.

Al-Zumar 39:53 also says:

لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ

lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati allāhi

“Do not despair of The God’s mercy.”

Qunūṭ and yaʾs share a semantic field in these passages.

The examined evidence nevertheless establishes no categorical rule that qunūṭ is precisely a stronger degree of despair in every use.

The two descriptions in Fuṣṣilat 41:49 may intensify and emphasize, but do not alone define two distinct psychological stages.

Study of Q N Ṭ

Y ʾ S and R J W: despair and hope

Rajāʾ concerns expectation and hope; many lexical definitions set yaʾs against it.

Having no hope of one particular event does not mean having no hope of anything.

In Yūsuf 12:80 the brothers lose hope of their request being accepted, whereas in Yūsuf 12:87 Jacob prohibits despair of divine relief.

These situations in one surah show that someone may expect no result from one particular action while hoping for relief through another route.

Yaʾs and rajāʾ must therefore be assessed with their respective objects in view.

Y ʾ S and Ḥ Z N: despair and sorrow

Ḥuzn concerns sorrow.

In Yūsuf 12:86, Jacob says:

إِنَّمَا أَشْكُو بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي إِلَى اللَّهِ

innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā allāhi

“I complain of my distress and sorrow only to The God.”

In Yūsuf 12:87, the same Jacob tells his sons not to despair of divine relief.

Together, the verses show that sorrow and hope can coexist in one person.

Ḥuzn and yaʾs are therefore not synonyms: someone may be sorrowful and still retain hope.

Study of Ḥ Z N

Y ʾ S and S ʾ M: despair and weariness

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 contains both yasʾamu and yaʾūs.

Saʾima concerns becoming weary of something.

Yaʾisa concerns giving up hope.

Someone may tire of repeated work yet hope for its outcome; another may no longer see a route to success without being tired of trying.

Weariness and despair are lexically distinct.

Y ʾ S and Ṣ B R: despair and steadfastness

Qur’anic ṣabr is not merely enduring pain: its uses include steadfastness, restraint, and perseverance.

Yaʾs concerns expectation and hope.

These concern different dimensions: steadfastness in behavior and orientation, and expectation of an outcome.

Someone may lose hope of a specific result while remaining steadfast in doing right.

Conversely, someone may have great hopes without the necessary perseverance in practice.

Ṣabr cannot therefore be reduced to remaining hopeful, nor yaʾs invariably defined as abandoning ṣabr.

Study of Ṣ B R

Giving up hope can naturally express yaʾs in some verses, but “intense,” “sinful,” or “blameworthy” should not be added in every instance.

“Do not despair of divine relief” suits Yūsuf 12:87.

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:4, the wording should convey no longer expecting menstruation, rather than psychological or moral despair.

“Have the believers not known…?” in Forqan’s Al-Raʿd 13:31 is defensible on one established lexical reading, while the dispute over yaʾs and knowledge should remain in the study.

13. Conclusion

Early Arabic Y ʾ S concerns giving up hope and ceasing to expect an outcome.

This is clear in many Qur’anic uses: disbelievers losing hope concerning believers’ religion, Joseph’s brothers giving up on their request, despair during hardship, and no longer expecting menstruation.

This range shows that yaʾs is neither necessarily sinful nor always a sign of moral weakness. Evaluation depends on its object, the person’s understanding of reality, and their response.

The Qur’an condemns despair concerning divine mercy and relief and calls for hope. In Jacob’s story, hope accompanies a command to search and act.

Yaʾūs can make despair intense or prominent in describing someone; in its two Qur’anic occurrences istayʾasa concerns reaching despair. Morphology should not support conclusions beyond the evidence.

Al-Raʿd 13:31 is the principal disputed use. Several lexical and interpretive explanations give knowing or becoming aware, while others connect it with knowledge of impossibility leading to giving up hope rather than recognizing a separate meaning.

The definition should retain both the well-attested usual meaning and the disputed knowledge use without conflating them.

A concise definition for Forqan:

Y ʾ S denotes giving up hope and ceasing to expect an outcome. Depending on its object, this may occur in an ordinary, commendable, or blameworthy context. Istayʾasa means losing hope or reaching despair; yaʾūs describes someone in whom despair is prominent. Early explanations also give knowing or becoming aware in Al-Raʿd 13:31, but its independence as a lexical meaning remains disputed.
