1. The root and its Qur’anic forms

Ḥ R R is an Arabic lexical family in which short vowels make a crucial difference. Ḥurr, with ḍammah on the ḥāʾ, denotes a free person as opposed to an enslaved person; ḥarr, with fatḥah, denotes heat as opposed to cold. In the same family, taḥrīr concerns emancipation and release from a constraint, and in other uses the purification or exclusive dedication of something. Ḥarīr names silk fabric.

In classical Arabic, being free was not used only of a person’s legal status. Ḥurr could describe something pure, excellent, or distinguished. Heat, by contrast, forms another branch associated with the temperature of the air, burning sensations, and intensity.

This diversity prevents us from defining every derivative through a single idea such as “freedom,” “release,” or “energy and heat.”

Ḥ R R occurs 15 times in 11 verses across 10 surahs of the Qur’an. Its six principal forms are:

FormCountUse
Ḥurr2A free person as opposed to an enslaved person
Ḥarr3Heat
Ḥarūr1Scorching heat
Ḥarīr3Silk
Taḥrīr5Freeing a person from bondage
Muḥarrar1Released and exclusively dedicated to a particular purpose

This count follows the Qur’anic morphological concordance. The two instances of ḥurr occur in Al-Baqarah 2:178, three instances of taḥrīr in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, and two of ḥarr in Al-Tawbah 9:81. The number of word occurrences therefore exceeds the number of verses.

Qur’anic morphological concordance: Ḥ R R occurrences

The Qur’an does not use an independent verb ḥarra meaning “to become free” or “to become hot.” Nor does it use ḥarrara as a conjugated verb. Its form II uses are confined to the verbal noun taḥrīr and the passive participle muḥarrar.

Two distinctions are central: freeing someone from another person’s ownership and bondage differs from dedicating something to a particular purpose; and neither the heat of the air nor silk fabric should be derived from freedom merely because the consonants coincide.

2. Semantic cores in classical Arabic

Two principal branches in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris identifies two foundations for the doubled root Ḥ R R in Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

The first foundation is:

«مَا خَالَفَ الْعُبُودِيَّةَ وَبَرِئَ مِنَ الْعَيْبِ وَالنَّقْصِ»

mā khālafa al-ʿubūdiyyata wa-bariʾa mina al-ʿaybi wa-al-naqṣi

That is, “what stands opposed to servitude and is free from defect and deficiency.”

The second foundation is:

«خِلَافُ الْبَرْدِ»

khilāfu al-bardi

That is, “the opposite of cold.”

Ibn Fāris thus treats freedom and purity in one branch and heat in another. His classification resists a single imagined origin for every form—for example, presenting freedom as inner heat or heat as the liberation of energy.

Such associations may be evocative as literature, but without historical and linguistic evidence they do not establish the root’s meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, volume 2, Ḥ R R

Ḥurr: opposed to an enslaved person

In classical Arabic, al-ḥurr denoted a person who was not in a condition of enslavement. Al-ʿabd and its female counterpart, al-amah, were familiar opposing terms.

Lisān al-ʿArab states explicitly:

«الْحُرُّ نَقِيضُ الْعَبْدِ»

al-ḥurru naqīḍu al-ʿabdi

That is, “ḥurr is the opposite of ʿabd.”

Its feminine form is ḥurrah, and a familiar plural for free men is aḥrār.

In a society where slavery existed, this distinction expressed a particular social and legal status.

The word ḥurr itself, however, does not mean just, upright, wealthy, or powerful. A free person may act oppressively, just as an enslaved person may act uprightly.

Legal freedom must therefore be distinguished from moral virtue.

Ḥurr as pure or excellent

Within the same branch, Arabs used ṭīn ḥurr for clay unmixed with sand, and ḥurr al-shayʾ for the excellent or choice part of something.

Ḥurr al-dār is also recorded for the central or best part of a house, and ḥurr al-fākihah for choice fruit.

Ibn Fāris connects these uses with purity and freedom from defects.

Material purity nevertheless differs from human freedom. Pure clay is not an enslaved person who has been emancipated; nor is a free person necessarily free from every moral failing.

These uses have acquired a semantic association in the language, but the properties of one must not be imposed on the other.

Taḥrīr: emancipation and rectification

In the social domain, ḥarrara al-ʿabd means to emancipate an enslaved person.

In other uses, taḥrīr can mean refining or putting something right. Lisān al-ʿArab records, for example:

«وَتَحْرِيرُ الْكِتَابَةِ: إِقَامَةُ حُرُوفِهَا وَإِصْلَاحُ السَّقْطِ»

wa-taḥrīru al-kitābati iqāmatu ḥurūfihā wa-iṣlāḥu al-saqaṭi

That is, “taḥrīr of writing means setting its letters right and correcting its omissions.”

Taḥrīr al-ḥisāb is likewise recorded for setting an account in order without errors or omissions.

Lisān al-ʿArab and other primary lexicons, Ḥ R R

These uses relate to rectification and purification, but they do not make the Qur’anic phrase taḥrīr raqabah mean writing a document or correcting an account.

In taḥrīr raqabah, the subject is a person’s release from bondage. In muḥarraran, concerning Mary’s mother’s vow, the subject is release and dedication to a religious purpose.

The object and context determine the distinction.

Ḥarr: heat as opposed to cold

The root’s other branch concerns heat.

Lisān al-ʿArab says:

«الْحَرُّ ضِدُّ الْبَرْدِ»

al-ḥarru ḍiddu al-bardi

That is, “heat is the opposite of cold.”

The same family includes ḥarārah for warmth or heat, ḥārr for something hot, and ḥarūr for intense heat or a scorching wind.

In extended uses, hardship, intense thirst, and inward burning can also be expressed within this semantic field.

Heat, hardship, and thirst are nevertheless not always the same meaning. Someone may become thirsty in hot weather, but thirst is not lexically identical to heat.

In the Qur’an, ḥarr refers to perceptible heat in Al-Tawbah 9:81 and Al-Naḥl 16:81. Ḥarūr occurs opposite shade in Fāṭir 35:21.

Ḥarīr: silk

Ḥarīr names fabric or clothing made of silk in Arabic.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

«الْحَرِيرُ: ثِيَابٌ مِنْ إِبْرِيسَمٍ»

al-ḥarīru thiyābun min ibrīsamin

That is, “ḥarīr: garments made of silk.”

Two uses of the same form must nevertheless be distinguished.

In some lexical attestations, ḥarīr also describes someone afflicted by inward burning, grief, or intense anger; ḥarīr meaning silk, by contrast, names a familiar material or garment.

An identical form alone does not establish that silk fabric received its name from burning, heat, or freedom.

The evidence examined does not establish the historical origin of the name for silk clearly enough to prove a definite connection with either heat or freedom.

A root study should therefore recognize silk as an independent, established use rather than the product of an imagined etymological picture.

3. Early poetic evidence and social uses

Ḥurr in verse attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd: worthy conduct

A poem attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

لَا يَكُنْ حُبُّكِ دَاءً دَاخِلًا لَيْسَ هَذَا مِنْكِ مَاوِيَّ بِحُرّْ

lā yakun ḥubbuki dāʾan dākhilan laysa hādhā minki Māwiyya bi-ḥurr

Translation: “Do not let my love for you become an inward illness; Māwiyyah, this conduct of yours is neither fine nor worthy.”

Ibn Fāris cites this verse for ḥurr describing the goodness or worthiness of conduct. Lisān al-ʿArab also transmits it, with variants in the first half, including dāʾan dākhilan, “an inward illness,” and dāʾan qātilan, “a fatal illness.”

Bi-ḥurr here does not mean that the addressee’s conduct is legally free from enslavement. The poet is calling that conduct unworthy.

This witness shows that some early uses of ḥurr carried a sense of goodness and nobility alongside legal freedom.

It does not make al-ḥurr in Al-Baqarah 2:178 mean a morally virtuous person. There, its opposition to al-ʿabd clearly indicates social and legal status.

Ḥurrah in al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī’s poetry

A poem by al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

شُمُسٌ مَوَانِعُ كُلِّ لَيْلَةِ حُرَّةٍ يُخْلِفْنَ ظَنَّ الْفَاحِشِ الْمِغْيَارِ

shumusun mawāniʿu kulli laylati ḥurratin yukhlifna ẓanna al-fāḥishi al-mighyāri

Sense translation: “Aloof women, withholding every unconsummated night: they frustrate the expectations of the lewd, intensely jealous man.”

Laylah ḥurrah in this verse concerns a night when the man does not obtain the sexual access he seeks. Lexical explanations refer particularly to an unconsummated wedding night. Kull in this witness is the first term of a genitive construction, with laylah as its dependent noun.

This is a specialized use in the social language and marital relations of early Arabic. It is transmitted in Ibn Durayd’s Jamharat al-Lughah and Ibn Fāris’s Maqāyīs al-Lughah, with references to al-Nābighah’s collected poetry.

Al-Muḥkam: the vocalization and attribution of al-Nābighah’s verse

The witness helps establish the range of ḥurr, but its sense must not be carried over into every occurrence.

Nor does this historical use establish a ruling on women’s agency or rights. The poet describes a particular situation using the social language and judgments of his time.

For lexical research, the relevant point is a use of ḥurr beyond the legal freedom of an enslaved person.

A ḥurrah cloud: abundance and excellence

A verse by ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says:

جَادَتْ عَلَيْهَا كُلُّ بِكْرٍ حُرَّةٍ فَتَرَكْنَ كُلَّ قَرَارَةٍ كَالدِّرْهَمِ

jādat ʿalayhā kullu bikrin ḥurratin fa-tarakna kulla qarāratin ka-al-dirhami

Sense translation: “Every fresh, abundant rain cloud poured upon it, leaving each hollow like a gleaming dirham.”

Ḥurrah here describes the cloud as excellent and generous in rainfall.

Lisān al-ʿArab cites this witness for ḥurrah describing a choice, rain-laden cloud.

Ḥurr could therefore describe the outstanding quality of something, as well as naming a person who was not enslaved.

Abundant rainfall must not become the root’s fixed definition. This verse praises a cloud’s rain; elsewhere, ḥurr may express a different quality.

Ḥarūr and ḥarīr in al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry

Al-ʿAjjāj’s rajaz verse includes:

وَنَسَجَتْ لَوَافِحُ الْحَرُورِ سَبَائِبًا كَسَرَقِ الْحَرِيرِ

wa-nasajat lawāfiḥu al-ḥarūri sabāʾiban ka-saraqi al-ḥarīri

Sense translation: “The scorching gusts of the hot wind wove strands like fine strips of silk.”

Two members of the Ḥ R R family occur together in this poetic image.

Al-ḥarūr concerns scorching wind or heat, while al-ḥarīr concerns silk fabric. The poet brings them together to construct an image.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: al-ʿAjjāj’s ḥarūr and ḥarīr witness

Their occurrence together does not establish that silk was historically named after hot wind.

The relationship here is a poetic comparison, not evidence of historical derivation.

Al-ʿAjjāj also composed during the Islamic period, and this particular rajaz passage is not securely dated. It is evidence of classical usage, not definite proof that this passage predates the Qur’an.

Together, these witnesses show the family’s diverse fields: social freedom, excellence, heat, and clothing.

4. Al-ḥurr bi-al-ḥurr: the free person in the law of qiṣāṣ

Al-Baqarah 2:178

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْقِصَاصُ فِي الْقَتْلَى ۖ الْحُرُّ بِالْحُرِّ وَالْعَبْدُ بِالْعَبْدِ وَالْأُنْثَىٰ بِالْأُنْثَىٰ ۚ فَمَنْ عُفِيَ لَهُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ شَيْءٌ فَاتِّبَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَأَدَاءٌ إِلَيْهِ بِإِحْسَانٍ ۗ ذَٰلِكَ تَخْفِيفٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَرَحْمَةٌ ۗ فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَهُ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kutiba ʿalaykumu al-qiṣāṣu fī al-qatlā al-ḥurru bi-al-ḥurri wa-al-ʿabdu bi-al-ʿabdi wa-al-unthā bi-al-unthā fa-man ʿufiya lahu min akhīhi shayʾun fa-ittibāʿun bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsānin dhālika takhfīfun min rabbikum wa-raḥmatun fa-mani iʿtadā baʿda dhālika fa-lahu ʿadhābun alīmun

Translation: “You who have believed, qiṣāṣ has been prescribed for you concerning those killed: the free person for the free person, the enslaved person for the enslaved person, and the female for the female. If anything is pardoned for someone by his brother, there should be pursuit in a recognized, fair manner and payment to him with kindness. This is an easing and mercy from your Lord. Whoever transgresses after that will have a painful punishment.”

The meaning of ḥurr in this verse

Al-ḥurr is definite and occurs twice: first as the subject of a nominal clause, then after bi- in bi-al-ḥurr.

Its contrast with al-ʿabd bi-al-ʿabd makes the social and legal sense clear.

Ḥurr here therefore denotes the free person as opposed to the enslaved person.

This differs from ḥurr describing pure clay or worthy conduct. It cannot be translated as “the virtuous person for the virtuous person.”

Nor does ḥurr by itself necessarily encode male sex here. It is grammatically masculine, while the verse also establishes another pairing involving al-unthā, “the female.”

Understanding all the verse’s legal relationships therefore requires examining the full construction, rather than one noun alone.

Does al-ḥurr bi-al-ḥurr mean that a free person’s life has greater worth?

At the level of wording, the verse establishes a relationship between familiar social categories in the setting of qiṣāṣ.

The root Ḥ R R itself, however, makes no ruling on the intrinsic worth of human life, the amount of compensation, or the permissibility of killing someone from another category.

Early commentaries differ over why this classification is mentioned.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits accounts explaining the verse against a background of tribal disputes and demands for disproportionate retaliation: a stronger group sought to kill someone of higher social standing from the opposing group in response to the death of someone of its own.

This historical explanation is relevant to investigating proportionality in qiṣāṣ.

The details of any one historical report should nevertheless be assessed independently rather than substituted for the verse’s meaning.

Conversely, al-ḥurr bi-al-ḥurr alone does not establish that a free person’s killing of an enslaved person lies outside responsibility, qiṣāṣ, or justice in every situation.

That question requires examining the wider set of qiṣāṣ verses, including Al-Māʾidah 5:45 and the prohibition of transgression in Al-Baqarah 2:178.

The clear lexical point is that ḥurr names freedom as opposed to enslavement; it is not an independent measure of people’s moral or human worth.

Legal freedom is distinct from moral virtue

In some Arabic uses, ḥurr also describes a noble person or someone of good character.

Here, however, the contrast with ʿabd gives priority to the legal sense.

We therefore cannot conclude that every free person is necessarily virtuous or that every enslaved person lacks virtue.

Nor does classifying people within a ruling addressed to a historical society by itself establish that the Qur’an presents every inequality in that society as its ultimate ideal.

Such a conclusion requires examining the Qur’an’s wider treatment of human rights and responsibilities.

Within the Ḥ R R family itself, one clear Qur’anic direction is freeing human beings from enslavement, explicitly mentioned in verses on expiation.

5. Muḥarraran: Mary’s mother’s vow and dedication to worship

Āl ʿImrān 3:35

إِذْ قَالَتِ امْرَأَتُ عِمْرَانَ رَبِّ إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لَكَ مَا فِي بَطْنِي مُحَرَّرًا فَتَقَبَّلْ مِنِّي ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

idh qālati imraʾatu ʿImrāna rabbi innī nadhartu laka mā fī baṭnī muḥarraran fa-taqabbal minnī innaka anta al-samīʿu al-ʿalīmu

Translation: “When the wife of ʿImrān said: My Lord, I have vowed to You what is in my womb, released and exclusively dedicated to You; so accept it from me. You are indeed the Hearing, the Knowing.”

The construction of muḥarraran

Muḥarraran is a masculine singular passive participle of form II.

Here it is accusative and functions as a circumstantial qualifier of mā fī baṭnī, “what is in my womb”: it describes the condition Mary’s mother intended for her child.

This description is nevertheless not identical to taḥrīr raqabah in the verses on emancipation.

The mother is speaking about an unborn child. She has vowed what is in her womb to The God.

In this context, muḥarraran concerns releasing and setting the child apart for worship or religious service. The verse does not explicitly describe an enslaved person emancipated from another person’s ownership.

Al-Ṭabarī explains it as setting the child apart from ordinary occupations and dedicating the child to worship and service in a place of worship.

Al-Ṭabarī: muḥarraran and Mary’s mother’s vow

Does muḥarraran mean “not enslaved”?

There is a lexical relationship between freedom from bondage and release from other attachments.

Here, however, nadhartu laka, “I have vowed to You,” is the principal contextual indication. The mother says that she has vowed the child to her Lord.

Muḥarraran thus acquires its contextual sense through that vow: the child is to be set aside from other occupations and dedicated to a particular religious purpose.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise distinguishes these uses. He recognizes emancipation as one use of taḥrīr and relates muḥarraran in Mary’s mother’s vow to being exclusively set apart for worship.

The phrase therefore cannot be translated as “I emancipated my child from slavery,” since the verse does not say that the child was enslaved.

Conversely, muḥarraran should not simply be omitted, leaving only “I vowed my child to You.” This independent qualifier specifies the character of the vow.

Released from what?

This question matters for understanding the verse.

In taḥrīr raqabah, the constraint removed is enslavement.

In muḥarraran concerning a vow, what is set aside is involvement in ordinary familial and worldly work or use, so that the person is dedicated to worship and religious service.

This explanation comes from lexical and exegetical reports. The verse itself does not provide a detailed list of activities the child would be forbidden to perform.

Nor does this phrase name the sanctuary or specify the service to be undertaken there.

Early commentaries relate the vow to service in an Israelite sanctuary, including Jerusalem’s sanctuary. Such reports matter for the historical background, but muḥarraran does not lexically mean “a servant of Jerusalem’s sanctuary.”

A basic translation should therefore preserve release and dedication together, leaving the sanctuary’s details to the commentary.

The mother’s vow and Mary’s own position

As the account continues, the mother speaks again after Mary’s birth, and The God describes accepting Mary.

The continuation shows that the subject is more than a lexical definition: a human vow, a child’s birth, and her acceptance form part of a religious narrative.

Muḥarraran alone nevertheless cannot determine every detail of dedicating a child, the respective duties of girls and boys, or the limits of individual authority in that society.

Some exegetical sources report that boys were customarily dedicated to sanctuary service and that Mary’s mother was surprised to give birth to a girl.

These reports require separate assessment as historical and exegetical material; they do not make muḥarraran mean “a male child.”

Mā fī baṭnī, “what is in my womb,” does not itself identify the child’s sex.

A translation should therefore not insert “son” into the vow without justification.

Is muḥarraran contradictory: free, yet committed to worship?

At first sight, this may seem contradictory: if someone has been released, how can that person simultaneously be dedicated to a task?

In classical Arabic, however, freedom in this branch may be expressed relative to a particular constraint.

Someone emancipated from slavery leaves the enslaver’s control.

In the usage concerning a vow, someone is set apart from other occupations and dedicated to a specific purpose.

Both involve separation from a prior attachment, but their direction and result differ.

Modern individual freedom should therefore not be imposed uncritically on every use of taḥrīr.

Nor should dedication through a vow make a free person lexically equivalent to someone with no responsibilities or commitments.

Freedom from a constraint and responsibility under a commitment are different questions.

6. Taḥrīr raqabah: freeing a human being from enslavement

This expression occurs five times: three in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, once in Al-Māʾidah 5:89, and once in Al-Mujādilah 58:3.

In all these uses, taḥrīr is a form II verbal noun, while raqabah identifies the human being whose emancipation is at issue.

Raqabah originally names the neck, but in this expression it refers to the whole person in bondage.

“Freeing a neck” is thus a figurative expression for freeing someone from enslavement. Raqabah belongs to R Q B, while the act of freeing is expressed by Ḥ R R.

The two words must be distinguished: taḥrīr expresses the act of release, and raqabah the person to be released.

Accidental killing and freeing a believing person

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ أَنْ يَقْتُلَ مُؤْمِنًا إِلَّا خَطَأً ۚ وَمَنْ قَتَلَ مُؤْمِنًا خَطَأً فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ وَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ يَصَّدَّقُوا ۚ فَإِنْ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمٍ عَدُوٍّ لَكُمْ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ ۖ وَإِنْ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمٍ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ مِيثَاقٌ فَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ وَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ ۖ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ تَوْبَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

wa-mā kāna li-muʾminin an yaqtula muʾminan illā khaṭaʾan wa-man qatala muʾminan khaṭaʾan fa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa-diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihi illā an yaṣṣaddaqū fa-in kāna min qawmin ʿaduwwin lakum wa-huwa muʾminun fa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa-in kāna min qawmin baynakum wa-baynahum mīthāqun fa-diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihi wa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayni tawbatan mina Allāhi wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalīman ḥakīman

Translation: “It is not for a believer to kill a believer except by mistake. Whoever kills a believer by mistake must free a believing person from bondage, and compensation must be delivered to the deceased’s family, unless they remit it as charity. If the deceased belongs to a people hostile to you while being a believer, a believing person must be freed from bondage. If the deceased belongs to a people with whom you have a covenant, compensation must be delivered to the family and a believing person freed from bondage. Whoever cannot find the means must fast two consecutive months, as repentance from The God. The God is Knowing, Wise.”

Taḥrīr raqabah muʾminah occurs three times in this verse.

Muʾminah importantly qualifies raqabah: the person emancipated must be a believer.

Ḥ R R itself does not mean belief. The condition of belief is supplied by a separate word from ʾ M N.

This distinction matters in legal study, because Al-Māʾidah 5:89 and Al-Mujādilah 58:3 do not explicitly state that condition.

The three repetitions of taḥrīr raqabah muʾminah occur within three different situations involving accidental killing. Some situations also require compensation, but compensation and emancipation are distinct obligations.

Taḥrīr must therefore not be defined as financial reparation or payment of compensation for a death.

Freeing a person is one action prescribed as redress for accidental killing, while compensation, when required, is delivered to the deceased’s family.

Does emancipation always lexically mean expiation?

No.

Taḥrīr means freeing; “expiation” belongs to another root.

Emancipation may be prescribed as a reparative obligation in some verses, but the root itself carries no such restriction.

In Al-Balad 90:13, fakku raqabah appears among good deeds. Al-Baqarah 2:177 and Al-Tawbah 9:60 also concern spending wealth toward freeing human beings.

These verses show that human liberation is not confined to expiation for wrongdoing.

Conversely, its social importance does not authorize changing each particular expiation ruling without attending to its wording.

Expiation for an oath

Al-Māʾidah 5:89

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ ۖ فَكَفَّارَتُهُ إِطْعَامُ عَشَرَةِ مَسَاكِينَ مِنْ أَوْسَطِ مَا تُطْعِمُونَ أَهْلِيكُمْ أَوْ كِسْوَتُهُمْ أَوْ تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ ۖ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ كَفَّارَةُ أَيْمَانِكُمْ إِذَا حَلَفْتُمْ ۚ وَاحْفَظُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu Allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā ʿaqqadtumu al-aymāna fa-kaffāratuhu iṭʿāmu ʿasharati masākīna min awsaṭi mā tuṭʿimūna ahlīkum aw kiswatuhum aw taḥrīru raqabatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyāmin dhālika kaffāratu aymānikum idhā ḥalaftum wa-iḥfaẓū aymānakum kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: “The God does not hold you accountable for inadvertent speech in your oaths, but holds you accountable for the oaths you have firmly bound. Their expiation is feeding ten needy people from the average food you give your families, or clothing them, or freeing a person from bondage. Whoever cannot find the means must fast three days. That is the expiation for your oaths when you swear; guard your oaths. The God thus makes His signs clear to you, so that you may give thanks.”

Taḥrīr raqabah is one of three stated options here.

The verse places freeing a person alongside feeding and clothing people in need.

Unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, however, it does not place muʾminah, “believing,” after raqabah.

A direct translation must preserve this difference.

Traditional jurisprudence discusses carrying the condition of belief from one expiation verse into another. Such an application is a matter of legal reasoning; it should not be inserted into a translation as a fixed component of the lexical meaning of taḥrīr raqabah.

At the level of the root, the expression concerns freeing a person from bondage.

Emancipation in the ruling on ẓihār

Al-Mujādilah 58:3

وَالَّذِينَ يُظَاهِرُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا قَالُوا فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَتَمَاسَّا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ تُوعَظُونَ بِهِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

wa-alladhīna yuẓāhirūna min nisāʾihim thumma yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū fa-taḥrīru raqabatin min qabli an yatamāssā dhālikum tūʿaẓūna bihi wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun

Translation: “Those who pronounce ẓihār concerning their wives, then return to what they said, must free a person from bondage before the two touch one another. By this you are admonished; The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Fa-taḥrīr raqabah concerns emancipation within the ruling on ẓihār. Commentaries explain yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū in relation to resuming the marital relationship, and also in relation to repeating the pronouncement of ẓihār. This interpretive difference is examined separately from the meaning of taḥrīr.

Al-Qurṭubī: differing interpretations of return in the ẓihār ruling

Min qabli an yatamāssā specifies when this obligation must be performed.

Taḥrīr itself should therefore not be defined as “freeing before marital contact.” The timing is supplied by a separate phrase in the sentence.

Ẓihār likewise belongs to Ẓ H R and concerns a particular act within the marital relationship. That act is not part of the meaning of Ḥ R R.

Here too, raqabah is not followed by muʾminah. This difference in the wording must be preserved.

Emancipation and human dignity

In all three settings, freeing a human being is presented as an action of value and social consequence in response to an error or conduct requiring redress.

The root itself nevertheless does not make these five occurrences a complete account of slavery, the legal conditions of enslaved people, or the Qur’an’s ultimate approach to ending slavery.

That study requires independent examination of other verses, including Al-Baqarah 2:177, Al-Tawbah 9:60, Al-Nūr 24:33, and Al-Balad 90:13.

The root and these contexts clearly express taḥrīr raqabah as freeing a human being from bondage and another person’s ownership.

Nor should this be reduced to a purely financial transaction. Payment may enable emancipation, but its ultimate subject is the changed status of a person released from bondage.

7. Ḥarr and ḥarūr: heat, hardship, and scorching

The heat of the air and of fire

Al-Tawbah 9:81

فَرِحَ الْمُخَلَّفُونَ بِمَقْعَدِهِمْ خِلَافَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ وَكَرِهُوا أَنْ يُجَاهِدُوا بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنْفُسِهِمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَقَالُوا لَا تَنْفِرُوا فِي الْحَرِّ ۗ قُلْ نَارُ جَهَنَّمَ أَشَدُّ حَرًّا ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا يَفْقَهُونَ

fariḥa al-mukhallafūna bi-maqʿadihim khilāfa rasūli Allāhi wa-karihū an yujāhidū bi-amwālihim wa-anfusihim fī sabīli Allāhi wa-qālū lā tanfirū fī al-ḥarri qul nāru jahannama ashaddu ḥarran law kānū yafqahūna

Translation: “Those left behind rejoiced at remaining seated, contrary to the messenger of The God, and disliked striving with their wealth and lives in the way of The God. They said: Do not go forth in the heat. Say: The fire of Hell is more intense in heat—if only they understood.”

Ḥarr occurs twice in this verse.

First it concerns environmental heat: fī al-ḥarr, “in the heat.”

Then it concerns the heat of Hell’s fire: ashaddu ḥarran, “more intense in heat.”

Both concern heat lexically, while their subjects and situations differ.

In the first instance, those who remained behind offer the heat of the weather as a reason not to set out.

In the second, the Qur’an compares Hell’s heat with the heat they invoke.

Ashadd belongs to SH D D and expresses greater intensity. “More intense” therefore does not come from ḥarr alone.

The translation must retain both heat and its degree of intensity.

Is heat intrinsically an invalid excuse?

The verse addresses a particular group who disliked participating and striving in the situation under discussion.

Their contextual failing is not simply recognizing that the weather is hot; it concerns the conduct and motives the Qur’an describes.

We cannot therefore conclude that anyone who avoids travel or strenuous work because of extreme heat necessarily behaves in the same blameworthy way.

Heat can pose a real danger to human health.

The existence of heat, a person’s capacity, the responsibility involved, and the motive for refraining must therefore be distinguished.

Ḥ R R itself expresses heat in this verse. The judgment on the group’s behavior arises from the full context.

Clothing that protects against heat

Al-Naḥl 16:81

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِمَّا خَلَقَ ظِلَالًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ أَكْنَانًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ سَرَابِيلَ تَقِيكُمُ الْحَرَّ وَسَرَابِيلَ تَقِيكُمْ بَأْسَكُمْ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يُتِمُّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُسْلِمُونَ

wa-Allāhu jaʿala lakum mimmā khalaqa ẓilālan wa-jaʿala lakum mina al-jibāli aknānan wa-jaʿala lakum sarābīla taqīkumu al-ḥarra wa-sarābīla taqīkum baʾsakum kadhālika yutimmu niʿmatahu ʿalaykum laʿallakum tuslimūna

Translation: “The God has made shade for you from what He created, and shelters for you in the mountains. He has made garments that protect you from heat, and garments that protect you from the violence of your fighting. Thus He completes His favor upon you, so that you may submit.”

Al-ḥarr here denotes heat from whose harm suitable clothing can protect a person.

Sarābīl is the plural of sirbāl and concerns garments or coverings.

The context presents two protective uses: against heat and against the harm of warfare.

Early exegetical explanations relate the first to clothing of materials such as cotton, linen, or wool, and the second to armor and other protective equipment.

Ḥarr itself nevertheless does not mean clothing or sunburn. Heat is what the clothing offers protection against.

Nor does this verse establish that clothing helps only against heat and has no protective role against cold.

The verse explicitly names heat. Adding “cold” to a direct translation, although consistent with human experience, would depart from its wording.

Ḥarūr: scorching heat opposite shade

Fāṭir 35:21

وَلَا الظِّلُّ وَلَا الْحَرُورُ

wa-lā al-ẓillu wa-lā al-ḥarūru

Translation: “Nor are shade and scorching heat alike.”

Ḥarūr follows the faʿūl pattern within the heat family.

Lexicons relate it to a hot, scorching wind or oppressive heat.

Early reports differ over when ḥarūr blows and how it differs from samūm.

Some identify ḥarūr as a hot nighttime wind and samūm as a hot daytime wind. Others allow ḥarūr to occur by night and day.

Al-Ṭabarī reports the disagreement and explains that ḥarūr can occur at either time. In Fāṭir 35:21, he takes its opposition to shade as evidence for perceptible heat against the coolness of shade.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing reports concerning ḥarūr

The brief definition should therefore not restrict ḥarūr to “a hot wind occurring only at night.”

Does ḥarūr here mean Hell itself?

Some early commentaries explain shade as Paradise and ḥarūr as Hell.

This reading is relevant to the surah’s figurative context.

Fāṭir 35:19, Fāṭir 35:20, Fāṭir 35:21, and Fāṭir 35:22 present successive oppositions: blind and seeing, darkness and light, shade and ḥarūr, and living and dead.

Together, these contrasts express spiritual differences and different human conditions.

This does not, however, make ḥarūr lexically equivalent to Hell.

Ibn Sīdah also insists on this distinction, retaining the direct meanings of shade and ḥarūr in their own right.

“Scorching heat” is therefore suitable for a basic translation. Its figurative relationship to misguidance or punishment belongs to contextual explanation.

Heat and freedom: distinct branches

Taken together, these verses show that ḥarr concerning temperature has no direct meaning of freedom from enslavement.

The heat of the air in Al-Tawbah and Al-Naḥl, and the scorching heat in Fāṭir, concern a different subject from al-ḥurr bi-al-ḥurr in Al-Baqarah.

Translating the consonants without attending to their vowels and context can therefore produce serious errors.

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, ḥurr means a free person.

In Al-Tawbah 9:81, ḥarr means heat.

Shared root consonants do not authorize merging the two meanings.

8. Ḥarīr: silk in descriptions of the hereafter’s blessings

Ḥarīr occurs three times in the Qur’an, always in descriptions of the blessings of the hereafter.

Al-Ḥajj 22:23

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُدْخِلُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَلُؤْلُؤًا ۖ وَلِبَاسُهُمْ فِيهَا حَرِيرٌ

inna Allāha yudkhilu alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa-luʾluʾan wa-libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīrun

Translation: “The God will indeed admit those who believed and did good deeds into gardens beneath which rivers flow. There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their clothing there will be silk.”

Ḥarīr is the predicate of the nominal clause wa-libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīr, “their clothing there is silk.”

The Qur’an thus specifies the kind of clothing.

The same expression, wa-libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīr, occurs in Fāṭir 35:33.

In Al-Insān 76:12, ḥarīran appears alongside jannah, “a garden,” as a reward for patience.

The direct meaning of ḥarīr

Ḥarīr in these three verses denotes silk, a familiar material for making fabric and clothing.

The translation need not turn it into “the garments of free people,” “warm clothing,” or “liberating attire.”

Such renderings lack sufficient etymological support.

Conversely, silk in the hereafter should not be restricted without evidence to a particular weave, color, thickness, or method of production.

The Qur’an names the material or kind of clothing without explaining every physical characteristic.

Was ḥarīr derived from ḥurr because of its softness or purity?

Some linguistic explanations may propose a relationship between silk and delicacy or excellence.

As a descriptive association this is understandable: silk is delicate fabric and has been valued in many societies.

Those qualities do not prove, however, that ḥarīr necessarily developed from ḥurr meaning free or pure.

Nor does the identical form of ḥarīr for silk and ḥarīr for someone burning with grief establish a shared historical origin.

Ḥarīr should therefore be translated with its established Qur’anic sense, silk, while historical relationships with the other branches remain undecided without conclusive evidence.

Do these silk verses also rule on worldly clothing?

The three verses themselves concern clothing in Paradise.

That description alone does not establish a general ruling permitting or prohibiting silk clothing in worldly life.

Later legal rulings should likewise not be incorporated into the lexical definition of ḥarīr.

The meaning relevant to the root study is clear: silk.

Whether wearing it is permitted or prohibited in this world is a separate question requiring its own relevant evidence.

9. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ R R (ḥurr and taḥrīr) and ʿ T Q (ʿitq)

ʿItq is one of the closest concepts to emancipation.

In Arabic, aʿtaqa al-ʿabd means to emancipate an enslaved person; ḥarrarahu has the same meaning in the context of slavery.

ʿ T Q also has other Arabic uses involving nobility, antiquity, and excellence.

The two families therefore overlap in emancipation, but not all their derivatives are synonymous.

The Qur’anic point is that Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, Al-Māʾidah 5:89, and Al-Mujādilah 58:3 explicitly use taḥrīr, rather than ʿitq.

A word-by-word study must preserve the Qur’an’s actual form.

Study of ʿ T Q

Ḥ R R (taḥrīr) and F K K (fakk)

Al-Balad 90:13 uses fakku raqabah.

Fakk concerns undoing and opening a bond; taḥrīr concerns freeing.

Both expressions can therefore converge on a similar result in the context of release from slavery.

Their lexical imagery nevertheless differs.

Fakk emphasizes opening the bond, while taḥrīr emphasizes freeing the person from enslavement.

This semantic proximity must not lead to confusing the roots.

Study of F K K

Ḥ R R (taḥrīr) and KH L Ṣ (khulūṣ)

Khulūṣ concerns purity and separation from admixture or other attachments.

Muḥarraran in Āl ʿImrān 3:35 relates to this semantic field, since the child is dedicated to worship and religious service.

Taḥrīr nevertheless does not mean religious devotion in every use.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 concerns freeing a person from slavery. Translating this as “purifying a raqabah” would fail to convey the expression’s natural legal sense.

Conversely, being exclusively dedicated to worship does not itself mean legal emancipation from slavery.

The two uses must therefore be distinguished.

Study of KH L Ṣ

Ḥ R R (ḥarr) and B R D (bard)

These are opposites in the field of temperature.

Ḥarr is heat, while bard is cold or coolness.

Al-Tawbah 9:81 concerns the heat of fire; in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:69, the fire becomes bardan wa-salāman, “coolness and safety,” for Abraham.

This opposition helps explain the words, but does not make all heat harmful or all cold beneficial.

Their effects and value depend on conditions and context.

Study of B R D

Ḥ R R (ḥarūr) and S M M (samūm)

Samūm and ḥarūr in some uses concern scorching wind and heat.

Lexicographers differed over a temporal distinction between them.

Some reports assign samūm to daytime and ḥarūr to nighttime, while other sources allow ḥarūr by both night and day.

Neither word should therefore be assigned a rigid, universal time boundary.

Samūm also belongs to S M M; semantic proximity does not make it a derivative of Ḥ R R.

Study of S M M

Ḥ R R (ḥurr) and Ḥ R M (ḥarām)

The roots share their first two consonants, but their meanings differ.

Ḥ R R in the emancipation use concerns release from enslavement.

Ḥ R M concerns inviolability, restriction, and prohibition.

Shared initial sounds may make these forms seem close to some readers, but taḥrīr and taḥrīm name different actions.

Taḥrīr raqabah means freeing a person from bondage.

Taḥrīm means prohibiting something or placing it under a restriction of inviolability.

This distinction must be preserved in studying Qur’anic rulings.

Study of Ḥ R M

Ḥ R R (ḥarr) and Ḥ R Q (ḥarq)

Ḥ R Q concerns burning and causing to burn.

Ḥarr denotes heat.

Intense heat may cause burns in some circumstances, but ḥarr itself does not mean setting something on fire.

Ashaddu ḥarran in Al-Tawbah 9:81 describes the greater intensity of the fire’s heat. The act of burning is not supplied by this word itself.

The Ḥ R R family must therefore not be equated throughout with the family of burning.

Study of Ḥ R Q

Ḥ R R (taḥrīr) and R Q B (raqabah)

These families occur together in the Qur’anic expression taḥrīr raqabah.

Taḥrīr names the act of freeing.

Raqabah identifies the person whose freedom is at issue.

The act of emancipation thus comes from taḥrīr, and its object from raqabah.

A translation should not merge these distinct contributions into one root definition.

Study of R Q B

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ḥurr in Al-Baqarah 2:178, “free” is appropriate. Where explanation is needed: a person who is not in a condition of enslavement.

For taḥrīr raqabah, the principal proposal is “freeing a person from bondage.” This expresses the act of taḥrīr and makes clear the figurative use of raqabah for the person in bondage.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, muʾminah must also be expressed: “freeing a believing person from bondage.” Al-Māʾidah 5:89 and Al-Mujādilah 58:3 do not state that qualification, so it should not be added without explanation.

For muḥarraran in Āl ʿImrān 3:35, “released and exclusively dedicated” is lexically more precise than “emancipated from slavery.” A natural sentence can read: “I have vowed to You what is in my womb, released and exclusively dedicated to You.” The commentary should explain being set apart for worship and religious service.

For ḥarr in Al-Tawbah 9:81 and Al-Naḥl 16:81, “heat” is suitable. In ashaddu ḥarran, the greater intensity comes from ashadd and must be rendered separately.

For ḥarūr in Fāṭir 35:21, “scorching heat” is preferable to “heat” alone, since early uses associate the form with hot wind and oppressive heat.

For ḥarīr in Al-Ḥajj 22:23, Fāṭir 35:33, and Al-Insān 76:12, the clear equivalent is “silk.” Softness, cost, or color need not be added to the basic translation.

11. Conclusion

Ḥ R R has several established semantic fields in classical Arabic.

Ibn Fāris identifies two principal foundations: freedom as opposed to slavery and freedom from defect or admixture; and heat as opposed to cold.

In the first branch, ḥurr denotes a free person and in extended uses can describe something noble, pure, or excellent.

Taḥrīr in its legal use means freeing a person from slavery. In Mary’s mother’s vow, muḥarraran concerns releasing and dedicating the child to a religious purpose.

Although related, these uses are not identical.

In the second branch, ḥarr expresses heat and ḥarūr scorching heat.

Ḥarīr names silk in the Qur’an. The evidence examined does not conclusively establish its precise historical relationship to freedom or heat.

The root occurs fifteen times in the Qur’an.

Al-Baqarah 2:178 concerns a free person as opposed to an enslaved person in the setting of qiṣāṣ.

Āl ʿImrān 3:35 concerns a vow dedicating a child to The God.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, Al-Māʾidah 5:89, and Al-Mujādilah 58:3 concern emancipation in different settings of redress.

Al-Tawbah 9:81, Al-Naḥl 16:81, and Fāṭir 35:21 concern heat and scorching.

Al-Ḥajj 22:23, Fāṭir 35:33, and Al-Insān 76:12 use ḥarīr in describing the clothing and blessings of the hereafter.

The main requirement in understanding this family is therefore to avoid flattening its different branches into a single meaning.

Legal freedom is distinct from moral virtue; emancipation differs from dedicating someone to worship; and heat and silk must not be derived from freedom and restricted to that sense.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

Ḥ R R is an Arabic family with two principal semantic fields: freedom and release from bondage or admixture, and heat as opposed to cold. In the Qur’an, ḥurr denotes a free person as opposed to an enslaved person; taḥrīr, freeing someone from bondage; muḥarrar, someone dedicated through a vow; ḥarr, heat; ḥarūr, scorching heat; and ḥarīr, silk. Some relationships among these uses are clear, but all cannot be reduced without historical evidence to one original image. Each form must be understood through its vowels, morphology, object, and verse context.
