Driving and repelling harshly: from mistreating an orphan to being thrust toward the Fire

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D ʿ ʿ denotes driving away, repelling, and exerting pressure upon another with violence and harshness.

Daʿʿahu describes someone driving another away forcefully. This may involve physical pressure, but may also accompany harsh treatment, humiliation, exclusion, and deprivation of rights.

Lexical sources explain the verb through dafʿ ʿanīf and dafʿ fī jafwah: pushing with violence and rough, unkind treatment.

Its difference from ordinary movement matters. Someone may push a person aside to protect them from danger, which is not necessarily injustice. In its established Qur’anic uses, however, daʿʿ involves harsh driving and degrading or severe treatment.

The root occurs three times in two Qur’anic verses: twice in Al-Ṭūr 52:13 and once in Al-Māʿūn 107:2.

In Al-Māʿūn, a person is described as yaduʿʿu al-yatīma: harshly repelling the orphan.

In Al-Ṭūr, people yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama daʿʿan: they are forcefully thrust toward Hellfire.

Both retain the semantic core of driving with pressure and violence, while the agent, object, and situation differ.

Al-Māʿūn criticizes a person’s mistreatment of an orphan. Al-Ṭūr describes the deniers’ fate on the Day of Recompense.

These uses can be compared across Qur’anic passages. It should not be asserted without evidence, however, that those thrust toward the Fire in Al-Ṭūr are necessarily the same individuals who repel the orphan in Al-Māʿūn.

Another distinction is between D ʿ ʿ and D ʿ W. Though similar in writing and sound, their meanings differ: daʿʿ concerns harsh driving, whereas daʿā concerns calling, summoning, and requesting.

2. Qur’anic forms and morphology

D ʿ ʿ is a doubled Arabic root: its second and third consonants are both ʿayn.

In ordinary inflection, these consonants may merge into one geminated consonant.

Its three Qur’anic occurrences are:

Yaduʿʿu — Al-Māʿūn 107:2

A third-person masculine singular active imperfect verb, Form I.

Meaning: harshly drives away or repels.

Yudaʿʿūna — Al-Ṭūr 52:13

A third-person masculine plural passive imperfect verb, Form I.

Meaning: they are harshly driven or thrust.

Daʿʿan — Al-Ṭūr 52:13

An accusative verbal noun from the same root.

Meaning: driving with pressure and violence.

In Al-Ṭūr, daʿʿan is an emphatic cognate accusative, reinforcing the verb’s action.

The verse therefore does more than say they are driven: by adding a verbal noun from the same root, it foregrounds and emphasizes the driving itself.

Yudaʿʿūna and daʿʿan occur in one verse. The root thus has three word occurrences, rather than three separate verses.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences in two verses

Arabic sources also record other forms of this family:

Daʿʿa–yaduʿʿu: drove with pressure and violence.

Daʿʿ: the verbal noun, severe and harsh driving.

Daʿaʿtuhu: I drove him away; an inflection displaying all three root consonants separately.

Daʿʿūhu and daʿʿū: inflected forms with an attached pronoun or personal ending.

Daʿdaʿa also exists in Arabic, but is morphologically quadriliteral. Its derivatives should not all be counted as direct derivatives of triliteral D ʿ ʿ without independent examination.

3. The semantic core in early dictionaries

Movement, pushing, and pressure

Ibn Fāris writes of the root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ مُنْقَاسٌ مُطَّرِدٌ، وَهُوَ يَدُلُّ عَلَى حَرَكَةٍ وَدَفْعٍ وَاضْطِرَابٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun munqāsun muṭṭaridun wa-huwa yadullu ʿalā ḥarakatin wa-dafʿin wa-iḍṭirābin

“One consistently applicable principle, denoting movement, pushing, and agitation.”

He then explains daʿʿ through dafʿ, pushing.

This definition identifies a kind of moving or driving.

Movement alone is nevertheless insufficient as a definition. Movement may be gentle, natural, or occur without another person’s intervention, whereas the established basic use of daʿʿ concerns force exerted from one side upon another.

Someone or something drives, while the other party moves under that pressure or is repelled.

In the Qur’anic uses, that action involves severity and violence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: movement, pushing, and agitation

Violence and rough treatment

Kitāb al-ʿAyn states:

دَعَّهُ يَدُعُّهُ، الدَّعُّ: دَفْعٌ فِي جَفْوَةٍ

daʿʿahu yaduʿʿuhu al-daʿʿu: dafʿun fī jafwatin

“He daʿʿa him; daʿʿ is pushing accompanied by rough treatment.”

Ibn Durayd likewise explains:

دَعَّهُ: دَفَعَهُ دَفْعًا عَنِيفًا

daʿʿahu: dafaʿahu dafʿan ʿanīfan

“He daʿʿa him: he pushed him violently.”

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits these definitions and emphasizes harsh, severe treatment in discussing the Qur’anic uses.

Two aspects are thus clear: driving or pushing, and its harsh or unkind manner.

These aspects should remain together.

Translating merely as “moved” loses the conduct’s intensity.

Translating only as “humiliated” weakens the image of driving and repelling.

“Driving harshly” therefore suitably introduces the root’s central meaning.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: pushing with rough treatment

Is daʿʿ always a bodily blow?

Physical pressure is one clear instance of this root’s uses.

Someone might push another by the back or shoulder, driving them away from a place.

Arabic also uses this family to explain repelling a person from their right.

Commenting on Al-Māʿūn 107:2, Al-Ṭabarī records daʿaʿtu fulānan ʿan ḥaqqihi: I repelled someone from their right.

A person may be harshly prevented from obtaining their right without a particular bodily blow necessarily occurring in every instance.

Daʿʿ can thus concern both physical driving and harsh conduct that denies a right.

Not every deprivation of a right must therefore lexically be daʿʿ. Violence, rough treatment, or repelling remains significant in this family.

Al-Ṭabarī: repelling the orphan from their right

4. Early Arabic witnesses

Repelling an orphan during hardship

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Kitāb al-Afʿāl, and other sources quote a verse illustrating this root:

أَلَمْ أَكْفِ أَهْلَكَ فِقْدَانَهُ
إِذَا الْقَوْمُ فِي الْمَحْلِ دَعُّوا الْيَتِيمَا

a-lam akfi ahlaka fiqdānahu / idhā al-qawmu fī al-maḥli daʿʿū al-yatīmā

A cautious rendering: “Did I not spare your household [the hardship of] his absence, when people in drought harshly drove away the orphan?” The referent of “his” is uncertain without the full context.

Its second hemistich describes people harshly repelling an orphan during drought and material hardship.

Al-maḥl here concerns drought and difficult living conditions.

Daʿʿū al-yatīmā expresses driving the orphan away.

This witness matters because it uses the same verb for treatment of an orphan.

In such circumstances, the orphan may need food, shelter, or support, while people harshly turn them away.

The verse’s historical attribution nevertheless requires caution.

The examined sources do not conclusively identify its poet or composition date. The editor of Kitāb al-Afʿāl explicitly says that the verse’s author could not be identified in the sources consulted.

Variants of the first hemistich are also recorded. The vocalization akfi here follows “providing sufficiently for or sparing”; the referent of the pronoun in fiqdānahu remains uncertain without the verse’s full context.

It can therefore serve as a witness from Arabic lexicographical tradition, but should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic poetry without independent evidence.

Kitāb al-Afʿāl: witness 3329 and uncertain attribution

Daʿdaʿah and shaking a vessel

Ibn Fāris also discusses daʿdaʿah within the entry.

One use describes shaking a measure or vessel to fit more contents inside.

For example, shaking a container filled with grain or food may reduce the empty spaces between its contents.

This use concerns movement and shaking.

Poetry attributed to Labīd also mentions a jafnah mudaʿdaʿah: a large food bowl filled through shaking or packing.

Triliteral daʿʿa should nevertheless be distinguished from quadriliteral daʿdaʿa.

Some uses of daʿdaʿah involve motion or shaking, and Ibn Fāris connects them.

Other Arabic forms of daʿ daʿ address animals or someone who has stumbled. These may be imitative vocal expressions.

Not every daʿ daʿ can therefore be taken to mean violent driving.

This caution prevents letter similarity from substituting for derivational evidence.

Duʿāʿ and unrelated uses

Dictionaries record duʿāʿ for a plant, black seeds, and in some accounts a winged insect.

Some poetry also associates duʿāʿ al-nakhl with scattered date palms or the spaces between them.

These uses do not themselves demonstrate harsh driving.

Merely sharing a dictionary entry does not justify using them to construct a new meaning for yaduʿʿu al-yatīma.

For Qur’anic usage, clearer evidence comes from daʿʿa itself and definitions of harsh pushing.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Muḥkam, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: daʿdaʿah and other uses

5. Yaduʿʿu al-yatīma: Al-Māʿūn 107:2

Al-Māʿūn 107:2

فَذَٰلِكَ الَّذِي يَدُعُّ الْيَتِيمَ

fa-dhālika alladhī yaduʿʿu al-yatīma

“Then that is the one who harshly drives away the orphan.”

Yaduʿʿu is active; its subject is an implicit pronoun referring back to alladhī.

Al-yatīma is the verb’s object.

In the verse’s direct construction, a person thus repels the orphan with pressure and violence.

Does it mean only bodily driving?

The verb can describe physical driving, but early sources also explain it in the context of denying the orphan their right.

An orphan might approach someone for food, support, or a rightful entitlement and be harshly turned away.

Such conduct may involve rough speech, humiliation, and rejection.

Not all these instances should be included in the verb’s independent meaning.

Yaduʿʿu alone does not say the person beat the orphan, seized their property, or expelled them from a house.

Those actions may exemplify injustice and repelling, but each requires specific evidence.

Translation should preserve the basic action of harsh driving.

Connection with denying recompense

Al-Māʿūn 107:1 states:

أَرَأَيْتَ الَّذِي يُكَذِّبُ بِالدِّينِ

a-raʾayta alladhī yukadhdhibu bi-al-dīni

“Have you seen the one who denies recompense?”

The second verse then begins with fa-dhālika.

This connection places treatment of the orphan within identifying someone who denies dīn or recompense.

The Qur’an thus does not limit the issue to a personal disagreement between two people: it relates harsh treatment of an orphan to a person’s stance toward responsibility and recompense.

This relationship must nevertheless be understood carefully.

The verse does not establish that everyone who mistreats an orphan once necessarily and fully knowingly denies the Day of Recompense.

Nor can everyone claiming faith be considered immune to such conduct.

The verse identifies a pattern of condemned behavior in connection with denying dīn.

Relation to wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīni

Al-Māʿūn 107:3 states:

وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ

wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīni

“And does not encourage feeding the needy.”

Another instance of misconduct is added to the picture.

The second verse concerns repelling the orphan; the third concerns failing to encourage feeding the needy.

One expresses violent conduct toward a vulnerable person, the other indifference to a deprived person’s need for food.

These behaviors may accompany each other without being identical.

Yaduʿʿu therefore does not lexically mean “does not feed.”

Nor does lā yaḥuḍḍu itself mean “repels the orphan.”

Each verb independently contributes to describing the person being criticized.

Is treatment of an orphan a criterion of religious conduct?

After discussing the orphan and needy person, the surah mentions people heedless of their ṣalāh, showing off, and withholding māʿūn.

This continuity shows that the surah’s criticism concerns more than professing faith or performing apparently religious acts.

Treatment of vulnerable people and withholding assistance also matter within this account.

Without argument, however, all the people and descriptions mentioned should not be treated as one identical individual in every respect.

Ṣalāh and māʿūn also require their own independent studies.

What is clear for D ʿ ʿ is condemnation of harsh and rejecting treatment of the orphan.

Connection with other verses about orphans

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 states:

فَأَمَّا الْيَتِيمَ فَلَا تَقْهَرْ

fa-ammā al-yatīma fa-lā taqhar

“As for the orphan, do not demean or oppress.”

Together with Al-Māʿūn 107:2, this verse forms an important semantic network.

One condemns harshly repelling the orphan.

The other prohibits dominating and oppressing the orphan.

Al-Fajr 89:17 also speaks of failing to honor the orphan.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:2 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:10 address orphans’ property rights and the danger of unjustly consuming their wealth.

These verses show that just treatment of an orphan extends beyond kind speech: their actual rights and needs also matter.

Daʿʿ, qahr, honoring, and consuming property nevertheless remain four different expressions.

They should not all be reduced to one lexical meaning, even within a shared ethical subject.

6. Yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama daʿʿan: Al-Ṭūr 52:13

Al-Ṭūr 52:13

يَوْمَ يُدَعُّونَ إِلَىٰ نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ دَعًّا

yawma yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama daʿʿan

“On the day when they are forcefully driven toward Hellfire.”

Yudaʿʿūna is a passive imperfect verb.

The agent performing the action is not named in the verb itself; the people being driven occupy the grammatical position of passive subject.

Ilā nāri jahannama identifies the movement’s destination.

Daʿʿan is an accusative verbal noun and emphatic cognate accusative reinforcing the driving.

Why add the verbal noun?

Grammatically, omitting emphatic daʿʿan would not leave the verb and its complements incomplete. How yawma connects with the preceding context is a separate syntactic question.

Adding the same-root verbal noun nevertheless foregrounds the driving itself.

In Arabic, this construction can emphasize the action’s occurrence or manner.

Persian can therefore convey its intensity through expressions equivalent to “with pressure,” “forcefully,” or “severely.”

This emphasis does not mean the verse specifies a number of blows or instances of driving.

Reinforcing the action through a verbal noun does not count repeated thrusts.

Nor does the verse specify the body part or physical instrument through which pressure is exerted.

Such details are absent from the sentence itself.

Who is being driven?

Al-Ṭūr 52:11 states:

فَوَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِلْمُكَذِّبِينَ

fa-waylun yawmaʾidhin li-al-mukadhdhibīna

“Then woe that day to the deniers.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:12 describes those absorbed in frivolous discourse and play.

Al-Ṭūr 52:13 then describes their being driven.

The group is therefore identified through the immediate context.

Yudaʿʿūna itself does not mean denying.

Denial comes from al-mukadhdhibīn and the relevant verses, while daʿʿ expresses the manner of driving.

Ilā nāri jahannama and the movement’s direction

The preposition ilā identifies the movement’s direction and destination.

The verse thus speaks of driving toward the Fire.

D ʿ ʿ itself does not mean entering Hell, just as it does not independently mean leaving somewhere.

The direction comes from the preposition and stated destination.

Al-Ṭūr 52:14 addresses them:

هَٰذِهِ النَّارُ الَّتِي كُنْتُمْ بِهَا تُكَذِّبُونَ

hādhihi al-nāru allatī kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūna

“This is the Fire that you used to deny.”

The driving in Al-Ṭūr 52:13 is consequently followed by confrontation with the reality they previously denied.

This connection comes from both verses together, rather than the meaning of daʿʿ itself.

Is this like repelling the orphan?

Lexically, it is the same verb: driving with pressure and severity.

The situations nevertheless differ fundamentally.

Al-Māʿūn condemns a person harshly repelling an orphan.

Al-Ṭūr describes deniers being driven toward the Fire on the Day of Recompense.

A shared root alone does not make the two acts morally equivalent.

In the first instance, the Qur’an criticizes oppressive conduct toward a vulnerable person.

In the second, it describes the fate of people identified in context as deniers.

A root’s meaning may thus remain stable while the event’s cause, setting, and moral evaluation depend on context.

Comparing the two uses is nonetheless helpful for understanding daʿʿ’s intensity and manner.

7. Variant reading and distinction from D ʿ W

Some reading and commentary sources report another form in Al-Ṭūr 52:13 with an ungeminated ʿayn.

On that reading, the verb can be analyzed through D ʿ W, calling and summoning.

Al-Qurṭubī and other sources transmit this report.

In the common Ḥafṣ reading, however, yudaʿʿūna has doubled ʿayn and belongs to D ʿ ʿ.

This is more than a minor pronunciation difference: it changes the verb’s derivational analysis and meaning.

The geminated form concerns forceful driving.

The reported ungeminated form may concern calling or summoning.

In the familiar reading, however, final daʿʿan clearly emphasizes driving.

For translation and root indexing based on Ḥafṣ, “they are forcefully driven” is therefore appropriate.

The different reading may be discussed in lexical research, but the two meanings should not be indiscriminately combined in one translation.

Al-Qurṭubī: harsh driving and the reported ungeminated reading

8. Differences from related roots

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and D ʿ W (daʿā): driving and calling

These roots have similar forms.

D ʿ ʿ is a doubled root concerning driving with pressure and violence.

D ʿ W concerns calling, summoning, requesting, and supplication.

Yaduʿʿu al-yatīma, for example, means repelling the orphan.

Yadʿū in the D ʿ W family may mean calling someone.

Yaduʿʿu al-yatīma must therefore not be translated as “invites the orphan.”

Likewise, in the familiar reading, yudaʿʿūna in Al-Ṭūr means being driven, rather than merely being summoned.

Gemination and vowel patterns contribute to this distinction.

Study of D ʿ W

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and W D ʿ (wadaʿa): driving and leaving

Arabic also has daʿ, meaning “leave” or “let be.”

This form belongs to W D ʿ, rather than D ʿ ʿ.

Daʿhu, for example, may mean “leave him alone.”

Daʿʿahu, with doubled ʿayn, is instead a past verb meaning “he harshly drove him away.” It must not be confused with imperative daʿhu from W D ʿ.

These look very similar in writing but differ in formation and meaning.

In the familiar reading, yaduʿʿu al-yatīma must not therefore be reduced to “leaves the orphan.”

The doubled ʿayn and harsh driving matter in Al-Māʿūn 107:2.

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and D F ʿ (dafaʿa): harsh driving and pushing

Arabic dafʿ has a broader field.

One may push something aside, ward off harm, defend a person, or hand something over to someone.

Established early definitions associate daʿʿ, however, with severe driving accompanied by rough treatment.

Every daʿʿ in the sense of driving may thus be explained through dafʿ, but not every dafʿ must be daʿʿ.

Warding off evil or aggression, for example, may be commendable in the Qur’an.

In Al-Māʿūn 107:2, however, driving away the orphan is condemned.

Moral evaluation should therefore not be extracted merely from the general presence of movement or pushing.

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and Ṭ R D (ṭarada): repelling and exclusion

Arabic ṭard concerns driving a person or thing away.

Al-Anʿām 6:52 prohibits driving away those calling upon their Lord. In Hūd 11:29, Noah says he will not drive away those who have believed.

In these contexts, exclusion may concern removing someone from a group or denying their presence and acceptance.

Daʿʿ may accompany exclusion, but pressure and violence are more prominent in its use.

Ṭard and daʿʿ are thus close in some situations without being complete synonyms.

Nor should every exclusion be equated with the mistreatment of the orphan in Al-Māʿūn without examining its reason.

Study of Ṭ R D

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and Q H R (qahara): repelling and oppression

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 prohibits qahr toward the orphan.

Qahr concerns domination and overpowering; in this context, it may refer to pressure and injustice toward a vulnerable person.

Daʿʿ also applies to the orphan in Al-Māʿūn.

The important distinction is that daʿʿ foregrounds driving and repelling, whereas qahr concerns domination and subjection to pressure.

Someone may harshly repel an orphan while also dominating and oppressing them.

Such overlap does not make the roots identical.

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and N H R (nahara): driving and harsh speech

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:10 states:

وَأَمَّا السَّائِلَ فَلَا تَنْهَرْ

wa-ammā al-sāʾila fa-lā tanhar

“And do not harshly repel the one who asks.”

Nahr here may concern harsh speech and sternly turning someone away.

Daʿʿ, by contrast, more strongly foregrounds the image of driving with pressure.

The two may coincide in actual conduct, as when someone shouts at and pushes away a needy person.

Daʿʿ does not itself mean shouting, just as nahr does not always require physical pressure.

“Shouts at the orphan” therefore conveys only some possible instances of Al-Māʿūn 107:2, rather than replacing the verb’s complete meaning.

Study of N H R

D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa) and Z J R (zajara): repelling and stern restraint

Arabic zajr concerns restraining and driving away, sometimes with a shout or stern treatment.

Some Qur’anic uses of this family involve a restraining command or severe response.

Daʿʿ may also repel someone from a place or right.

In zajr, restraint and stern rebuke may be prominent; in daʿʿ, the driving itself with pressure and violence matters.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary across every use of the roots.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Al-Māʿūn 107:2

فَذَٰلِكَ الَّذِي يَدُعُّ الْيَتِيمَ

fa-dhālika alladhī yaduʿʿu al-yatīma

Proposed translation:

“Then that is the one who harshly drives away the orphan.”

This preserves both repelling and its harsh manner.

“Sternly pushes the orphan away” is also natural and defensible.

“Invites the orphan,” however, comes from another root.

“Leaves the orphan alone” also fails to convey harsh driving fully.

“Consumes the orphan’s rightful property” may be a related example within examining oppressive conduct, but is not a direct equivalent of the verb.

“Beats the orphan” likewise adds a particular bodily action not explicitly stated by the verse.

“Harshly drives away” or “sternly pushes away” is therefore preferable.

Al-Ṭūr 52:13

يَوْمَ يُدَعُّونَ إِلَىٰ نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ دَعًّا

yawma yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama daʿʿan

The existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered in English:

“On the day when they are forcefully driven toward Hellfire.”

This suitably conveys the movement’s direction, the passive verb, and the verbal noun’s emphasis.

“Are severely driven” is also appropriate.

“Are invited to Hell,” however, does not fit the familiar geminated reading.

“Are cast into Hell” may picture the outcome but does not precisely convey driving.

Nor should translation add chains, blows to the neck, or dragging on the face, since these details are not present in the verse itself.

Non-Qur’anic forms

Daʿʿahu: he drove him away with pressure and violence.

Daʿaʿtuhu: I drove him away.

Daʿʿ: severe and harsh driving.

Forms related to daʿdaʿah should be translated according to their particular use, rather than all being made to mean violently driving a person.

10. Conclusion

Arabic D ʿ ʿ denotes driving and repelling with pressure and violence.

Ibn Fāris explains it through movement, pushing, and agitation. Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ foreground harsh driving accompanied by rough treatment.

The root occurs three times in two Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Māʿūn 107:2, active yaduʿʿu describes a person harshly driving away the orphan.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:13, passive yudaʿʿūna describes deniers being driven toward Hellfire. The verbal noun daʿʿan emphasizes that driving.

The uses share a semantic core while differing in situation and moral evaluation.

Al-Māʿūn condemns mistreatment of a vulnerable person. Al-Ṭūr describes the deniers’ fate.

Physical driving must also be distinguished from repelling someone from their right. Both may fall within daʿʿ’s field, but the root does not determine every detail of injustice or harsh conduct.

D ʿ ʿ should not be confused with D ʿ W, calling and inviting, or W D ʿ in forms such as daʿ, leaving or letting be.

Proposed final root definition: D ʿ ʿ denotes driving, repelling, and exerting pressure upon another with violence and rough treatment. In human conduct, it may accompany humiliation, exclusion, and denial of rights, but those consequences require context. The Qur’an uses the root both to condemn repelling the orphan and to describe deniers being driven toward the Fire, without the shared root making the causes or moral evaluations of the events identical. Brief root meaning: Driving and repelling with pressure, violence, and rough treatment.
