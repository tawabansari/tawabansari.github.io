Qur’anic Root KH Ḍ R

Qur’anic and lexical forms

خَضِرًا
(khaḍiran)
A noun for green growth; Al-Anʿām 6:99; once

خُضْر
(khuḍr)
Plural form; five Qur’anic occurrences with different case endings

الْأَخْضَرِ
(al-akhḍari)
Masculine singular adjective; Yā Sīn 36:80; once

مُخْضَرَّةً
(mukhḍarratan)
Feminine active participle, Form IX; Al-Ḥajj 22:63; once

خَضِرَ ـ يَخْضَرُ
(khaḍira–yakhḍaru)
Lexical verb: became green; these forms do not occur in the Qur’an

خُضْرَة
(khuḍrah)
Noun for greenness or green; this form is not Qur’anic

اِخْضَرَّ ـ يَخْضَرُّ

اِخْتَضَرَ

Greenness, freshness, and vitality: from plant growth to the color of clothing and the appearance of blessings

The Arabic root خ ض ر is fundamentally associated with the color green, but its uses extend beyond naming a color. This family describes fresh vegetation, land made green through growth, living trees, green clothing, and even certain dark colors.

In nature, green vegetation commonly accompanies water, freshness, and growth. Plant-related uses can therefore convey freshness and vitality alongside color. What the word itself expresses should nevertheless be distinguished from what the plant’s condition or the context supplies: not everything green is alive and growing.

The Qur’an uses the root both for vegetation and the earth’s transformation and for ears of grain, trees, and paradisal blessings. These uses show how a lexical family retains its color meaning while acquiring a more specific sense in different contexts. The root occurs eight times in eight verses and seven surahs: five instances of khuḍr and one each of khaḍir, akhḍar, and mukhḍarrah.

1. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • خَضِرَ ـ يَخْضَرُ, khaḍira–yakhḍaru: became green; of plants, became green and fresh.
  • خُضْرَة, khuḍrah: greenness or the color green; in some uses, freshness and vitality.
  • أَخْضَر, akhḍar: green, masculine adjective; its feminine is khaḍrāʾ and its plural khuḍr.
  • خَضِر, khaḍir: green and fresh, particularly of plants and green growth.
  • خَضَّرَ ـ يُخَضِّرُ, khaḍḍara–yukhaḍḍiru: made something green or caused it to become green.
  • اِخْضَرَّ ـ يَخْضَرُّ, ikhḍarra–yakhḍarru: became green or acquired a green color.
  • مُخْضَرّ / مُخْضَرَّة, mukhḍarr / mukhḍarrah: something that has become green or is in a green condition.
  • اِخْضَوْضَرَ, ikhḍawḍara: became green or greener.
  • اِخْتَضَرَ, ikhtaḍara: cut or harvested vegetation or something while still green and fresh; in extended uses, brought something to an end before maturity.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. The actual Qur’anic derivatives are:

خَضِرًا, خُضْر, أَخْضَر, and مُخْضَرَّةً.

Khuḍr appears with different case endings, including khuḍrin, khuḍran, and khuḍrun. These syntactic differences do not constitute independent derivatives.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eight occurrences and four derived forms

2. Semantic core and range of Arabic usage

Green and its connection with growth

The clearest meaning of خ ض ر is greenness. Ibn Fāris likewise places the root within the color field in Maqāyīs al-Lughah, relating other uses to it.

A natural example is vegetation that has emerged from the ground and remains green. Such growth commonly has moisture, softness, and freshness; dried vegetation may instead lose its greenness and become yellow or withered.

Two closely related but distinct features thus appear:

First, the color green, which may describe anything green, living or inanimate.

Second, the freshness and vitality of vegetation, accompanying this color in growth and agricultural contexts.

Thawbun akhḍaru, for example, means a green garment; zarʿun khaḍirun may additionally evoke fresh, flourishing crops. Freshness in the latter comes from the plant-related use and established sense of khaḍir, rather than a requirement that every use entail being alive.

The range of green in early Arabic

Classical Arabic akhḍar does not always follow modern color classifications.

Lexicons sometimes use khuḍrah for dark colors, particularly green approaching black. Katībatun khaḍrāʾ, for example, describes a military force whose dark armor and iron equipment dominate its appearance.

In descriptions of some horses and camels, akhḍar may also denote a dark or grayish color mixed with black. Some early uses of khuḍrah for human complexion likewise describe darkness or a dusky complexion.

Ibn Fāris explains that lush, intensely green vegetation may look nearly black from a distance. Arabic color naming therefore allows some overlap between greenness and degrees of darkness.

This range does not make green and black identical in every context. Each use depends on its referent: green grain, a garment’s color, and dark armor provide different settings.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: green and dense vegetation appearing dark

Botanical, economic, and figurative uses

Since greenness connects with an early stage of growth, khuḍar and khuḍār in some Arabic uses denote green plants, vegetables, or fresh crops.

Mukhāḍarah is also recorded in the language of transactions for selling fruit or produce while still green and unripe. Green here concerns the crop’s growth stage, but the root itself does not mean buying and selling; that transactional meaning belongs to an economic term.

Lexicons also record ikhtaḍara for cutting vegetation while green and fresh. Related expressions concern a young life ending prematurely, as though cut before full growth.

Cutting is not the underlying meaning of green. A particular derived verb instead concerns cutting something green or fresh and extends into other settings.

Dictionaries even record khaḍara al-nakhla for cutting parts of date palms. The verbal and technical family thus extends beyond the color adjective, but this does not transfer cutting into Qur’anic akhḍar or mukhḍarrah.

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab: fresh plants, mukhāḍarah, and ikhtiḍār

3. Early Arabic witnesses

Early poetry and lexical reports use خ ض ر for vegetation, color, shade, and natural objects.

A witness from Al-ʿAjjāj

A rajaz passage attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj, an early Islamic poet, describes the shelter of a wild bovine:

بِالْخُشْبِ دُونَ الْهَدَبِ الْيَخْضُورِ

bi-al-khushbi dūna al-hadabi al-yakhḍūri

Translation:

“Among the woody branches, beneath the green leaves and foliage.”

The lexical explanation identifies al-hadab as the leaves of the arṭā tree and al-yakhḍūr as their green color. Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam give al-khushb, with ḍammah on khāʾ and sukūn on shīn; the quotation above corrects this vocalization.

Ibn Qutaybah’s Al-Maʿānī al-Kabīr: the wild bovine’s shelter and arṭā leaves

This witness shows that poetic Arabic used forms such as yakhḍūr, as well as akhḍar, to express green, fresh vegetation.

Al-ʿAjjāj was an Islamic-period poet. This is therefore evidence of early Arabic near the beginnings of Islam, rather than a securely pre-Islamic witness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-ʿAjjāj’s witness with al-khushb and the meaning of yakhḍūr

Katībatun khaḍrāʾ

Lexical reports use katībatun khaḍrāʾ for a military force whose dark armor and iron equipment dominate its appearance.

Literally, it is a green military force; this use does not necessarily concern green clothes or a battlefield covered in plants. Dark military equipment contributes to the designation.

This example matters for Arabic color naming. Its presence in lexicons nevertheless does not independently establish that this exact expression was current before Islam.

Ikhtaḍara al-kalaʾa

Arabic records ikhtaḍara al-kalaʾa for cutting or harvesting vegetation while green.

It expresses more than color: an action performed on the plant while fresh.

The connection with greenness is clear, but it does not establish cutting as the root’s original meaning. The available witnesses do not prove that historical priority.

4. Qur’anic uses

The main settings are vegetation, the earth’s transformation, green versus dry plants, green trees, and green paradisal clothing and furnishings.

A. Green vegetation and grain growth — Al-Anʿām 6:99

فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ نَبَاتَ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ فَأَخْرَجْنَا مِنْهُ خَضِرًا نُخْرِجُ مِنْهُ حَبًّا مُتَرَاكِبًا

fa-akhrajnā bihi nabāta kulli shayʾin fa-akhrajnā minhu khaḍiran nukhriju minhu ḥabban mutarākiban

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Through it We brought forth the growth of every thing; then from it We brought forth green vegetation, from which We bring out grain arranged in layers.”

Al-Anʿām 6:99

Khaḍiran denotes green, fresh vegetation. Before it, the verse discusses sending down water and bringing forth plants; afterward it mentions layered grain.

It is therefore more than a color detached from the subject: green growth is involved in the process of vegetation and grain production.

Not every stage of plant growth belongs to khaḍir’s lexical meaning. Greenness and freshness emerge from the word and its botanical use; producing grain comes from the independent clause nukhriju minhu ḥabban mutarākiban.

Grammatically, khaḍiran is the object of fa-akhrajnā and is used substantivally for green vegetation. Natural translation can therefore say green growth or a green plant, rather than leaving green without a referent.

B. The earth becoming green after rain — Al-Ḥajj 22:63

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَتُصْبِحُ الْأَرْضُ مُخْضَرَّةً إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ

a-lam tara anna allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-tuṣbiḥu al-arḍu mukhḍarratan inna allāha laṭīfun khabīrun

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Have you not seen that The God sent water down from heaven, so the earth becomes green? The God is indeed subtle and fully aware.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:63

Mukhḍarratan is the feminine active participle of Form IX ikhḍarra and describes the earth’s green condition. In this sentence it is the accusative predicate of tuṣbiḥu; being an active participle should not be confused with being passive.

Greenness describes the land here, rather than just one plant or a small part. Growing vegetation gives the land a green appearance, which the Qur’an connects with water sent down.

The contrast with Al-Anʿām 6:99 matters: khaḍiran there is the green growth itself, while mukhḍarratan in Al-Ḥajj describes land on which greenness has appeared.

The word alone does not establish that the entire earth turns green at once or without time passing. The verse connects water and greening, rather than specifying plant growth’s timing or biology.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 also describes land stirring and growing when water arrives. Together these verses place greening within the Qur’an’s broader image of revival and growth after rain.

C. Green versus dry ears of grain — Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46

The Egyptian king’s dream includes:

وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ

wa-sabʿa sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And seven green ears of grain, and others dry.”

Yūsuf 12:43

Green ears recur in the request for interpretation in Yūsuf 12:46. Sabʿ there, however, is genitive in fī sabʿi. The quotation above is from Yūsuf 12:43; its accusative sabʿa should not be attributed verbatim to Yūsuf 12:46.

Khuḍrin is the plural of akhḍar and describes the ears, contrasting with yābisāt, dry ones.

The dream therefore contrasts more than two colors: green and dry ears represent different conditions of the plant.

Khuḍr itself nevertheless does not mean fertility, prosperous years, or abundant crops. These meanings emerge through the interpretation and the account of cultivation, harvests, and drought years in Joseph’s story.

Khuḍr describes the ears within the dream; its connection with abundant years is supplied by the narrative and interpretation.

Nor does the contrast with yābisāt require khuḍr to oppose dryness everywhere in the Qur’an. When it describes paradisal garments, it no longer denotes a stage of plant growth.

D. Green trees and fire — Yā Sīn 36:80

الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الشَّجَرِ الْأَخْضَرِ نَارًا فَإِذَا أَنْتُمْ مِنْهُ تُوقِدُونَ

alladhī jaʿala lakum mina al-shajari al-akhḍari nāran fa-idhā antum minhu tūqidūna

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The One who made fire for you from the green tree, from which you then kindle.”

Yā Sīn 36:80

Al-akhḍari describes al-shajari. The adjective is singular because al-shajar is a collective generic noun that can take a singular adjective.

The verse places a green tree beside fire. Human experience associates green trees with moisture and growth, and fire with burning. Their juxtaposition draws attention to The God’s power to produce fire from trees.

Akhḍar itself does not mean moist, combustible, or fire-producing; its direct contextual meaning is green. The tree and the statement as a whole supply the other features.

The verse names no particular tree and gives no detailed fire-making method. Historical tools can illuminate its audience’s circumstances, but a particular tree or physical mechanism should not be inserted into the Qur’anic wording.

This also shows that خ ض ر is not restricted to agricultural produce or attractive scenery: it can describe a tree within an argument about creation and divine power.

E. Green garments in Paradise — Al-Kahf 18:31 and Al-Insān 76:21

Al-Kahf states:

وَيَلْبَسُونَ ثِيَابًا خُضْرًا مِنْ سُنْدُسٍ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٍ

wa-yalbasūna thiyāban khuḍran min sundusin wa-istabraqin

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And they wear green garments of sundus and istabraq.”

Al-Kahf 18:31

Al-Insān also states:

عَالِيَهُمْ ثِيَابُ سُنْدُسٍ خُضْرٌ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٌ

ʿāliyahum thiyābu sundusin khuḍrun wa-istabraqun

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Upon them are green garments of sundus and istabraq.”

Al-Insān 76:21

Khuḍr describes garment color in both verses. In Al-Kahf 18:31, khuḍran is accusative and modifies thiyāban. In Al-Insān 76:21, Ḥafṣ’s reading has nominative khuḍrun modifying thiyābu; some other readings have the genitive modifying sundus. The variation in reading and case does not alter the description of green color.

Al-Muḥarrar al-Wajīz: nominative khuḍr in Ḥafṣ’s reading, modifying thiyāb

These uses matter because greenness is not restricted to plants or living things. A green garment need not possess biological freshness or be growing.

Khuḍr therefore means green directly here. Beauty, comfort, and paradisal blessing emerge from the descriptions as a whole and should not all enter the lexical definition of the color.

F. Green rafraf — Al-Raḥmān 55:76

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ رَفْرَفٍ خُضْرٍ وَعَبْقَرِيٍّ حِسَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā rafrafin khuḍrin wa-ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Reclining upon green rafraf and beautiful ʿabqarī furnishings.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:76

Khuḍrin describes rafraf. Explanations include coverings, spreads, or furnishings for comfort, but that noun requires its own lexical study.

Khuḍr directly conveys the rafraf’s green color.

Together with the garment verses, this shows green used for some paradisal coverings and furnishings.

Such uses suit the passages’ blessings and beauty. The root itself nevertheless establishes neither a fixed religious or moral value for green in every setting nor that all paradisal clothing must be green.

5. Semantic boundaries and neighboring roots

KH Ḍ R (khaḍira) compared with N Ḍ R (naḍara)

ن ض ر concerns freshness, vitality, and pleasing radiance in the Qur’an. Al-Insān 76:11, for example, mentions naḍratan wa-surūran, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:22 describes faces as nāḍirah.

These uses concern freshness or pleasing appearance without necessarily involving green.

خ ض ر, however, fundamentally denotes green and also accompanies freshness in plants.

The roots can therefore approach one another in describing fresh vegetation without being complete synonyms.

Khaḍiran in Al-Anʿām 6:99 can be green, fresh vegetation. For thiyāban khuḍran in Al-Kahf 18:31, however, green garments is clearer and more precise than fresh garments.

KH Ḍ R (khaḍira) compared with KH Ḍ D (khaḍada)

خ ض د concerns bending, breaking, or cutting branches and removing thorns. In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28, sidrin makhḍūdin describes the sidr tree.

خ ض ر instead concerns green, with freshness and growth in botanical uses.

Both roots may concern trees and branches, and Arabic records cutting-related uses in both families. Shared referents or certain verbal uses do not make them one root.

Makhḍūd in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 does not express the sidr’s greenness, just as akhḍar in Yā Sīn 36:80 does not express the tree’s being cut.

Study of KH Ḍ D

KH Ḍ R (khaḍira) compared with D H M (dahama)

Shortly before the green rafraf, Al-Raḥmān describes two gardens:

مُدْهَامَّتَانِ

mudhāmmatāni

Proposed translation: “Two deeply dark, richly colored gardens.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:64

Mudhāmmatāni comes from د ه م, rather than خ ض ر. It indicates intense darkness and can evoke dense, very dark greenness when describing gardens.

This accords with lexical accounts of lush green approaching darkness, but does not make the word a derivative of خ ض ر.

Nor does it establish black as the necessary meaning of khuḍr in the garment or grain verses, each of which has its own context.

Is Khiḍr a Qur’anic name?

Exegetical traditions commonly call the person whom Moses meets in Al-Kahf Khiḍr, but Al-Kahf 18:65 introduces him as:

فَوَجَدَا عَبْدًا مِنْ عِبَادِنَا

fa-wajadā ʿabdan min ʿibādinā

Proposed translation of the excerpt: “Then they found a servant among Our servants.”

The Qur’anic text does not name this person Khiḍr. This traditional name should therefore not count as a textual occurrence of خ ض ر in the Qur’an.

The historical explanation of that name and associated reports is separate from the root’s Qur’anic meaning.

6. Interpretation challenges and translation proposals for Forqan

The central translation issue is distinguishing color, the plant’s condition, and contextual meaning.

Translating every occurrence merely as freshness loses green in the garment and tree verses. Considering only green color everywhere, however, weakens the particular senses of khaḍiran for fresh growth and mukhḍarratan for land made green.

Conversely, older dictionaries’ occasional use for dark or nearly black shades does not justify translating all Qur’anic green as black without clear contextual evidence.

Suggested equivalents are:

خَضِرًا — Al-Anʿām 6:99: “green vegetation” or “green, fresh growth.”

خُضْرٍ — Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46: “green,” describing ears of grain.

خُضْرًا — Al-Kahf 18:31: “green,” describing garments.

مُخْضَرَّةً — Al-Ḥajj 22:63: “green” or, naturally in a sentence, “the earth becomes green.”

الْأَخْضَرِ — Yā Sīn 36:80: “green,” describing the tree.

خُضْرٍ — Al-Raḥmān 55:76: “green,” describing rafraf.

خُضْرٌ — Al-Insān 76:21: “green,” modifying thiyābu in Ḥafṣ’s reading.

A brief root summary may state:

Brief root meaning: Greenness and the color green; in botanical usage, freshness and vitality.

This preserves color as the principal meaning and its connection with plant condition, without imposing vitality, life, abundance, or beauty on every derivative.

Conclusion

The Arabic root خ ض ر fundamentally concerns green. Since green plants commonly accompany water, freshness, and growth, some botanical uses also encompass vitality and freshness.

Early Arabic color naming did not always follow modern boundaries. Khuḍrah sometimes described very dark green or nearly black colors; some derived verbs also extended into cutting fresh plants, agriculture, and transactions. These are valid lexical uses, but not all belong to every Qur’anic word.

The Qur’an uses the family for green growth, land made green, green ears, green trees, and green coverings and furnishings. Growth is evident in Al-Anʿām 6:99 and Al-Ḥajj 22:63; green contrasts with dryness in Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46; paradisal garments and rafraf instead involve the color of things not necessarily alive.

The basic meaning should therefore be greenness and the color green, with freshness and vitality accompanying botanical uses.

This distinction preserves lexical meaning while recognizing each verse’s role in describing growth, blessings, or objects’ outward appearance. Brief root meaning: Greenness and the color green; in botanical usage, freshness and vitality.
