Qur'anic Root

Qur'anic Root M R A

The human person, the human woman, narrative relations, and individual responsibility by which a person is known through heart, earning, speech, and action.

Core Meaning

The main branch of م ر أ indicates the human person as a definite, recognizable, and responsible individual.

مَرْء and امْرُؤ refer to a human being or person, and in some contexts to a man. امْرَأَة is a human woman, and in marital contexts it may be translated as a person's woman or wife. مَرِيء points to something pleasant, wholesome, and compatible; something that agrees with the receiver without harm or difficulty.

In Qur'anic usage, mar' is first a person whose heart, earning, action, and responsibility belong to that person. Imra'ah too is first a woman and a human person before she is presented as the counterpart of someone else.

Important Forms

This family is not morphologically simple. The forms امْرُؤ, امْرَأ, and امْرِئ are not three separate words; they are case forms of one noun: امْرُؤ in the nominative, امْرَأ in the accusative, and امْرِئ in the genitive.

امْرَأَة is the feminine form of imru' and originally indicates a woman. In combinations such as the woman of Nuh, the woman of Pharaoh, or imra'atuhu, relation to a man is also specified; depending on context it may be translated as “his woman” or “his wife.”

Mar'; The Human Person

مَرْء means a human being or person and should not be narrowed to “man” in every occurrence.

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَحُولُ بَيْنَ الْمَرْءِ وَقَلْبِهِ

And know that The God comes between the person and his heart.

Al-Anfal 8:24

The subject of the verse is the relation of every human being to the heart. Translating mar' as “man” unnecessarily narrows the scope of the verse.

يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ الْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ

The day when the person flees from his brother.

Abasa 80:34

Mar' here is an individual placed among the closest family relations, yet at the moment of answerability the person faces his own matter.

Imru', Imra', and Imri'

امْرُؤ is the nominative form of this noun:

إِنِ امْرُؤٌ هَلَكَ لَيْسَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ

If a person dies without having a child.

Al-Nisa 4:176

In this verse, imru' is the subject of the clause and is in the nominative case.

امْرَأ is the accusative form of the same noun:

مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ امْرَأَ سَوْءٍ

Your father was not a bad man.

Maryam 19:28

Because the reference is to the father, “man” is natural here. The change from imru' to imra' comes from grammatical position, not from a change in root or core meaning.

امْرِئ is the genitive form of the same noun:

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ مَا اكْتَسَبَ مِنَ الْإِثْمِ

For each person among them is what he acquired of sin.

Al-Nur 24:11

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ بِمَا كَسَبَ رَهِينٌ

Each person is held in pledge by what he earned.

Al-Tur 52:21

These uses clarify the individual core of the word: each imri' is known and held responsible by his own earning.

Imra'ah; Human Woman

امْرَأَة means a human woman.

وَإِنِ امْرَأَةٌ خَافَتْ مِنْ بَعْلِهَا نُشُوزًا أَوْ إِعْرَاضًا

And if a woman fears discord or turning away from her husband.

Al-Nisa 4:128

In this verse, imra'ah itself is simply “a woman.” The marital relation is made clear by بَعْلِهَا. Therefore the base meaning of imra'ah is “woman,” not that it inherently means wife.

امْرَأَتُهُ means his woman, and in a marital context, his wife.

وَامْرَأَتُهُ حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ

And his woman, carrier of firewood.

Al-Masad 111:4

The pronoun “his” relates the woman to Abu Lahab, but the description حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ states her own role and responsibility.

Mari'; Wholesome and Compatible

مَرِيء is used for something wholesome, agreeable, and harmless:

فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا

Then consume it pleasantly and wholesomely.

Al-Nisa 4:4

This branch is more distant in meaning from mar' and imra'ah. Some Arabic lexicons also register muru'ah and mari' in this same material, but this lexical grouping by itself does not prove that all the forms arose from one simple material image. Ibn Faris also treats this material as irregular and identifies imra'ah as the feminine of imru'.

Linguistic Note

The main core of م ر أ in the branch of mar' and imra'ah is human particularity: a human being who can be pointed to, recognized apart from others, and have action and responsibility attributed to himself or herself.

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ بِمَا كَسَبَ رَهِينٌ

Each person is held in pledge by what he earned.

Al-Tur 52:21

Mar' in this verse is defined by the person's own earning, not by another's faith, covering denial, status, or family relation.

Imra'ah too is originally a human woman. When she is attached to the name of a man, as in the woman of Nuh or the woman of Pharaoh, that relation identifies her; it does not remove her moral responsibility from herself.

Therefore two matters must be distinguished: narrative and social relation, where the woman is introduced through a known relation; and moral dependency, where her faith, covering denial, or outcome would automatically follow the man. The Qur'an uses narrative relation, but rejects moral dependency.

Material Image

The branch of mar' and imra'ah does not have a material image like cutting, fire, or movement. Its tangible image is a person standing among many relations without dissolving into any of them: the person and his heart; the person and his brother; the person and his parents; the person and his companion of life; the person and his children; the person and his own earning.

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ

For each person among them that day is a matter that will occupy him.

Abasa 80:37

The relations exist, but each person faces his own matter and answerability.

In the branch of mari', the material image is clearer: food enters the body and agrees with it without pain or incompatible effect. One may consider the possibility that compatibility with the person or his nature is present in this branch, but this relation should not be presented as the certain origin of mar' or imra'ah.

Classical Usage

In classical Arabic, imru' is one of the irregular nouns whose ending changes with grammatical case. Imra'ah is the feminine of imru'. Lexical sources also record forms such as mar'ah and marrah in some usages.

Introducing people through family or social relations was common in Arabic: father of so-and-so, mother of so-and-so, woman of so-and-so, child of so-and-so, holder of a certain station, or someone from a certain people. This mode did not necessarily mean that the person's whole identity was dependent on that relation; the relation functioned as an identifier.

Claims about a direct connection between M R A and Akkadian or Aramaic words meaning child, lord, or possessor do not have enough clear support for this article. Sound similarity among Semitic words is not enough to establish common origin and primary meaning, so it is better not to bring those claims into the main discussion.

The Person in Relation to the Heart

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَحُولُ بَيْنَ الْمَرْءِ وَقَلْبِهِ

And know that The God comes between the person and his heart.

Al-Anfal 8:24

Mar' here is not a specifically male title; it is the human being in relation to the heart and the center of decision. This verse is among the strongest evidence for translating mar' as “person” or “human being.”

The Person Before Family Bonds

يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ الْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ ۝ وَأُمِّهِ وَأَبِيهِ ۝ وَصَاحِبَتِهِ وَبَنِيهِ

The day when the person flees from his brother, his mother and father, his companion of life, and his children.

Abasa 80:34-36

Mar' stands at the center of a network of relations, but no relation transfers his responsibility to another.

Each Person Held by His Own Earning

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ بِمَا كَسَبَ رَهِينٌ

Each person is held in pledge by what he earned.

Al-Tur 52:21

This verse is fundamental for understanding all uses of imra'ah. A woman or man may be identified through another person, but each remains held by his or her own earning.

Each Individual's Share of Sin

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ مَا اكْتَسَبَ مِنَ الْإِثْمِ

For each person among them is what he acquired of sin.

Al-Nur 24:11

Even within a collective sin, the verse does not flatten responsibility across everyone without distinction. Each individual has a share according to his own participation and earning.

Imra'ah as Woman Before Marital Relation

وَإِنِ امْرَأَةٌ خَافَتْ مِنْ بَعْلِهَا نُشُوزًا أَوْ إِعْرَاضًا

And if a woman fears discord or turning away from her husband.

Al-Nisa 4:128

Imra'ah introduces the woman; ba'luha explains her relation to the husband. This structure shows that imra'ah does not inherently mean “belonging to a husband.” She is first a woman, and then the sentence states her relation to the husband.

Imra'ah and Moral Independence in Surah Al-Tahrim

ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا امْرَأَتَ نُوحٍ وَامْرَأَتَ لُوطٍ

The God set forth an example for those who covered truth: the woman of Nuh and the woman of Lut.

Al-Tahrim 66:10

وَضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا امْرَأَتَ فِرْعَوْنَ

And The God set forth an example for those who had faith: the woman of Pharaoh.

Al-Tahrim 66:11

These two verses establish a clear principle: sharing a household with a prophet does not produce faith; sharing a household with a tyrant does not erase faith. A woman and a man may move in different ethical and creedal directions. The marital relation does not transfer responsibility or outcome.

The function of “the woman of Nuh” and “the woman of Pharaoh” is not merely to record marital status; the relation to the husband is part of the argument of the verse. The woman of Nuh, despite nearness to Nuh, chooses another path, and the woman of Pharaoh, despite nearness to Pharaoh, chooses the path of faith.

The Difference Between Imra'ah and Zawj

Zawj indicates one of two parts of a pair and the relation of being paired or joined. Either side can be the zawj of the other. In Arabic, the woman may be called the zawj of the man, and the man the zawj of the woman.

Imra'ah focuses on the woman herself as a female individual. So imra'ah means woman as person, while zawj means one side of a pair and a bond.

This difference can be meaningful in the Qur'an's choice of words, but it should not be turned into a mechanical rule.

The claim that the Qur'an always uses imra'ah when there is discord, barrenness, or separation, and always uses zawj when there is harmony, does not fit all occurrences. For example, the woman of Ibrahim is still called imra'atuhu in a family and companionate setting:

فَأَقْبَلَتِ امْرَأَتُهُ فِي صَرَّةٍ

Then his woman came forward with a cry.

Al-Dhariyat 51:29

The woman of Imran also speaks in prayer, vow, and personal responsibility, not in a context proving discord with Imran.

Therefore the more precise distinction is this: zawj highlights the angle of relation and pairing. Imra'ah highlights the woman herself, her speech, action, or responsibility. Sometimes this choice is accompanied by harmony or divergence between woman and man, but the word alone does not prove the presence or absence of harmony.

The Shift from Imra'ah to Zawj in the Story of Zakariyya

In Zakariyya's supplication, the wording is:

وَكَانَتِ امْرَأَتِي عَاقِرًا

And my woman was barren.

Maryam 19:5

And after the supplication is answered:

وَأَصْلَحْنَا لَهُ زَوْجَهُ

And We set right his zawj for him.

Al-Anbiya 21:90

This shift is notable. In the first supplication, Zakariyya speaks of the condition of his woman and her barrenness, so the woman herself and her bodily condition stand at the center of the sentence. In the second verse, after setting right, her relation to Zakariyya and the completion of the possibility of childbearing are highlighted, so zawj appears.

This example shows that the choice between imra'ah and zawj can shift the angle of meaning, but one example cannot be turned into an absolute rule for every Qur'anic use.

The Woman of Al-Aziz and the Shift of Identifier in the Story of Yusuf

In Yusuf 12:23, the Qur'an does not say “the woman of al-Aziz.” It says:

وَرَاوَدَتْهُ الَّتِي هُوَ فِي بَيْتِهَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ

And the one in whose house he was sought him from himself.

Yusuf 12:23

This identifier highlights the house and the relation of power: Yusuf is in her house; the woman possesses the space in which the event unfolds; she closes the doors; and the spatial and household power is part of the pressure of the scene.

Later, the women of the city say:

امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ تُرَاوِدُ فَتَاهَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ

The woman of al-Aziz seeks her young man from himself.

Yusuf 12:30

Here her social identifier is highlighted: the woman of a powerful official whose behavior has become the subject of public speech in the city.

So in one story the Qur'an can introduce one woman through different identifiers: the one in whose house Yusuf was, from the angle of position and power inside the house; and the woman of al-Aziz, from the angle of social station and public conversation.

The shift of identifier does not create a new moral identity; it highlights the narrative angle and a part of the relation present in that scene.

Imra'atuhu in Surah Al-Masad

وَامْرَأَتُهُ حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ

And his woman, carrier of firewood.

Al-Masad 111:4

At the direct linguistic level, imra'atuhu is the woman related to Abu Lahab, and in the marital context it points to his woman or wife.

But the judgment about her is not produced by being Abu Lahab's wife. The verse immediately states her own role: حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ, carrier of firewood.

So the structure of the verse is not that Abu Lahab is evil and his guilt or description is transferred to the woman. Each enters the surah through his or her own action and role: Abu Lahab through his hands, wealth, and earning; his woman through carrying firewood.

At a reflective level, this description may be understood as “structural participation in kindling the fire”: she is not only a family companion; she has an active role in providing the material, condition, or continuation of the fire. But this is a reflective extension of the image “carrier of firewood.” The precise meaning of carrying firewood should be studied separately in the roots H M L and H T B.

Historical Person and Recurring Type

A Qur'anic person can be both a person in an event and a type of recurring behavior.

In Surah Al-Masad, Abu Lahab can stand as an example of someone whose power, wealth, and earning enter the path of tabab. His woman too can stand as an example of someone who actively carries the material and ground for the continuation of that same fire.

But this typological function is understood from the whole surah and from their descriptions, not from imra'ah lexically meaning the feminine form of a behavioral pattern.

So the careful reading is this: imra'atuhu at the direct level is his woman; at the reflective level, this woman, because of her independent role, can become a recurring image of participation in a fire-kindling and destructive structure.

Distinguishing Similar Uses

Mar' as person and imru' as man: Mar' and imru' originally indicate a human person, but context may clarify gender. In مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ امْرَأَ سَوْءٍ, because the speech concerns a father, “man” is suitable. In يَحُولُ بَيْنَ الْمَرْءِ وَقَلْبِهِ, “person” or “human being” is more precise.

Imra'ah as woman and imra'ah as wife: Imra'ah by itself is woman. When it is attached to a man and the context clarifies a marital relation, it may be translated as “his woman” or “his wife.” “Woman” preserves the form of the word more closely; “wife” clarifies the kind of relation.

Imra'ah and zawj: Imra'ah highlights the woman as an individual. Zawj highlights the relation of pairing. But imra'ah is not always a sign of discord, and zawj is not always a sign of complete harmony. The choice depends on the angle of speech, the subject of the sentence, and the relation being highlighted.

Relation to the husband and personal independence: Identifying a woman through her husband does not mean taking away her identity and responsibility. In Surah Al-Tahrim, those very relations are used to prove moral independence.

Personal name and narrative identifier: The Qur'an does not always introduce individuals by personal name. It may introduce someone through family relation, house, social station, people, action, or a special description. Each identifier highlights part of the message of the scene.

Mari' and mar': Mari' relates to compatibility and wholesomeness; mar' relates to the human person. Both are recorded in one lexical material, but one should not say without enough evidence that mari' necessarily means what is compatible with “personhood.” This connection can be reflective and possible, not a fixed definition of the root.

Grammar and Structure

The changing form of imru':

إِنِ امْرُؤٌ هَلَكَ

If a person dies.

Al-Nisa 4:176

Imru' is in the nominative case.

مَا كَانَ أَبُوكِ امْرَأَ سَوْءٍ

Your father was not a bad man.

Maryam 19:28

Imra' is in the accusative case.

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ

For each person among them.

Al-Nur 24:11

Imri' is in the genitive case. These differences are grammatical and should not be turned into differences in root meaning.

Construct relation in “the woman of Nuh”: In the construction “the woman of Nuh,” imra'ah means woman, and Nuh is the person through whose relation this woman is identified. The exact kind of relation is clarified by context. The construct relation alone does not state the woman's whole identity.

The pronoun in imra'atuhu: In وَامْرَأَتُهُ, the pronoun “his” returns to Abu Lahab. The direct meaning is “his woman.”

The structure allati huwa fi baytiha:

الَّتِي هُوَ فِي بَيْتِهَا

This structure, instead of a name or marital relation, highlights a spatial relation: the woman in whose house Yusuf was. بَيْتِهَا also reveals the woman's position in the scene and her power over the closed space of the house.

Hammalata al-hatab:

وَامْرَأَتُهُ حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ

حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ is an independent description or state of the woman. This phrase itself shows that mentioning her is not merely an attachment to Abu Lahab's name; a specific role is attributed to her.

Nearby Roots and Terms

M R A and A N S / insan: Insan is the general name for the human being. Mar' emphasizes a definite person and individual in a particular situation: insan is the type or human creature; mar' is the human person being pointed to.

M R A and B Sh R: Bashar emphasizes bodily appearance and visible human form. Mar' focuses on the person, the heart, earning, and individual responsibility.

M R A and N F S: Nafs indicates the self, soul, or inward identity. Mar' is the person to whom nafs, heart, relations, and earning are attributed.

Imra'ah and untha: Untha specifies the female sex in contrast with dhakar and is used for humans and other creatures. Imra'ah is a human woman and points to a female person within human and social space.

Imra'ah and nisa': Imra'ah is the singular woman. Nisa' is women in the plural, but it is not the regular plural built from the same form as imra'ah.

Imra'ah and zawj: Zawj is one of the two sides of a pair and emphasizes the relation of being paired. Imra'ah highlights the woman herself as a female person.

Imra'ah and sahibah: Sahibah emphasizes companionship and accompanying. Imra'ah indicates the person's womanhood. Both may in a context point to a companion of life, but their semantic angles are not the same.

Mari' and hani': Hani' emphasizes pleasantness and ease in receiving or consuming. Mari' indicates compatibility and absence of harmful consequence. In هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا, one highlights pleasantness, and the other compatibility and wholesomeness.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For مَرْء: person, human being, human individual.

For امْرُؤ, امْرَأ, and امْرِئ, depending on context: person, individual, human being, or man when gender is clear from the context.

For كُلُّ امْرِئٍ بِمَا كَسَبَ رَهِينٌ: Each person is held in pledge by what he earned; each individual is held by what he acquired; each human being is pledged by the result of his own action.

For امْرَأَة: woman, a woman, that woman.

For امْرَأَتُهُ: his woman; or his wife, in a context where the kind of relation must be made clear.

For امْرَأَةُ نُوح: the woman of Nuh; or the wife of Nuh. For Forqan, “the woman of Nuh” better preserves the direct structure of the word and the person's independence.

For امْرَأَةُ فِرْعَوْن: the woman of Pharaoh; or the wife of Pharaoh.

For وَامْرَأَتُهُ حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ: and his woman, carrier of firewood; and his woman, carrying firewood; and his woman who keeps carrying firewood. “His woman” is closer to the direct form of the word than “his wife,” but the independence of the woman's responsibility is understood from the description hammalata al-hatab, not only from the choice of “woman.”

For مَرِيء: wholesome, agreeable, harmless, compatible in consumption.

For هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا: pleasantly and wholesomely; with ease and compatibility; pleasant and harmless.

Summary

The root م ر أ, in its main branch, indicates the human person as a definite, recognizable, and responsible individual. Mar', imru', imra', and imri' belong to one family, and their visible differences mostly come from grammatical position. Imra'ah is a human woman, and in construct phrases it may identify the woman or wife of the named person.

Qur'anic usage shows that family relation can serve as an identifier and as part of a verse's argument, but it does not transfer moral responsibility. The woman of Nuh is not saved by Nuh's righteousness; the woman of Pharaoh is not condemned by Pharaoh's wrongdoing; and the woman of Abu Lahab is introduced through her own description and role.

The difference between imra'ah and zawj is real but not mechanical. Imra'ah focuses on the woman herself and her particularity; zawj emphasizes the relation of pairing and one side of a bond. This difference is sometimes accompanied by divergence or harmony, but one cannot say every imra'ah is discordant and every zawj is harmonious.

In Surah Al-Masad, imra'atuhu directly points to the woman of Abu Lahab. Her condemnation does not arise from the marital relation; the description حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ expresses her independent participation in the scene. At a reflective level, she can become a recurring image of structural participation in fire-kindling, but that layer should not replace the direct meaning of imra'ah.

The distinctive core of the main branch of M R A is the human person before being reduced to family relations: an individual known and answerable through his or her own heart, earning, speech, and action.
