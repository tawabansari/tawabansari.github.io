Qur’anic Root A R B

Need, purpose, and practical use: from Moses’ maʾārib to men’s irbah

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مَآرِبُ
(maʾāribu)
Qur’anic nominative plural noun: Ṭā Hā 20:18; practical uses and needs

الْإِرْبَةِ
(al-irbati)
Qur’anic feminine genitive noun: Al-Nūr 24:31; need, here sexual desire

مَأْرَبَة
(maʾrabah)
Lexical singular of maʾārib; this form is absent from the Qur’an

أَرَب / إِرْب
(arab / irb)
Lexical forms for need, desire, or purpose; not Qur’anic

أَرِبَ
(ariba)
Lexical Form I verb, discussed through the poem and the senses of need and skill

أَرِيب
(arīb)
Lexical adjective: shrewd, competent, or skilled; not Qur’anic

مُؤَارَبَة
(muʾārabah)
Lexical Form III verbal noun: resourcefulness and shrewd dealings; not Qur’anic

إِرْب / آرَاب
(irb / ārāb)
Lexical member and bodily members; not the meaning of irbah in Al-Nūr

أُرْبَة
(urbah)
Lexical noun for a share; this form is absent from the Qur’an

تَأْرِيب
(taʾrīb)
Lexical Form II verbal noun: tightening or making firm; not Qur’anic

The Arabic root A R B concerns a need, desire, or purpose someone seeks to fulfill. Sometimes this is a direct need; sometimes it involves something done to achieve a purpose. Other Arabic uses extend to shrewdness, skill, a share, and even bodily members. These meanings should nevertheless not all be derived from a single image or definition without historical examination.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an: maʾāribu in Ṭā Hā 20:18 and al-irbati in Al-Nūr 24:31. Both concern need and purpose, but their settings differ. Moses speaks of things he does with his staff, whereas Al-Nūr mentions men who have no sexual need or desire in relation to women.

These occurrences show how a root can have different applications in different settings without requiring a wholly independent lexical meaning for each application.

1. The semantic core and the root’s forms

The recorded Arabic forms have several branches. In the branch of need and purpose, arab, irb, irbah, and maʾrabah denote a need, desire, or purpose. Maʾārib is the plural of maʾrabah and denotes needs, purposes, or tasks for which a means is employed.

Lā araba lī fī hādhā means that I have no need or interest in this and pursue no purpose in it. The verb ariba ilā al-shayʾi is also recorded for needing or wanting something.

In another branch, arīb describes someone shrewd, knowledgeable about affairs, or skilled. Muʾārabah can concern resourcefulness and shrewdness in dealing with someone else. This branch does not invariably carry the negative sense of deception: competence in understanding and managing affairs also belongs to its usage.

Irb can also denote a bodily member, with the plural ārāb. Some early uses have urbah for a share of something. Taʾrīb and related verbs are also recorded for tightening a knot or making something firm.

These lexical branches matter because they show that A R B extends beyond need. The Qur’anic meanings of maʾārib and irbah should nevertheless not be constructed by combining all these branches at once.

The Qur’an contains only the two noun forms maʾāribu and al-irbati. The root’s verbs, arīb, muʾārabah, ārāb, and the other derivatives mentioned here do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

2. Early Arabic usage: need, resourcefulness, and purpose

In early Arabic, arab could denote a need drawing someone toward something or prompting action to attain it.

An Arabic proverb states:

مَأْرَبَةٌ لَا حَفَاوَةٌ

maʾrabatun lā ḥafāwatun

“A need is involved, rather than affection and warm attention.”

This describes someone visiting another person because of a need, rather than simply out of friendship and concern. Maʾrabah denotes the purpose prompting the visit.

Poetry attributed to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm, an Arabic poet of the pre-Islamic period, also contains:

أَرِبْتُ بِدَفْعِ الْحَرْبِ حَتَّى رَأَيْتُهَا
عَنِ الدَّفْعِ لَا تَزْدَادُ غَيْرَ تَقَارُبِ

aribtu bi-dafʿi al-ḥarbi ḥattā raʾaytuhā / ʿani al-dafʿi lā tazdādu ghayra taqārubi

“I sought to avert war, until I saw that, despite efforts to drive it away, it only drew closer.”

This verse is recorded in Qays’ collected poetry, although its precise date of composition is unclear. Aribtu here concerns seeking and contriving to avert war. Lexical accounts sometimes explain the use through need, and sometimes through skill and management. The collected poetry has ḥattā raʾaytuhā / ʿani al-dafʿi; some lexical sources, including Maqāyīs, transmit lammā raʾaytuhā / ʿalā al-dafʿi. These textual differences do not remove the witness’s relevance to the discussion of need and skill.

This overlap shows that need and resourcefulness can approach one another in particular uses: someone seeks a way and means to achieve a purpose. It does not make need equivalent to cunning or shrewdness in every occurrence.

Uses concerning a share or a bodily member also require caution. Irb for a member and ārāb for members do not by themselves establish that need, intellect, a member, and a firm knot all originated in one image. These are recorded branches within a lexical family, but their historical order and precise relationships are not all equally clear.

For the two Qur’anic occurrences, the branch of need and purpose has the more direct and clearer support.

3. Maʾārib in the account of Moses’ staff

In Ṭā Hā, The God asks Moses what is in his right hand. Moses replies:

قَالَ هِيَ عَصَايَ أَتَوَكَّؤُا عَلَيْهَا وَأَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي وَلِيَ فِيهَا مَآرِبُ أُخْرَىٰ

qāla hiya ʿaṣāya atawakkaʾu ʿalayhā wa-ahushshu bihā ʿalā ghanamī wa-liya fīhā maʾāribu ukhrā

“He said, ‘This is my staff. I lean on it and use it to beat down leaves for my sheep, and I do other things with it as well.’”

Ṭā Hā 20:18

Maʾāribu is the plural of maʾrabah here. Moses first names two specific uses of the staff: leaning on it and beating down leaves for his sheep. Wa-liya fīhā maʾāribu ukhrā then refers to further uses he does not list individually.

In this setting, maʾārib is more than inward, abstract needs. It concerns tasks the staff helps accomplish. “Uses,” “practical benefits,” and “necessary tasks” therefore all approach the phrase’s semantic field.

The word itself nevertheless identifies no particular additional use. Maʾāribu ukhrā does not establish that Moses necessarily used the staff for defense, carrying loads, crossing water, or any other specific task. Such activities may be familiar uses of a staff, but the verse does not name them.

The sentence’s construction also matters. Liya relates the maʾārib to Moses, and fīhā connects these needs and uses to the staff. The phrase therefore means more than “I have other needs”: Moses has other tasks for that very staff, or fulfills other needs through it.

Ukhrā is feminine singular despite the plural maʾārib. This is an ordinary Arabic construction for a nonhuman plural and does not mean that Moses had only one additional need.

The next verse commands Moses to cast down the staff, bringing that familiar object into the scene of a divine sign. This connection matters for the account without changing the lexical meaning of maʾārib. In Moses’ answer, the word still concerns his practical tasks and purposes, rather than the miracle that follows.

4. Irbah in Al-Nūr: need in relation to women

The root’s second occurrence is in Al-Nūr 24:31:

وَقُل لِّلْمُؤْمِنَاتِ يَغْضُضْنَ مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِنَّ وَيَحْفَظْنَ فُرُوجَهُنَّ وَلَا يُبْدِينَ زِينَتَهُنَّ إِلَّا مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَلْيَضْرِبْنَ بِخُمُرِهِنَّ عَلَىٰ جُيُوبِهِنَّ وَلَا يُبْدِينَ زِينَتَهُنَّ إِلَّا لِبُعُولَتِهِنَّ أَوْ آبَائِهِنَّ أَوْ آبَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ أَوْ أَبْنَائِهِنَّ أَوْ أَبْنَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ أَوْ إِخْوَانِهِنَّ أَوْ بَنِي إِخْوَانِهِنَّ أَوْ بَنِي أَخَوَاتِهِنَّ أَوْ نِسَائِهِنَّ أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُنَّ أَوِ التَّابِعِينَ غَيْرِ أُولِي الْإِرْبَةِ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ أَوِ الطِّفْلِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ وَلَا يَضْرِبْنَ بِأَرْجُلِهِنَّ لِيُعْلَمَ مَا يُخْفِينَ مِنْ زِينَتِهِنَّ وَتُوبُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا أَيُّهَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

wa-qul li-al-muʾmināti yaghḍuḍna min abṣārihinna wa-yaḥfaẓna furūjahunna wa-lā yubdīna zīnatahunna illā mā ẓahara minhā wa-l-yaḍribna bi-khumurihinna ʿalā juyūbihinna wa-lā yubdīna zīnatahunna illā li-buʿūlatihinna aw ābāʾihinna aw ābāʾi buʿūlatihinna aw abnāʾihinna aw abnāʾi buʿūlatihinna aw ikhwānihinna aw banī ikhwānihinna aw banī akhawātihinna aw nisāʾihinna aw mā malakat aymānuhunna awi al-tābiʿīna ghayri ulī al-irbati mina al-rijāli awi al-ṭifli alladhīna lam yaẓharū ʿalā ʿawrāti al-nisāʾi wa-lā yaḍribna bi-arjulihinna li-yuʿlama mā yukhfīna min zīnatihinna wa-tūbū ilā allāhi jamīʿan ayyuha al-muʾminūna laʿallakum tufliḥūn

“Tell believing women to lower their gaze and guard their chastity, and not display their adornment except what is apparent of it. Let them draw their head coverings over their necklines and not display their adornment except to their husbands, their fathers, their husbands’ fathers, their sons, their husbands’ sons, their brothers, their brothers’ sons, their sisters’ sons, their women, those in their ownership, dependent male attendants who have no sexual need for women, or children not yet aware of women’s private matters. Let them not stamp their feet so that their hidden adornment becomes known. Believers, all of you, return to The God, so that you may prosper.”

Al-Nūr 24:31

The expression under discussion is awi al-tābiʿīna ghayri ulī al-irbati mina al-rijāl.

Ulī means possessors or holders. Ghayri ulī al-irbah thus describes people who do not possess irbah. What kind of need does irbah denote here?

The context supplies the answer. The verse concerns displaying adornment before specified people. The men under discussion are described as al-tābiʿīn: attendants or dependents associated with a household or group. Ghayri ulī al-irbah then distinguishes them from other men.

In this setting, irbah denotes sexual need or desire toward women. The natural meaning is therefore that these men have no sexual desire or attraction toward women in the matter under discussion.

The root alone does not supply this sexual meaning. Ṭā Hā 20:18 uses the same root for tasks involving a staff. Mina al-rijāl and the context of adornment and women’s privacy establish the sexual application of irbah in Al-Nūr 24:31.

Does lacking irbah mean physical incapacity?

Some early explanations restrict this group to men who are sexually incapable, elderly, cognitively limited, or belong to other categories. None of those descriptions, however, occurs in ghayri ulī al-irbah itself.

Lack of sexual desire is distinct from physical incapacity. Someone may be physically capable without sexual desire toward women. Conversely, inability to engage in sexual intercourse does not necessarily mean absence of sexual attraction or desire.

Ghayri ulī al-irbah does not by itself imply lack of intellect or cognitive ability either. Although irb concerns intellect and shrewdness in another lexical branch, Al-Nūr’s context is not a test of men’s intelligence or mental ability.

These branches should therefore not be confused: lacking irbah does not establish that someone lacks reason, understanding, or skill.

After this group, the verse names children with a separate description: alladhīna lam yaẓharū ʿalā ʿawrāti al-nisāʾ. This concerns the children’s stage of awareness and recognition of women’s private matters. Dependent men without irbah and the children mentioned are thus two groups with distinct descriptions, although both relate to the verse through their position concerning adornment and women’s privacy.

Irbah does not mean a sexual organ

Arabic also records irb for a bodily member. This use should nevertheless not be transferred directly to Al-Nūr 24:31.

In context, ghayri ulī al-irbah does not mean men lacking a sexual organ. It expresses an absence of need or desire, rather than the absence of a particular bodily member.

The words’ different spellings and vocalizations, and their different semantic branches, must remain distinct in lexical research. Irb for a member and irbah for a need sharing a root does not make their applications identical in this verse.

“Men who have no sexual desire toward women” is consequently closer to the phrase’s general indication and context than translations restricting them to a particular medical, bodily, or cognitive category.

This conclusion clarifies the word’s semantic boundaries. Determining all the social and legal rulings concerning this group belongs to a wider study of the verse itself.

5. A R B in relation to ḥājah, shahwah, and raghbah

The Qur’anic branch of A R B is close to need, desire, and purpose, but this proximity does not make all these words identical.

A R B (arab) and Ḥ W J (ḥājah)

Study of Ḥ W J

Ḥājah denotes a need. Yūsuf 12:68 contains ḥājatan fī nafsi yaʿqūba: something within Jacob that he sought to fulfill. Al-Ḥashr 59:9 also has ḥājatan, describing an inward response to what others were given.

Arab can likewise denote a need, but many lexical uses foreground a desire or purpose someone pursues. Maʾrabah can therefore concern a task undertaken to achieve a purpose.

This does not establish an absolute rule that every ḥājah is passive and every arab involves a plan or resourcefulness. Lexicons often explain the two through one another, and their practical distinction is not always certain.

A R B (irbah) and SH H W (shahwah)

Study of SH H W

Shahwah concerns desire and attraction and can occur in a sexual setting, as in Al-Aʿrāf 7:81.

Irbah, however, is not necessarily sexual desire in its lexical meaning. It can denote a general need, referring to sexual need or desire only in an appropriate setting.

The important distinction is that shahwah itself emphasizes desire and attraction, while irbah can express a need or purpose someone seeks to fulfill. In Al-Nūr 24:31, their semantic fields approach one another because sexual need toward women is involved.

A R B (arab) and R GH B (raghbah)

Study of R GH B

Raghiba concerns inclination, desire, or turning toward something. Different prepositions can change its semantic direction.

Arab can likewise mean a desire or purpose, without necessarily denoting that same inward inclination. In Moses’ staff account, maʾārib concerns practical needs and tasks more than a feeling of attraction to the staff.

These are nevertheless tendencies. None of these roots should be defined so narrowly that valid Arabic uses are excluded.

6. Proposed translations for Forqan

A single fixed Persian equivalent is unsuitable for every lexical branch of A R B. In the two Qur’anic occurrences, “need” offers a sound common basis, while each context determines a more natural equivalent. The same contextual distinction matters in English.

In Ṭā Hā 20:18, the proposed translation of wa-liya fīhā maʾāribu ukhrā is:

“And I do other things with it as well.”

“And it has other uses for me” is also semantically sound. To make need and the fulfillment of purpose more explicit, one could say, “And I fulfill other needs with it too.” The first version, however, flows more naturally in Moses’ Persian reply and connects well with the staff’s two preceding uses.

In Al-Nūr 24:31, the proposed translation of al-tābiʿīna ghayri ulī al-irbati mina al-rijāl is:

“Dependent male attendants who have no sexual desire toward women.”

This specifies irbah’s general meaning through context without restricting the men to a particular medical, age, or cognitive category.

In other Arabic uses, arab can mean “need,” “desire,” or “purpose,” while arīb can be rendered “shrewd,” “competent,” or “skilled,” according to context. Irb in its bodily sense means a member, and ārāb can denote bodily members. These are valid lexical meanings but should not be presented as Qur’anic forms.

7. Conclusion

A R B has several semantic branches in Arabic. Both Qur’anic occurrences belong to need, desire, and purpose. Intellect and shrewdness, a share and a member, and tightening a knot are also recorded within the lexical family, but should not all be reduced to one hypothetical meaning or original image.

In Ṭā Hā 20:18, maʾāribu concerns Moses’ practical tasks and needs fulfilled through his staff. “Other uses” is therefore a natural and precise rendering in this setting.

In Al-Nūr 24:31, al-irbati concerns men’s relationship with women and denotes sexual need or desire. The absence of this need should not be equated with physical incapacity, lack of intellect, or absence of a bodily member.

Forqan’s final definition of A R B:

“In the principal Qur’anic branch, a need, desire, or purpose someone seeks to fulfill. In one setting, this need can concern a task and practical benefit; in another, sexual desire or need. Other lexical branches include shrewdness and skill, a share and a member, and making something tight or firm. Each requires its own evidence and should be examined separately from Qur’anic usage.”
