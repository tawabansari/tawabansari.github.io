Leaning, support, resting on something, and attributing speech to a source

The Arabic root S N D concerns leaning, resting one thing against another, support, and reliance. Physically, an object inclines toward and rests on another. Related usages include backing, assistance, citation, and attribution of speech to a speaker or source; these semantic connections alone do not establish the historical order of their emergence.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as musannadah in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4, comparing a group of hypocrites to pieces of wood propped against something.

Musannadah directly describes how the wood is positioned. In context, the image contrasts impressive appearance and engaging speech with actual character and conduct.

Having support is neither a virtue nor a fault in itself. Wood can lean against a wall, a person receive support, and a claim be attributed to evidence or a source. The type of reliance belongs to the root's field; its value and validity depend on the subject and context.

1. Early Arabic meaning and witnesses attributed to pre-Quranic times

Leaning, connection, and support

Early Arabic records sanada and related forms for leaning, relying, and resting one thing against another.

Sanada ilā al-shayʾ means leaning against something.

Asnada al-shayʾa ilā al-shayʾ means placing one thing against another or making it rest upon it.

Istanada ilayhi likewise means leaning on it or drawing support from it.

Two roles are involved: the thing leaning or being propped, and its support.

When wood is propped against a wall, their roles differ: the wood rests on the wall, which supports it.

Musnad and musannad therefore require attention to morphology and the roles of agent and object. Something propped against another need not itself support anything else.

Early lexical analysis also describes joining or placing one thing beside or upon another, illuminating the relationship between leaning and support.

This does not make every derivative physical. Supporting a person or attributing a statement establishes social or semantic relationships, distinct from wood leaning against a wall.

Sanad: an ascending mountainside

Sanad also denotes elevated ground rising along a mountain or a slope ascending from its foot.

Sanada fī al-jabal means climbing or ascending a mountain.

Its imagery concerns slope, elevation, and the mountain's structure.

The terrain noun and the act of propping remain distinct. Their recording under one root does not establish their precise historical relationship through imagery alone.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī begins a famous ode:

يَا دَارَ مَيَّةَ بِالْعَلْيَاءِ فَالسَّنَدِ
أَقْوَتْ وَطَالَ عَلَيْهَا سَالِفُ الْأَبَدِ

yā dāra mayyata bi-al-ʿalyāʾi fa-al-sanadi / aqwat wa-ṭāla ʿalayhā sālifu al-abadi

“O abode of Mayyah at al-ʿAlyāʾ and al-Sanad! It stands deserted, and a long span of time has passed over it.”

Al-sanad helps identify the dwelling's location.

Early lexical explanations connect it here to a rising mountainside; al-ʿAlyāʾ and al-Sanad may also name particular geographical locations in the poem.

The witness illuminates old geographical usage without independently dating a place name's derivation from the general terrain noun.

Nor does it establish mountain or ascent as the original sense of every derivative.

The ode survives in the collection attributed to al-Nābighah and in literary transmission. Recognized attribution remains distinct from independently dating each transmitted line.

It witnesses al-sanad in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times, while the broader field of leaning and support emerges from the range of recorded Arabic usages.

Sānada and mutual support

Sānada also means supporting, helping, or standing alongside another.

Sānada al-rajula means helping him or backing his undertaking.

Tasānadū can describe people relying on one another.

Physical support extends to human relationships: as an object may need support for stability, a person can need others' assistance in acting.

Support does not necessarily entail obedience, total agreement, or a worthy common purpose.

People can assist one another in good or wrongdoing. Moral value depends on the purpose and result, not the root itself.

Some early uses describe groups moving mutasānidīn under separate banners. The family cannot therefore be defined by a fixed rule of complete unity among all participants.

2. Lexical forms and Quranic usage

The root occurs once in the Quran:

Musannadah — Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

It is the feminine passive participle of Form II, sannada.

Sannada in this branch means propping something against another support.

Musannad describes something that has been propped up.

Musannadah is its feminine form, modifying khushub in the verse.

Khushub is a plural of khashabah, pieces of wood. Arabic nonhuman plurals can take a feminine singular adjective, so this agreement is regular.

The singular adjective does not mean that the verse describes only one piece of wood. The comparison concerns multiple pieces.

The passive participle describes wood that has been positioned against something; it does not itself identify load-bearing columns.

Other Arabic forms include sanad, sanada, asnada, istanada, sānada, tasānada, musnad, misnad, and isnād. These forms do not occur in the Quran.

Form II musannad must also be distinguished from Form IV passive participle musnad. Their meanings can overlap without identical morphology.

3. Ka-annahum khushubun musannadah in Al-Munāfiqūn

The sole occurrence is in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4:

وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ وَإِنْ يَقُولُوا تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ هُمُ الْعَدُوُّ فَاحْذَرْهُمْ قَاتَلَهُمُ اللَّهُ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

wa-idhā raʾaytahum tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum wa-in yaqūlū tasmaʿ li-qawlihim ka-annahum khushubun musannadatun yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim humu al-ʿaduwwu fa-ḥdharhum qātalahumu Allāhu annā yuʾfakūn

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

“When you see them, their bodies impress you, and if they speak, you listen to their words. They seem like pieces of wood propped up. They suppose every shout is against them. They are the enemy, so beware of them. May The God fight them; how they are turned away!”

Impressive appearance and engaging speech

The verse opens with two features.

First, their physical appearance attracts attention and admiration.

Second, their manner of speaking leads the listener to attend to their words.

Neither feature is objectionable in itself.

An attractive appearance or ability to speak is not proof of hypocrisy or corruption.

The criticism concerns the gap between their impressive exterior and their actual conduct.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:1 reports their spoken testimony to Muhammad's messengership while declaring them liars in that professed testimony; the criticism concerns the sincerity of their claim, not the truth of the messengership.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:2 says that they make their oaths a shield and obstruct God's way.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 thus concerns people whose engaging speech and impressive appearance conflict with their actual conduct, rather than judging beauty or eloquence alone.

Why propped pieces of wood?

Khushubun musannadah offers a tangible image.

Imagine pieces of wood leaning against a wall or another support.

They may be tall, thick, smooth, or impressive, but appearance alone does not reveal their present function or why they were placed there.

Two features matter: their outward stature and their propped position.

The verse names pieces of wood, not installed pillars or growing trees with roots and branches.

Propped wood can look firm while resting on something else.

In context, this pictures a gap between an impressive exterior and inward and practical reality.

Does musannadah mean useless?

Some interpretations explain the wood as set against a wall rather than put to use in a roof, wall, or useful implement.

This can illuminate an aspect of the image.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the comparison's implication.

Musannadah does not itself mean useless, hollow, dead, or unintelligent. It describes being propped against something.

The unfruitfulness of deceptive speech and appearance emerges from the complete comparison and the group's behavior.

The verse specifies no particular wall, storage place, wood species, or internal quality.

Wood against a wall is therefore an explanatory illustration, not a set of definite unstated details.

Does comparison with wood deny intelligence?

Wood does not speak, judge, or bear moral responsibility as humans do.

This can inform the comparison: impressive people lacking the insight and sincerity that should direct their conduct.

It should not become a biological or psychological claim that they lack intelligence or awareness.

The verse itself describes calculation and conduct: they use speech and suppose every shout is against them.

The criticism concerns character and responsibility, not brain structure or biological capacity.

4. Outward support and inward instability

After the comparison, the verse says:

يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ

yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

“They suppose every shout is against them.”

This reveals another aspect of the group's condition.

Their initial appearance may be firm and impressive, while the continuation depicts fear and suspicion that make every shout seem directed at them.

Outward appearance thus differs from inward security.

This fits the propped-wood image: apparent firmness does not itself demonstrate independence or stability.

The connection is contextual analysis. Musannadah alone does not mean fear, suspicion, or hypocrisy.

Fear of a sound or anxiety is not itself evidence of hypocrisy. The verse describes a particular group through a combination of features.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:5 says that when invited to come so that God's messenger may seek forgiveness for them, they turn their heads away and withdraw arrogantly.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7 and Al-Munāfiqūn 63:8 report withholding financial support from the messenger's companions and claims of superior social might.

The image belongs to a connected portrait: impressive appearance, engaging speech, inconsistent conduct, inward fear, arrogance, and attempts to harm the community.

These describe the group, not separate meanings to insert into the root.

Having support is not identical to genuine stability

Support can provide security in human life.

People may rely on family, friends, knowledge, experience, and cooperation.

Someone may instead rely only on appearance, position, fame, wealth, or others' backing while avoiding truth and responsibility.

This illustrates a broader application of the image.

Eloquent speech and social backing alone do not establish honesty or trustworthiness.

Judgment must consider claims, actions, and consequences beyond appearances.

This does not condemn every dependence. People need one another, and cooperation can be entirely commendable.

The verse addresses hypocrisy and inconsistency, not support as such.

5. Sanad, isnād, and the development of support terminology

A familiar modern Persian and Arabic use of sanad is a document or record offered to establish or register a claim.

It relates to support and reliance.

Just as an object rests on another, acceptance of a claim can rest on a document, witness, or source.

This semantic connection does not make Quranic musannadah mean documented or supported by records.

Attributing speech to its speaker

Asnada al-kalāma ilā qāʾilihi means attributing speech to its speaker.

It establishes a relationship between a statement and the person credited with it.

This is understandable within connection and reliance: a statement is attached to a speaker to identify its origin.

The act of attribution does not prove its accuracy.

A statement may genuinely come from the named person or be attributed mistakenly or falsely.

Attribution and authentication are different.

Isnād in hadith terminology

In hadith terminology, isnād concerns relating a report through its transmitters to its speaker or earliest source. It can also denote the chain of transmitters, termed sanad.

This specialized usage developed within Islamic scholarly transmission and should not be confused with the Quranic adjective for propped wood.

A chain can show a claimed transmission route without proving the report's truth or every stage of its transmission.

Assessment considers transmitter reliability, continuity, wording, differing reports, and independent evidence.

Historical attribution and a report's authority as a basis for religious rulings are separate questions; the word isnād alone settles neither.

Hadith terminology therefore cannot determine the meaning of Quranic musannadah.

Sanad as a document

In administrative and legal usage, sanad can name a record of ownership, debt, agreement, or an event.

This too relates to evidential support.

Calling a text a document does not make it accurate, valid, or sufficient for its claim.

A document can be sound, incomplete, incorrect, or forged. Its status depends on evaluation and evidence rather than its name.

Establishing a fact need not depend exclusively on a written document: observation, experiments, reliable testimony, material traces, and other evidence can contribute in their appropriate settings.

The modern document sense is one kind of support for a claim, not the root's sole meaning or a synonym for established truth.

6. Comparison with related roots

S N D and ʿ M D: leaning and a pillar

ʿ M D concerns pillars, bases, and structural supports.

S N D in its reliance branch emphasizes resting upon or being supported by something.

A pillar can carry a roof, while wood against a wall need not support a building.

This matters for khushubun musannadah.

“Firm columns” would confuse what is propped with what supports something else.

S N D and R K N: support and refuge

Rukn can denote a corner, foundation, supporting force, or something relied upon.

Sanad likewise concerns support and reliance.

Some Quranic uses of rukn concern a power offering refuge or backing. Musannadah here describes the position of propped wood.

The fields overlap without complete synonymy.

S N D and Ẓ H R: the back and backing

Ẓahr physically denotes the back and in other usages relates to assistance.

Sanad can likewise convey backing.

Ẓahr does not always mean sanad, and sanad need not denote a bodily back.

Shared imagery assists comparison without proving common derivation.

S N D and W TH Q: support and reliability

W TH Q concerns firmness and trustworthiness.

Something may have a weak or unreliable support.

A statement may be attributed to a particular person without trustworthy attribution.

Having a sanad or reliance is therefore not identical to reliability.

This matters when assessing reports and documents: their name does not guarantee validity.

S N D and N F Q: outward reliance and hypocrisy

Quranic hypocrisy concerns inconsistency between outward profession and inward reality.

S N D does not have that meaning.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 describes hypocrites through propped wood, but proximity does not put hypocrisy into musannadah's lexical definition.

Musannadah describes being propped; hypocrisy is the group's moral and behavioral condition.

A comparison must not redefine every derivative of a root.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The sole Quranic form is musannadah in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4.

The central phrase:

كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ

ka-annahum khushubun musannadah

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

Proposed translation:

“They seem like pieces of wood propped up.”

This preserves the passive participle and the comparison.

“They seem like pieces of wood that have been leaned against something” is also clear but longer.

“Wood propped against a wall” is tangible, but the verse does not name a wall. That detail belongs in explanation rather than direct translation.

“Useless wood” or “lifeless wood” can express aspects of the comparison, but are not direct equivalents of musannadah.

“Firm wood,” “strong columns,” and “supporting timber” likewise fail to preserve its passive role: it is propped, not necessarily supporting something else.

Forqan's lexical information can distinguish:

Word in the verse: musannadah.

Root: S N D.

Brief root meaning: leaning, propping, and resting one thing on another support.

Meaning here: pieces of wood propped against something, contrasting impressive appearance with the hypocrites' actual behavior.

The word itself should not be equated with hypocrisy, lack of intelligence, or uselessness; such implications arise from the full comparison and context.

Important non-Quranic forms

Sanad can mean support or backing, and in modern usage a document.

Asnada means propping or attributing something to another.

Istanada means leaning on or relying upon something.

Sānada means helping or backing.

Musnad can describe something attributed to or resting upon another.

Isnād concerns propping or attribution and has specialized scholarly and administrative meanings.

Geographical sanad denotes rising, elevated mountainside terrain.

These forms illuminate the family but are not independently occurring Quranic derivatives.

Conclusion

S N D concerns leaning, resting one thing against another, and support. Tangible reliance extends semantically to assistance, backing, and attribution to a speaker or source.

Sanad also names rising mountain terrain. The poem attributed to al-Nābighah witnesses this form in the pre-Islamic tradition, without dating the derivation of every sense.

The family occurs once in the Quran, as musannadah in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4.

It directly describes pieces of wood propped against something.

The image portrays people whose impressive appearance and speech conflict with their actual conduct, alongside fear, suspicion, and hostility.

Musannadah does not itself mean hypocritical, unintelligent, useless, or deceptive. Those are contextual implications, not fixed root meanings.

The root can be defined as follows:

S N D denotes leaning, propping, and reliance between something and its support. Related meanings include backing, assistance, and attribution to a source. Quranic musannadah describes pieces of wood propped against something.

This preserves the tangible meaning while distinguishing the broader family's usages from the Quranic occurrence.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 highlights apparent firmness versus genuine stability. Impressive stature, engaging speech, social standing, or others' support alone do not establish truthfulness. Trustworthiness appears in consistency between words, actions, and fidelity to truth.
