Life, living, means of livelihood, and the quality of life

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic ʿ–y–sh denotes living, continuing in life, making a living, and the conditions and resources by which a person or living being lives.

ʿĀsha yaʿīshu means “lived” and “lives.” ʿAysh also denotes life and the living of it.

In early Arabic, this family was not limited to being alive rather than dead. ʿAysh could concern life's duration, how it is lived, and the things on which it depends.

Someone may live many years in hardship and deprivation, while another has food, shelter, and comfort. Both are alive, but the quality of their ʿaysh differs.

Food, water, and other necessities also fall within the semantic field because people use them to sustain life.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: ʿaysh as life

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, the pre-Islamic poet, says in his Muʿallaqah:

أَرَى الْعَيْشَ كَنْزًا نَاقِصًا كُلَّ لَيْلَةٍ
وَمَا تَنْقُصِ الْأَيَّامُ وَالدَّهْرُ يَنْفَدِ

arā al-ʿaysha kanzan nāqiṣan kulla laylatin / wa-mā tanquṣi al-ayyāmu wa-al-dahru yanfadi

Translation: I see life as a treasure diminishing every night; whatever days and time diminish will eventually run out.

Al-ʿaysh denotes human life, whose remaining duration decreases as time passes.

The image of a continually diminishing treasure shows that ʿaysh here is not money or income. What diminishes is the opportunity to live.

The verse is recorded in Ṭarafah's recognized Muʿallaqah and is an important witness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Its lexical significance is clear: in Arabic associated with the pre-Quranic period, ʿaysh could denote life itself and its duration, rather than only financial resources.

A witness concerning means of sustaining life

Classical lexicons transmit the following poetic witness under the name Abū Duʾād:

أَعَاشَنِي بَعْدَكَ وَادٍ مُبْقِلُ

aʿāshanī baʿdaka wādin mubqilu

Translation: After you, a green valley kept me alive.

The continuation speaks of eating the valley's plants.

Aʿāshanī comes from aʿāsha: something sustained a person's life or provided the means to live.

Life and its sustaining factors are directly connected here. The valley, through its plants and available food, becomes a means of continued life.

The witness is recorded under Abū Duʾād's name in early lexical sources. It establishes the meaning of sustaining life in the transmitted Arabic tradition; precisely identifying the speaker and dating this particular verse require further evidence.

The witnesses illustrate two related aspects: in Ṭarafah's verse, ʿaysh is life and its duration; in the second, sustaining life concerns making continued living possible.

The semantic core

The early uses support this definition of ʿ–y–sh:

Living and passing through life, together with the conditions, manner, and resources on which its continuation depends.

This includes three related matters:

First, life itself and the passage of its time.

Second, how life is lived: comfortably, with difficulty, satisfyingly, or in deprivation.

Third, what sustains life, such as food, water, shelter, work, and resources for daily needs.

These are related but distinct. Having resources does not necessarily mean being satisfied with life, while difficult living does not always mean a complete absence of material resources.

All three aspects appear in the Quranic semantic field.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal Arabic forms include:

ʿĀsha yaʿīshu: to live and pass through life.

ʿAysh: life, living, and in some uses what a person lives on.

ʿĪshah: a kind, condition, or manner of life.

Maʿīshah: life and its conditions; also food, resources, and means on which life depends.

Maʿāyish: the plural of maʿīshah; means, resources, and ways of living.

Maʿāsh: life and what sustains it; also a time or place for providing life's necessities.

Maʿīsh: another form for life or its means.

Aʿāsha: to sustain someone's life or provide their means of living.

ʿAyyasha: to place someone in circumstances for living or provide means of living.

ʿĀyasha: to live with another and share in life with them.

Muʿāyashah: coexistence and living with others.

Taʿāyush: people or groups living alongside one another.

Taʿayyush: seeking life's necessities or living on available resources.

ʿĀʾish: someone who lives; in some lexical uses, someone enjoying a good life.

These forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran.

Quranic forms

The Quran uses four nominal forms of this root:

  • Maʿāyisha in Al-Aʿrāf 7:10 and Al-Ḥijr 15:20.
  • Maʿīshah and its forms with attached pronouns in Ṭā Hā 20:124, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58, and Al-Zukhruf 43:32.
  • ʿĪshah in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:21 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:7.
  • Maʿāshan in Al-Nabaʾ 78:11.

The root therefore occurs eight times.

ʿĀsha, yaʿīshu, aʿāsha, and the family's other recognized verbal forms do not occur in the Quranic text.

Maʿāyish is the plural of maʿīshah. Its yāʾ belongs to the root, and the commonly read Quranic form is written without a hamzah in the plural.

A usage distinction should also be observed between ʿīshah and maʿīshah. The former more often describes the kind or condition of life; the latter can include both living conditions and the means of sustaining them. This is a tendency, not an absolute rule for all Arabic.

3. “Maʿāyish”: means and resources for life on earth

The earth and the provision of ways to live

وَلَقَدْ مَكَّنَّاكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلْنَا لَكُمْ فِيهَا مَعَايِشَ ۗ قَلِيلًا مَا تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-la-qad makkannākum fī al-arḍi wa-jaʿalnā lakum fīhā maʿāyisha qalīlan mā tashkurūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:10

Translation: We have indeed established you on the earth and provided means of living for you within it. How little you give thanks!

Al-Aʿrāf 7:10

Maʿāyish here relates to the earth and the resources God has provided for human life.

The plural refers to diverse ways and means of living.

These may include fertile land, water, food, plants, animals, necessary materials, and opportunities through which people meet their needs.

A farmer using land, water, and seed draws on resources that make food production possible. Someone making tools or producing goods through a skill likewise uses a means of livelihood.

Maʿāyish is therefore not limited to one job, one product, or a particular sum of money.

The same verse includes makkannākum, concerning the opportunity to settle and the ability to use the earth.

Its continuation speaks of human ingratitude, but this does not come from ʿ–y–sh itself. It comes from the separate phrase qalīlan mā tashkurūn.

Maʿāyish denotes life's means and resources; gratitude is the expected moral response to them.

Means of life for humans and other beings

وَجَعَلْنَا لَكُمْ فِيهَا مَعَايِشَ وَمَنْ لَسْتُمْ لَهُ بِرَازِقِينَ

wa-jaʿalnā lakum fīhā maʿāyisha wa-man lastum lahu bi-rāziqīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:20

Translation: We provided means of living for you within it, and also those for whom you are not providers.

Al-Ḥijr 15:20

This verse likewise concerns means of living provided on earth.

The preceding Al-Ḥijr 15:19 speaks of the earth, mountains, and things growing in due measure. Maʿāyish thus relates to natural resources on which life rests.

The continuation, wa-man lastum lahu bi-rāziqīn, refers to those for whom you are not providers.

Several grammatical analyses relate man to the preceding clause. One reading takes it as beings God has placed alongside human means of livelihood. Another emphasizes that God has also provided means of life for other beings, although humans do not provide for them.

Despite the syntactic difference, both readings indicate that the means of living are not confined to human activity and ability.

Animals, birds, and other beings also have resources for continued life on earth. People may feed some of them, but that does not make humans the ultimate source of their provision.

Al-Ḥijr 15:21 speaks of the treasuries of things being with God and their being sent down in due measure.

Maʿāyish in this passage therefore concerns resources that enable beings to continue living.

Lexically, the key point is that maʿāyish extends beyond monetary income or daily food to the range of resources on which life depends.

4. “Maʿīshah”: living conditions and provision

The distribution of livelihood in worldly life

أَهُمْ يَقْسِمُونَ رَحْمَتَ رَبِّكَ ۚ نَحْنُ قَسَمْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ مَعِيشَتَهُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا ۚ وَرَفَعْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ دَرَجَاتٍ لِيَتَّخِذَ بَعْضُهُمْ بَعْضًا سُخْرِيًّا ۗ وَرَحْمَتُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ مِمَّا يَجْمَعُونَ

a-hum yaqsimūna raḥmata rabbika naḥnu qasamnā baynahum maʿīshatahum fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-rafaʿnā baʿḍahum fawqa baʿḍin darajātin li-yattakhidha baʿḍuhum baʿḍan sukhriyyan wa-raḥmatu rabbika khayrun mimmā yajmaʿūn

Al-Zukhruf 43:32

Translation: Is it they who distribute your Lord's mercy? We have distributed their livelihood among them in worldly life and raised some above others in degrees, so that some may make use of others' services. Your Lord's mercy is better than what they accumulate.

Al-Zukhruf 43:32

Maʿīshatahum concerns people's livelihood and means of living in this world.

Its placement alongside al-ḥayāti al-dunyā indicates that the subject is human living conditions in the present world.

People differ in abilities, opportunities, resources, and circumstances. Some have fertile land, some industrial skills, some engage in trade, and others meet people's needs through services and labor.

These differences make people dependent on one another's work and resources in social life.

Nevertheless, differences in livelihood are not the same as differences in human worth or moral standing.

Greater wealth does not by itself make someone superior before God, nor do fewer resources by themselves make someone morally or humanly inferior.

The verse begins by placing the distribution of the Lord's mercy beyond the authority of those claiming greatness, and ends by valuing that mercy above what people accumulate.

The distribution of livelihood should therefore not be taken to establish that every inequality, form of exploitation, or distribution of wealth is intrinsically just or morally approved by God.

The lexical sense is clear: maʿīshah concerns the conditions and resources through which people live and meet their needs.

Arrogance and ingratitude in prosperity

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ بَطِرَتْ مَعِيشَتَهَا فَتِلْكَ مَسَاكِنُهُمْ لَمْ تُسْكَنْ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا وَكُنَّا نَحْنُ الْوَارِثِينَ

wa-kam ahlaknā min qaryatin baṭirat maʿīshatahā fa-tilka masākinuhum lam tuskan min baʿdihim illā qalīlan wa-kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58

Translation: How many settlements We destroyed that had become arrogant in their prosperity! Those are their dwellings, scarcely inhabited after them, and We were the inheritors.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58

Maʿīshatahā concerns a settlement's livelihood and prosperity.

Qaryah is grammatically feminine singular but can encompass the people of a settlement. Arrogance and rebellion therefore concern its community, not buildings or land possessing moral agency.

Baṭirat is significant. Baṭar concerns arrogant self-indulgence and an improper response to prosperity and blessings.

The condemnation thus does not arise from livelihood or material provision itself, but from the conduct of people made arrogant and rebellious by it.

A society may have abundant natural resources, production, wealth, and comfort and use them well. Another may turn the same resources into instruments of arrogance, injustice, or corruption.

Livelihood has the same meaning in both societies; their moral difference comes from their conduct.

Maʿīshah here should therefore not be translated as “luxury-seeking,” “corruption,” or “rebellion.” Such ideas belong to the verb and context, not to ʿ–y–sh itself.

A constricted and difficult life

وَمَنْ أَعْرَضَ عَنْ ذِكْرِي فَإِنَّ لَهُ مَعِيشَةً ضَنْكًا وَنَحْشُرُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ أَعْمَىٰ

wa-man aʿraḍa ʿan dhikrī fa-inna lahu maʿīshatan ḍankan wa-naḥshuruhu yawma al-qiyāmati aʿmā

Ṭā Hā 20:124

Translation: Whoever turns away from My remembrance will have a constricted life, and We shall gather him blind on the Day of Resurrection.

Ṭā Hā 20:124

Maʿīshah here is qualified by ḍankan.

Ḍank concerns constriction, difficulty, and hardship.

Syntactically, ḍankan describes maʿīshatan. Arabic can use it adjectivally from a verbal noun, which explains its form despite the feminine noun maʿīshah.

Maʿīshah is not merely food or income here. Constriction can concern life's condition and quality.

Someone may have substantial financial resources yet experience anxiety, dissatisfaction, or trouble; another may have less money but more peace and contentment.

These examples distinguish material resources from experienced quality of life, but do not by themselves determine the precise form of constriction intended by maʿīshatan ḍankan.

Interpretations have included difficult worldly life, constriction after death, and punishment in the hereafter. The word maʿīshah alone does not specify when the constriction occurs.

The verse separately describes gathering the person on the Day of Resurrection. That sequence matters, but the time and nature of the constricted life should not be restricted to one experience without considering all relevant indications.

The verse speaks of someone turning away from God's remembrance. Every poor, ill, or afflicted person cannot therefore be identified as its referent.

Material poverty and hardship alone do not demonstrate turning away from God, just as wealth and comfort alone do not demonstrate guidance or divine approval.

Lexically, the occurrence shows that maʿīshah can be qualified in terms of life's difficulty and quality, rather than being limited to income or possessions.

5. “Maʿāsh” and “ʿīshah”: time for living and life's quality

Daytime as a time of livelihood

وَجَعَلْنَا النَّهَارَ مَعَاشًا

wa-jaʿalnā al-nahāra maʿāshā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:11

Translation: We made the day for living and livelihood.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:11

Maʿāshan here is connected with daytime.

Arabic maʿāsh can denote what sustains a person, as well as a time or place for meeting life's needs.

Since the sentence concerns al-nahār, the temporal sense is prominent.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 presents sleep as rest, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:10 likens the night to a covering. The day is then presented as a time for livelihood.

This connection concerns the ordering of human life and the use of time to meet its needs.

A farmer can see and work the land in daylight. Craftspeople and merchants can also work during the day. Daylight facilitates movement and many everyday activities.

Maʿāsh is nevertheless not restricted to one occupation or economic activity.

Life is more than earning income. Travel, family care, use of natural resources, and daily activities also belong to living.

Nor does the verse prohibit nighttime work or require everyone, everywhere and under all circumstances, to follow an identical work and rest schedule.

It describes daytime's place in life, rather than specifying every person's working arrangements.

Lexically, maʿāsh here concerns the day as a suitable time for activity and making a living.

Specialized modern uses of maʿāsh for salary or pension in some linguistic contexts should not be imposed on the verse. Its meaning here is understood through daytime and living.

A satisfying life in Al-Ḥāqqah

فَهُوَ فِي عِيشَةٍ رَاضِيَةٍ

fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:21

Translation: He will then be in a satisfying life.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:21

This concerns someone given the record of their deeds in their right hand.

ʿĪshah denotes the kind and condition of life, qualified by rāḍiyah.

In form, rāḍiyah is a feminine active participle from r–ḍ–y, meaning pleased or content.

Here the description is attributed to life itself. Life does not literally possess human will or satisfaction; the rhetorical expression conveys a pleasant, satisfying condition of living.

In other words, the person lives a life with which they are pleased.

ʿĪshah can thus describe life's kind and quality rather than merely being alive.

The following verses speak of a lofty garden and its blessings. The satisfying life is therefore associated with reward in the hereafter.

Yet ʿīshah itself does not mean only “Paradise.” Paradise is the contextual instance of the satisfying life, not the root's independent lexical meaning.

A satisfying life in Al-Qāriʿah

فَهُوَ فِي عِيشَةٍ رَاضِيَةٍ

fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah

Al-Qāriʿah 101:7

Translation: He will then be in a satisfying life.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:7

The same expression occurs in Al-Qāriʿah.

The preceding verse concerns someone whose scales are heavy.

Those whose scales are light face a different outcome.

ʿĪshatin rāḍiyatin therefore also concerns pleasant, satisfying life at the human being's final outcome here.

Its repetition clarifies how ʿīshah can describe living in terms of satisfaction, comfort, and provision.

These two uses do not establish that ʿīshah is restricted throughout Arabic to life in the hereafter.

Arabic also uses ʿīshah for a manner of worldly life. The earlier poetic witness concerned the related form ʿaysh and should not be treated as a direct witness to the form ʿīshah.

The distinction between this world and the hereafter comes from context, not from ʿīshah alone.

6. Semantic distinctions and translation proposals

ʿ–Y–Sh compared with Ḥ–Y–Y (ḥayāh)

Ḥ–y–y denotes life and being alive and is used in the Quran for life, giving life, and life after death.

ʿ–y–sh also concerns life, but its Quranic uses emphasize living conditions, resources, and how life is sustained.

Ḥayāh can name life as opposed to death, while maʿīshah in Al-Zukhruf 43:32 concerns living conditions and resources.

The distinction is not absolute. Ṭarafah's verse shows that early Arabic ʿaysh could also name life itself and its duration.

One cannot therefore say that ḥayāh always means only being alive and ʿaysh always means only comfort or quality of life.

ʿ–Y–Sh compared with R–Z–Q (rizq)

Quranic rizq concerns the provision and benefit beings receive.

Maʿīshah concerns life and the conditions or means through which people meet their needs.

Land, water, tools, skills, and opportunities to work may be means of livelihood; food or benefits obtained through them may also belong to the field of rizq.

The concepts overlap. Food may be both provision and something by which a person lives.

Rizq emphasizes provision and receiving a benefit, while maʿīshah may describe the whole set of living conditions and resources.

The juxtaposition of maʿāyish and bi-rāziqīn in Al-Ḥijr 15:20 is significant for this relationship.

ʿ–Y–Sh compared with ʿ–M–R (ʿumr)

ʿUmr concerns life's duration and the time a person spends in the world.

ʿAysh may refer to duration, but also encompasses life's quality and conditions.

Two people may, for example, live equally long lives under very different conditions and with different resources.

This illustrates their semantic tendencies without establishing that ʿaysh never denotes duration.

ʿ–Y–Sh compared with M–T–ʿ (matāʿ)

Matāʿ concerns something used or enjoyed and is also used for worldly benefits and resources in the Quran.

Maʿīshah concerns living and the conditions in which those benefits are used.

A house, clothing, and tools can be worldly matāʿ while also contributing to someone's means of livelihood.

Matāʿ and maʿīshah are not exact synonyms: one may identify the useful object itself, the other life and the arrangement of its resources.

ʿ–Y–Sh compared with R–Gh–D (raghad)

Arabic raghad concerns abundance, ease, and pleasant provision.

ʿAysh does not inherently mean prosperity or abundance: life may be difficult, constricted, or satisfying.

Living under harsh conditions is still someone's ʿaysh or maʿīshah, even without ease and abundance.

ʿAysh and maʿīshah should therefore not invariably be translated as “prosperous life” or “comfortable life.”

Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish life itself, the kind of life, and its sustaining resources.

ʿĀsha yaʿīshu means “lived” and “lives.”

ʿAysh may be “life” or “living.”

Where life's quality is at issue, ʿīshah may be rendered “life” or “condition of life.”

Depending on the verse, maʿīshah may mean “livelihood,” “life,” “living conditions,” or “means of living.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:10 and Al-Ḥijr 15:20, maʿāyish is best rendered “means of life” or “means of living.”

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:11, maʿāsh is naturally expressed through “living and livelihood,” since daytime is presented as its time.

The three uses of maʿīshah require contextual translation:

In Ṭā Hā 20:124, maʿīshatan ḍankan means “a constricted life.”

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58, maʿīshatahā concerns “its livelihood” or “its prosperity.”

In Al-Zukhruf 43:32, maʿīshatahum may be “their livelihood” or “their means of living.”

For ʿīshatin rāḍiyatin in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:21 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:7, “a satisfying life” is suitable.

This conveys rāḍiyah naturally without requiring life itself to possess feelings or will.

The entire root meaning need not be packed into every base translation. The translation should remain clear and concise, with lexical distinctions explained in the discussion.

7. Conclusion

Arabic ʿ–y–sh denotes living, passing through life, and the resources and conditions by which life continues.

Early poetic witnesses show both ʿaysh as life and its duration and its connection with what enables continued living. Their precise dates and attributions should be assessed according to the evidence for each witness.

The Quran uses four nominal forms of the root.

Maʿāyish concerns means and resources for life on earth, used by humans and other beings to sustain life.

Maʿīshah may concern living conditions and resources, as in Al-Zukhruf 43:32, or life's difficulty and hardship, as in Ṭā Hā 20:124.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:58, it concerns a community's prosperity, while arrogance and rebellion come from baṭirat rather than maʿīshah itself.

Maʿāsh in Al-Nabaʾ 78:11 identifies daytime as a time for activity and livelihood.

ʿĪshah in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:21 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:7 describes a satisfying life in the final outcome.

These uses cannot be confined to money, income, food, or bodily survival. Equally, the root should not be redefined as psychological peace, spiritual happiness, or a philosophical concept absent from some of its uses.

Life and living are the shared basis; life's kind, its resources, and the moral value of human responses to them come from the verses' constructions and contexts.

Final definition of ʿ–y–sh:

ʿ–y–sh denotes living and passing through life, and its derivatives encompass the conditions, manner, and means of continued living. ʿĪshah more often concerns life's kind and quality; maʿīshah, life and its means; maʿāyish, ways and resources for living; and maʿāsh, life itself or its sustaining time and means. Comfort, hardship, satisfaction, and rebellion are supplied by the relevant descriptions and contexts, not by the root alone.
