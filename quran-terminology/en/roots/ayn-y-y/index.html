Being unable to accomplish a task, exhaustion, and inability to express oneself

1. The root in early Arabic

In Arabic, ʿ–y–y concerns being unable to accomplish a task, find the right way to perform it, or express one's meaning, as well as weariness and exhaustion from effort.

ʿAyiya yaʿyā is used when someone is baffled by a task, cannot find how to perform it, or is unable to continue.

This inability can arise in several situations.

First, someone wants to accomplish a task but does not know how. A person facing a complicated problem may, despite trying, be unable to find a solution.

Second, someone wants to speak or answer a question but cannot express the intended meaning properly. Here the root concerns difficulty in speech and response.

Third, prolonged activity may exhaust a person or animal and prevent further movement or work. This use is particularly associated with iʿyāʾ and the verb aʿyā.

The family is therefore not restricted to bodily fatigue. Much of its usage concerns being baffled by a task, answer, or problem and unable to accomplish it.

First poetic witness: inability to answer

The well-known pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says near the beginning of one of his odes:

وَقَفْتُ فِيهَا أُصَيْلَانًا أُسَائِلُهَا
عَيَّتْ جَوَابًا وَمَا بِالرَّبْعِ مِنْ أَحَدِ

waqaftu fīhā uṣaylānan usāʾiluhā / ʿayyat jawāban wa-mā bi-al-rabʿi min aḥadi

Translation: I stood there toward evening, questioning the dwelling; it could offer no answer, and no one was in the abode.

The poet stands before the traces of an abandoned dwelling, as though asking about its former inhabitants.

ʿAyyat jawāban means that it could not answer.

The ruined dwelling is not actually a speaking being that has lost its voice. Through personification, the poet depicts its silence and his unanswered question.

The verse shows that early Arabic used ʿ–y–y in the domain of answering and expression, where someone or something fails to provide an answer.

The line belongs to al-Nābighah's well-known ode and is an established witness within the poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Its semantic evidence is clear: in poetry attributed to pre-Quranic Arabic, ʿ–y–y also concerns inability to answer or express oneself.

Second witness: inability to manage an undertaking

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ reads:

عَيُّوا بِأَمْرِهِمْ كَمَا
عَيَّتْ بِبَيْضَتِهَا الْحَمَامَةُ

ʿayyū bi-amrihim kamā / ʿayyat bi-bayḍatihā al-ḥamāmah

Translation: They were baffled by their undertaking, as the dove was baffled over its egg.

The continuation describes the dove and the twigs it has gathered to hold its egg.

In the poet's image, the dove cannot prepare a suitable place for the egg and fails to arrange it successfully.

ʿAyyū bi-amrihim means that the group failed to manage and organize its undertaking.

ʿAyyat bi-bayḍatihā likewise describes the dove's difficulty concerning its egg.

This witness differs semantically from al-Nābighah's verse.

Al-Nābighah's verse concerns answering.

This verse concerns managing and accomplishing a task.

The root therefore extends beyond speech to tasks whose proper method a person cannot find.

Early lexical and grammatical sources attribute the verse to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ. Its wording varies in transmission, so the reading quoted here should be distinguished from other versions.

It is useful evidence for recorded early Arabic meaning, but its exact wording and attribution should not be treated without qualification as certain or contemporaneously documented in the pre-Islamic period.

Weariness and exhaustion in Arabic

Classical Arabic uses aʿyā al-rajulu for someone becoming weary and exhausted through effort or movement.

Aʿyāhu al-sayru means that walking or travelling exhausted the person.

A traveller may cover a long distance and finally be unable to continue. Here fatigue limits the capacity to act.

The usage concerns exhaustion and reduced ability from effort, beyond an ordinary feeling of tiredness.

Lexical sources record this meaning for aʿyā. Recording a use in classical dictionaries does not by itself establish precisely when it arose before the Quran.

The evidence identifies inability in tasks and responses, and exhaustion through activity, as established semantic fields. Without independent historical evidence, none should be declared the definite origin of every other branch.

The semantic core

The semantic core of ʿ–y–y may be defined as follows:

Being unable to accomplish something: failing to find the way to do it, express one's meaning, or sustain the effort needed to continue and complete it.

Three aspects can be distinguished:

Inability to find how to perform a task;

Inability to speak or express the intended meaning;

And fatigue or exhaustion that removes or limits the capacity to act.

These aspects are related but not identical. A physically strong person may be baffled by a problem, while someone who knows exactly how to perform a task may be too exhausted to continue.

Accurate translation therefore requires identifying the kind of inability from the construction and context.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms of this Arabic family include:

ʿAyiya yaʿyā: was baffled by a task, could not find how to perform it, or became unable to accomplish it.

ʿAyya: the contracted form of ʿayiya, with a corresponding meaning.

ʿIyy: inability to express oneself clearly or find how to perform a task.

ʿAyiyy: someone unable to express a meaning or perform a task properly.

ʿAyyān: in certain Arabic uses, someone baffled or unable to manage an undertaking.

ʿAyāʾ: inability or difficulty; in some combinations, severe difficulty or intractability.

Aʿyā: became weary or exhausted; transitively, exhausted or baffled someone.

Iʿyāʾ: severe fatigue, exhaustion, and inability resulting from effort.

Muʿyin: a person or thing experiencing exhaustion or, in the corresponding transitive construction, causing exhaustion or difficulty.

Taʿāyā and taʿayyā: was baffled by a task or unable to perform it properly.

Istaʿyā: in some Arabic uses, became baffled by a task.

Muʿāyāh: presenting a problem or statement whose answer or solution is difficult to find.

Uʿyiyyah: a riddle or difficult problem that challenges someone seeking a solution.

Arabic also records dāʾun ʿayāʾun for a difficult, incurable illness.

The illness is described this way because it is difficult to treat or physicians cannot find a cure.

This does not establish illness as the root's original meaning. Difficult illness is one context in which inability is expressed.

ʿAyiyy may also describe someone unable to articulate the intended meaning properly. Without contextual evidence, this should not be restricted to a permanent physical inability to speak.

Forms actually found in the Quran

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both times as a Form I verb:

  • Yaʿya in Al-Aḥqāf 46:33.
  • A-fa-ʿayīnā in Qāf 50:15.

Yaʿya is a third-person masculine singular imperfect verb, following lam.

A-fa-ʿayīnā contains the past-tense ʿayīnā, “we were unable,” preceded by interrogative hamzah and fa-.

Both Quranic uses therefore belong to the simple ʿayiya form, not Form IV.

ʿIyy, ʿayiyy, ʿayāʾ, iʿyāʾ, muʿāyāh, and the family's other familiar forms do not occur in the Quran.

Both Quranic occurrences concern The God's creative power, and both deny inability.

3. Wa-lam yaʿya bi-khalqihinna: no inability in creation

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّ اللَّهَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَلَمْ يَعْيَ بِخَلْقِهِنَّ بِقَادِرٍ عَلَىٰ أَنْ يُحْيِيَ الْمَوْتَىٰ بَلَىٰ إِنَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

a-wa-lam yaraw anna Allāha alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-lam yaʿya bi-khalqihinna bi-qādirin ʿalā an yuḥyiya al-mawtā balā innahu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr

Al-Aḥqāf 46:33

Translation: Have they not seen that The God, who created the heavens and the earth and was not unable to create them, is able to bring the dead to life? Yes indeed; He is powerful over everything.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:33

Here, yaʿya comes from ʿayiya yaʿyā.

Following lam, the verb is jussive and its final weak letter is deleted.

Wa-lam yaʿya denies inability.

Understanding the phrase also requires attention to its complement:

Bi-khalqihinna.

That is, in creating them.

The pronoun -hinna refers to the heavens and the earth.

The clause thus concerns inability in a specified undertaking: creating the heavens and the earth.

Does it mean only not becoming tired?

The Arabic root is associated with fatigue and exhaustion, so “did not grow weary of creating them” is defensible.

Its semantic range nevertheless extends beyond bodily fatigue.

ʿAyiya bi-al-amri can describe being baffled by an undertaking, unable to find how to accomplish it or bring it to completion.

The verse concerns creative power and then the ability to revive the dead.

Denying ʿiyy in this construction therefore also denies inability to create.

The ability to accomplish a great undertaking can serve as evidence for the possibility of another. By recalling the creation of the heavens and earth, the Quran draws attention to The God's power to revive the dead.

In this argument, “was not unable” directly connects with power.

This does not invalidate “did not grow weary”; it shows that inability must also be considered in this context.

Connection with the power to revive the dead

The verse continues with bi-qādirin ʿalā an yuḥyiya al-mawtā.

Qādir belongs to q–d–r and denotes ability.

The verse therefore presents two related points:

The God was not unable to create the heavens and earth.

The same God is able to revive the dead.

Here inability contrasts with power.

The verse concerns an act whose full magnitude and manner exceed human comprehension. It does not imply that The God first uses tools, energy, or bodily strength as humans do and might subsequently tire.

Using a concept familiar from human experience, the verse denies inability of The God.

The root itself does not explain the physical manner of creation or the mechanism of reviving the dead.

The verse clearly denies inability in creation and affirms the power to revive the dead.

4. A-fa-ʿayīnā bi-al-khalqi al-awwal: were We unable to accomplish the first creation?

أَفَعَيِينَا بِالْخَلْقِ الْأَوَّلِ بَلْ هُمْ فِي لَبْسٍ مِنْ خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ

a-fa-ʿayīnā bi-al-khalqi al-awwali bal hum fī labsin min khalqin jadīd

Qāf 50:15

Translation: Were We unable to accomplish the first creation? Rather, they are in confusion about a new creation.

Qāf 50:15

A-fa-ʿayīnā contains the past-tense verb ʿayīnā.

ʿAyīnā means “we were unable.”

The initial hamzah marks a question, while fa- connects the clause with the preceding discourse.

The construction is a rhetorical question denying the proposition: were We unable to accomplish the first creation?

The intended answer is clear: The God was not unable to accomplish the first creation.

The first creation and a new creation

Earlier verses in Qāf discuss the sky, earth, mountains, vegetation, rain, and the revival of dead land.

Qāf 50:11 links the land's revival after rain with humanity's emergence again.

Qāf 50:15 then mentions al-khalqi al-awwal and khalqin jadīd.

Al-khalqi al-awwal refers to the first creation.

Khalqin jadīd refers to a fresh creation.

In context, the verse addresses the possibility of re-creation and responds to doubt about it.

Its question is whether the first creation defeated The God's power such that re-creation should be deemed impossible.

Here the root thus directly concerns the ability to create.

Does a-fa-ʿayīnā mean only “did We grow weary”?

“Did We grow weary of the first creation?” is possible within the root's semantic field.

“Were We unable to accomplish the first creation?” has a clearer connection with the argument here.

The reason is the contrast between the first creation and a new creation.

The verse raises the possibility of re-creation against human doubt; it does not state that The God needed rest after the first creation.

Arabic ʿayiya bi-amrin can denote inability to accomplish a task.

In this context, inability to accomplish the first creation is therefore an appropriate meaning to consider.

“Were We unable” must itself be understood within the rhetorical question: the Quran denies inability rather than attributing it to The God.

Distinguishing inability from labs

The verse continues:

Bal hum fī labsin min khalqin jadīd.

The word labs belongs to l–b–s and here concerns confusion, ambiguity, and uncertainty in understanding something.

The verse therefore distinguishes two matters.

First, The God's power: the first creation did not defeat His ability.

Second, the condition of those confused and uncertain about a new creation.

Divine inability is denied, while human confusion about re-creation is reported.

This contrast does not make labs and ʿiyy the same root or lexical meaning. One concerns obscurity in understanding; the other here concerns inability to accomplish a task.

5. Comparison with fatigue and inability elsewhere in the Quran

ʿ–Y–Y (ʿiyy) and L–Gh–B (lughūb)

Arabic l–gh–b concerns fatigue and exhaustion from effort.

The Quran says:

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِنْ لُغُوبٍ

wa-la-qad khalaqnā al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin wa-mā massanā min lughūb

Qāf 50:38

Translation: We certainly created the heavens and the earth and what is between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us.

Qāf 50:38

Lughūb here concerns fatigue and exhaustion.

The topic relates to Al-Aḥqāf 46:33 and Qāf 50:15: all three concern creation and deny weariness or inability of The God.

Their lexical differences should nevertheless be preserved.

Lughūb is more closely associated with weariness and exhaustion.

The family of ʿ–y–y includes inability to accomplish a task or find how to perform it. Fatigue and exhaustion are particularly associated with aʿyā and iʿyāʾ; every meaning of the family should not be assigned automatically to ʿiyy in every context.

The families thus overlap in some uses but are not complete synonyms.

Someone may tire after prolonged work yet still know how to continue. Someone else may be baffled by a problem without physical fatigue.

Inability is therefore not always caused by bodily exhaustion.

ʿ–Y–Y (ʿiyy) and ʿ–J–Z (ʿajz)

ʿ–j–z concerns inability and lack of power to do or attain something.

ʿ–y–y also concerns inability in some uses, but can emphasize being baffled about how to perform or organize a task.

Someone may have the physical strength to build an object but not know how its parts fit together, and thus be unable to accomplish the task.

Another person may know the method well but lack the physical ability to carry it out.

Both involve inability, but of different kinds.

This example does not mean that ʿajz is always physical and ʿiyy always intellectual. ʿAjz has a wider range, and the family of ʿ–y–y also includes bodily exhaustion.

The distinction is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:33, the contrast with divine power brings the semantic fields close together.

ʿ–Y–Y (ʿiyy) and N–Ṣ–B (naṣab)

One use of n–ṣ–b concerns suffering, effort, and fatigue from activity.

For example, working in heat and difficult conditions may cause distress and fatigue.

Hardship and effort do not themselves entail inability to accomplish the task.

A person may continue correctly and finish despite considerable difficulty.

In its inability-related branch, ʿ–y–y may instead describe no longer finding the means or strength to continue.

Naṣab and ʿiyy are related in contexts of fatigue and difficulty, but are not one concept.

ʿ–Y–Y (ʿiyy) and B–K–M (bakam)

B–k–m concerns muteness and inability to express oneself clearly.

In speech-related uses, ʿ–y–y can likewise concern inability to answer or communicate a meaning.

There is nevertheless a difference.

Abkam can describe someone whose capacity for speech or clear expression is blocked or limited.

ʿAyiyy can describe someone able to speak but baffled when answering a question or expressing a point.

A student may speak fluently yet fail to find or express an answer to a difficult question.

This differs from a lasting inability to speak.

The lexical distinction does not establish a definitive medical or neurological classification. The issue is the words' semantic ranges.

ʿ–Y–Y (ʿiyy) and W–H–N (wahn)

W–h–n concerns weakness and lack of strength.

This may appear in a person's body, resolve, or capacities.

Weakness does not always entail actual inability to perform a particular task.

A weak person may work successfully with suitable resources and methods, while a strong person may be baffled by a difficult problem.

Wahn therefore emphasizes weakness, whereas ʿiyy can emphasize inability to find a way or bring a task to completion.

Distinction from similar roots

ʿ–y–y and ḥ–y–y are different roots.

Quranic ḥ–y–y concerns life, giving life, modesty, and other branches. Two identical final letters do not make life and inability one word family.

ʿ–y–y also differs from w–ʿ–y, which concerns receiving, retaining, and remembering something.

ʿAyiya and aʿyā differ morphologically, although both belong to ʿ–y–y.

In particular, a-fa-ʿayīnā in Qāf 50:15 must not be mistaken for Form IV. Its initial hamzah is interrogative, not the derivational hamzah of Form IV.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Persian equivalent for ʿ–y–y should reflect the kind of inability and the verb's complement; the same contextual distinction applies in English.

ʿAyiya bi-al-amri can mean “was baffled by the task,” “could not accomplish it,” or “could not find how to perform it.”

ʿAyiya fī al-kalāmi means “was at a loss in speaking” or “could not express the intended meaning properly.”

In speech-related use, ʿiyy can mean difficulty in expression or inability to convey one's meaning.

In the appropriate context, ʿayiyy means baffled, unable to express oneself, or unable to organize an undertaking.

In physical contexts, aʿyā can mean “became tired” or “became exhausted.”

Aʿyāhu al-amru means “the task baffled him.”

In contexts of activity and movement, iʿyāʾ naturally means severe fatigue or exhaustion.

Translating wa-lam yaʿya in Al-Aḥqāf 46:33

Two main renderings are defensible:

“And did not grow weary of creating them.”

And

“And was not unable to create them.”

The first emphasizes weariness and exhaustion.

The second makes the absence of inability to create clearer.

Since the verse continues with The God's power to revive the dead, “was not unable” is appropriate.

“Did not grow weary” should nevertheless not be deemed lexically invalid, since fatigue and exhaustion belong to the family's semantic field.

Translating a-fa-ʿayīnā in Qāf 50:15

Suggested translation:

“Were We unable to accomplish the first creation?”

This agrees with the interrogative construction and the subject of re-creation.

“Did We grow weary of the first creation?” also conveys an established meaning, although inability to accomplish creation is more prominent in the argument here.

In both verses, the translation should consider Arabic usage and its relationship to creative power.

7. Conclusion

Arabic ʿ–y–y concerns inability to accomplish a task, inability to express one's meaning, and fatigue or exhaustion from activity.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets records uses concerning inability to answer and manage a task. The precise attribution and wording of each witness should be assessed separately; the second witness's variations should remain acknowledged in the conclusion.

In al-Nābighah's verse, ʿayyat jawāban concerns inability to answer.

In the verse attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ, ʿayyū bi-amrihim concerns inability to manage and accomplish an undertaking.

Classical Arabic also associates aʿyā with fatigue and exhaustion from effort and movement.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both as Form I verbs.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:33 denies The God's inability to create the heavens and earth and connects this with His power to revive the dead.

In Qāf 50:15, a rhetorical question about inability in the first creation confronts human doubt concerning a new creation.

These uses show that Quranic ʿ–y–y extends beyond the feeling of fatigue and can concern inability to accomplish or complete an undertaking.

It should also be distinguished from nearby roots such as ʿ–j–z, l–gh–b, n–ṣ–b, w–h–n, and b–k–m. They may overlap in some uses, but each has its own semantic field and constructions.

Final definition of ʿ–y–y:

ʿ–y–y denotes being unable to accomplish a task, whether through not knowing how to perform it, inability to express oneself, or exhaustion that limits continued activity. Its two Quranic occurrences concern creation and deny inability of The God. A rendering that denies inability is therefore accurate and defensible in both, while absence of weariness also lies within the root's lexical range.
