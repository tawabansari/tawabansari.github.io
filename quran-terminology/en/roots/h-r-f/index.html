Part One: Lexical Background and Basic Image

1. Introduction to the Root

The root “Ḥ R F” in Arabic is connected with two closely related fields of meaning:

  • the edge, side, and boundary of a thing;
  • turning, or turning something, away from a direction and position.

“حَرْفُ الشَّيء” is the edge and border of it, such as the edge of a sword, the side of a mountain, or the bank of a river. Something that stands on a “ḥarf” is not in the body and middle of the matter, but on a boundary where the possibility of inclining, slipping, or turning back is greater.

From this same field, verbs are formed that denote moving aside or changing direction. “حَرَّفَه” means he turned a thing from its direction or position, and “تَحَرَّفَ” means he changed his position by changing direction.

The Qur'anic uses of this root can be studied in three fields:

  • standing in an edge-like and unstable position;
  • turning speech away from its place;
  • changing position for a military purpose.

These uses are related to one another, but they do not have one identical ethical direction. Edge-like worship is a sign of instability, conscious distortion of speech is blameworthy, and changing position to continue battle can be a legitimate move.

2. Pre-Islamic Witnesses

“Ḥarf” was used in Arabic poetry before the revelation of the Qur'an. One of its known uses was as a description of a strong, lean she-camel suited for difficult journeys.

Al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani says:

هَلْ تُبَلِّغَنِّيهِمْ حَرْفٌ مُصَرَّمَةٌ
أُجُدُّ الفَقارِ وَإِدْلاجٌ وَتَهْجيرُ

In this line, “ḥarf” describes a tested she-camel able to carry the poet to his destination through night travel and movement in the heat of the day.

Al-Harith ibn Hillizah also says:

أَنْمِي إِلى حَرْفٍ مُذَكَّرَةٍ
تَهِصُ الحَصى بِمَواقِعٍ خُنْسِ

Here too, “ḥarf” is used for a strong she-camel whose hard steps crush the small stones.

Lexicographers are not agreed on the reason for this naming. Some likened the she-camel, in her leanness and sharpness, to the edge of a sword, while others compared her strength to the side of a mountain. Therefore, none of these explanations can be treated with certainty as the historical origin of the word. What is clear is the presence of “ḥarf” in pre-Islamic language and its relation with shape, edge, firmness, and movement.

3. The Material Image

The most tangible image of this root is a surface that reaches its ending point and arrives at its edge or boundary.

Among its lexical uses are:

  • “حَرْفُ السَّيْف”: the edge of a sword;
  • “حَرْفُ الجَبَل”: the side or ridge of a mountain;
  • “حَرْفُ النَّهْر”: the bank of a river;
  • “حَرْفُ الشَّيء”: the boundary and side of a thing.

In these examples, “ḥarf” stands opposite the body, the middle, and the inside. Standing on an edge may not fit complete stability, because with a slight change a person can incline toward one side or the other.

Nevertheless, instability is not a fixed component of the meaning of every “ḥarf.” The edge of a mountain or a sword may be hard and firm. The meaning of instability in “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” is obtained from the combination of the image of the edge with the continuation of the verse.

4. Lexical Branches

The main uses of this root can be known through several branches:

4.1. Edge and Side

This branch is clear in the noun “حَرْف.” A ḥarf is the boundary where a surface or body comes to an end.

4.2. Standing on One Side

“عَلى حَرْف” can denote standing on an edge or standing on one face and state. The context of the sentence determines whether such a state is stable or unstable.

4.3. Moving Aside and Changing Direction

“اِنْحَرَفَ عنه” means he moved aside from him or from that path. In this use, a person or thing departs from its previous extension.

4.4. Turning Something from Its Place

“حَرَّفَه” means he turned it away from its side, path, or state. Distorting speech belongs to this same field: speech is moved aside from its place or direction.

4.5. Changing Position

“تَحَرَّفَ” can mean changing one’s own direction and position. This change is not negative in itself; purpose and context determine its value.

In lexical reports, a branch related to measuring and determining quantity is also mentioned for this root. This branch has no role in Qur'anic usage, and there is no need to bring it back to the meaning of edge or distortion.

5. The Semantic Core

Between the main uses of this root two components are visible:

  1. being on an edge or side;
  2. turning, or turning something, away from a direction and position.

On this basis, the semantic core can be stated as follows:

Edge and boundary; and from it, inclining or turning away from the first direction and position.

This definition should not be turned into a certain historical theory. It cannot be proven that all branches necessarily arose from one single material image; but the imagistic connection between “edge,” “inclining,” and “turning from place” is clear in the Qur'anic uses.

6. Qur'anic Forms

This root appears in the Qur'an in three structures:

  • حَرْف: a noun, meaning edge or an edge-like position;
  • يُحَرِّفُ: a form II verb, meaning to turn something from its direction or place;
  • مُتَحَرِّف: the active participle of form V, meaning one who changes his position by changing direction.

The verbal noun “taḥrīf” does not occur in the Qur'an, although its verb is used. Likewise, the noun “ḥarf” does not occur in the Qur'an with the meaning of a letter of the alphabet.

Part Two: “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” and Edge-Like Worship

7. The Use of “Ḥarf” in Surah Al-Hajj

The Qur'an says:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَعْبُدُ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ فَإِنْ أَصَابَهُ خَيْرٌ اطْمَأَنَّ بِهِ وَإِنْ أَصَابَتْهُ فِتْنَةٌ انْقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ

Al-Hajj 22:11

“عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” is the image of someone who has not stood in the middle of the path and on a firm station, but has stood on an edge. Such a person remains in his position only as long as circumstances proceed according to what he wants; but when trial reaches him, he easily turns back.

“Ḥarf” in this verse does not directly mean doubt, hypocrisy, or weakness of faith. Its direct meaning is “edge.” Instability, conditionality, and readiness to turn back are understood from the image of the edge and from the continuation of the verse.

8. The Qur'anic Definition of “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ”

The verse itself explains what worship upon a ḥarf is like:

  • if good reaches the person, he becomes settled by it;
  • if a trial reaches him, he turns back upon his face;
  • his remaining is dependent on immediate benefit and favorable conditions;
  • his position changes when the situation changes.

For this reason, the phrase “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” in this context can be explained as follows:

Edge-like, conditional, and unstable worship that continues with good and collapses with trial.

This definition is obtained from the full structure of the verse; not because the noun “ḥarf” everywhere in Arabic means conditional faith.

9. The Contrast Between “Ḥarf” and “Inqalaba”

The imagistic structure of the verse is very coherent:

عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ
اِنْقَلَبَ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ

First, the person stands on an edge; then, when trial comes, he practically turns over upon his face and turns back. “Ḥarf” describes his initial condition, and “inqilab” describes the movement that results from that condition.

Trial does not necessarily create instability in him; rather, it exposes the prior instability within him. From the beginning he was standing in an edge-like position, and good and trial reveal what the basis of his remaining was.

10. Settling by “Good”

The verse does not say that this person became settled in The God. Rather, it says:

فَإِنْ أَصَابَهُ خَيْرٌ اطْمَأَنَّ بِهِ

The pronoun “بِهِ” returns to “good.” His calm is dependent on the good he has received, not necessarily on firm recognition of The God.

This difference is important for understanding the verse. A person may appear calm in days of ease, but the object of his confidence is the benefit that has reached him. When conditions change, that calm also disappears.

11. The Boundary Between Indication and Interpretation

In analyzing “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ,” three levels must be kept separate:

  • direct meaning: ḥarf means edge, side, or boundary;
  • contextual indication: standing on a ḥarf, in this verse, shows instability and readiness to turn back;
  • interpretive reflection: this state can describe benefit-centered, conditional worship that lacks inner firmness.

If “ḥarf” is translated directly as “doubt,” the original image of the verse disappears. On the other hand, a bare translation, “on an edge,” without explanation, is not complete for a Persian-speaking reader. A proper translation should preserve the image and also show its contextual indication.

Part Three: Turning Speech from Its Place

12. The Morphological Structure of “حَرَّفَ”

“حَرَّفَ” is form II. In Qur'anic usage, the agent turns something from its direction or place.

The relation of the well-known forms of this family can be seen as follows:

  • “اِنْحَرَفَ”: he himself moved aside from a direction or path;
  • “حَرَّفَ الشيءَ”: he turned that thing from its direction or place;
  • “تَحَرَّفَ”: he changed his own direction and position.

In the distortion of speech, the main issue is not simply the occurrence of any kind of change; rather, it is turning speech away from the position or direction in which it had stood.

13. Understanding and Then Distorting

The Qur'an says about a group:

وَقَدْ كَانَ فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ يَسْمَعُونَ كَلَامَ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ يُحَرِّفُونَهُ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا عَقَلُوهُ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

Al-Baqarah 2:75

The order of verbs in this verse is decisive:

  1. they hear the word of The God;
  2. they understand it;
  3. after understanding it, they distort it;
  4. they do this while knowing.

Thus distortion in this verse is not the result of not hearing or not understanding. The phrases “مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا عَقَلُوهُ” and “وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ” highlight the element of awareness.

The root “Ḥ R F” by itself does not prove intentionality; intentionality in this verse is obtained from the explicit mention of understanding and knowledge.

14. The Object of Distortion and the Scope of Judgment

In the verse of Al-Baqarah, the object of hearing is “كَلَامَ اللَّهِ,” and the pronoun “ه” in “يُحَرِّفُونَهُ” returns to that same speech.

Grammatically, the verse speaks about a specific speech that they had heard and understood. Likewise, the expression “فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ” limits the scope of judgment: the discussion is about a group among them, not all of a people or all followers of a tradition.

This restriction must not be removed in translation or interpretation. Just as distorting speech is wrongful, turning the scope of a limited judgment into a sweeping ruling can itself be a kind of moving the expression outside its boundary.

15. “عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ”

In two verses it says:

يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ

An-Nisa 4:46; Al-Maidah 5:13

“مَوَاضِع” is the plural of “مَوْضِع” and comes from the root “W Ḍ ʿ.” A place is where something has been put or made to stand.

The preposition “عَنْ” also shows distancing from a point of origin. The semantic structure of the phrase is as follows:

  • “al-kalim”: words or statements;
  • “mawadiʿih”: the places of those words;
  • “ʿan”: moving away from those places;
  • “yuḥarrifūn”: the act of turning and moving aside.

Therefore, “distorting words from their places” means moving speech away from the place, direction, connection, or function in which it stood.

16. “مِنْ بَعْدِ مَوَاضِعِهِ”

In another verse it says:

يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَوَاضِعِهِ

Al-Maidah 5:41

Here, instead of “عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ,” the expression “مِنْ بَعْدِ مَوَاضِعِهِ” appears. This structure emphasizes a stage after the speech has stood or been determined in its place: the speech first had its place and was then turned away from it.

The difference between the two structures can be summarized as follows:

  • “عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ”: moving away from the places;
  • “مِنْ بَعْدِ مَوَاضِعِهِ”: turning after having stood in the places.

This difference is subtle and should not be turned into two completely unrelated meanings. In both, the words do not remain in their own place.

17. Distortion and Forgetting

In Surah Al-Maidah, two behaviors are placed beside each other:

يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ وَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ

Al-Maidah 5:13

Distortion and forgetting are not synonyms in this expression:

  • distortion turns speech from its place;
  • forgetting removes part of what had been reminded from attention and practice.

A statement may be preserved but turned from its place; and part of a message may be given over to forgetting or abandonment. The pairing of these two verbs shows that the Qur'an distinguishes between semantic displacement and leaving aside part of a reminder.

18. Distortion and Acceptance of a Desired Result

After mentioning distortion in Al-Maidah: 41, it says:

يَقُولُونَ إِنْ أُوتِيتُمْ هَذَا فَخُذُوهُ وَإِنْ لَمْ تُؤْتَوْهُ فَاحْذَرُوا

This sentence shows result-centered acceptance: if the desired answer is given to them, they should accept it, and if another result is presented, they should avoid it.

In this context, distortion can serve a result that has already been wanted. First the result is determined, and then speech is taken in a direction that secures that result.

This motive is understood from the context of the verse; the root “Ḥ R F” does not in every use denote pursuit of benefit.

19. What Exactly Does Distortion Specify?

The verb “يُحَرِّفُونَ” by itself does not determine the technical method of distortion. Turning speech from its place may occur in different ways in different situations, such as:

  • changing the expression;
  • relocating part of the speech;
  • partial and selective transmission;
  • separating speech from its context;
  • directing the meaning toward another result;
  • using speech in an unsuitable place.

The root “Ḥ R F” does not name all these methods separately. The shared element is that speech does not remain in its first direction or place.

For this reason, reducing every Qur'anic instance of distortion to “change in the written copy” is more than the word indicates. Conversely, the claim that distortion can never include wording change is also not obtained from the root itself.

20. Error in Understanding and Conscious Distortion

Not every interpretive difference or error can be called distortion. Several conditions must be distinguished:

  • unintended error in understanding;
  • honest difference in interpretation;
  • forgetting or incomplete transmission that is not deliberate;
  • consciously turning speech from its place.

In Al-Baqarah: 75, understanding and knowledge are explicitly mentioned. Therefore, the criticism of the verse is not directed toward a simple error in understanding, but toward an act performed after understanding.

Part Four: Changing Direction to Continue Battle

21. The Meaning of “مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ”

The Qur'an says about turning one’s back in battle:

وَمَنْ يُوَلِّهِمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ دُبُرَهُ إِلَّا مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ أَوْ مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ

Al-Anfal 8:16

“مُتَحَرِّف” is the active participle of “تَحَرَّفَ”; that is, someone who changes his direction or position. The phrase “لِقِتَالٍ” specifies the purpose of this change: he changes position in order to continue battle or carry out a military maneuver.

Thus “mutaḥarrif” in this verse does not mean a deviant person or a traitor. His outward movement may resemble retreat, but his aim is not abandoning the field; rather, it is a tactical change of direction or position.

This use shows that the basic act of changing direction in the root “Ḥ R F” is not negative in itself. Its value is determined by purpose and context.

22. Why “مُتَحَرِّف” and Not “مُنْحَرِف”?

In this verse, the Qur'an uses “مُتَحَرِّف” in form V, not the well-known modern form “مُنْحَرِف.”

Form V in this structure fits a movement carried out by the agent himself: the fighter changes his position. Nevertheless, one should not conclude from the morphological pattern alone that this movement is necessarily deliberate, strategic, and legitimate. These features are obtained from the whole phrase:

  • “mutaḥarrifan” appears in the structure of exception;
  • the lam of purpose comes in “لقتال”;
  • it is contrasted with blameworthy turning of the back.

So the morphological pattern provides the capacity of changing position by the agent, but the military purpose is determined by “لِقِتَالٍ.”

23. The Difference Between “مُتَحَرِّف” and “مُتَحَيِّز”

The verse states two exceptions separately:

  • “مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ”;
  • “مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ.”

“Mutaḥarrif” is someone who changes his direction or position in order to continue battle.

“Mutaḥayyiz” is someone who joins another group or force. In taḥayyuz, being placed alongside a “fi'ah” is prominent; but in taḥarruf, the change of direction and position itself is the focus.

Therefore:

  • taḥarruf: tactical change of path or position;
  • taḥayyuz: joining a group or supporting force.

Part Five: Later Uses of “Ḥarf”

24. Does “Ḥarf” Mean a Letter of the Alphabet in the Qur'an?

In later Arabic, “ḥarf” acquired several technical uses:

  • one of the signs of the alphabet;
  • a sound or written unit;
  • in grammar, one of the classes of words beside noun and verb.

But the Qur'an does not use the noun “ḥarf” in any of these technical meanings. Its only nominal use in the phrase “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” means edge or an edge-like position.

Likewise, “الْكَلِم” in the verses of distortion means sayings or words, not individual alphabetic letters. Calling the initial signs of some surahs “disconnected letters” is a term in Qur'anic sciences, not a definition that the Qur'an itself gives for those signs.

25. The Hadith Expression “سَبْعَةِ أَحْرُفٍ”

In hadith literature, a famous report is transmitted with this wording:

أُنْزِلَ الْقُرْآنُ عَلَى سَبْعَةِ أَحْرُفٍ

In the method of this study, hadith is not a source for legislating religion or determining the meaning of Qur'anic verses. Nevertheless, hadith reports may be considered from the angle of showing the use of words in later Arabic literature. The importance of this report in this article is only from this angle: the word “aḥruf” is used in a non-Qur'anic combination.

The meaning of “aḥruf” in this report is not clear or agreed upon. Various explanations have been proposed for it, such as modes of recitation, dialects, aspects of expression, and linguistic forms; but the phrase itself defines none of these meanings.

Therefore, from this report one cannot conclude that:

  • “ḥarf” in Al-Hajj: 11 means recitation or dialect;
  • the Qur'an uses the word “ḥarf” to mean a letter of the alphabet;
  • “seven aḥruf” are the same as the later known readings;
  • hadith usage can determine the Qur'anic meaning of “ḥarf.”

The expression “seven aḥruf” stands outside the text of the Qur'an and later became a broad subject in hadith literature, readings, and Qur'anic sciences. This later use should not be imposed on the clear Qur'anic use of “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ.” In that verse, the context itself explains the meaning of “ḥarf” through good, trial, confidence, and turning back.

26. The Technical Meaning of Ḥarf in Arabic

The connection between the meaning “edge and boundary” and the technical meaning of a letter of the alphabet may be explained through the idea of delimitation and a composing unit: a letter is a unit that, beside another letter, forms the spoken or written shape of a word.

Nevertheless, this explanation does not prove the certain historical emergence of the term. The technical use of “ḥarf” became specialized in the sciences of writing, recitation, and grammar.

In grammatical terminology, too, ḥarf is not only a sign of the alphabet; it can be a word such as a preposition, conjunction, or conditional particle. Therefore, “alphabetic letter” and “grammatical particle” are two separate specialized uses, and neither of them is the meaning of “ḥarf” in Al-Hajj: 11.

27. Well-Known Non-Qur'anic Words

In Arabic and Persian today, many words from this family are common, such as:

  • انحراف;
  • منحرف;
  • تحریف;
  • حرفه;
  • احتراف;
  • حرفه‌ای.

All these forms do not occur in the Qur'an. Their commonness in present-day language should not cause their modern meanings to be imposed on the Qur'anic forms.

Especially “حرفه” in modern Persian usually means profession and specialization. This meaning has no role in “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ,” “يُحَرِّفُونَ,” or “مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ.”

Part Six: Semantic Boundaries

28. “حَرْف,” “طَرَف,” and “جَانِب”

These three words overlap, and an absolute boundary should not be constructed between them.

Ṭ R F (ṭaraf) mostly denotes an end, corner, or part of a thing. J N B (janib) shows the side or flank of something and is not necessarily unstable or boundary-like.

Ḥ R F (ḥarf), in the use being discussed, highlights the edge and boundary itself. Therefore, “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” produces a stronger image of standing on a boundary and the possibility of turning back.

This is a tendency, not a claim that “ṭaraf” and “janib” can never have a meaning close to ḥarf.

29. “Taḥrīf” and “Tabdīl”

B D L (badal) denotes something replacing something else. In tabdil, one thing is exchanged for another.

But taḥrīf is not necessarily complete replacement. The same speech may remain, but be separated from its context, directed toward another meaning, or used in an unsuitable place.

Therefore:

  • tabdil: replacing one thing with another;
  • taḥrīf: turning something from its direction or place.

Both may gather in a single act, but they are not lexically identical.

30. “Taḥrīf” and “Taghyīr”

GH Y R (ghayyara) has a general meaning and includes every kind of alteration. Change may be right or wrong, small or large, verbal or semantic.

Taḥrīf is a specific kind of change in which moving away from position or direction is highlighted. Therefore, every taḥrīf is change, but not every change is taḥrīf.

31. “Ḥ R F” and “Ṣ R F”

Ṣ R F (ṣarafa) can also denote turning back, moving away, or changing direction. For example, “صَرَفَهُ عَنْهُ” means he turned him back from something or moved him away from it.

There is overlap between ṣarf and taḥrīf, but their semantic tendencies are not identical:

  • “ṣarf” more strongly highlights the act of turning back, directing, or moving away from the destination and object;
  • “ḥarf” in its nominal meaning is connected with edge and boundary, and in taḥrīf the turning away from place or direction is in focus.

This difference is not absolute. The preposition, object, and context of each sentence determine which dimension is more prominent.

32. “Taḥrīf” and “Lay”

The Qur'an says:

يَلْوُونَ أَلْسِنَتَهُمْ بِالْكِتَابِ

Al Imran 3:78

L W Y (layy) denotes twisting and bending. In this verse, twisting the tongue while reciting or presenting speech is highlighted.

By contrast, taḥrīf emphasizes turning words away from their places. These two behaviors may accompany each other in one situation, but they are not identical:

  • “layy”: twisting the tongue or the mode of expression;
  • taḥrīf: turning speech from its place or direction.

33. “Taḥrīf” and “Kitmān”

K T M (katama) means covering and not making something apparent. In concealment, speech or truth is hidden.

In taḥrīf, the original speech may be transmitted openly, but it has been turned from its direction. Therefore:

  • concealment: not saying or covering;
  • taḥrīf: presenting in a turned place or direction.

Someone may conceal a truth without distorting it; and someone may distort speech without hiding the speech itself.

34. “Taḥrīf” and “Ilḥād”

The root L Ḥ D (laḥada) is also related to inclining away from a straight path. The Qur'an says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُلْحِدُونَ فِي آيَاتِنَا لَا يَخْفَوْنَ عَلَيْنَا

Fussilat 41:40

“يُلْحِدُونَ فِي آيَاتِنَا” denotes inclination and crooked dealing in relation to the signs. But the Qur'an uses the root “L Ḥ D” in this verse, not “Ḥ R F.”

The tendency difference between the two expressions is as follows:

  • ilḥād in the signs: inclining and crooked dealing in relation to the signs;
  • taḥrīf of words: turning words from their places.

Distortion may be one manifestation of ilḥād in the signs, but this is an interpretive conclusion. The two roots are lexically and morphologically separate and should not be mixed together.

35. “Ḥarf” and “Zaygh”

Z Y GH (zaygh) mostly denotes inclining away from the right path, especially in the field of the heart, judgment, and guidance.

Distorting words may arise from inward zaygh, but “zaygh” describes the deviated state or inclination, while “taḥrīf” describes the act of turning speech. A causal relation between the two is acceptable only if context supports it.

Part Seven: The Connection and Difference of Qur'anic Uses

36. One Shared Logic with Three Different Directions

Among the three main Qur'anic uses of this root, there is a limited but clear connection:

“عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ”

The person stands on an edge. Instability becomes clear from the continuation of the verse: good holds him, and trial turns him back.

“يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ”

Speech is turned away from its place. In some verses, this is done after understanding and with awareness.

“مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ”

The fighter changes his direction or position, but his goal is to continue battle.

The shared element is edge and change of direction; but one ethical judgment should not be made for all three:

  • “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” shows instability in worship;
  • “يُحَرِّفُونَ” in the context of the verses is a conscious and blameworthy act;
  • “مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ” is a purposeful and permitted move.

So shared root does not mean complete shared ethical value.

37. Grammatical Structures

In “يَعْبُدُ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ,” the prepositional phrase “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” explains the manner of worship. The discussion is not about the physical place of worship; a state is expressed through a spatial image.

In “يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ,” “al-kalim” is the object of the verb, and “ʿan mawadiʿih” shows the point of departure.

In “مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ,” “mutaḥarrifan” describes the person’s state within the exception structure, and “li-qital” specifies the purpose of the change of position.

Therefore, an important part of the meaning of each expression is obtained not from the root alone, but from the preposition, object, purpose marker, and continuation of the sentence.

38. Translation Suggestions

For the main forms of this root, the following translations may be suggested:

  • حَرْف: edge, side, boundary;
  • عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ: on an unstable edge; in a conditional and unsteady manner;
  • حَرَّفَ: to turn from its place or direction;
  • يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ: they turn the words away from their places;
  • مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ: one who changes position to continue battle.

Translating “they distort” alone does not explain the meaning of the word for the reader, because it repeats the same Arabic word in Persian form. “Turning away from place” better conveys the image of the root and the Qur'anic qualifier.

Likewise, translating “mutaḥarrif” as “deviant” is misleading, because “deviant” in modern Persian usually has a negative ethical charge, while Al-Anfal: 16 speaks of a purposeful movement in battle.

39. Necessary Boundaries

To prevent unjustified expansion of the root’s meaning, several points must be observed:

  • “ḥarf” in Al-Hajj: 11 does not directly mean doubt or hypocrisy.
  • The instability of “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ” is understood from the continuation of the verse.
  • Not every interpretive difference is distortion.
  • Distortion by itself does not determine the technical method of manipulating speech.
  • Distortion cannot be limited only to written change.
  • “Mutaḥarrif” in Al-Anfal: 16 does not carry a negative ethical charge.
  • The military purpose of taḥarruf is understood from “لِقِتَالٍ.”
  • “Ḥarf” does not occur in the Qur'an with the meaning of an alphabetic letter.
  • “Seven aḥruf” is a hadith expression outside the text of the Qur'an.
  • Hadith usage and later terminology should not be imposed on the meaning of “ḥarf” in the verses.
  • The verses about distortion speak about specific groups and should not be turned into a sweeping judgment about an entire people.

40. Conclusion

The root “Ḥ R F” in the material field denotes the edge, side, and boundary of a thing. From this semantic field, the meanings of standing in an edge-like position, changing direction, and turning something away from its place take shape.

In “عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ,” a person worships The God from an edge-like and unstable position. Good makes him settled, but trial causes him to turn back. “Ḥarf” gives the image of the edge, and the conditionality of the worship is understood from the whole verse.

In “يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ,” words are turned away from their places. The root by itself does not determine the method of distortion; but in Al-Baqarah: 75, awareness and understanding before distortion are explicitly stated.

In “مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ,” the fighter changes direction or position in order to continue battle. This use shows that movement away from one direction is not negative in itself, and its value is determined by purpose and context.

“Ḥarf” as a letter of the alphabet and the hadith expression “seven aḥruf” are not part of the Qur'anic use of this word. These later uses may be studied for the history of Arabic language and terminology, but their uncertain or technical meanings should not be imposed on the verses of the Qur'an.

Thus the basic meaning of this root can be summarized as follows:

“Ḥ R F”: edge and boundary; and from it, standing on a side or turning and being turned away from the first direction and position.
