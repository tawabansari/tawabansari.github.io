Leanness, reduced bulk, and concealment: from horses and camels to inward secrets

In early Arabic, ض م ر has two recognized semantic branches: loss of flesh and bodily bulk, producing a lean or compact form; and concealment from others’ sight or knowledge.

In the first branch, ضَمَرَ describes horses, camels, and other living beings becoming lean or losing flesh. An animal with a tucked belly and flanks and a lean body is called ضَامِر. This condition may result from long travel, strenuous activity, insufficient food, or preparation for running and racing.

In the second branch, أَضْمَرَ means to conceal something or keep it from appearing. Related ضَمِير denotes something hidden in a person’s heart or thought: something that exists but is not directly known to others.

These branches should not be forced into a single meaning. A leaner body and a concealed thought might both suggest diminished outward manifestation, but that resemblance alone does not prove that one meaning developed from the other.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as ضَامِرٍ in Al-Ḥajj 22:27. This belongs to the first branch: an animal’s leanness.

1. Early Arabic meaning and poetic evidence

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period uses ضمر and its derivatives to describe horses and their bodily condition. These witnesses establish leanness as a recognized usage in early Arabic.

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes horses that have gone on campaign:

غَزَتْ سِمَانًا فَآبَتْ ضُمَّرًا خُدُجًا
مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَنَّبُوهَا بُدَّنًا عُقُقًا

ghazat simānan fa-ābat ḍummaran khudujan / min baʿdi mā jannabūhā buddanan ʿuquqan

Translation: “They went on campaign fat and returned lean, having foaled prematurely, after being led alongside, stout and pregnant.”

The contrast between سِمَانًا and ضُمَّرًا is decisive. The horses set out fat but returned lean after campaigning and prolonged movement. ضُمَّر is the plural of ضَامِر and describes their bodies. خُدُجًا refers to premature foaling, and عُقُقًا to pregnancy; neither means leanness.

This usage shows that leanness can result from exertion and wear, but the word itself does not mean war, travel, or illness. Those factors come from the poem’s context.

A poem attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm also reads:

ضَوَامِرَ كَالْقِدَاحِ تَرَى عَلَيْهَا
يَبِيسَ الْمَاءِ مِنْ حُوٍّ وَشُقْرِ

ḍawāmira ka-al-qidāḥi tarā ʿalayhā / yabīsa al-māʾi min ḥuwwin wa-shuqri

Translation: “Lean horses like arrow shafts, with traces of dried moisture upon them, dark-coloured and chestnut.”

Here ضَوَامِر describes horses with slender, elongated bodies. The comparison with arrow shafts makes their leanness and form tangible.

This witness does not imply that every ضامر animal is sick or weak. A lean horse may be ready for running and rapid movement; reduced excess flesh can, in suitable circumstances, accompany agility.

For the second branch, concealment, a verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā is relevant:

أَرَانَا إِذَا أَضْمَرَتْكَ الْبِلَادُ
نُجْفَى وَتُقْطَعُ مِنَّا الرَّحِمُ

arānā idhā aḍmaratka al-bilādu / nujfā wa-tuqṭaʿu minnā al-raḥimu

Translation: “We find that when the lands hide you away, we are treated coldly and our ties of kinship are severed.”

Here أَضْمَرَتْكَ الْبِلَادُ means that the lands conceal a person: distance removes him from other people’s sight and reach.

This usage concerns absence and concealment, not bodily leanness.

Valuable as these witnesses are, attribution to pre-Islamic poets should be distinguished from independently establishing each verse’s date. The lines survive through transmissions and collections compiled later. They inform our understanding of early Arabic semantics but do not by themselves determine the precise origin of each semantic branch.

2. The two main branches and their forms

Leanness and reduction in bulk

The following forms belong to this branch:

ضَمَرَ / ضَمُرَ: to become lean or lose flesh.

ضُمُور: leanness, loss of flesh, or bodily contraction.

ضَامِر: an animal or person with a lean, slender body.

ضُمَّر and ضَوَامِر: plurals of ضامر.

ضَمَّرَ: to bring someone or an animal into a lean condition, especially to condition a horse through feeding and exercise.

اضْطَمَرَ and انْضَمَرَ: to become slender, reduced in bulk, or contracted.

مِضْمَار: the place or period of conditioning a horse for running and racing; also the course over which horses race.

تَضْمِير: conditioning a horse for strenuous activity or racing, involving loss of excess fat and flesh and improved physical readiness.

Traditional horse conditioning regulated feeding and exertion to bring an animal out of fatness and prepare it to move and run. The aim was a lighter, better-prepared body rather than pathological emaciation.

This shows that ضَامِر is not invariably negative. An animal may become thin through illness, or develop a slender, agile body through activity and exercise.

Agility and racing ability are nevertheless not fixed parts of ضَامِر. The word describes bodily condition; actual capability must be assessed separately.

The family is not restricted to animals. Related usages also describe a lean person, a slender branch, or something that contracts and becomes thin as it loses moisture.

Concealment and remaining hidden

The following forms are important in this branch:

أَضْمَرَ: to conceal something or keep it within oneself.

إِضْمَار: concealing or leaving something unspoken.

مُضْمَر: something kept concealed.

ضَمِير: what is hidden within a person: a secret, thought, or intention not disclosed.

ضَمَائِر: the plural of ضمير.

ضِمَار: property, a claim, a promise, or something beyond reach whose recovery or fulfilment is uncertain.

For example, مال ضِمَار describes property beyond its owner’s reach or without reliable hope of recovery. The concern is absence and inaccessibility, not property becoming lean.

The lexical use of ضمير is likewise understood through this branch. Someone may speak while keeping a thought or intention concealed from the listener.

In Arabic grammar, ضمير became a technical name for forms referring to the speaker, addressee, or another person or thing, such as أنا, أنت, and هو. This grammatical designation must not be identified with the original meaning of every root usage.

“Moral conscience,” sometimes expressed by ضمير in present-day Persian, is another specialized sense. The Arabic meaning of something hidden in the heart does not imply that the word always meant moral conscience.

Among these forms, the Quran uses only ضَامِر. The absence of ضمير and إضمار does not mean that concealed intention, inward secrets, or unspoken thought are absent as concepts. Other verses express them through different words and constructions.

3. ضَامِر in Al-Ḥajj

Al-Ḥajj 22:27

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ يَأْتِينَ مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ

wa-adhdhin fī al-nāsi bi-al-ḥajji yaʾtūka rijālan wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmirin yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīq

Al-Ḥajj 22:27

Translation: “Proclaim the pilgrimage among the people: they will come to you on foot and upon every lean mount, coming from every distant path.”

This is the root’s only Quranic occurrence.

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:26, concerns the House’s location and the command to Abraham to avoid association and purify the House. Al-Ḥajj 22:27 then commands the proclamation of pilgrimage among the people.

The invitation describes two ways people reach their destination: on foot and mounted.

رِجَالًا here is the plural of رَاجِل and denotes people travelling on foot. It should not be understood as men in contrast to women.

وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ, by contrast, concerns those who arrive riding animals.

Morphologically, ضَامِرٍ is an active participle on the pattern فَاعِل. Following كُلِّ, it is the genitive second term of the construct. The singular combines with كُلِّ to encompass the members of the group.

In Arabic, ضامر may describe a camel, horse, or another lean animal. Here عَلَىٰ and the contrast with travelling on foot indicate a riding animal. Camels are a clear referent in the setting of Arabian travel, but ضامر itself does not exclusively mean “camel.”

Why is the riding animal described as ضامر?

The word and the verse’s context contribute different parts of the explanation.

ضَامِر describes bodily condition: being lean and slender.

مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ refers to distant routes. This context evokes long journeys that can affect both human and animal bodies.

On such a journey, sustained movement, energy expenditure, and difficult terrain may reduce an animal’s flesh and fat. This makes ضامر appropriate to the image of distant travel.

The root itself nevertheless does not mean “becoming lean through long travel.” Travel is a possible contextual explanation, not part of the word’s independent meaning.

Nor does the verse say that all these animals are sick, weak, or near collapse. A lean animal may still be capable of moving and carrying a rider.

Why is يَأْتِينَ feminine plural?

ضَامِرٍ first appears in the singular, followed by the feminine plural verb يَأْتِينَ.

This difference can be explained grammatically.

In a common grammatical analysis, كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ is singular in form but denotes a plurality of mounts. يَأْتِينَ follows that collective meaning. Feminine agreement with nonhuman referents does not by itself establish that all the animals are female.

On this analysis, people arrive riding lean mounts, and those mounts come from distant routes.

In this analysis, يَأْتُوكَ refers to the people answering the pilgrimage invitation, whereas يَأْتِينَ refers to their mounts. This is not the only analysis recorded: some take the latter verb to refer to the arriving people understood as groups.

Neither analysis restricts the invitation to men or women. رِجَالًا means travellers on foot, and the feminine form يَأْتِينَ does not independently determine the sex of the pilgrims or mounts.

The image’s place in the verse

ضامر describes a means of travel, not the aim of pilgrimage.

The invitation is to pilgrimage and the House, while people’s ways of arriving differ. Some walk and others ride. The distances also vary.

The image fits travel in an age when covering long distances, whether on foot or by animal, could require considerable effort.

Mention of ضامر does not establish a lean mount, suffering, or a particular means of transport as a condition of pilgrimage. The verse describes people’s arrival; it does not command making animals thin or imposing additional hardship.

A comprehensive study of pilgrimage should consider this verse alongside capability, benefits for people, and the principles concerning the House.

4. Leanness, weakness, contraction, and concealment

ض م ر (ḍamara) compared with ض ع ف (ḍaʿufa)

Weakness concerns diminished ability, strength, or power. The bodily branch of ضمور concerns leanness, slenderness, or contraction.

A lean animal may be weak, but the two descriptions are not necessarily equivalent.

A conditioned racehorse may have a slender body with little fat while remaining powerful in movement. Conversely, a large animal may be weak through illness or another condition.

Rendering ضامر as “incapable” or “weak” without sufficient contextual support erases this distinction.

ض م ر (ḍamara) compared with ض م م (ḍamma)

ض م م denotes bringing together, joining, or gathering things. The bodily branch of ض م ر concerns reduced bulk or slenderness.

Although contracting skin or bodily form may help explain ضمور, this semantic resemblance does not make the roots identical.

Joining two things may increase their combined bulk, whereas bodily ضمور generally involves loss of flesh or contraction.

ض م ر (ḍamara) compared with خ ف ي (khafiya)

خ ف ي concerns being hidden or not apparent. This overlaps with the second branch of ض م ر.

أَضْمَرَ فِي نَفْسِهِ usually describes something a person keeps concealed within, whereas concealment in the خ ف ي family is broader and need not involve inward thought or intention.

The distinction is not absolute in every construction. The verb, object, and context determine which aspect is prominent.

Al-Ḥajj 22:27 provides no indication that ضامر concerns hidden intention or an inward secret. It belongs to the bodily branch.

5. ضمير: lexical meaning and later terminology

ضمير is among this family’s best-known words in modern Persian and Arabic. Familiarity with it may suggest that ض م ر always fundamentally meant inwardness, awareness, or conscience.

The linguistic evidence does not support that restriction.

Lexically, ضَمِير is something kept within: a secret, thought, desire, or unspoken decision.

This concept is neither intrinsically good nor bad. Someone may conceal a good intention or harbour an intention to harm. Moral value depends on the intention and action, not merely their concealment.

In grammar, ضمير denotes forms referring to persons or things and functioning within sentence structure. This received a specialized formulation in grammatical scholarship.

Present-day Persian also uses expressions equivalent to “an awakened conscience” or “an aware inner self.” Their reference to moral judgment should not be projected without evidence onto every historical derivative of the root.

The Quran discusses inward intentions, what people disclose or conceal, and The God’s knowledge of what lies within breasts. It expresses these concepts through other words.

Understanding a Quranic concept must therefore not depend solely on the presence of a particular term. A concept may occur throughout the Quran even when the term used for it in scholarship or modern Persian does not.

Conversely, the family’s concealment branch does not authorize translating ضامر in the pilgrimage verse as a hidden intention or a person with a particular inner disposition.

6. Suggested translations for Forqan

In Al-Ḥajj 22:27, ضَامِر can be rendered “lean,” “thin,” or “slender.”

“Lean” closely expresses having little flesh. “Slender” conveys outward shape but may not adequately convey the reduced flesh.

The Persian equivalent meaning “thin” is short and natural. Coupled with the reference to a riding animal, it is readily understandable in the verse.

Because ضامر is not exclusive to one animal species, “lean mount” preserves its lexical range better than treating the word invariably as “lean camel.” Camels remain a clear referent in the historical travel context.

Suggested rendering:

وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ — “and upon every lean mount”

An explanation may clarify that these are lean, slender animals used for long journeys.

Other non-Quranic forms require equivalents appropriate to their branch: ضُمُور denotes leanness and reduced bulk; تَضْمِير, when concerning horses, conditioning; إِضْمَار, concealing; مُضْمَر, concealed or unspoken; and lexical ضَمِير, something hidden within.

These equivalents should not be merged in the Quranic usage of ضامر.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic ض م ر has two semantic branches: loss of flesh or bulk and bodily slenderness; and being or keeping something concealed.

The first includes ضامر, ضمور, تضمير, and مضمار. Early poetic evidence illustrates descriptions of horses’ bodily condition, while lexical sources also record conditioning them for movement and activity.

In the second branch, إضمار, مضمر, ضمير, and ضمار concern concealment, remaining hidden, or absence from sight and reach.

Only ضَامِرٍ occurs in the Quran. In Al-Ḥajj 22:27 it describes the lean mounts people ride to pilgrimage. Distant travel provides a possible explanation for their condition, but the root itself means neither travel nor suffering.

The family can be summarized in two distinct branches without asserting that one historically derives from the other:

ض م ر:

1. Loss of flesh or bulk and the body or an object becoming slender or compact.

2. Being or keeping something hidden, especially beyond others’ sight or knowledge.

This preserves the family’s lexical range without forcing every derivative into a single artificial image or projecting later technical senses of ضمير onto the clear Quranic usage of ضامر.
