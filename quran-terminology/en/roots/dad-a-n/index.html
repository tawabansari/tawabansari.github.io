Sheep and wool-bearing livestock: from flock keeping and using wool to the Qur’anic distinction between sheep and goats

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic ض أ ن (Ḍ ʾ N) concerns sheep, particularly the wool-bearing category of livestock. In established Arabic usage, ضَأْن (ḍaʾn) stands against مَعْز (maʿz): the former names sheep and the latter goats.

This distinction was familiar in Arab pastoral life. Both were kept for meat, milk, skins, and their coats, while Arabic naming distinguished wool-bearing sheep from hair-bearing goats.

Ḍ ʾ N occurs only once in the Qur’an, as الضَّأْنِ (al-ḍaʾni) in Al-Anʿām 6:143.

Principal Arabic forms include:

  • ضَأْن (ḍaʾn): sheep; a collective designation for wool-bearing sheep, including males and females.
  • ضَأَن (ḍaʾan): another form of the collective noun.
  • ضَائِن (ḍāʾin): a male sheep or a description of a wool-bearing sheep.
  • ضَائِنَة (ḍāʾinah): a female sheep.
  • ضَوَائِن (ḍawāʾin): plural of ḍāʾinah: female sheep.
  • ضَئِين (ḍaʾīn): another collective form for sheep.
  • أَضْؤُن (aḍʾun): another plural of ḍaʾn.
  • ضَيْن و ضِين (ḍayn, ḍīn): early dialectal forms without hamzah.
  • أَضْأَنَ (aḍʾana): a person’s or group’s sheep became numerous.
  • ضَأَنَ (ḍaʾana): in a particular use, separated sheep from goats.
  • ضِئْنِيّ (ḍiʾnī): pertaining to sheep; in a recorded use, a vessel or bag made from sheepskin.

Early sources also use ḍāʾin for a gentle person or someone with a soft, physically weak body. These uses are examined separately from the principal sheep sense.

None of these verbal or adjectival forms occurs in the Qur’an. Only al-ḍaʾn appears in Al-Anʿām 6:143.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence at Al-Anʿām 6:143

2. The semantic core: sheep as a livestock category

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a clear principle for Ḍ ʾ N:

الضَّادُ وَالْهَمْزَةُ وَالنُّونُ أُصَيْلٌ صَحِيحٌ، وَهُوَ بَعْضُ الْأَنْعَامِ

al-ḍādu wa-al-hamzatu wa-al-nūnu uṣaylun ṣaḥīḥun wa-huwa baʿḍu al-anʿāmi

“Ḍād, hamzah, and nūn form a sound root concerning a category of livestock.”

He then names this category ḍaʾn and records aḍʾana for a person’s sheep becoming numerous.

Ḍaʾn thus primarily names an animal category rather than an activity such as grazing, shearing, or sacrificing.

Flock gathering, bodily softness, or separating animals should therefore not be presented as the certain origin of the name for sheep.

Such features may occur in derivatives and related uses, while ḍaʾn’s established meaning remains sheep.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ḍaʾn as a livestock category

Distinguishing sheep and goats

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الضَّائِنُ مِنَ الْغَنَمِ: ذُو الصُّوفِ

al-ḍāʾinu mina al-ghanami dhū al-ṣūfi

“Ḍāʾin is the wool-bearing animal among ghanam.”

By contrast, māʿiz names a goat.

Arabic pastoral usage employed ghanam as a broader designation covering both sheep and goats.

Ḍaʾn and maʿz are thus two categories within ghanam.

Sheep are usually associated with wool, whereas familiar Arabic usage describes goats as hair-bearing.

This lexical distinction relates to the familiar difference between the animals.

It should nevertheless not become an exceptionless natural law requiring every sheep breed to have long wool or no goat’s coat ever to resemble wool.

Present-day biological knowledge recognizes sheep and goat breeds differing in coats, fibre quantities, and appearance.

The Arabic definition reflects familiar categories in its pastoral setting rather than replacing a comprehensive biological classification.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam: wool-bearing sheep distinguished from goats

Ḍaʾn includes males and females

Ḍaʾn collectively names sheep.

Ḍāʾin can name a male sheep, with ḍāʾinah its feminine form.

Familiar Arabic uses kabsh for a ram and naʿjah for a ewe.

Ḍaʾn should therefore not be translated exclusively as female sheep.

Nor is it confined to an age group: a lamb is a young sheep, whereas ḍaʾn is not restricted to youth.

An adult male, a ewe, and a lamb can all belong to ḍaʾn.

“Sheep” is therefore the natural suitable equivalent.

3. Ḍaʾn in early Arabic life and language

Flock keeping and increasing sheep numbers

Arabic أَضْأَنَ الرَّجُلُ (aḍʾana al-rajulu) means that a man’s sheep became numerous.

The use concerns an increase in livestock numbers.

For pastoral communities, sheep numbers could matter for food, clothing, and trade.

More sheep could provide greater access to milk, meat, wool, and skins.

Aḍʾana nevertheless does not itself mean becoming wealthy or making a profit: it reports numerous sheep.

Someone may have many animals yet face difficult living conditions because of drought, animal disease, or other problems.

The economic consequences of flock keeping should not replace the verb’s lexical meaning.

Separating sheep from goats

The dictionaries record:

اِضْأَنْ ضَأْنَكَ وَامْعَزْ مَعْزَكَ

iḍʾan ḍaʾnaka wa-imʿaz maʿzaka

“Separate your sheep and separate your goats.”

Here the two livestock categories are set apart.

Pastoralists may do this for counting, tending, grazing, milking, or other activities.

This use nevertheless does not establish separation as Ḍ ʾ N’s original meaning.

The verb here is formed from the livestock category’s name and concerns separating that category.

The separating sense of the verb therefore does not make Qur’anic al-ḍaʾn mean “those separated” or “those set apart.”

Asās al-Balāghah: numerous sheep and separating livestock

Sheepskin and a skin bag

Early Arabic records سِقَاءٌ ضِئْنِيٌّ (siqāʾun ḍiʾniyyun) for a bag made from sheepskin.

Some explanations describe a large skin bag used to churn curdled milk.

A verse attributed to a poet named Ḥumayd says:

وَجَاءَتْ بِضِئْنِيٍّ كَأَنَّ دَوِيَّهُ
تَرَنُّمُ رَعْدٍ جَاوَبَتْهُ الرَّوَاعِدُ

wa-jāʾat bi-ḍiʾniyyin ka-anna dawiyyahu / tarannumu raʿdin jāwabat-hu al-rawāʿidu

Translation:

“She brought a sheepskin bag whose rumbling was like thunder answered by other thunder.”

Ḍiʾnī here describes the bag as made from sheepskin.

The poet compares the sound of working it to rumbling thunder.

The sheep’s name could thus describe a material or implement made from its skin.

Ḍaʾn itself does not mean a skin bag, a water vessel, or milk. These are products or implements associated with sheep.

Tāj al-ʿArūs attributes this verse to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr. Its composition date remains unclear, and the attribution alone does not establish it as a firmly dated pre-Islamic witness.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: the churning skin and attribution to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr

Figurative uses of ḍāʾin

Lisān al-ʿArab and Asās al-Balāghah also use رَجُلٌ ضَائِنٌ (rajulun ḍāʾinun) for a gentle man or someone with a soft, physically weak body.

Some reports describe someone maintaining a relatively good physique despite eating little.

رَمْلَةٌ ضَائِنَةٌ (ramlatun ḍāʾinatun) is also recorded for an expanse of soft sand.

A sheep’s softness or appearance supplies the basis of comparison with a person or ground in these uses.

The senses nevertheless do not express fixed features of every sheep.

Gentleness should also not be equated with moral weakness or lack of will. Assessing conduct depends on its own context and evidence.

These figurative uses illuminate Arabic’s range without being al-ḍaʾn’s direct Qur’anic meaning.

Asās al-Balāghah and Lisān al-ʿArab: figurative ḍāʾin

4. Poetic witnesses and early forms

The dictionaries record several collective or plural forms for sheep, including ḍaʾn, ḍaʾan, ḍaʾīn, ḍīn, and aḍʾun.

Some are attributed to particular Arabic dialects.

A verse quoted by Yaʿqūb in discussing transposed consonants says:

إِذَا مَا دَعَا نَعْمَانُ آضُنَ سَالِمٍ
عَلَنَّ وَإِنْ كَانَتْ مَذَانِبُهُ حُمْرَا

idhā mā daʿā naʿmānu āḍuna sālimin / ʿalanna wa-in kānat madhānibuhu ḥumrā

Cautious sense rendering, following the lexical explanation: “When Naʿmān calls Sālim’s sheep, they respond, though its watercourses are red.”

Its lexical explanation identifies āḍun as a transposed form of aḍʾun.

The witness broadly concerns sheep and the movement of livestock towards pasture.

The explanation describes abundant vegetation attracting buzzing insects. Shepherds hear them, recognize pasture, and drive their animals towards it.

The verse matters as a witness for the rare plural aḍʾun.

Sources vary in the wording of certain words, and the poet’s definite identity is unclear.

It should therefore not establish an exact history for every plural form or a certain theory of the root’s origin.

What is clear is the variety of early Arabic sheep names, while the Qur’an uses al-ḍaʾn.

Lisān al-ʿArab: āḍun, metathesis, and variant poetic readings

5. The Qur’anic use: mina al-ḍaʾni ithnayni

The root’s sole Qur’anic use is in Al-Anʿām:

ثَمَانِيَةَ أَزْوَاجٍ مِنَ الضَّأْنِ اثْنَيْنِ وَمِنَ الْمَعْزِ اثْنَيْنِ قُلْ آلذَّكَرَيْنِ حَرَّمَ أَمِ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ أَمَّا اشْتَمَلَتْ عَلَيْهِ أَرْحَامُ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ نَبِّئُونِي بِعِلْمٍ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

thamāniyata azwājin mina al-ḍaʾni ithnayni wa-mina al-maʿzi ithnayni qul āl-dhakarayni ḥarrama ami al-unthayayni ammā ishtamalat ʿalayhi arḥāmu al-unthayayni nabbiʾūnī bi-ʿilmin in kuntum ṣādiqīna

“Eight, in paired categories: two from sheep and two from goats. Say: Has He forbidden the two males or the two females, or what the wombs of the two females contain? Inform me with knowledge, if you are truthful.”

Al-Anʿām 6:143

The meaning of al-ḍaʾni

Al-ḍaʾn here names the sheep category.

The word follows the preposition min:

مِنَ الضَّأْنِ

mina al-ḍaʾni

“From sheep.”

Ithnayni, “two,” then identifies two individuals from that category.

A parallel expression follows for goats:

وَمِنَ الْمَعْزِ اثْنَيْنِ

wa-mina al-maʿzi ithnayni

“And two from goats.”

The construction places sheep and goats together and identifies two individuals from each.

Ithnayni: male and female

The verse continues with the question:

قُلْ آلذَّكَرَيْنِ حَرَّمَ أَمِ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

qul āl-dhakarayni ḥarrama ami al-unthayayni

“Say: Has He forbidden the two males or the two females?”

This clarifies that the two from each category are male and female.

There is one male sheep and one female, and likewise one male goat and one female.

The following verse repeats the structure for camels and cattle.

Across the two verses, thamāniyata azwājin therefore concerns four livestock categories, each represented by male and female.

Zawj here can name either complementary member. The expression should not be turned without evidence into eight pairs, or sixteen animals.

Study of Z W J

Al-ḍaʾn itself specifies neither sex nor a particular number. The male–female distinction comes from ithnayni and the following questions.

Do male and female sheep differ in the ruling?

The verse asks those declaring certain livestock forbidden:

Has God forbidden the two males, the two females, or what the females’ wombs contain?

The questions challenge religious categories lacking evidence.

Earlier verses discuss people imposing restrictions on crops and livestock, even distinguishing men’s and women’s access to what is inside the animals’ bellies.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 thus challenges the claim of divine prohibition rather than simply identifying sheep’s sex.

It ends:

نَبِّئُونِي بِعِلْمٍ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

nabbiʾūnī bi-ʿilmin in kuntum ṣādiqīna

“Inform me with knowledge, if you are truthful.”

Someone attributing a ruling to God must support the claim with evidence.

This message comes from the whole verse rather than ḍaʾn’s lexical meaning.

Ḍaʾn still names sheep; prohibition and the claimant’s responsibility emerge from the verbs and questions.

Al-Ṭabarī: male and female, eight paired members, and the challenge to prohibitions

6. Ḍaʾn among Qur’anic livestock

Al-Anʿām 6:142: livestock and their uses

The preceding verse states:

وَمِنَ الْأَنْعَامِ حَمُولَةً وَفَرْشًا كُلُوا مِمَّا رَزَقَكُمُ اللَّهُ

wa-mina al-anʿāmi ḥamūlatan wa-farshan kulū mimmā razaqakumu allāhu

“And among livestock, beasts of burden and smaller animals. Eat from what The God has provided for you.”

Al-Anʿām 6:142

The verse concerns livestock from which people benefit.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144 then name four categories: sheep, goats, camels, and cattle.

Ḍaʾn is one of the familiar domestic livestock categories in this setting.

The animal may be kept for food and other benefits.

Its name nevertheless does not mean food, meat, or milk: these are products and benefits associated with it.

Study of N ʿ M

Al-Anʿām 6:144: the question repeated for camels and cattle

The next verse states:

وَمِنَ الْإِبِلِ اثْنَيْنِ وَمِنَ الْبَقَرِ اثْنَيْنِ

wa-mina al-ibili ithnayni wa-mina al-baqari ithnayni

“And two from camels and two from cattle.”

The same question follows about forbidding males, females, or what the females’ wombs contain.

Claimants are then asked whether they were present when God issued such a command.

The repetition shows that the discussion is not confined to one livestock species.

It concerns claims of divine prohibition affecting provision and familiar livestock.

Mentioning ḍaʾn therefore does not establish a special ruling about the sheep’s inherent nature.

Sheep are one example in a discussion whose main question concerns the legitimacy of claimed prohibitions.

Al-Naḥl 16:80: using wool

Al-Naḥl states:

وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīnin

“And from their wool, fine hair, and hair, furnishings and enjoyment for a time.”

Al-Naḥl 16:80

Three kinds of livestock fibre are named.

Established Arabic usage associates aṣwāf with sheep’s wool.

Awbār chiefly concerns camel hair, and ashʿār here concerns goat hair.

This distinction connects clearly with ḍaʾn and maʿz in Al-Anʿām 6:143.

Arabic pastoral naming recognized sheep as wool-bearing and goats as hair-bearing livestock.

Ḍaʾn itself nevertheless does not mean wool. Wool belongs to Ṣ W F and is one benefit obtained from sheep.

The animal’s name and the material obtained from its body should remain distinct.

7. Ḍaʾn and lawful or forbidden use

Al-Anʿām 6:136, Al-Anʿām 6:137, Al-Anʿām 6:138, Al-Anʿām 6:139, and Al-Anʿām 6:140 discuss groups imposing religious divisions and particular prohibitions on livestock and crops.

They attributed shares to God and imagined partners, restricted the use of certain livestock, and made distinctions concerning males, females, or what animals carried in their bellies.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 directly tests these claims: has God forbidden males, females, or what females’ wombs contain?

Religious prohibition cannot be declared merely on the basis of habit, preference, aversion, or inherited custom.

Three matters nevertheless remain distinct: sheep themselves, their food and other benefits, and the religious ruling governing use.

Ḍ ʾ N names the sheep itself.

Its meat, milk, and wool have their own names and applicable rulings.

Permitted or forbidden uses require examination of legislative verses and their stated conditions.

The animal’s name thus neither establishes a particular prohibition nor makes every food-related condition irrelevant.

Al-Māʾidah 5:103 also names inherited livestock restrictions, such as baḥīrah, sāʾibah, waṣīlah, and ḥām, and denies their institution by God.

These verses clarify the boundary between personal choice in using food and attributing a general ruling to God.

The separate article “Lawful and Forbidden in the Qur’an” examines this subject in detail. For Ḍ ʾ N, it is enough to keep clear that the word directly names the animal rather than a legal ruling.

8. Comparison with related roots and words

Ḍ ʾ N (ḍaʾn) and M ʿ Z (maʿz)

Ḍaʾn concerns sheep and maʿz goats.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 places them together.

Early Arabic chiefly associated sheep with wool and goats with hair.

Both are domestic livestock whose meat, milk, skin, and other benefits people may use.

Similar economic uses or biological proximity nevertheless do not make their names identical.

“Sheep and goats” is therefore an appropriate translation.

Ḍ ʾ N (ḍaʾn) and GH N M (ghanam)

Arabic pastoral usage employed ghanam as a broader designation for sheep and goats.

Ḍaʾn names one category within it.

All ḍaʾn thus belongs to ghanam, but not every animal covered by ghanam is necessarily ḍaʾn, because the broader term also includes goats.

This matters for translation.

Ghanam should not always be restricted to wool-bearing sheep without examining the text.

Al-ḍaʾn in Al-Anʿām 6:143, by contrast, clearly stands against al-maʿz and identifies sheep.

Ḍ ʾ N (ḍaʾn) and N ʿ J (naʿjah)

Arabic naʿjah denotes a female sheep, an ewe.

Ṣād 38:23 states:

إِنَّ هَٰذَا أَخِي لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

inna hādhā akhī lahu tisʿun wa-tisʿūna naʿjatan wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidatun

“This brother of mine has ninety-nine ewes, while I have one ewe.”

The verse uses naʿjah rather than ḍaʾn.

Naʿjah specifies a female sheep, whereas ḍaʾn collectively covers males and females.

Al-ḍaʾn in Al-Anʿām 6:143 should therefore not be rendered exclusively as “ewes.”

Ḍ ʾ N (ḍaʾn) and K B SH (kabsh)

Arabic kabsh denotes a ram, an adult male sheep.

Ḍaʾn collectively names sheep.

A ram thus belongs to ḍaʾn, but ḍaʾn is not confined to rams.

Kabsh does not occur in the Qur’an as this animal’s name.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:107 mentions dhibḥin ʿaẓīmin, a great sacrifice, without itself naming a ram.

Narrative explanations identifying the animal should not replace the Qur’an’s actual wording.

Ḍ ʾ N (ḍaʾn) and Ṣ W F (ṣūf)

Ḍaʾn names the animal; ṣūf names its woolly covering.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 uses aṣwāfihā, “their wool.”

The families are plainly connected in pastoral life but differ in root and meaning.

Wool can be shorn and made into clothing or other articles, while ḍaʾn itself does not mean clothing, thread, or wool.

The animal should remain central to the root definition, with wool identified as a familiar characteristic and one of its benefits.

Study of Ṣ W F

Ḍ ʾ N (ḍaʾn) and Ḍ N N (ḍanna)

These roots have similar but different consonants.

Ḍ ʾ N contains hamzah and names sheep in Qur’anic usage.

Ḍ N N consists of ḍād, nūn, and nūn and concerns stinginess and withholding.

The reading bi-ḍanīnin in Al-Takwīr 81:24 belongs to that family.

Written or phonetic resemblance does not identify the root for sheep with stinginess or withholding.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

ضَأْن (ḍaʾn): sheep; the wool-bearing sheep category.

الضَّأْن (al-ḍaʾn): sheep; in Al-Anʿām 6:143, sheep as a livestock category.

ضَائِن (ḍāʾin): a male sheep or wool-bearing sheep.

ضَائِنَة (ḍāʾinah): a female sheep, an ewe.

ضَوَائِن (ḍawāʾin): ewes or female sheep.

ضَئِين (ḍaʾīn): sheep; another collective form.

أَضْؤُن (aḍʾun): sheep; another early plural.

أَضْأَنَ (aḍʾana): someone’s sheep became numerous.

ضِئْنِيّ (ḍiʾnī): pertaining to sheep; for a vessel, a sheepskin bag.

رَجُلٌ ضَائِنٌ (rajulun ḍāʾinun): figuratively, a gentle man or someone with a soft body, depending on context.

An appropriate rendering of Al-Anʿām 6:143 is:

مِنَ الضَّأْنِ اثْنَيْنِ وَمِنَ الْمَعْزِ اثْنَيْنِ

mina al-ḍaʾni ithnayni wa-mina al-maʿzi ithnayni

“Two from sheep and two from goats.”

This natural rendering preserves the distinction between the livestock categories.

The lexical explanation can clarify that the two are male and female, as the verse’s following questions show.

Al-ḍaʾn itself should nevertheless not be replaced with “ram and ewe,” “sacrificial sheep,” “mutton,” or “sheep’s wool.”

Those identify particular individuals, benefits, or uses associated with the animal.

10. Conclusion

Arabic Ḍ ʾ N collectively names wool-bearing sheep, distinguished from goats within the broader pastoral designation ghanam.

Ḍaʾn includes males and females. Ḍāʾin and ḍāʾinah name male and female individuals, and dialects and early sources record further plurals.

Related uses concern increasing sheep numbers, separating them from goats, making sheepskin implements, and comparisons involving softness.

These uses do not alter ḍaʾn’s principal meaning.

The Qur’an contains only al-ḍaʾn. In Al-Anʿām 6:143 it stands alongside al-maʿz, with a male and female of each category identified.

The context challenges restrictions people imposed on livestock and attributed to God.

The main question therefore concerns evidence and the legitimacy of a claimed prohibition, rather than ḍaʾn itself meaning lawful or forbidden.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Sheep; the wool-bearing livestock category within ghanam, distinguished from goats and including males and females.

Meaning in Al-Anʿām 6:143:

Al-ḍaʾni: sheep; one of the domestic livestock categories whose male and female are discussed in the verse.
