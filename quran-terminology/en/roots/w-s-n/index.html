Drowsiness, the heaviness of approaching sleep, and dozing: from early Arabic usage to the Qur’anic denial of any drowsiness in The God

Early Arabic W S N concerns drowsiness and the condition of approaching sleep or falling into light sleep. Wasan and sinah describe the slackness and heaviness accompanying approaching sleep: the eyes may grow heavy, attention to the surroundings diminish, and ordinary wakefulness give way to sleep.

Wasnān describes a drowsy person; wasnā is its feminine. In established Arabic usage, the description can concern someone not fully asleep whose eyes and behavior nevertheless show drowsiness.

Arabic distinguishes sinah from nawm. Sinah more often concerns drowsiness, dozing, and the onset or lighter state of sleep; nawm is the general name for sleep and can include a fuller sleeping state. Their semantic fields overlap, however, and should not be separated by an absolute boundary in every use.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as sinatun in Al-Baqarah 2:255. After describing Himself as al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm, The God states:

لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ

lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawm

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep.”

The phrase denies drowsiness and sleep of The God. In context, this denial relates to His life, sustaining governance, knowledge, sovereignty, and uninterrupted preservation of the heavens and earth.

Human drowsiness is a natural condition, not inherently a sin or moral weakness. Elsewhere the Qur’an even associates drowsiness with believers’ security and calm. In Al-Baqarah 2:255, however, this condition of beings that need sleep is denied of The God.

1. Early Arabic usage and lexical evidence

Wasan: the onset of sleep and drowsy heaviness

Wasina al-rajulu yawsanu wasanan describes someone becoming drowsy or falling into light sleep.

Early dictionaries explain wasan through nuʿās and awwalu al-nawm: drowsiness and the beginning of sleep.

Some lexical accounts describe the heaviness of sleep; others describe drowsiness without full sleep.

This variation shows that Arabic has words with closely related ranges for the transition from waking to sleeping.

After hours of work or travel, for example, someone may feel heavy eyelids and reduced attention. They can still hear and respond but lack their earlier alertness and concentration.

Such a condition falls within the field of wasan.

If it continues, they may briefly doze or enter a longer sleep.

This does not establish that everyone passes through identical stages in a fixed linguistic sequence. Arabic words express familiar experiences of sleep and drowsiness; they are not necessarily a precise medical classification of sleep stages.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Aʿshā: nuʿās and wasan

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, Maymūn ibn Qays, known as a pre-Islamic poet, contains these lines:

تُعَاطِي الضَّجِيعَ إِذَا أَقْبَلَتْ

tuʿāṭī al-ḍajīʿa idhā aqbalat

بُعَيْدَ النُّعَاسِ وَقَبْلَ الْوَسَنْ

buʿayda al-nuʿāsi wa-qabla al-wasan

Translation:

“She offers to her bedfellow when she turns toward him, shortly after nuʿās and before wasan.” The following line compares what she offers from her lips to wine; the quoted line alone should not be treated as a complete account of the image.

The poet describes a time near sleep, using nuʿās and wasan for the condition then experienced.

The witness matters because wasan appears as a recognizable state associated with sleep and drowsiness.

The two words lie within a closely related period near sleep but are separately named.

Other lexical explanations sometimes call wasan the beginning of sleep and nuʿās a degree of drowsiness. The order in one poem should therefore not become a universal rule for their sequence throughout Arabic.

Both words clearly belong to the field of drowsiness, and wasan was intelligible in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The line is transmitted under al-Aʿshā’s name in early lexical accounts, with variants in the phrase describing the time of sleep. It is valuable lexical evidence, but neither a fixed temporal boundary between nuʿās and wasan nor an exact independent date for its composition is established here.

ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ: drowsy but not asleep

A poem by the Umayyad poet ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ states:

وَسْنَانُ أَقْصَدَهُ النُّعَاسُ فَرَنَّقَتْ

wasnānu aqṣadahu al-nuʿāsu fa-rannaqat

فِي عَيْنِهِ سِنَةٌ وَلَيْسَ بِنَائِمِ

fī ʿaynihi sinatun wa-laysa bi-nāʾimi

Translation:

“Drowsy, struck by somnolence, with a haze of drowsiness hovering in his eye, yet not asleep.”

The surrounding poem compares the beloved’s eyes to those of a young wild bovine, one of the jaʾādhir of Jāsim. Calling it simply a gazelle would obscure the witness’s more specific wording.

Wasnān describes being drowsy.

Nuʿās is the condition that has affected him.

Sinah appears in his eye, yet the poet explicitly says he is not nāʾim, asleep.

The witness illustrates a subtle distinction: drowsiness and heavy eyes can be present without full sleep.

ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ belongs to the Umayyad period, so this is not pre-Islamic evidence. It illustrates the root’s established usage in the first Islamic centuries.

Together with al-Aʿshā’s verse, it shows the close relationship of wasan, nuʿās, and nawm without requiring a rigid, exceptionless classification.

Wasnān and wasnā: drowsiness visible in the face

Wasnān is recorded for a drowsy man and wasnā for a drowsy woman.

Wasin and wasinah are also adjectival forms of the family.

In literary descriptions, imraʾatun wasnā can describe a woman with heavy eyes and a languid or half-drowsy gaze.

Such a gaze may be considered attractive in descriptions of beauty.

Elsewhere the condition accompanies slackness, indolence, or inactivity.

Depending on subject and context, drowsiness can therefore receive positive, neutral, or negative associations.

Beauty, laziness, and dignity are not essential meanings of W S N.

The word itself describes drowsiness; evaluation comes from context.

Other Arabic uses

Istawsana is recorded for becoming drowsy.

Tawassana fulānan can mean approaching someone during their sleep or drowsiness.

In poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī, tawassana appears in an image of rain and clouds, comparing winds fertilizing heavy clouds with animal mating.

This is a particular poetic extension of the verb, not a meaning to import into Qur’anic sinah.

Some Arabic reports also use wasina for a faint-like condition caused by the air in a well. Its reduced awareness resembles drowsiness, but being overcome by harmful air is not identical with natural dozing.

In mā huwa min hammī wa-lā min wasanī, wasan is explained as a need, wish, or concern of the speaker.

The expression is recorded lexically, but its historical relationship to drowsiness is uncertain.

The family thus has subsidiary uses, while the clear sense of drowsiness and approaching sleep remains the basis for sinah in Al-Baqarah 2:255.

2. Linguistic forms and the structure of sinah

The Qur’an uses only the noun sinatun from W S N.

Other Arabic forms include wasina, yawsanu, wasan, wasnah, wasnān, wasnā, istawsana, and tawassana; they do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

Why does sinah belong to W S N?

At first sight, sinah might appear to come from a root beginning with sīn, since the Qur’anic form has no visible wāw.

Arabic morphological analysis nevertheless assigns it to W S N.

In the traditional morphological explanation, the initial wāw has been omitted and the final tāʾ is compensatory. This is an analysis of the word’s form, not proof of a dated historical sequence.

A comparable analysis is familiar in some nouns from initial-wāw roots, such as ʿidah from W ʿ D.

The final tāʾ is therefore not a root consonant, and the missing wāw does not establish an independent root.

This places the Qur’anic form correctly within W S N.

Sinah, sanah, and sunnah

Without vowel marks, all three may appear as سنة, yet their forms and meanings differ.

Sinah, with kasrah on sīn, concerns drowsiness and dozing and belongs to W S N.

Sanah, with fatḥah, means a year or period of time and belongs in discussions of the year-related roots, particularly S N W and S N H.

Sunnah, with ḍammah and doubled nūn, means a way, practice, or established pattern and belongs to S N N.

For example, alfa sanatin in Al-Baqarah 2:96 means a thousand years, not a thousand dozes.

Sinatun in Al-Baqarah 2:255, by contrast, concerns approaching sleep, not the passage of a year.

Qur’anic sunnata allāh concerns The God’s established way, not the drowsiness denoted by sinah.

These words illustrate how unvocalized writing can conceal genuine differences of root and meaning.

Central meaning

The sleep-related central meaning of W S N can be stated as follows:

Drowsiness and the heaviness of approaching sleep, when ordinary wakefulness diminishes and a person approaches dozing or light sleep.

This relates to nawm and nuʿās without making them one word.

Drowsiness itself means neither sin, deliberate neglect of responsibility, nor necessarily deep and prolonged sleep.

3. Sinah in the Throne Verse: denying drowsiness of The God

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Baqarah 2:255:

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ

allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa al-ḥayyu al-qayyūmu lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawmun lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi man dhā alladhī yashfaʿu ʿindahu illā bi-idhnihi yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum wa-lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shayʾin min ʿilmihi illā bi-mā shāʾa wasiʿa kursiyyuhu al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā wa-huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Baqarah 2:255

Translation:

“The God—there is no deity but Him, the Living, the Sustainer. Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth. Who can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is before them and what is behind them; they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and preserving them does not burden Him. He is the Exalted, the Great.”

Sinatun follows the verb taʾkhudhuhu.

It is an indefinite feminine noun and the verb’s subject.

Nawmun is also indefinite and nominative, coordinated with sinatun by wāw; the repeated lā reinforces the negation of both conditions.

The pronoun hu in taʾkhudhuhu refers to The God.

The phrase means that neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes The God.

The meaning of lā taʾkhudhuhu

Taʾkhudhuhu comes from ʾ KH DH and here concerns a condition overtaking someone.

Arabic and Persian can similarly say that sleep “took” someone or weariness “overcame” them.

This does not give sleep hands with which to seize a person physically; it describes a condition arising and taking hold.

Al-Baqarah 2:255 negates this construction.

Drowsiness, even as dozing or the heaviness of approaching sleep, does not overtake The God.

The phrase does not merely deny voluntary sleeping; it denies being overtaken by a condition experienced by beings capable of sleep.

For beings needing sleep, drowsiness can arise without a prior decision. Someone may wish to stay awake yet experience heavy eyelids and diminished attention through fatigue or the need for sleep.

The verse denies such a condition of The God.

Why are sinah and nawm placed together?

Lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawm places two different words together.

Sinah emphasizes drowsiness and approaching sleep.

Nawm expresses sleeping itself.

Their combination prevents the denial from being restricted to full sleep.

If only sleeping were denied, a human reader might distinguish full sleep from slight drowsiness.

The Qur’an instead denies both separately.

It thus attributes no degree of drowsiness or sleep to The God.

This does not establish that Arabic sinah and nawm were entirely separate stages without overlap. Lexical evidence shows variation in the boundaries of drowsiness, dozing, and light sleep.

What is clear is the comprehensive denial of drowsiness and sleep.

The relationship to al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm

Before denying drowsiness, the verse states:

الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ

al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“The Living, the Sustainer.”

These descriptions matter for the next sentence.

Ḥayy concerns life.

Qayyūm belongs to Q W M and here describes The God as self-subsisting, on whom the sustaining and governance of beings depend.

The denial of drowsiness and sleep follows these descriptions.

In beings needing sleep, ordinary awareness of their surroundings and the ability to attend consciously to affairs diminish at times. A sleeping person does not have the same immediate awareness and capacity to attend to ongoing tasks as when awake; this describes practical limitation, not a general ruling about moral responsibility.

The God is presented as the Sustainer and governor, whom drowsiness and sleep do not overtake.

The denial is therefore connected with His sustaining governance, rather than an isolated statement about sleeping.

No bodily structure or biological nature of The God should be inferred from this.

The Qur’an denies sleep and drowsiness of Him. This sentence does not offer a biological account of divine nature, and physiological models of created beings should not be imposed upon it.

The denial of sleep and uninterrupted divine knowledge

The verse continues:

يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ

yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“He knows what is before them and what is behind them.”

This expresses the breadth of divine knowledge.

Human sleep can alter conscious awareness of surrounding events. Something may happen while someone sleeps and become known to them only after waking.

The Throne Verse places the denial of sleep alongside divine knowledge.

Their connection indicates that The God’s knowledge and governance do not depend on alternating periods of wakefulness and rest.

This follows from the verse’s combined descriptions, not from sinah meaning ignorance or forgetfulness.

Sinah means drowsiness; knowledge is expressed through a different root and clause.

Preserving the heavens and earth without burden

Toward the verse’s end:

وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا

wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“Preserving them does not burden Him.”

This relates to the denial of drowsiness and sleep.

Prolonged human work and vigilance can lead to fatigue and a need for rest and sleep.

In the Throne Verse, neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes The God, and preserving the heavens and earth does not burden Him.

Yaʾūduhu comes from ʾ W D and here concerns burdening or weighing heavily upon someone. Rendering it through weariness expresses the burden’s effect, rather than making ʾ W D identical with sleep.

Thus lā yaʾūduhu denies burdensome difficulty, while the clause containing sinah denies drowsiness.

The meanings are related but distinct.

A person may be tired without being drowsy, or become drowsy without prior heavy physical labor.

The verse denies each limitation in its own place.

Would denying sinah not also deny sleep?

If drowsiness is the onset of sleep, is denying it alone enough to deny sleep?

Ordinarily drowsiness may precede sleep, but the sequence is not experienced or expressed identically in every circumstance.

By naming sinah and nawm separately, the Qur’an explicitly denies both.

Neither word needs to be treated as redundant or assigned a meaning remote from established usage.

Naming two related conditions can clarify and complete the scope of a negation.

Here, neither the slightest drowsiness nor sleep itself is attributed to The God.

4. Comparison with neighboring roots

W S N and N ʿ S: wasan and nuʿās

Both concern drowsiness and the condition near sleep.

Established uses of wasan express the heaviness of approaching sleep and dozing.

Nuʿās also concerns drowsiness, diminished alertness, and sometimes light sleep.

In ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ’s poem, both occur in an image of drowsiness without full sleep.

Qur’anic nuʿās occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:154 and Al-Anfāl 8:11.

Both associate it with amanah, security and reassurance.

Āl ʿImrān describes drowsiness after grief and difficult fighting conditions. Al-Anfāl likewise places drowsiness among divine blessings in the difficult circumstances preceding battle.

It is therefore not necessarily weakness, negligence, or irresponsibility in these verses.

Al-Baqarah 2:255, by contrast, denies sinah of The God.

A natural human condition can provide comfort in particular circumstances yet be denied of The God in relation to His sustaining governance.

Forqan’s study of N ʿ S examines nuʿās more fully.

Study of N ʿ S

W S N and N W M: drowsiness and sleep

Nawm is the general name for sleep, during which ordinary waking awareness and relations with the surroundings change.

Wasan more often emphasizes drowsiness, dozing, and approaching sleep.

Al-Baqarah 2:255 places them in one sentence without making them identical.

Al-Furqān 25:47 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 associate human sleep with subāt: rest and a temporary break from ordinary activity.

Al-Rūm 30:23 also presents sleeping by night and day as a divine sign.

Nawm is therefore part of the natural order of human life.

The Throne Verse denies that sleep of The God.

This distinction should not become a condemnation of human sleep. Needing sleep reflects biological limitation and dependence, not inherently sin or moral failure.

Forqan’s study of N W M separately examines sleep and its various uses.

Study of N W M

W S N and R Q D: drowsiness and sleeping

R Q D concerns sleeping or being in a state of sleep or repose.

Al-Kahf 18:18 says that one would think the cave’s inhabitants awake, although they were ruqūd, asleep.

Ruqūd is contrasted with wakefulness.

Wasan more strongly emphasizes drowsiness and approaching sleep.

Someone may be wasnān while still regarded as awake.

Ruqūd in the cave account, by contrast, concerns their sleeping state.

The word itself does not specify the years involved or all the sleep’s characteristics. Those details come from other verses in the account.

W S N and S B T: drowsiness and repose

Sleep-related subāt concerns rest and a temporary interruption of ordinary activity.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 describes human sleep as subāt.

Wasan concerns drowsiness and the onset of sleep.

Both can belong to one natural experience without meaning the same thing.

Subāt describes sleep’s role in repose; sinah in Al-Baqarah 2:255 names a condition denied of The God.

W S N and GH F L: drowsiness and inattention

Ghaflah concerns inattention or overlooking something.

Wasan concerns drowsiness.

Drowsiness can diminish attention, but not every instance of inattention arises from it.

Someone fully awake may neglect an important responsibility despite having awareness and opportunity.

Conversely, someone may become naturally drowsy after prolonged activity and fulfilling responsibilities without moral negligence.

Some figurative Arabic uses extend sinah to heedlessness. This does not make sinah in Al-Baqarah 2:255 directly synonymous with moral negligence.

The verse retains the basic drowsiness sense; its denial amid divine knowledge and sustaining governance has wider implications for continuous care.

W S N and F T R: drowsiness and slackening

Futūr concerns slackness, subsiding intensity, and reduced activity.

Drowsiness may accompany slackening capacity and attention.

Not every slackening is drowsiness, however.

Activity may decrease after prolonged effort, or a condition’s intensity subside, without sleep being involved.

Wasan primarily concerns approaching sleep, whereas futūr applies more broadly.

Slackness is therefore a possible feature of wasan, not evidence that the roots are synonymous everywhere.

5. Human drowsiness and divine sustaining governance

Sleep and drowsiness are natural parts of human life.

People use physical and mental capacities, need rest, and may fall asleep.

Sleep can provide repose and renewed capacity.

The Qur’an’s nawm and nuʿās passages likewise relate these conditions to blessing, comfort, and security.

Human capacity and attention, unlike divine governance, are limited.

Sleep may leave someone unaware of an event or unable to act at that moment.

Fatigue may bring a need for rest.

These natural limitations show human dependence and need.

Al-Baqarah 2:255 describes The God as Living and Sustainer: neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him, nor does preserving the heavens and earth burden Him.

Thus the denial of sinah is more than an isolated statement about a short doze: among the verse’s descriptions, it contributes to the expression of uninterrupted divine knowledge, sustenance, and care.

This implication should not become a bodily account of divine nature.

Nor should human drowsiness become a moral defect or weakness of faith.

What belongs to humanity’s natural need is denied of The God here.

This clarifies the distinction between limited, dependent human life and divine sustaining governance.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For sinah in Al-Baqarah 2:255, the Persian equivalents of “dozing,” “drowsiness,” and “light sleep” deserve consideration.

“Drowsiness” has a broader range and makes approaching sleep clear.

The Persian word for “doze” is brief and natural, suggesting drowsiness together with briefly slipping into sleep.

“Light sleep” may suit some uses, but makes the distinction between approaching sleep and sleeping less clear.

“Heedlessness,” “unawareness,” “laziness,” and “weak faith” are not direct equivalents.

A suitable natural rendering is:

“Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep.”

The current Persian Forqan verse likewise uses the brief wording meaning “Neither a doze takes Him nor sleep.”

For a more precise lexical explanation:

“Sinah: drowsiness and the heaviness of approaching sleep; a condition near dozing or light sleep.”

This preserves the distinction from nawm.

Non-Qur’anic forms can be rendered contextually: wasina, “became drowsy”; wasnān, “drowsy”; wasnā, the feminine description; and istawsana, “became affected by drowsiness.”

Sinah should not become “an interruption in governing the universe” in the verse translation. That implication comes from the verse as a whole, not the root’s independent meaning.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic W S N concerns drowsiness, dozing, and the onset or lighter state of sleep.

Wasan and sinah name the condition; wasnān and wasnā describe drowsy individuals.

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā relates wasan to nuʿās and the condition near sleep in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition.

In the Umayyad poet ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ’s verse, wasnān likewise describes heavy, drowsy eyes whose owner is not yet fully asleep.

These witnesses clarify the semantic field, but the boundaries and sequence of nuʿās, wasan, and nawm vary across lexical accounts.

W S N occurs only in Al-Baqarah 2:255, as sinatun.

Sinah stands alongside nawm, and both are denied of The God.

This follows al-ḥayyu al-qayyūm and precedes the descriptions of sovereignty, extensive knowledge, and preservation of the heavens and earth.

Together, the verse makes clear that drowsiness and sleep, which can diminish human attention and accompany a need for rest, do not overtake The God.

Elsewhere the Qur’an presents nuʿās and nawm among blessings and sources of human repose. Drowsiness therefore has no inherently negative moral value.

Sinah must also remain distinct from sanah, “year,” and sunnah, “way” or “established practice.” Similar spelling does not establish one root or meaning.

The final definition is:

W S N denotes drowsiness and the heaviness of approaching sleep, when ordinary wakefulness diminishes toward dozing or light sleep. Sinah names this condition and is denied of The God alongside nawm in Al-Baqarah 2:255. The denial relates in context to His life, sustaining governance, knowledge, and uninterrupted care.

For Persian Forqan, the equivalent of “doze” is brief and natural, while “drowsiness and the onset of sleep” can clarify the root’s meaning in the lexical explanation.
