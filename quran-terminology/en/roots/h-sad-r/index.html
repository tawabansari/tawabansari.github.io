1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-consonant Arabic root Ḥ Ṣ R concerns preventing, restricting, placing in difficulty, and obstructing movement or an undertaking. Sometimes prevention is imposed externally, as through a siege or an obstacle blocking a route; sometimes it arises from a person’s physical condition or resources; and sometimes it concerns the inner life, as in constriction of the breast or refraining from something one could do.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an, in six verses across five surahs. Its uses show that ḥaṣr is not limited to military siege, and that its derivatives cannot all be restricted to one external situation.

Important forms of this family include:

  • حَصَرَ / يَحْصُرُ (ḥaṣara / yaḥṣuru): to restrict, prevent, or besiege. Qur’anic: yes, in the imperative wa-uḥṣurūhum in Al-Tawbah 9:5.
  • حَصِرَ / يَحْصَرُ (ḥaṣira / yaḥṣaru): to become constrained, unable to proceed, or limited in capacity. Qur’anic: yes, as ḥaṣirat in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90.
  • أَحْصَرَ / يُحْصِرُ (aḥṣara / yuḥṣiru): to prevent movement or an undertaking. Qur’anic: yes, in the passive uḥṣirtum in Al-Baqarah 2:196 and uḥṣirū in Al-Baqarah 2:273.
  • حَصُور (ḥaṣūr): someone who refrains or is kept from something, particularly in matters of desire and sexual relations. Qur’anic: yes, describing John in Āl ʿImrān 3:39.
  • حَصِير (ḥaṣīr): a confinement or enclosing place; in another use, a woven mat. Qur’anic: yes, in Al-Isrāʾ 17:8.
  • حَصْر (ḥaṣr): prevention, restriction, or siege. Qur’anic: no, as an independent verbal noun.
  • حِصَار (ḥiṣār): a siege or situation in which routes of movement or access are blocked. Qur’anic: no, in this nominal form.
  • حَاصَرَ / مُحَاصَرَة (ḥāṣara / muḥāṣarah): to besiege or constrain from several directions. Qur’anic: no, in Form III.
  • مَحْصُور (maḥṣūr): prevented, confined, or restricted. Qur’anic: no, in this form.
  • مُحْصَر (muḥṣar): someone whom an obstacle prevents from reaching a destination or performing an undertaking. Qur’anic: no, in this nominal form, although its related passive verb occurs.
  • حَصِر (ḥaṣir): inwardly constrained, faltering in speech, or unable to proceed with something. Qur’anic: no, in this adjectival form.
  • حَصِيرَة (ḥaṣīrah): an enclosed place or a place for collecting and keeping something. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِنْحَصَرَ / اِنْحِصَار (inḥaṣara / inḥiṣār): to become restricted or confined. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِحْتَصَرَ (iḥtaṣara): in certain early uses, to fasten or restrain something. Qur’anic: no.

Morphologically, two Qur’anic occurrences are Form I verbs, two are Form IV verbs, and two are the nouns ḥaṣūr and ḥaṣīr.

Despite sharing a root, these forms do not always have the same semantic role. In Al-Baqarah 2:196, someone is prevented from continuing an undertaking; in Al-Tawbah 9:5, a group is commanded to besiege others; in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90, people’s breasts are constrained about entering a war; and in Āl ʿImrān 3:39, ḥaṣūr praises John.

Root study should therefore begin with the shared meaning while examining each derivative separately.

2. The linguistic core and early Arabic witnesses

Ḥaṣr: preventing and restricting capacity

Arabic ḥaṣarahu describes one person restricting, confining, or preventing another from movement and action.

In military usage, ḥaṣarahu al-ʿaduww means that the enemy constrained a person and restricted routes of movement.

This concerns siege, but is not exclusive to war. Something can prevent a person from traveling, speaking, fulfilling a responsibility, or reaching a destination without an enemy laying siege.

Ibn Fāris’ analysis in Maqāyīs al-Lughah brings together al-jamʿ, al-ḥabs, and al-manʿ: gathering, detention, and prevention.

Their common aspect is movement being restricted or something being placed within a specified limit.

“Gathering” should nevertheless not replace ḥaṣr in every Qur’anic use. When the Qur’an describes people being prevented or a group being besieged, it does not directly mean assembling people.

A person can, for example, be confined somewhere without anyone having gathered them for an assembly. Conversely, people can be together in one place without being confined.

Gathering and confining are therefore connected in some uses, but not absolute synonyms.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: restriction, detention, and prevention

External and inward restriction

An important feature of this root is that the obstacle can be external or internal.

Military siege is external restriction. The enemy may block routes of passage or limit the possibility of leaving or reaching a destination.

By contrast, illness, incapacity, hesitation, or psychological pressure may prevent a person from proceeding.

Ḥaṣira ṣadruhu concerns constriction of the breast. It can express feeling constrained, blocked, or unable to act in relation to something.

Ḥaṣira fī kalāmihi is also used of someone faltering in speech or whose tongue cannot speak fluently.

The mouth or tongue need not be physically blocked; the person’s avenue of expression is, as it were, restricted.

This extension from an external obstacle to inward constraint is recognized in Arabic.

It does not establish, however, that every Qur’anic use concerns a psychological condition. Al-Tawbah 9:5 concerns actual siege, rather than merely feeling confined.

The material image: enclosing and blocking a route

To approach the root’s meaning, imagine a person whose routes out of a place are blocked from several directions.

The person may retain the ability to move, but cannot freely reach every desired destination.

This distinction matters: confinement need not mean losing all strength and capacity; the use of that capacity may instead be restricted.

The same image appears in the siege of a city. Its residents may still have strength, implements, and resources, while routes of exit, entry, or supply are restricted.

Physical uses of the root also describe interruption of certain natural bodily functions, including urinary or bowel retention.

Ḥaṣīr also denotes a mat whose strands or components are woven together.

The connection with collecting its components can be explained: the strands are held within a bounded, connected structure.

This image must not become the certain origin of every meaning, however. Available evidence cannot establish that besieging people developed from matmaking, or the reverse.

What is clearer is the shared field of restriction, enclosure, prevention, and blocked possibilities of movement or action.

Early Arabic witnesses

A verse attributed to Ibn Mayyādah reads:

وَمَا هَجْرُ لَيْلَى أَنْ تَكُونَ تَبَاعَدَتْ
عَلَيْكَ وَلَا أَنْ أَحْصَرَتْكَ شُغُولُ

wa-mā hajru Laylā an takūna tabāʿadat / ʿalayka wa-lā an aḥṣaratka shughūlu

Meaning: “Your separation from Laylā is not because she has become distant from you, nor because occupations have held you back.”

Aḥṣaratka shughūl describes occupations preventing someone from reaching a destination or carrying out an undertaking.

The obstacle is not an enemy besieging a route, but life’s occupations and concerns.

This witness matters for the broader meaning of aḥṣara. Ibn Mayyādah, however, lived after Islam, and the verse should not be presented as certain pre-Islamic evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ibn Mayyādah’s witness and ḥaṣr versus iḥṣār

In Labīd’s Muʿallaqah, a verse describes his horse and compares its neck to a tall palm:

أَسْهَلْتُ وَانْتَصَبَتْ كَجِذْعِ مُنِيفَةٍ
جَرْدَاءَ يَحْصَرُ دُونَهَا جُرَّامُهَا

ashaltu wa-intaṣabat ka-jidhʿi munīfatin / jardāʾa yaḥṣaru dūnahā jurrāmuhā

Meaning: “I came down to level ground, and the horse raised its neck like the trunk of a tall, bare palm whose fruit pickers are unable to reach it.” The palm is a comparison for the horse’s raised neck, rather than the independent subject of the entire verse.

Yaḥṣaru here describes inability to proceed and constrained capacity in the face of difficult access. Lexical transmissions also have aʿraḍtu instead of ashaltu and ṣurrāmuhā instead of jurrāmuhā. The reading here follows the Dīwān and Al-Zawzanī’s commentary.

Labīd lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without establishing when this particular verse was composed, it should therefore not be confidently labeled pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Labīd’s Dīwān, Muʿallaqah: ashaltu and jurrāmuhā in the description of the horse

Another verse, by Jarīr, reads:

وَلَقَدْ تَسَقَّطَنِي الْوُشَاةُ فَصَادَفُوا
حَصِرًا بِسِرِّكِ يَا أُمَيْمُ ضَنِينَا

wa-laqad tasaqqaṭanī al-wushātu fa-ṣādafū / ḥaṣiran bi-sirriki yā Umaymu ḍanīnan

Meaning: “The talebearers tried to draw words from me, but found me restrained and closely guarding your secret, O Umaym.”

Ḥaṣiran here describes a person keeping a secret and refraining from disclosing it.

This belongs to inward restraint, rather than military siege.

Jarīr was also an Umayyad poet; this is evidence from early Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation.

Asās al-Balāghah: Jarīr’s witness on keeping a secret

Together the witnesses show that ḥaṣr can concern an external obstacle, obstructing occupations, inability to proceed with something, and keeping something within oneself.

The uses are related, but each verse’s meaning must be determined from morphology and context.

3. Uḥṣirtum: being prevented from completing ḥajj and ʿumrah

Al-Baqarah 2:196

Verse text

وَأَتِمُّوا الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ لِلَّهِ فَإِنْ أُحْصِرْتُمْ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ وَلَا تَحْلِقُوا رُءُوسَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْهَدْيُ مَحِلَّهُ فَمَنْ كَانَ مِنْكُمْ مَرِيضًا أَوْ بِهِ أَذًى مِنْ رَأْسِهِ فَفِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ فَإِذَا أَمِنْتُمْ فَمَنْ تَمَتَّعَ بِالْعُمْرَةِ إِلَى الْحَجِّ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ فِي الْحَجِّ وَسَبْعَةٍ إِذَا رَجَعْتُمْ تِلْكَ عَشَرَةٌ كَامِلَةٌ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ أَهْلُهُ حَاضِرِي الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

wa-atimmū al-ḥajja wa-al-ʿumrata li-Allāhi fa-in uḥṣirtum fa-mā istaysara mina al-hadyi wa-lā taḥliqū ruʾūsakum ḥattā yablugha al-hadyu maḥillahu fa-man kāna minkum marīḍan aw bihi adhan min raʾsihi fa-fidyatun min ṣiyāmin aw ṣadaqatin aw nusukin fa-idhā amintum fa-man tamattaʿa bi-al-ʿumrati ilā al-ḥajji fa-mā istaysara mina al-hadyi fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyāmin fī al-ḥajji wa-sabʿatin idhā rajaʿtum tilka ʿasharatun kāmilatun dhālika li-man lam yakun ahluhu ḥāḍirī al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-iʿlamū anna Allāha shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

English translation

Complete ḥajj and ʿumrah for The God. If you are prevented, then whatever offering is readily available; do not shave your heads until the offering reaches its place. Whoever among you is ill or has an affliction of the head owes compensation through fasting, charity, or rites. When you are secure, whoever benefits from ʿumrah until ḥajj, then whatever offering is readily available; whoever finds none should fast three days during ḥajj and seven upon returning. That is ten complete days. This concerns one whose family does not reside at the Sacred Mosque. Be mindful of The God, and know that The God is severe in retribution.

Examining the use

Uḥṣirtum is a Form IV perfect passive verb.

The passive matters because the verse does not say that the addressees voluntarily abandoned ḥajj or ʿumrah. An obstacle has prevented them from continuing or completing the undertaking.

This meaning connects with the verse’s opening:

“وَأَتِمُّوا الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ لِلَّهِ.”

The command is to complete ḥajj and ʿumrah, immediately followed by the possibility of being prevented.

The verse thus recognizes both completing the rites and a real obstacle disrupting their fulfillment.

Being prevented from doing something differs from not wanting to do it.

Someone may wish to complete a responsibility and even have prepared for it, yet be unable to continue as planned because of an external obstacle or a condition beyond ordinary control.

Uḥṣirtum should therefore first be understood as “you are prevented.” Identifying the obstacle is a further stage of inquiry.

Does iḥṣār mean only an enemy siege?

Arabic lexical explanations record an old disagreement about ḥaṣara and aḥṣara.

Some lexicographers distinguish ḥaṣarahu al-ʿaduww, an enemy blocking a person’s route, from aḥṣarahu al-maraḍ, illness or an obstacle preventing travel or an undertaking.

Others regard the two forms as close in the field of prevention and allow both for different kinds of obstacle.

An entirely certain boundary cannot therefore restrict ḥaṣr always to an enemy and iḥṣār always to illness.

Lexical evidence itself shows that speakers and scholars did not draw this boundary uniformly.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ, and Maqāyīs: lexical disagreement on ḥaṣr and iḥṣār

In Al-Baqarah 2:196, the word itself does not specify the obstacle either.

Fa-idhā amintum shows the contextual significance of security and the end of an insecure situation. Illness and affliction of the head are also mentioned in the same verse.

These conditions do not necessarily have exactly the same ruling and cause. Illness or head affliction is addressed with compensation in another part of the verse, and should not be wholly identified with iḥṣār without examination.

Understanding iḥṣār’s scope requires distinguishing the ruling on being prevented from completing ḥajj and ʿumrah from compensation for illness or head affliction.

The verse addresses both, but their descriptions are not identical.

Iḥṣār and an external obstacle

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 describes people barring believers from the Sacred Mosque and preventing the offering from reaching its place.

A blocked route to the House and obstruction of rites are consequently real Qur’anic concerns.

That verse’s verb belongs to another root, however. Uḥṣirtum should therefore not be defined solely from one historical event, nor should other obstacles be arbitrarily excluded.

Forqan’s published article on ḥajj also distinguishes the ability required to begin a journey from the situation of someone prevented while undertaking the rites.

Ḥajj in the Qur’an

This distinction matters for Al-Baqarah 2:196. Someone unable to begin traveling need not be in the same position as someone who has begun ḥajj or ʿumrah and then encounters an obstacle.

Lexically, iḥṣār concerns being prevented; the scope of the particular ḥajj ruling must nevertheless be determined from the full verse and related passages.

Lexical conclusion

Uḥṣirtum in Al-Baqarah 2:196 means “you are prevented” or “an obstacle prevents you from continuing.”

It is neither confined to enemy siege nor arbitrarily extended to every small difficulty or feeling of incapacity.

What is clear is a real obstacle to completing an undertaking already assumed.

4. Uḥṣirū: restricted movement and earning a livelihood

Al-Baqarah 2:273

Verse text

لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الَّذِينَ أُحْصِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ تَعْرِفُهُمْ بِسِيمَاهُمْ لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ

li-al-fuqarāʾi alladhīna uḥṣirū fī sabīli Allāhi lā yastaṭīʿūna ḍarban fī al-arḍi yaḥsabuhumu al-jāhilu aghniyāʾa mina al-taʿaffufi taʿrifuhum bi-sīmāhum lā yasʾalūna al-nāsa ilḥāfan wa-mā tunfiqū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha bihi ʿalīmun

English translation

For the needy who have been restricted in the way of The God, unable to travel through the land. One unaware of their situation thinks them wealthy because of their restraint. You recognize them by their signs; they do not press people for help. Whatever good you spend, The God knows it.

Examining the use

Like uḥṣirtum in Al-Baqarah 2:196, uḥṣirū is a passive Form IV verb.

The subject matter, however, differs.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 concerned prevention in ḥajj and ʿumrah. Here the needy face restrictions in the way of The God.

The following expression clarifies the restriction:

“لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ.”

Arabic ḍarb fī al-arḍ concerns movement and travel through the land. In some Qur’anic contexts such travel seeks livelihood and the bounty of The God.

The verse therefore describes people unable to move about freely to attend to life’s affairs.

Uḥṣirū itself does not specify the restriction’s precise cause.

A responsibility they have assumed, their circumstances, or an external obstacle may have restricted movement and earning a livelihood.

Identifying a historical instance requires independent evidence.

Must these needy people be imprisoned or besieged?

No. The verb itself does not establish that.

Uḥṣirū can denote being prevented from movement, but need not mean imprisonment in a building or an enemy siege.

The verse itself describes them in circumstances others may fail to recognize.

An openly visible imprisonment or military siege would not necessarily be identical to the description of their hidden need, although that possibility cannot be ruled out solely through this argument.

The more significant point is the verse’s three characteristics: need, restricted movement, and restraint in asking others.

The issue is therefore more than lacking wealth: the person also lacks ordinary means of meeting that need or is restricted in using them.

This clarifies iḥṣār’s relationship with actual human capacity.

Someone may physically be able to walk, yet particular circumstances prevent travel for work, trade, or livelihood.

Lā yastaṭīʿūna ḍarban fī al-arḍ should therefore not arbitrarily be interpreted as total physical incapacity.

Iḥṣār and restraint

The verse continues with al-taʿaffuf.

This belongs to ʿ F F and concerns restraint from inappropriate behavior or humiliating requests.

Uḥṣirū and al-taʿaffuf are independent concepts.

The first describes restriction and prevention; the second describes a person’s conduct when facing need.

A person may experience poverty and restriction without restraint; another may exercise restraint without that economic condition.

Both characteristics occur together in this group.

Their restraint should therefore not be treated as the certain cause of iḥṣār, nor should iḥṣār be translated as refraining from asking for help.

Lexical conclusion

Uḥṣirū in Al-Baqarah 2:273 concerns prevention and restricted possibilities of movement and activity.

The context connects this restriction with need and inability to travel through the land.

“They have been restricted in the way of The God” therefore preserves the direct meaning. The following clauses explain the restriction and their economic situation.

This use is important evidence that Qur’anic iḥṣār does not concern only enemies and war.

5. Ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum: breasts constrained about entering a war

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90

Verse text

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ يَصِلُونَ إِلَىٰ قَوْمٍ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ مِيثَاقٌ أَوْ جَاءُوكُمْ حَصِرَتْ صُدُورُهُمْ أَنْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ أَوْ يُقَاتِلُوا قَوْمَهُمْ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَسَلَّطَهُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ فَلَقَاتَلُوكُمْ فَإِنِ اعْتَزَلُوكُمْ فَلَمْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ وَأَلْقَوْا إِلَيْكُمُ السَّلَمَ فَمَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ سَبِيلًا

illā alladhīna yaṣilūna ilā qawmin baynakum wa-baynahum mīthāqun aw jāʾūkum ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum an yuqātilūkum aw yuqātilū qawmahum wa-law shāʾa Allāhu la-sallaṭahum ʿalaykum fa-la-qātalūkum fa-ini iʿtazalūkum fa-lam yuqātilūkum wa-alqaw ilaykumu al-salama fa-mā jaʿala Allāhu lakum ʿalayhim sabīlan

English translation

Except those who join a people with whom you have a covenant, or come to you with breasts constrained about fighting you or their own people. Had The God willed, He could have given them power over you, and they would have fought you. If they withdraw from you, do not fight you, and offer you peace, The God has granted you no way against them.

Examining the use

Ḥaṣirat is a Form I perfect verb.

Its grammatical subject is ṣudūruhum: their breasts.

The verse does not directly say that an enemy has besieged them somewhere or that a physical obstacle blocks their route.

It describes an inward condition through constriction of the breast.

Arabic can use the breast in descriptions of psychological capacity, receptiveness, anxiety, constriction, or difficulty taking action.

A particular matter is specified here: fighting the addressees or their own people.

The constriction therefore concerns difficulty or unwillingness to enter this conflict.

This accords with the sentence’s construction and context.

Does ḥaṣirat mean being afraid?

Fear can be one reason to refrain from fighting, but ḥaṣirat alone does not mean “they were afraid.”

Someone may withdraw out of fear. Another may refrain because of kinship or family ties, commitment to a covenant, or unwillingness to participate in the conflict.

The verse itself assigns no single psychological cause to all these people.

Rendering ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum solely as “they were afraid” would therefore narrow its range.

“Their breasts are constrained about fighting” stays closer to the Arabic and describes their condition without adding a certain cause.

Are these people unable to fight?

Ḥaṣirat cannot establish complete physical or military incapacity.

The verse continues that, had The God willed, He could have given them power over the addressees and they would have fought.

Their ability or opportunity to fight is therefore not absolutely denied.

The inward constriction here concerns refraining from entering the war, rather than necessarily lacking all fighting capacity.

This distinction matters greatly.

A person may be able to fight yet unwilling to fight either side. Refraining in that situation should not arbitrarily be equated with incapacity.

Withdrawal and peace

The verse ends with three forms of conduct: withdrawal, not fighting, and offering peace.

It then states that the addressees have been granted no way against these people.

The inward condition thus connects with their outward conduct.

The two are nevertheless distinct.

Ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum describes their condition; withdrawal, not fighting, and offering peace are specific actions that follow.

Ḥaṣr should consequently not be translated as “making peace.”

Peace is part of the context, but comes from another root and expression.

The verse also shows the Qur’an distinguishing those entering war from those withdrawing.

The following verse, Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, describes another group whose conduct differs from withdrawal and restraining hands from aggression.

The groups discussed in these two verses should therefore not all be treated alike.

The syntax of ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum

An old Arabic grammatical disagreement concerns this expression’s syntactic role.

Some take it as a circumstantial clause: people came while their breasts were constrained about fighting.

Others explain the construction through an understood qad, since a particular grammatical analysis requires this link for a perfect verb to function circumstantially.

Another analysis takes it as an informative statement following the account of their coming.

Grammatical accounts also mention an imprecatory reading, although the informative, descriptive meaning fits the context more clearly.

The clause’s precise role therefore requires grammatical assessment. In the relevant readings, ḥaṣirat nevertheless retains its principal association with constriction and restriction.

Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ: syntactic analyses of ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum

Lexical conclusion

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:90, ḥaṣirat concerns constriction and restricted inward capacity.

It neither denotes direct military siege, identifies fear as the certain cause, nor establishes physical incapacity.

In this context it appropriately expresses the breast being constrained about entering the war.

6. Wa-uḥṣurūhum: siege in confrontation

Al-Tawbah 9:5

Verse text

فَإِذَا انْسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ فَاقْتُلُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَيْثُ وَجَدْتُمُوهُمْ وَخُذُوهُمْ وَاحْصُرُوهُمْ وَاقْعُدُوا لَهُمْ كُلَّ مَرْصَدٍ فَإِنْ تَابُوا وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَوُا الزَّكَاةَ فَخَلُّوا سَبِيلَهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

fa-idhā insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu fa-uqtulū al-mushrikīna ḥaythu wajadtumūhum wa-khudhūhum wa-uḥṣurūhum wa-uqʿudū lahum kulla marṣadin fa-in tābū wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātawū al-zakāta fa-khallū sabīlahum inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

English translation

When the sacred months have passed, kill the associators wherever you find them, seize them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them at every lookout. If they repent, establish ṣalāt, and give zakāt, then leave their way open; The God is forgiving and merciful.

Examining the use

Wa-uḥṣurūhum is a Form I imperative.

Unlike passive uḥṣirtum in Al-Baqarah 2:196, the addressees here are commanded to besiege another group.

The meaning is clear: constraining, restricting routes of movement, or besieging.

The context supports this because the verb accompanies commands about finding, seizing, and taking up positions of ambush.

These verbs are nevertheless not synonyms.

Wajadtumūhum concerns finding them; khudhūhum means seizing them; uḥṣurūhum concerns siege and restricting movement; and uqʿudū lahum kulla marṣad concerns taking up positions at lookouts.

Uḥṣurūhum should therefore not become “find them” or “kill them.”

Each verb has its own role.

What is a siege?

Its principal meaning concerns restricting the other side’s routes of movement, exit, entry, or access.

Military force may impose a siege, but ḥaṣr itself need not mean direct physical contact with a person.

Siege can precede seizure or occur during confrontation.

Here it accompanies other severe commands, but their meanings cannot all be merged into one verb.

This distinction is essential in translation.

Those concerned and the limits of context

Al-Tawbah 9:5 must not be examined apart from the surah’s opening.

Al-Tawbah 9:1, Al-Tawbah 9:2, Al-Tawbah 9:3, and Al-Tawbah 9:4 concern disavowal involving groups of associators, covenants, a period of respite, and an exception for those who have neither failed in their covenant nor supported others against the addressees.

Al-Tawbah 9:6 also prescribes protection for an associator seeking it, an opportunity to hear the word of The God, and conveyance to a place of safety.

The context thus concerns specified groups and relationships. Wa-uḥṣurūhum cannot be detached from this setting to derive a ruling to besiege every person of a different belief.

The explicit meaning of siege must not be concealed either. The verse does contain a command to restrict and besiege the other side.

Its scope and the people concerned, however, emerge from the complete verses rather than Ḥ Ṣ R alone.

Must ḥaṣr always mean complete encirclement?

Military siege often involves restricting routes from several directions.

The verb itself cannot establish that every route out has been completely and physically blocked from every direction in every instance.

The manner of siege can differ; its details emerge from context or independent evidence.

“Restricting routes of movement and access” is therefore an appropriate explanation, but the precise form of siege is not part of the verb’s morphological meaning.

Lexical conclusion

Wa-uḥṣurūhum in Al-Tawbah 9:5 means “besiege them” or “constrain them and restrict their routes of movement.”

This reveals the root’s external, practical branch.

Siege, seizure, finding, and killing nevertheless remain four different concepts expressed here by independent verbs.

7. Ḥaṣūr: restraint in the description of John

Āl ʿImrān 3:39

Verse text

فَنَادَتْهُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَهُوَ قَائِمٌ يُصَلِّي فِي الْمِحْرَابِ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكَ بِيَحْيَىٰ مُصَدِّقًا بِكَلِمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَسَيِّدًا وَحَصُورًا وَنَبِيًّا مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

fa-nādathu al-malāʾikatu wa-huwa qāʾimun yuṣallī fī al-miḥrābi anna Allāha yubashshiruka bi-Yaḥyā muṣaddiqan bi-kalimatin mina Allāhi wa-sayyidan wa-ḥaṣūran wa-nabiyyan mina al-ṣāliḥīna

English translation

The angels called to him as he stood performing ṣalāt in the sanctuary: “The God gives you good news of John, confirming a word from The God, a leader, self-restrained, and a prophet among the righteous.”

Examining the use

Ḥaṣūran belongs to Ḥ Ṣ R and has the pattern faʿūl.

It differs from the verbal uses because it describes a person rather than directly reporting confinement or besieging.

Arabic uses ḥaṣūr for someone refraining from sexual relations or approaching women, or prevented from doing so by a condition.

Voluntary restraint differs from involuntary incapacity.

This distinction is essential when examining the description of John.

Two semantic fields of ḥaṣūr

Lexical explanations record at least two interpretations.

First, someone refraining from sexual relations through restraint, chastity, or personal decision.

Second, someone prevented from such relations through lack of desire or physical incapacity.

Both senses are recognized in accounts of Arabic usage.

Their lexical recording nevertheless does not make both equally appropriate to John’s description.

Ḥaṣūran occurs amid praise: confirming, a leader, ḥaṣūr, and a prophet among the righteous.

The context therefore points toward a meaning consistent with praising John’s character.

Conscious restraint and mastery of desires have that suitability.

Physical incapacity is not, by itself, an ethical virtue. Attributing a physical impairment or sexual incapacity to John solely through ḥaṣūr therefore lacks sufficient argument.

Does ḥaṣūr mean someone who never marries?

The word itself cannot establish every detail of John’s marital life with certainty.

In lexical uses concerning sexual relations, ḥaṣūr can express refraining from approaching women; the verse does not directly state whether John married.

Anyone concluding from the description that John never married should therefore make clear that this is inferred from the description and other evidence, rather than explicitly stated by the verse.

Nor does John’s description establish a universal rule that remaining unmarried is a greater virtue than marriage for everyone.

Other verses mention prophets’ spouses and children. One particular description cannot negate the value of marriage or family life.

Does ḥaṣūr mean suppressing every human desire?

The word itself does not yield that meaning.

Self-restraint differs from suppressing all natural human needs.

Someone may have a desire but decide to observe a limit. That differs from lacking desire altogether or being physically incapable.

Arabic ḥaṣūr can denote refraining and restraining oneself; the behavior concerned and its limits require context and independent evidence.

Sexual relations are prominent in lexical explanations of Āl ʿImrān 3:39. The verse itself, however, does not detail John’s private behavior.

It is therefore preferable to preserve “self-restrained” in direct translation, explaining in lexical reflection that Arabic also particularly uses the word for abstaining from sexual relations.

The role of faʿūl

The pattern faʿūl can express a marked or enduring quality.

The pattern alone nevertheless cannot establish that John had no sexual desire or was physically incapable of relations.

Nor should ḥaṣūr be assumed without examination to be necessarily an active participial description of someone restraining himself; some uses also explain it in a sense close to a passive description.

The pattern offers semantic possibilities, without itself determining the kind of prevention.

Here the context of praise strengthens the restraint reading, while details of cause and manner still require caution.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: two senses of ḥaṣūr and the context of praise

Lexical conclusion

Ḥaṣūran in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 describes John within the field of restraint or refraining.

Given the praise in context, “self-restrained” is an appropriate equivalent.

The word alone neither establishes sexual incapacity, lifelong celibacy as necessary, nor a general rule that remaining unmarried is superior to marriage.

8. Ḥaṣīr: confinement or bedding?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:8

Verse text

عَسَىٰ رَبُّكُمْ أَنْ يَرْحَمَكُمْ وَإِنْ عُدْتُمْ عُدْنَا وَجَعَلْنَا جَهَنَّمَ لِلْكَافِرِينَ حَصِيرًا

ʿasā rabbukum an yarḥamakum wa-in ʿudtum ʿudnā wa-jaʿalnā jahannama li-al-kāfirīna ḥaṣīran

English translation

Your Lord may have mercy on you; if you return, We return. We have made Gehenna a confinement for the disbelievers.

Examining the use

Ḥaṣīran is an accusative noun from Ḥ Ṣ R.

Arabic ḥaṣīr has several recognized uses, including confinement or a place restricting and detaining a person, and a mat woven from plant fibers.

Both meanings also appear in explanations of Al-Isrāʾ 17:8.

Unlike the clear use of wa-uḥṣurūhum in Al-Tawbah 9:5, a legitimate semantic disagreement therefore occurs here and should not be ignored.

Ḥaṣīr as confinement

On this reading, ḥaṣīran denotes a place confining and detaining the disbelievers.

The meaning has a clear connection with the root’s principal field.

If Gehenna is a ḥaṣīr for a group, it can be understood as a place where they are trapped and confined.

“Confinement” or “a place of detention” is appropriate on this reading.

The word alone nevertheless does not express every detail of duration, physical form, or possible exit.

Those questions require other verses concerning Gehenna.

Ḥaṣīr as a mat

Arabic also uses ḥaṣīr for a woven mat.

Plant fibers are placed together and interwoven to create a surface for sitting or sleeping.

Some lexical explanations connect the name with the strands being brought together and held within limits.

Based on this recognized sense, some early readings understand Al-Isrāʾ 17:8 as making Gehenna a bed or mat for disbelievers.

This can be compared with mihād in Al-Aʿrāf 7:41, which describes bedding from Gehenna.

The bedding sense therefore cannot be dismissed without examination.

Are both meanings lexically identical?

No.

“Confinement” emphasizes restriction and being prevented from leaving.

“Bedding” denotes what is spread beneath someone or provides their resting place.

The images can connect in describing Gehenna, but are not lexically identical.

A person may be confined in a place that is also their bed; that coincidence does not make confinement and bedding synonymous.

The construction of Al-Isrāʾ 17:8 allows both possibilities to be examined semantically.

Gehenna can be presented as confinement for the disbelievers, or as their bedding.

Some early discussions, including Al-Ṭabarī’s commentary, prefer bedding because ḥaṣīr as a mat was regarded as more familiar in Arabic. Conversely, confinement has support from the root family and recorded uses.

The confinement reading should consequently not be presented with such certainty that bedding is denied any existence in Arabic.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Isrāʾ 17:8: both readings and his preference for bedding

Does ḥaṣīr mean a modern prison?

Even if confinement is chosen, a modern prison’s entire architectural and legal structure should not be projected onto the verse.

Confinement here is a linguistic concept: a place restricting and enclosing a person.

The verse does not specify a building’s form, walls, or implements of detention.

Accepting bedding likewise does not establish that Gehenna literally contains a mat made of reeds or leaves.

Comparison or figurative use need not transfer every material characteristic of a familiar object to the subject described.

Lexical conclusion

Ḥaṣīran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:8 has two legitimate lexical readings: confinement and bedding.

The proposed translation uses “confinement” for its more direct connection with restriction and prevention; bedding nevertheless also has support in Arabic and parallel Qur’anic imagery.

A final choice should therefore examine context and linguistic usage more closely, rather than rest solely on an assumed etymological image.

9. Distinguishing morphological forms and related roots

Ḥaṣara and aḥṣara

Both ḥaṣara and aḥṣara concern restriction or prevention in Qur’anic usage.

Their morphological constructions nevertheless differ.

Ḥaṣara is Form I. Its imperative in Al-Tawbah 9:5 commands besieging a group.

Aḥṣara is Form IV. Its passive forms in Al-Baqarah 2:196 and Al-Baqarah 2:273 describe people prevented from an undertaking or free movement.

Some lexical explanations distinguish an external obstacle, such as an enemy, from obstacles such as illness. The same sources nevertheless show that this distinction does not hold throughout Arabic usage.

Form IV therefore cannot invariably mean an internal obstacle, nor Form I invariably an external one.

This cannot be established as an absolute law in Qur’anic verses either.

Uḥṣirū and uḥṣurū

The written forms are very close, but their constructions differ.

أُحْصِرُوا in Al-Baqarah 2:273 is a Form IV perfect passive: they have been prevented.

احْصُرُوا in Al-Tawbah 9:5 is a Form I imperative: besiege.

In the first, people receive the effect of prevention. In the second, the addressees are commanded to impose siege.

Morphological analysis and translation must fully preserve this distinction.

Ḥaṣirat and ḥaṣūr

Ḥaṣirat in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 is a verb expressing constriction of the breast.

Ḥaṣūr in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 describes a person.

They share a family, but the verbal form in Al-Nisāʾ cannot supply the entire meaning of John’s description.

The praise surrounding John connects ḥaṣūr with restraint, while ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum describes people constrained about entering a war.

Ethical restraint and inward constraint about a conflict are therefore different conditions.

Ḥaṣūr and ḥaṣīr

Both have patterns capable of describing people or things, but differ in construction and use.

Ḥaṣūr has the pattern faʿūl in John’s description.

Ḥaṣīr has the pattern faʿīl in the description of Gehenna.

The different patterns do not establish that one is invariably active and the other invariably passive in meaning.

Both require recorded linguistic use and the verse’s context.

Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara) compared with M N ʿ (manaʿa)

Study of M N ʿ

Manʿ denotes preventing, obstructing, or stopping access or an undertaking.

Ḥaṣr also concerns prevention, but often emphasizes restricted possibilities of movement, action, or capacity.

Someone can be prevented from doing something without being confined somewhere. A besieged person can also lose several kinds of movement or access.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute law.

Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara) compared with Ḥ B S (ḥabasa)

Study of Ḥ B S

Arabic ḥabs concerns keeping or detaining a person or thing.

Ḥaṣr can have a close meaning.

In uses such as Al-Tawbah 9:5, however, siege and restricted routes of movement are prominent; ḥabs can concern keeping a person in a particular place.

Siege and imprisonment are therefore not always the same action, although they can connect in some situations.

Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara) compared with Ḍ Y Q (ḍāqa)

Study of Ḍ Y Q

Ḍayq concerns narrowness and restricted capacity.

Ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 can be explained through constriction of the breast.

Ḍayq, however, has a broader Arabic use for narrowness of place, possibility, circumstances, or feeling.

Ḥaṣr also emphasizes prevention and restriction within its semantic family.

The roots are consequently very close in some uses, but should not be treated as absolute synonyms in every verse.

Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara) compared with Ḥ J Z (ḥajaza)

Study of Ḥ J Z

Arabic ḥajz concerns separating, placing a barrier, and preventing passage.

This can approach ḥaṣr, since a barrier may block movement.

Uses of ḥaṣr involving inward constriction or self-restraint, however, need not be explained through a material barrier between two things.

The families must therefore also be examined independently.

Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara) compared with Ḥ SH R (ḥashara)

Study of Ḥ SH R

Qur’anic ḥashr concerns gathering and assembling people.

Ḥaṣr concerns preventing and restricting.

People may have gathered in a place during a siege, but assembly and siege remain distinct concepts.

Maḥshūr should therefore not be rendered as maḥṣūr, nor maḥṣūr as maḥshūr.

Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara) compared with Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥaṣana)

Study of Ḥ Ṣ N

Arabic Ḥ Ṣ N concerns fortresses, strength, and protection.

A group may take refuge in a fortress and then be besieged by an enemy.

Ḥ Ṣ N and Ḥ Ṣ R nevertheless remain independent roots.

A fortress can provide protection; ḥaṣr can express restricted movement or access.

Having a fortress does not lexically mean being besieged, nor must confinement occur in a fortress.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ḥ Ṣ R should not receive one fixed equivalent in every verse, since its verbs and nouns occupy different fields.

Fa-in uḥṣirtum in Al-Baqarah 2:196 is best translated as “if you are prevented.” It preserves the passive and the existence of an obstacle without deciding its kind in advance.

Alladhīna uḥṣirū fī sabīli Allāh in Al-Baqarah 2:273 closely corresponds to “those restricted in the way of The God.” Contextual explanation should clarify the connection with inability to travel through the land and their condition of need.

Ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 is best rendered as “their breasts are constrained.” The same verse identifies the object of this constraint: fighting the addressees or their own people.

Wa-uḥṣurūhum in Al-Tawbah 9:5 means “besiege them.” “Siege” is a clear and natural Persian equivalent in this use.

Ḥaṣūran in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 can be translated as “self-restrained.” Lexical reflection should explain its particular Arabic use for abstaining from sexual relations, without treating the word as proof of John’s physical incapacity.

Ḥaṣīran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:8 can be translated as “a confinement.” Bedding nevertheless also has Arabic support and requires attention in detailed examination of the verse.

Translation should distinguish being prevented, preventing others, inward constriction, self-restraint, and a place of confinement throughout these uses.

The important translation principle is that the kind of restriction, its cause, and its ethical value must not be deduced from the word alone.

A pilgrim being prevented, restricted movement for someone in need, refraining from war, besieging a group, and John’s restraint all belong to ḥaṣr’s semantic field, but differ in responsibility, cause, and consequence.

11. Final synthesis

Arabic Ḥ Ṣ R concerns preventing, restricting, and being constrained.

The restriction can be external or internal. Enemy siege, illness, life’s occupations, restricted movement, inward constriction, and consciously refraining can all belong to this family’s broad field.

Each Qur’anic use must nevertheless be understood independently.

Uḥṣirtum in Al-Baqarah 2:196 describes an obstacle disrupting performance or completion of ḥajj and ʿumrah.

Uḥṣirū in Al-Baqarah 2:273 concerns needy people restricted in the way of The God and unable to move about the land in the ordinary way.

Ḥaṣirat ṣudūruhum in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 describes people whose breasts are constrained about fighting the addressees or their own people.

Wa-uḥṣurūhum in Al-Tawbah 9:5 commands besieging a specified group in confrontation and covenant relationships.

Ḥaṣūran in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 praises John and concerns restraint, without establishing physical incapacity or the certain absence of all desire.

Ḥaṣīran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:8 concerns Gehenna, with legitimate lexical disagreement between confinement and bedding.

The six uses show that ḥaṣr is not inherently always blameworthy or always praiseworthy.

Being prevented from ḥajj or experiencing economic restriction involves facing an obstacle. Refraining from war can occur amid peace and withdrawal. John’s restraint occurs among terms of praise. Military siege belongs to a particular context of confrontation.

Each use’s ethical and legal value must therefore be understood from its subject, those concerned, circumstances, and accompanying verses.

Nor does ḥaṣr invariably mean absolute incapacity. A person may have physical or theoretical capacity yet be prevented in a particular situation; someone may also refrain through choice and self-restraint.

The distinction between incapacity, being prevented, and voluntary restraint must be preserved.

Final definition: In its principal field, Ḥ Ṣ R denotes preventing or restricting possibilities of movement, access, performance, or expression of behavior. The restriction may be imposed externally, arise from personal circumstances, or appear as inward restraint and self-control.

Its basic equivalent is therefore “preventing and restricting,” while “besieging,” “becoming constrained,” “inward constriction,” “being self-restrained,” and “confinement” suit particular forms with contextual support.

Understanding the root requires three essential distinctions: what or who is restricted, where the obstacle or restriction arises, and whether the verse concerns an unwanted condition, a voluntary act, or an evaluative description.

Without them, prevention of worship may be equated with voluntarily abandoning it, restraint with physical incapacity, and siege with seizure or killing.

Preserving morphology, the verb’s complement, and each verse’s context reveals the derivatives’ semantic connection without erasing their real differences.
