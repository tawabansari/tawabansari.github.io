Leaving, leaving behind, omitting, and breaking a pledge

Arabic gh–d–r concerns leaving or leaving something behind. This family includes ghadr, breaking a pledge or abandoning fidelity, and ghadīr, a pool where water remains after floodwater passes.

In matters of commitment, ghadr occurs when someone accepts an undertaking but fails to honour it. It is not simply leaving a person or thing, but abandoning a commitment that was expected to be fulfilled.

By contrast, ghādara may generally mean leaving, departing, or leaving something behind without any breach of faith or wrongdoing.

The Quran uses the root twice, both in Al-Kahf: nughādir in Al-Kahf 18:47 and yughādiru in Al-Kahf 18:49. Both are Form III verbs meaning leaving or omitting. Neither means breaking a pledge.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Early Arabic and lexical accounts record several uses of this family.

Leaving and leaving behind: someone or something is left, remains after others depart, or falls behind companions.

Ghadr in a pledge: someone fails to fulfil an accepted commitment, breaches it, or acts against the trust established by it.

Ghadīr: water remaining in a hollow after rain or a flood.

Ghadāʾir: tresses, long locks, or braids hanging from the head.

The family is therefore not restricted to the ethical sense of betrayal. Leaving and remaining are important, and some derivatives carry no moral judgment.

A shared root does not establish that every derivative arose from one historical image. Each branch needs examination through its recorded meaning and usage.

Ghadr: abandoning fidelity to a pledge

In ethical and social usage, ghadr contrasts with wafāʾ, fidelity.

Fulfilling a pledge means observing an accepted undertaking; ghadr means abandoning or violating it.

Two groups may agree not to attack each other. If one exploits the other's trust and attacks contrary to the agreement, its conduct is ghadr.

Leaving a city or ceasing to accompany a group, without any relevant undertaking, is not by itself ghadr.

The adverse moral judgment arises from an obligation and its expected fulfilment, not from every use of a verb meaning to leave.

Ghādir, ghaddār, and ghadūr are recorded for a treacherous person. Their reproachful sense should not be extended to the entire root family.

2. Evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

Treachery contrasted with fidelity

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, a prominent poet of the late pre-Islamic period, concerns al-Samawʾal's fidelity:

فَقَالَ ثُكْلٌ وَغَدْرٌ أَنْتَ بَيْنَهُمَا
فَاخْتَرْ وَمَا فِيهِمَا حَظٌّ لِمُخْتَارِ

fa-qāla thuklun wa-ghadrun anta baynahumā / fa-khtar wa-mā fīhimā ḥaẓẓun li-mukhtār

“He said: bereavement and treachery—you stand between them; choose, though neither offers a fortunate choice.”

The story referenced by the poem places al-Samawʾal between preserving an entrusted possession and losing his child.

Ghadr here is neither leaving a place nor leaving something behind. It concerns breaking faith over something entrusted to one's protection.

The ethical sense thus stands opposed to fidelity and trustworthiness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently date each verse. This traditional poetic witness cannot establish when the word first entered use.

Ghudārah: something left behind

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Afwah al-Awdī reads:

فِي مُضَرَ الْحَمْرَاءِ لَمْ يَتْرُكْ
غُدَارَةً غَيْرَ النِّسَاءِ الْجُلُوسِ

fī Muḍara al-ḥamrāʾi lam yatruk / ghudāratan ghayra al-nisāʾi al-julūs

“Among Muḍar al-Ḥamrāʾ, he left no remnant except the women who remained seated.”

Ghudārah denotes something or people remaining after an event.

The subject is a remnant, not fidelity or betrayal.

The witness clarifies leaving and remaining. Lexical sources attribute it to al-Afwah al-Awdī, but its independent date has not been established here.

Ghadāʾir: long locks of hair

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

غَدَائِرُهُ مُسْتَشْزِرَاتٌ إِلَى الْعُلَا
تَضِلُّ الْعِقَاصُ فِي مُثَنًّى وَمُرْسَلِ

ghadāʾiruhu mustashzirātun ilā al-ʿulā / taḍillu al-ʿiqāṣu fī muthannan wa-mursal

“Its tresses rise upward; the twisted locks disappear among doubled and flowing strands.”

Ghadāʾir, plural of ghadīrah, denotes long locks or tresses here.

This illustrates ghadīrah as a term for hair, distinct from ghadīr for a pool and ghadr for breach of faith.

One may imagine a connection between hanging tresses and something left loose, but that does not establish the historical origin of the name.

These three witnesses and the lexical accounts show distinct settings: fidelity, remnants, and descriptions of hair.

3. Ghadīr: a material image of water left behind

Ghadīr is a particularly clear material use of this family.

It denotes water collected in a depression, left by rainfall or passing floodwater.

After heavy rain and flowing floodwater, some water may remain in hollows. Such collected water is called a ghadīr.

The flood has passed, while some of its water remains.

Lexicographers explain the name through this remaining water: something the flood left behind.

That explanation connects ghadīr semantically with ghādara.

Some lexical accounts offer another explanation: a pool may lose its water and disappoint those relying on it, connecting its name figuratively with betrayal.

This is a semantic analogy, not proof that the original name arose from water betraying its users.

The more direct definition is collected water remaining after rain or a flood.

A ghadīr need not be permanent: its water may evaporate or soak into the ground. Endless persistence is not part of the definition.

4. Important forms and derivatives

Recorded members of the family include:

  • Ghadara: broke a pledge or abandoned fidelity.
  • Ghadr: breach of faith or treachery.
  • Ghādir: treacherous; the active participle of the simple verb.
  • Ghaddār: repeatedly or markedly treacherous.
  • Ghadūr: treacherous; in another use, an animal that falls behind its companions.
  • Ghādara: left, abandoned, or left behind.
  • Mughādarah: leaving or departing from a place.
  • Aghdara: left something behind.
  • Ghudrah and ghudārah: a remainder.
  • Ghadīr: water collected after rain or a flood.
  • Ghudrān: pools; plural of ghadīr.
  • Ghadīrah: a long lock of hair; in another use, an animal left behind by its herd.
  • Ghadāʾir: tresses or long locks.
  • Mughdirah: describing a very dark night that makes travel difficult.

Some forms look similar, but their morphological differences matter.

Ghādir is the active participle of simple ghadara and may mean treacherous. Ghādara is the past tense of Form III and means leaving or leaving behind.

They resemble each other in unvowelled writing, but differ in structure and function.

Only the Form III verb occurs in the Quran, as nughādir and yughādiru.

5. Quranic uses

Al-Kahf 18:47: nobody is omitted from the gathering

وَيَوْمَ نُسَيِّرُ الْجِبَالَ وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ بَارِزَةً وَحَشَرْنَاهُمْ فَلَمْ نُغَادِرْ مِنْهُمْ أَحَدًا

wa-yawma nusayyiru al-jibāla wa-tarā al-arḍa bārizatan wa-ḥasharnāhum fa-lam nughādir minhum aḥadā

Al-Kahf 18:47

“And the day We set the mountains in motion, and you see the earth exposed, and We gather them, leaving none of them behind.”

Nughādir follows the gathering of humanity.

Fa-lam nughādir minhum aḥadan says nobody among them is left outside that gathering.

Nughādir is a first-person plural imperfect verb in Form III. Lam puts it in the jussive and negates it in the portrayal of the event.

Minhum specifies the group; aḥadan within the negative construction makes the exclusion comprehensive.

The sentence does not merely say many people are gathered: none of that group is omitted.

This is not the ethical sense of betrayal. The verb has its general sense of leaving or leaving behind.

Mountains move, the earth is exposed, and humanity is gathered: the scene depicts a comprehensive gathering at the Resurrection.

The next verse continues with people presented before their Lord and confronting an appointed meeting some had denied.

Al-Kahf 18:49: no small or great deed is omitted

وَوُضِعَ الْكِتَابُ فَتَرَى الْمُجْرِمِينَ مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا فِيهِ وَيَقُولُونَ يَا وَيْلَتَنَا مَالِ هَٰذَا الْكِتَابِ لَا يُغَادِرُ صَغِيرَةً وَلَا كَبِيرَةً إِلَّا أَحْصَاهَا وَوَجَدُوا مَا عَمِلُوا حَاضِرًا وَلَا يَظْلِمُ رَبُّكَ أَحَدًا

wa-wuḍiʿa al-kitābu fa-tarā al-mujrimīna mushfiqīna mimmā fīhi wa-yaqūlūna yā waylatanā mā li-hādhā al-kitābi lā yughādiru ṣaghīratan wa-lā kabīratan illā aḥṣāhā wa-wajadū mā ʿamilū ḥāḍiran wa-lā yaẓlimu rabbuka aḥadā

Al-Kahf 18:49

“The record is set down, and you see the wrongdoers apprehensive about what it contains. They say: woe to us! What is this record that leaves out nothing small or great without counting it? They find what they did present, and your Lord wrongs no one.”

Yughādiru is again Form III, but its subject differs from the earlier occurrence.

In Al-Kahf 18:47, The God speaks of gathering humanity and leaving nobody behind.

Here the record of deeds leaves out nothing small or great.

The same verb can concern two different objects in nearby sentences without changing its basic meaning.

The first concerns people; the second, small and great matters pertaining to their deeds.

Lā yughādiru means it leaves nothing out or does not omit.

Ṣaghīratan wa-lā kabīratan concerns small and great matters or deeds, as the continuation indicates. Their size comes from these adjectives, not gh–d–r.

Illā aḥṣāhā explains that none remains uncounted and unrecorded.

The two verbs contribute differently:

Yughādiru concerns leaving or omitting.

Aḥṣā concerns counting and enumerating what is in view.

Together they convey comprehensive recording. Counting should not be made part of the root gh–d–r itself.

Two uses in a single context

The Quran uses the same verb for two subjects only two verses apart.

In Al-Kahf 18:47, nobody is omitted from the gathering.

In Al-Kahf 18:49, nothing small or great is omitted from the reckoning.

The first concerns the gathering of humanity; the second, the accounting of deeds.

This contextual connection shows how ghādara in a negative construction expresses leaving nothing outside the relevant whole.

Al-Kahf 18:49 ends by saying the Lord wrongs nobody.

Comprehensive accounting stands alongside divine justice. No deed is overlooked, but this does not make all deeds equal in value or establish that each minor deed alone determines someone's final destiny.

The verb means leaving or omitting; negation yields “not omitting.” Assessment of deeds and just judgment come from the whole verse and related passages.

6. Ethical ghadr and Quranic pledges

Although ghadr meaning treachery does not occur in the Quran, other verses discuss broken pledges and abandoned fidelity.

Al-Naḥl 16:91 commands fulfilment of the divine pledge and forbids breaking confirmed oaths.

Al-Anfāl 8:56 describes people who repeatedly breach a pledge made with them.

Al-Anfāl 8:58 concerns fear of a group's betrayal and openly returning the treaty to them on equal terms.

These verses clarify fidelity, but their key words belong to other roots, including ʿ–h–d, n–q–ḍ, and kh–w–n.

Their subject matter does not make them lexical occurrences of gh–d–r.

Two situations should also be distinguished:

An undertaking accepted on specified terms may later end through a clear and legitimate process. That is not itself treachery.

Secretly violating an undertaking while another still trusts it, however, relates to ghadr.

The ethical issue is abandoning required fidelity, not simply leaving.

Every separation, departure, or end of cooperation should therefore not be called betrayal without examining the actual commitments.

7. Comparison with nearby roots

Gh–D–R and T–R–K: leaving and omission

Taraka can mean leaving, abandoning, or leaving something behind in the Quran.

Its field overlaps with ghādara in this respect.

One sentence may describe a person leaving something; another, leaving it behind. Structure and perspective determine the wording.

T–r–k does not necessarily imply betrayal. Likewise, the ethical sense of ghadr does not make every use of ghādara morally adverse.

In Al-Kahf 18:47 and Al-Kahf 18:49, ghādara means omitting and approaches some uses of taraka.

Gh–D–R and Kh–W–N: breach of faith and betrayal

Khiyānah has a broad field involving trust, pledges, responsibility, and entrusted possessions.

Ethical ghadr particularly concerns broken fidelity and abandoned pledges.

Appropriating entrusted property against an agreement is betrayal of trust. Secretly breaching a security pact also relates to ghadr.

The concepts can meet in one act without being lexically identical.

Gh–D–R and N–Q–Ḍ: treachery and undoing

Naqḍ concerns undoing or dismantling something established.

Applied to a pledge, it means breaking it.

Ghadr can involve breaking a pledge, while highlighting abandoned fidelity and acting against trust.

Ghadr involving a pledge entails a breached commitment, but every use of naqḍ need not express deception, secrecy, or exploitation of trust.

Those features depend on conduct and context.

Gh–D–R and N–K–Th: broken commitments

Nakth is used for breaking pledges in the Quran.

It approaches ethical ghadr because both may describe someone failing to honour an undertaking.

Their proximity does not erase their distinct lexical histories.

Nakth highlights undoing a commitment; ghadr highlights abandoned fidelity, understood alongside the branch of leaving.

Gh–D–R and Gh–B–R: remaining and leaving behind

These roots are close in spelling and overlap in some meanings.

Quranic ghābir denotes one remaining, as al-ghābirīn in Lot's story.

Quranic ghādara is the verb of leaving or leaving behind.

One may describe remaining, the other the act of leaving something behind.

The shared idea does not establish one root or historical derivation from the other.

Gh–D–R and Gh–D–W: separate roots

Gh–d–w independently concerns morning and movement at that time.

Ghadāh and related morning expressions should not be confused with gh–d–r.

Two shared initial letters do not establish historical kinship or identical meaning.

8. Challenges in understanding and translation

Is every departure ghadr?

This family makes an important distinction.

Ghādara can mean leaving a place or leaving something behind, without an intrinsic judgment of good or bad.

Ghadr as treachery concerns abandoned fidelity that someone had undertaken to maintain.

Leaving a dangerous place to escape is not treachery by itself.

Accepting a commitment and then exploiting another's trust to violate it may constitute ghadr.

The difference lies in the undertaking, responsibility, and manner of departure.

Does Form III always mean reciprocity?

Ghādara is Form III, but its Quranic uses do not describe two parties mutually leaving each other.

Al-Kahf 18:47 concerns gathering humanity without omitting anyone.

Al-Kahf 18:49 concerns a record leaving out nothing small or great.

Form III alone therefore does not establish participation or reciprocity.

Was a ghadīr named for water's betrayal?

Lexical sources record the explanation that a pool may dry when needed and disappoint its users.

This figurative account does not establish betrayal as the original meaning of its name.

The more direct explanation is water remaining after rain or a flood.

The natural image helps clarify the family, without establishing naming history through analogy alone.

Does lā yughādiru mean “does not forget”?

Al-Kahf 18:49 leaves no small or great matter outside the reckoning.

The result may be explained as nothing being left out.

Ghādara does not directly mean forgetting. Forgetting and omitting are distinct.

Someone may knowingly set something aside or exclude it without forgetting it.

“Does not omit” stays close to the verb. Complete counting and recording come from illā aḥṣāhā and the whole verse.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Quranic verb should be translated according to its object and construction.

Nughādir in Al-Kahf 18:47: “we leave” or “we omit”; with lam, “we left none of them behind.” Negation belongs to lam, not the verb alone.

Yughādiru in Al-Kahf 18:49: “it leaves” or “it omits”; with lā, “it leaves out nothing small or great.” Negation belongs to lā.

For other lexical derivatives:

Ghadr: treachery, breach of a pledge, or abandoned fidelity.

Ghādir: the simple verb's active participle, treacherous.

Ghaddār: repeatedly or markedly treacherous.

Mughādarah: leaving or departing from a place.

Ghudrah and ghudārah: a remainder.

Ghadīr: a pool or water remaining after rain or a flood.

Ghadāʾir: tresses or long locks.

Do not impose ethical treachery on the two Quranic uses of ghādara. Nor restrict it to departing from a place: its negative constructions concern omitting nobody and no deeds.

Conclusion

Gh–d–r concerns leaving, abandoning, and leaving behind. Its ethical branch describes abandoned fidelity and broken pledges.

Ghadīr denotes water remaining in a hollow after rain or a flood. It offers a concrete image of what is left behind, without establishing the historical origin of every derivative.

Ethical ghadr is not every departure. It requires a commitment whose expected fidelity was violated.

The Quran uses nughādir and yughādiru, both meaning leaving or omitting.

Al-Kahf 18:47 omits nobody from the gathering; Al-Kahf 18:49 omits no small or great matter from the accounting.

The verb's meaning is leaving or omitting; the negative constructions express leaving nothing outside the relevant whole. They concern comprehensive gathering and accounting respectively.

Distinguish general leaving, ethical treachery, and the specific meanings of ghadīr and ghadāʾir. A shared root does not make every use identical.
