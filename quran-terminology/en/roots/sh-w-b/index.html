Mixing and admixture: from food and drink in early Arabic to an admixture of scalding water in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH W B concerns mixing one thing with another, so that a substance or element enters something else and a combination results. Shawb names this mixing; shāba yashūbu expresses mixing one thing with another.

Attested uses include mixing water with milk, diluting drinks, adding ingredients to food, and combining honey with other liquids. Something previously separate enters another substance and takes on a new condition.

The root also concerns loss of purity, mixing true and false statements, or introducing a defect or impurity. Yet mixing does not inherently mean corruption, contamination, or fraud. A mixture may improve something or diminish its quality through an undesirable addition. Its value and outcome depend on the ingredients and circumstances.

The Quran uses the root once, as shawb in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67. Following the description of eating from the zaqqūm tree, the verse mentions an admixture of ḥamīm, intensely hot water.

Its basic meaning remains admixture. The adverse character comes from the circumstances of the people of the Fire and the substance named, rather than from the root itself.

1. Early Arabic usage and poetic witnesses

Mixing one thing with another

Shāba al-shayʾa bi-l-shayʾ means mixing one thing with another.

Shubtuhu means “I mixed it,” while ashūbuhu means “I mix it.” Mashūb describes something mixed with another substance.

Mixing water with milk is a clear example.

Milk may be drunk undiluted or diluted with water. After water is added, the drink is a mixture of milk and water rather than milk alone.

Shawb can express this change.

Another use concerns mixing honey with water or another drink. This may produce a desired beverage and need not indicate deception or diminished value.

The root should therefore not be restricted to “adulterating.” Loss of purity is one possible result; mixing may serve different purposes and produce different outcomes.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Sulayk ibn al-Sulakah reads:

سَيَكْفِيكَ صَرْبَ الْقَوْمِ لَحْمٌ مُعَرَّصٌ

sa-yakfīka ṣarba al-qawmi laḥmun muʿarraṣun

وَمَاءُ قُدُورٍ فِي الْقِصَاعِ مَشِيبُ

wa-māʾu qudūrin fī al-qiṣāʿi mashību

Translation:

“Partly cooked meat and broth mixed in bowls will spare you the people’s sour milk.”

Mashīb here describes something mixed with other ingredients.

Early explanations connect the mixing with broth, seasonings, and ingredients added in preparing food. Muʿarraṣ has been explained as meat dried in the open, cut up, or incompletely cooked; “partly cooked” in the translation follows the last explanation.

The witness places the root in everyday food preparation. Mixing is a tangible action here, rather than an ethical concept or elaborate metaphor.

The line has variant transmissions. It is attributed to al-Sulayk ibn al-Sulakah and also to al-Mukhabbal al-Saʿdī; its final word is transmitted as both mashīb and mashūb.

Both readings retain the sense of being mixed, but neither attribution nor wording should be treated as certain without acknowledging the variation.

Al-Sulayk belongs to the pre-Islamic poetic tradition. The line matters for early usage, although its individual date has not been independently established here.

Mixing milk with water

Another well-known line reads:

تِلْكَ الْمَكَارِمُ لَا قَعْبَانِ مِنْ لَبَنٍ

tilka al-makārimu lā qaʿbāni min labanin

شِيبَا بِمَاءٍ فَعَادَا بَعْدُ أَبْوَالَا

shībā bi-māʾin fa-ʿādā baʿdu abwālā

Translation:

“Those are noble deeds, not two bowls of milk mixed with water that afterwards became urine.”

Shībā describes the mixing of the two bowls of milk with water.

The poet contrasts noble generosity with two bowls of diluted milk and their eventual fate after consumption. This belittles a gift; it does not establish that the mixing itself instantly turned milk into urine.

The line is transmitted under different names, including Abū al-Ṣalt al-Thaqafī and al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī. Some accounts explain the second attribution as reuse of an earlier line. It should not be assigned an uncontested author or date here.

Its lexical sense remains clear: milk was mixed with water and changed from its initial condition.

The negative assessment belongs to the poetic contrast. The speaker disparages a small gift; this does not make every mixing of milk and water reprehensible or fraudulent in Arabic.

Shawb and shiyāb

Both shawb and shiyāb are recorded in the field of mixing.

Shiyāb can name what is mixed with another substance: the ingredient added to form a combination.

This distinction is readily understood in everyday life.

When water is added to milk, one can discuss the act of mixing or the water used for dilution.

The first emphasizes mixing; the second emphasizes the added ingredient.

These perspectives are related, but the family’s forms should not be treated as interchangeable in every sentence.

Shawb in speech, transactions, and conduct

The root extends beyond material mixtures.

Some constructions use shāba for cheating, deception, or introducing something improper into a transaction.

Yashūbu wa-yarūbu is applied to someone whose speech or conduct alternates or mixes things, including being right on one occasion and wrong on another. Other explanations concern intermittent defense and inactivity; one explanation should not be generalized to every use.

In these uses, the image of mixture extends from substances to speech and behavior.

A report may mix accurate information with false claims. It is not wholly false, but combining truth with unsupported assertions can make them harder to distinguish.

A seller may likewise add a cheaper substance to an expensive product without informing the buyer and sell it as pure.

Mixing then accompanies deception.

The deception arises from concealing facts and the seller’s conduct; mixture itself is not inherently deceptive.

The basic meaning remains, while ethical and transactional implications depend on the circumstances.

Shāʾibah and shawāʾib

Shāʾibah and its plural shawāʾib belong to this family.

Shāʾibah names an admixture that may compromise purity or uniformity.

In contamination contexts, shawāʾib denotes undesirable materials or elements introduced into something.

Contemporary Persian also uses shāʾibah for suspected impropriety, such as suspected corruption in a transaction or bias in judgment.

This modern use should not restrict Quranic shawb to moral contamination or corruption.

Shāʾibah can carry a negative sense, but not every shawb is an admixture of corruption.

2. Semantic core and Quranic form

The Quran uses SH W B in one form:

La-shawban in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67.

Shawb is a noun and verbal noun concerning mixing. Here it is indefinite and accusative, preceded by an emphatic lām.

In inna lahum ʿalayhā la-shawban min ḥamīm, shawban is the delayed nominal subject of inna, while lahum functions as the preceding predicate.

The initial lām emphasizes the statement; it is neither part of the root nor a new derivative.

The root consists of shīn, wāw, and bāʾ.

Shāba yashūbu, shiyāb as a term for mixing or an added ingredient, the passive participle mashūb, shāʾibah, shawāʾib, and verbs such as ishtāba are known in Arabic but absent from the Quran.

Different initial vowels are recorded for the noun. The Ḥafṣ reading used here has shawban, with fatḥah on shīn.

Its semantic core is:

Mixing one thing with another, producing a combination in which a substance or element is added to something else.

Shawb alone specifies neither the substance nor its quantity, purpose, or value.

A combination may be useful, pleasant, undesirable, or harmful. Those qualities follow from the ingredients and circumstances.

3. Shawb in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67: an admixture of ḥamīm

The root’s sole Quranic occurrence is Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67:

ثُمَّ إِنَّ لَهُمْ عَلَيْهَا لَشَوْبًا مِنْ حَمِيمٍ

thumma inna lahum ʿalayhā la-shawban min ḥamīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67

Translation:

“Then, upon it, they will have an admixture of scalding water.”

The verse belongs to a description of the outcome for those who resisted guidance and followed error.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:62 mentions the zaqqūm tree. The following verses describe it and the food the people of the Fire will eat.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:66 reads:

فَإِنَّهُمْ لَآكِلُونَ مِنْهَا فَمَالِئُونَ مِنْهَا الْبُطُونَ

fa-innahum la-ākilūna minhā fa-māliʾūna minhā al-buṭūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:66

Translation:

“They will certainly eat from it and fill their bellies with it.”

Following this food, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67 mentions an admixture of ḥamīm.

The meaning of shawban

Shawb here denotes an admixture or something mixed.

The word does not name an independent kind of substance. It denotes mixture; min ḥamīm identifies the intensely hot water involved.

Shawban min ḥamīm can therefore mean “an admixture of intensely hot water.”

Shawb alone does not specify what else the water is mixed with or in what proportions.

Its position after eating zaqqūm connects it with the preceding food. Without independent evidence, however, one cannot assert that zaqqūm and ḥamīm were mixed in one vessel before eating or that another named substance was added.

The verse does not state those details.

The meaning of ḥamīm

Ḥamīm belongs to Ḥ M M and here means intensely hot, scalding, or boiling water.

Shawb and ḥamīm have distinct meanings.

Shawb expresses mixture; ḥamīm identifies the water characterized by extreme heat.

Rendering shawb simply as “boiling water” would discard the meaning of admixture.

Nor should ḥamīm itself be defined as the mixture.

The phrase combines two concepts: admixture and intensely hot water.

The role of ʿalayhā

The suffix -hā refers back to the zaqqūm tree or the food taken from it in the preceding verses.

In Arabic, eating something and then drinking “upon it” can mean drinking after the food.

ʿAlayhā thus connects the admixture with eating zaqqūm.

ʿAlā should not mechanically be understood to mean that liquid is necessarily poured down onto the tree itself.

Nor does ʿalayhā alone establish that mixing occurs in a food vessel or inside the stomach.

The relationship must be assessed through the passage and other verses.

Does the verse explicitly mention drinking?

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67 contains no explicit verb for drinking.

It states that, upon that food, they have an admixture of ḥamīm.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:54, however, says of the same zaqqūm meal:

فَشَارِبُونَ عَلَيْهِ مِنَ الْحَمِيمِ

fa-shāribūna ʿalayhi mina al-ḥamīm

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:54

Translation:

“Then, upon it, you will drink from the scalding water.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:52 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:53 first mention eating zaqqūm and filling the bellies; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:54 then mentions drinking ḥamīm.

This connection clarifies the reading of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67: intensely hot drink follows the zaqqūm meal.

The direct wording of each verse must nevertheless remain distinct.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67 emphasizes shawb and admixture; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:54 explicitly mentions drinking.

The base translation of Al-Ṣāffāt should therefore retain “admixture,” leaving the explanation of drinking to contextual analysis.

Is zaqqūm mixed with ḥamīm?

Taken together, these verses place eating zaqqūm and drinking ḥamīm within the same description of otherworldly punishment.

Several questions about the exact mixing must remain separate.

The expression may concern a mixture involving ḥamīm, or emphasize the connection between the meal and drink in their experience.

What the verse clearly states is an admixture of ḥamīm after the description of eating zaqqūm.

It names neither a second ingredient nor proportions, vessel, or precise preparation method.

Blood, pus, or bodily discharges should therefore not be inserted into the translation as certain ingredients solely on the basis of external reports.

Such explanations may occur in commentary, but are neither the word’s direct meaning nor the verse’s explicit statement.

The following scene

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:68 reads:

ثُمَّ إِنَّ مَرْجِعَهُمْ لَإِلَى الْجَحِيمِ

thumma inna marjiʿahum la-ilā al-jaḥīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:68

Translation:

“Then their return will certainly be to the blazing Fire.”

The verse continues describing the same group’s outcome.

Eating zaqqūm and the admixture of ḥamīm precede the mention of returning to the Fire.

The arrangement presents the sequence of scenes.

It does not by itself establish a precise map of otherworldly locations, the distance between the tree and the Fire, or a bodily route between them.

Such details cannot be derived from SH W B itself.

4. Shawb and related terms for mixing and purity

SH W B (shawb) and KH L Ṭ (khalṭ)

Khalṭ concerns mixing or intermingling and has varied Quranic uses.

Al-Tawbah 9:102 speaks of mixing good deeds with bad ones. Al-Baqarah 2:220 uses a related form for association with orphans.

KH L Ṭ thus extends from material mixtures to social association and mixed conduct.

Shawb also concerns mixing, with additions to food and drink prominent in its attested early uses.

Both roots, for example, can describe mixing water with milk.

They should not be separated by an absolute rule assigning one exclusively to materials and the other to people.

Quranic shawb occurs with ḥamīm, whereas khalṭ appears across a broader range of situations.

Forqan’s study of KH L Ṭ provides a separate examination of the related root.

Study of KH L Ṭ

SH W B (shawb) and M Z J (mazj)

Mazj also concerns combining two or more things and has well-established uses concerning drinks.

Al-Insān 76:5 reads:

إِنَّ الْأَبْرَارَ يَشْرَبُونَ مِنْ كَأْسٍ كَانَ مِزَاجُهَا كَافُورًا

inna al-abrāra yashrabūna min kaʾsin kāna mizājuhā kāfūrā

Al-Insān 76:5

Translation:

“The righteous will drink from a cup whose admixture is camphor.”

Al-Insān 76:17 describes a drink whose admixture is ginger.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27 reads:

وَمِزَاجُهُ مِنْ تَسْنِيمٍ

wa-mizājuhu min tasnīm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

Translation:

“And its admixture is from Tasnīm.”

These passages show that mixed drinks can also feature among the blessings of Paradise.

Mixing that involves an undesirable substance in one context may contribute to a welcome drink in another.

Shawb and mazj overlap, but their uses require separate examination.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67 uses shawb, while the heavenly drink passages use mizāj.

That difference does not make shawb inherently a bad mixture or mazj inherently a good one. The ingredients and context determine the assessment.

SH W B (shawb) and KH L Ṣ (purity)

In uses concerning purity and freedom from admixture, khulūṣ describes separation from unlike or unwanted elements.

Shawb, by contrast, expresses the presence of admixture.

Undiluted milk differs from milk mixed with water.

Yet purity does not always mean superiority, nor mixture inferiority.

An ingredient may be added to improve flavor or make a desired drink.

In nonmaterial usage, ikhlāṣ concerns intention, religion, and the direction of worship. Those concepts should not be reduced directly to a mixture of milk and water.

Shawb and khulūṣ may contrast in some contexts, but that relationship does not exhaust either family.

SH W B (shawb) and L B S (labs)

One branch of L B S concerns obscuring and making matters confused.

Al-Baqarah 2:42 speaks of mixing or covering truth with falsehood.

The issue involves difficulty in recognizing truth, beyond two things simply being together.

Shawb can concern true and false speech mixed together, but does not necessarily imply deliberate concealment of truth.

Establishing deception or concealment requires examining the subject and the person’s conduct.

Overlap in some contexts should not be taken as complete identity of meaning.

SH W B (shawb) and SH Y B (shayb)

These are different roots. Both have a medial weak consonant, and some verbal forms share the same written shape; the present tense and context help distinguish them.

SH W B gives shāba yashūbu, “to mix.”

SH Y B gives shāba yashību, “to become gray-haired.”

Maryam 19:4 uses shayban for gray hair, from SH Y B.

Shawb, mixture, should not be identified with shayb, gray hair.

The poetic shībā can also be the passive dual of the mixing verb, rather than a form concerning gray hair. Morphology and context determine the distinction.

Resemblance in writing does not establish a shared root.

5. Interpretive challenges and social applications

Mixture is not always contamination

A common error is equating shawb with corruption or contamination.

Modern Persian frequently uses shāʾibah for suspected corruption, collusion, or impropriety.

This specific sense does not exhaust the root’s historical range.

The family used for unwanted additions to goods also describes combining food and drink.

Water may be added to milk with the consumer’s knowledge and consent. That mixing is not inherently deceptive.

Selling the diluted milk as pure introduces concealment and deception.

The act of mixing, the quality of the result, and honesty in describing it must therefore be distinguished.

Mixing truth with false statements

The root’s nonmaterial uses are also significant.

A speaker may combine true statements with false ones.

Some true statements do not necessarily validate every conclusion.

A single error likewise does not by itself prove an intention to deceive.

Assessment requires examining each claim, its relationship to other claims, and the speaker’s knowledge and conduct separately.

The material image of mixing can thus help explain questions of knowledge and judgment.

This social and epistemic application should not be presented as the direct meaning of shawb in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67.

There the subject is an admixture of ḥamīm, rather than truth and falsehood mixed in speech.

Shawb in descriptions of the Hereafter

The Al-Ṣāffāt passage places an admixture of scalding water alongside the zaqqūm meal.

The setting is punishment.

Shawb alone does not establish every substance’s physical nature, the mixing process, or its precise effects.

Other verses, such as Muḥammad 47:15, describe the effects of intensely hot water inside the body. This can inform the study of ḥamīm, but all its details should not be made part of shawb’s lexical meaning.

The lexical sense is admixture. The severity and harm come from the setting and the substances described.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable Persian equivalents of shawb correspond to “mixing,” “admixture,” and “mixture.”

“Mixing” emphasizes the action or state.

“Admixture” more readily denotes what results from combining substances.

“Mixture” is also familiar and natural; Persian prose may choose āmīzeh according to context and to avoid repetition.

Āmīzeh, “admixture,” suits Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67, which describes something provided for the people of the Fire involving ḥamīm.

The proposed rendering, without changing the installed verse translation, is:

“Then, upon it, they will have an admixture of scalding water.”

Here “admixture” renders shawb and “scalding water” renders ḥamīm.

“Upon it” preserves the connection with the zaqqūm meal. Commentary can explain that the admixture awaits them after eating it.

Renderings such as “then they drink boiling water” relate to other descriptions of the scene but omit shawb’s distinct meaning.

“Contaminated drink” or “a mixture of pus and blood” introduces details not supplied by Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67 itself.

Non-Quranic forms can be rendered contextually: shāba, “mixed”; mashūb, “mixed”; shāʾibah, “an admixed element” or, in particular uses, “an impurity”; and shawāʾib, “impurities” or “admixed contaminants.”

For shawb itself, mixing and admixture should remain the basis, rather than fraud, corruption, or contamination.

Conclusion

SH W B concerns mixing one thing with another. Milk mixed with water, ingredients added to food, and honey combined with liquids illustrate its familiar material sense.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period supports that field, although some attributions and readings differ and the precise dates of these lines have not been independently established here.

Extended uses may concern impurities, contamination, cheating, or mixing true and false speech. None is a necessary feature of the basic meaning.

The Quran uses the root only in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67, as shawb.

After eating from the zaqqūm tree, the verse describes an admixture of ḥamīm. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:52, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:53, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:54 likewise connect the meal with drinking intensely hot water afterward.

Shawb is the mixture or admixture; ḥamīm is the intensely hot water involved. Its adverse character comes from the setting of punishment in the Fire.

Mizāj in the heavenly drink passages shows that mixing materials is not inherently negative. The ingredients and circumstances determine its character and outcome.

The resulting definition is:

SH W B denotes mixing one thing with another and forming a combination. Shawb names this mixing or admixture, applying to food and drink and, by extension, speech and conduct. Contamination, deception, and corruption are possible consequences of particular mixtures, rather than necessary meanings of the root.

For Forqan’s Persian rendering of shawb in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:67, āmīzeh, “admixture,” is appropriate; ḥamīm must retain its separate meaning.
