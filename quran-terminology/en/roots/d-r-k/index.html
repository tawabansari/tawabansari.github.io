Reaching and apprehending: sensory perception, arriving at an outcome, and joining one another

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic D R K concerns reaching or attaining something. This can be physical, as when a pursuer catches someone; temporal, sensory, or cognitive, as when fruit ripens, eyes perceive something, or someone gains knowledge.

Related uses include people joining one another, remedying something before an opportunity passes, an event overtaking someone, and reaching a place’s lowest part.

Modern Persian dark kardan usually means understanding a statement’s meaning. Qur’anic Arabic has a broader field.

The Qur’an speaks of death overtaking people, drowning overtaking Pharaoh, groups joining, and the sun not overtaking the moon. “Understanding” does not fit these uses.

With the perception of eyes, sensory apprehension matters. It is related to physical reaching without being restricted to it.

The root has twelve occurrences in eleven verses. Principal branches include reaching and overtaking, visual perception, joining and timely intervention, and dark as a lower level or depth.

These are related but cannot all become one translation. Each verse requires attention to what reaches what, and whether the issue is physical arrival, perception, knowledge, joining, or a level within a place.

2. Qur’anic forms and the early Arabic semantic core

In the text of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, the root occurs twelve times in eleven verses across five morphological categories.

Adraka: Form IV, in six occurrences: Al-Nisāʾ 4:78, Al-Nisāʾ 4:100, Al-Anʿām 6:103 twice, Yūnus 10:90, and Yā Sīn 36:40.

Iddāraka and tadāraka: assimilated and unassimilated Form VI forms; three occurrences in Al-Aʿrāf 7:38, Al-Naml 27:66, and Al-Qalam 68:49.

Dark: a noun in al-darki al-asfali, Al-Nisāʾ 4:145.

Darak: a noun or verbal noun concerning pursuit and being overtaken, Ṭā Hā 20:77.

Mudrak: a Form IV passive participle, plural la-mudrakūna in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:61.

Other Arabic forms include:

Adraka: reached, perceived, attained, or overtook something.

Idrāk: reaching, attaining, or apprehending.

Dark and darak: reaching and attaining; elsewhere, a place’s lowest part or level.

Darrāk: frequently or effectively attaining what is sought.

Tadāraka: caught up with or joined another, or recovered and remedied something before the opportunity passed.

Istadraka: recovered or remedied what was being lost; with speech, corrected an omission or error.

Dirāk: things following or reaching one another in succession.

Mudrak: a place or goal of apprehension; the place noun formed from adraka takes ḍammah on mīm. Distinguish this nominal use from the identical-looking passive participle, as in mudrakūna.

Darakāt: lower levels or layers beneath one another.

These illuminate the lexical family, but not all occur in the Qur’an. In particular, the verbal nouns idrāk, tadāruk, and istidrāk do not occur there in those nominal forms.

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris identifies one principle:

لُحُوقُ الشَّيْءِ بِالشَّيْءِ وَوُصُولُهُ إِلَيْهِ

luḥūqu al-shayʾi bi-al-shayʾi wa-wuṣūluhu ilayhi

“One thing catching up with and reaching another.”

This emphasizes a relationship: one reaches another, comes into contact or connection, or enters its range of attainment.

A pursuer who remains at a distance has not yet achieved idrāk in the catching-up sense.

Upon reaching the other person, adrakahū can be used.

Elsewhere the root extends beyond physical pursuit: death can overtake someone without pursuing like a bodily person.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains dark through catching up, then records pursuit, maturity, visual perception, joining, remedying, and reaching depths.

Al-Rāghib also explains idrāk through reaching an endpoint and attaining something, distinguishing sensory and other uses.

Reaching and attaining provide a useful starting point; each use determines the manner and object.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: reaching, maturity, and joining

3. Tangible uses in early Arabic

Catching someone or something in pursuit

Pursuit offers one of the root’s clearest uses.

Dictionaries record:

“أَدْرَكْتُ الشَّيْءَ”

“I reached the thing.”

Farasun daraku al-ṭarīdati describes a horse that catches the game or quarry it pursues.

Speed alone is not the root’s meaning. A fast horse may fail to catch its quarry; success may depend on speed, distance, or other conditions.

Darak emphasizes achieving contact or attainment, rather than merely moving fast.

This distinction matters in Moses’ story: Pharaoh’s forces pursued the Israelites, and the issue was whether they would catch them.

Reaching maturity and ripeness

Adraka al-ghulāmu describes a boy reaching puberty.

The verb is also used of a girl.

The person reaches a developmental stage, rather than physically reaching someone or somewhere.

Another expression is:

“أَدْرَكَتِ الثِّمَارُ”

“The fruit ripened.”

Both examples involve reaching a stage previously not attained.

Human puberty and fruit ripening are biologically different processes; their linguistic common ground is reaching a stage or limit.

This does not necessarily mean intellectual maturity or correct understanding. Physical development and sound knowledge are different matters.

The bucket rope and access to well water

A less familiar use is darak for part of the equipment used to draw well water.

Dictionaries describe a short rope attached between the main rope and the bucket’s wooden crosspiece, so that this section contacts water and protects the main rope from rotting.

Ibn Fāris explains this protective function and adds that the bucket is reached through it. Its definition should therefore not be reduced to extending a rope to reach water.

This use belongs to everyday drawing of water from deep wells.

It concerns connecting the equipment and gaining access to the bucket; depth is its setting rather than a necessary meaning of the name.

It does not prove that the root originally meant a well rope or that all idrāk uses derive from that tool.

What is recorded is the name of a component connecting the main rope to the bucket and protecting it from water damage.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: bucket attachment and protection of the main rope

Liability and a consequence that reaches someone

Arabic legal and transactional language also uses darak for liability or a claim that may arise against someone after a sale.

The expression ḍamān al-darak belongs to this setting.

It can describe a guarantee assigning responsibility to a seller or guarantor if a valid ownership claim arises concerning the sold property.

The semantic connection is intelligible: a claim or consequence reaches someone and becomes their responsibility.

This technical expression belongs to legal and juristic language. Its details cannot all be treated as pre-Qur’anic meanings without historical investigation.

Nor should this legal use be transferred to verses about death, eyes, or resurrection.

Tadāruk and arriving in succession

Tadāraka al-qawmu describes people arriving successively and joining one another.

The construction concerns a relationship among several people or events rather than one individual’s arrival.

Dictionaries also record successive rains or soil moisture reaching one another.

The family can be used when moisture from a later rainfall joins that from an earlier one.

Such uses illustrate joining and successive arrival, bridging the interval between events.

In some contexts tadāruk extends to reaching something before it is too late and remedying it.

Correcting an error is not necessary to every use: the groups’ convergence in Al-Aʿrāf 7:38 does not mean they remedy their mistakes.

Early dictionaries: mudrak, tadāruk, pursuit, and liability

4. A pre-Islamic poetic witness

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in his Muʿallaqah, describing the ending of war between ʿAbs and Dhubyān:

تَدَارَكْتُمَا عَبْسًا وَذُبْيَانَ بَعْدَمَا
تَفَانَوْا وَدَقُّوا بَيْنَهُمْ عِطْرَ مَنْشِمِ

tadāraktumā ʿabsan wa-dhubyāna baʿdamā / tafānaw wa-daqqū baynahum ʿiṭra manshimi

“You two came to the aid of ʿAbs and Dhubyān after they had worn one another down in war and ground between them the perfume of Manshim”—an image of a destructive pact of warfare.

Tadāraktumā addresses two people who worked to end hostility and establish peace between the tribes.

It concerns intervening in a crisis and preventing further destruction, beyond merely arriving at a location.

This witness helps explain the tadāruk branch.

From basic reaching, the use describes someone arriving at a situation and acting to remedy it before further harm.

The verb does not always mean making peace.

Here peace and remedy follow from the war and the two addressees’ roles.

There is also a clear semantic relationship with tadārakahu niʿmatun min rabbihi in Al-Qalam 68:49: an arrival connects with a changed outcome.

The Qur’anic verb concerning divine favor should nevertheless not acquire every feature of two humans’ mediation.

Zuhayr’s Muʿallaqah: tadāraktumā witness

5. Death and drowning overtake: an event reaching someone

Death reaches people: Al-Nisāʾ 4:78

Al-Nisāʾ 4:78 states:

أَيْنَمَا تَكُونُوا يُدْرِكْكُمُ الْمَوْتُ وَلَوْ كُنْتُمْ فِي بُرُوجٍ مُشَيَّدَةٍ

aynamā takūnū yudrikkumu al-mawtu wa-law kuntum fī burūjin mushayyadatin

Wherever you are, death will overtake you, even in fortified towers.

Death is the subject of yudrikkumu, and the people are its object.

The construction describes an event from which relocation or strong buildings cannot forever protect someone.

Idrāk here does not mean a person becomes aware of death.

Someone may not know their time of death yet be overtaken by it.

Yudrikkumu does not itself state death’s medical cause, exact timing, or manner.

These details do not follow from the root.

Death and migration: Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 describes someone leaving home to migrate toward God and His messenger, then being overtaken by death.

The expression is again yudrikhu al-mawtu.

The word still concerns death reaching someone, but the context differs from Al-Nisāʾ 4:78.

The first stresses death’s reach and the impossibility of absolute escape.

The second concerns the intention and action of someone who began migration but died before reaching the intended destination.

The verse speaks of that person’s reward with God.

This reward follows from a separate clause, not from adraka itself.

The core sense is the same, while the consequences and ethical subject differ.

Drowning overtakes Pharaoh: Yūnus 10:90

Yūnus 10:90 states:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَدْرَكَهُ الْغَرَقُ

ḥattā idhā adrākahu al-gharaqu

“Until drowning overtook him.”

Al-gharaqu is the subject; the object pronoun -hu refers to Pharaoh.

Pharaoh then declares belief.

Idrāk can therefore describe an event that overtakes someone.

Lexically, drowning has reached Pharaoh. The standing of his declaration at that moment requires the following verses and other passages about belief, repentance, and decisive signs.

Adrakahū should not directly become “his repentance was rejected” or “his opportunity ended.”

Those are contextual questions involving other verses’ judgments.

Nor is this merely mental perception; it is an event affecting Pharaoh’s actual condition.

6. Pursuers catching up: Moses and the Israelites

Lā takhāfu darakan: Ṭā Hā 20:77

Ṭā Hā 20:77

وَلَقَدْ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي فَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا لَا تَخَافُ دَرَكًا وَلَا تَخْشَىٰ

wa-laqad awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an asri bi-ʿibādī fa-iḍrib lahum ṭarīqan fī al-baḥri yabasan lā takhāfu darakan wa-lā takhshā

“We revealed to Moses: ‘Take My servants by night and open a dry path for them through the sea; do not fear being overtaken, and do not be afraid.’”

Darakan is a noun or verbal noun concerning pursuit and being overtaken.

The context describes the Israelites’ departure and pursuit by Pharaoh and his forces.

Lā takhāfu darakan thus concerns not fearing the enemy’s catching up.

Some early explanations understand wa-lā takhshā as fear of drowning, which fits the path opened through the sea.

Darak itself does not mean drowning. Pursuers and the sea’s danger are distinct contextual issues.

The root’s basic meaning should not therefore be restricted to wartime enemy pursuit.

Pursuit is one setting in which reaching or attaining occurs.

Innā la-mudrakūna: Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:61

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:61

فَلَمَّا تَرَاءَى الْجَمْعَانِ قَالَ أَصْحَابُ مُوسَىٰ إِنَّا لَمُدْرَكُونَ

fa-lammā tarāʾā al-jamʿāni qāla aṣḥābu Mūsā innā la-mudrakūna

“When the two groups came into sight of one another, Moses’ companions said, ‘We will certainly be overtaken.’”

La-mudrakūna is a plural Form IV passive participle.

Its emphatic lām and the construction express the companions’ certainty about the danger of the enemy catching them.

Seeing the groups close to one another, they assume Pharaoh’s army will certainly reach them.

This is the companions’ statement, rather than a definitive Qur’anic declaration that their expectation occurs.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:62, Moses answers:

“كَلَّا إِنَّ مَعِيَ رَبِّي سَيَهْدِينِ”

“No indeed! My Lord is with me and will guide me.”

Mudrakūna concerns being overtaken, but the speakers judge a future outcome, and Moses rejects their certainty.

The passive participle describes someone or something reached or brought within a pursuer’s grasp.

Here that outcome is not established outside the speakers’ expectation.

The relationship between the two verses

Ṭā Hā 20:77 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:61 share a setting.

Ṭā Hā gives Moses a divine command with reassurance against being overtaken.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ shows the companions inferring certain pursuit from appearances.

The sense of reaching is shared, but one occurs in divine reassurance and the other in fearful human speech.

Divine information and human judgment should not be confused with different root meanings.

7. Lā tudrikuhu al-abṣāru: sensory perception and its limits

Al-Anʿām 6:103

لَا تُدْرِكُهُ الْأَبْصَارُ وَهُوَ يُدْرِكُ الْأَبْصَارَ وَهُوَ اللَّطِيفُ الْخَبِيرُ

lā tudrikuhu al-abṣāru wa-huwa yudriku al-abṣāra wa-huwa al-laṭīfu al-khabīru

“Eyes do not apprehend Him, while He apprehends eyes; He is the Subtle, the Aware.”

D R K occurs twice here.

The first verb, tudrikuhu, is attributed to eyes and negated.

The second, yudriku, is attributed to God, with eyes as its object.

This contrast is central to the verse’s construction.

Sensory apprehension through eyes

Adraktuhu bi-baṣarī can mean apprehending or seeing something with one’s eyes.

This is visual perception rather than bodily movement toward another object.

Eyes can perceive things from a distance without touching them.

The semantic field thus extends from physical reaching to sensory apprehension of an object.

Al-Anʿām 6:103 uses the verb negatively:

“لَا تُدْرِكُهُ الْأَبْصَارُ”

Eyes do not apprehend Him.

The negation concerns eyes apprehending God.

Apprehension and encompassing

Lexical and exegetical sources discuss seeing versus idrāk.

One explanation allows idrāk to indicate complete attainment or encompassing beyond seeing.

Someone might see something without apprehending all its reality, limits, or attributes.

Some therefore distinguish seeing from idrāk, arguing that negating the latter need not always negate every kind of seeing.

Others interpret eyes’ idrāk here as visual perception and draw a broader conclusion about seeing God.

A short lexical definition alone cannot settle the whole debate about the vision of God.

The verse expressly denies eyes’ apprehension of God and affirms His apprehension of eyes.

The exact relationship between apprehension and vision across Arabic usage, and other verses about seeing or meeting God, require independent study.

Al-Qurṭubī: reported differences concerning apprehension and vision

Nor should idrāk here be arbitrarily restricted to complete knowledge of God’s essence: the subject is eyes, whose sensory meaning matters.

Wa-huwa yudriku al-abṣāra

The second clause attributes idrāk to God.

Eyes’ inability to apprehend Him does not imply His inability to apprehend or know eyes.

Divine apprehension should not be defined through human eyesight’s operation.

The verse ends by describing Him as al-Laṭīf al-Khabīr.

Khabīr concerns awareness and knowledge; Laṭīf requires its own semantic study.

The verse relates the limits of eyes’ apprehension to the scope of divine apprehension.

The root does not itself disclose the ontological manner or mechanism of that apprehension.

Abṣār and baṣāʾir

Al-Anʿām 6:104 immediately speaks of baṣāʾir.

Abṣār in Al-Anʿām 6:103 and baṣāʾir in Al-Anʿām 6:104 share B Ṣ R but differ in use.

Abṣār in Al-Anʿām 6:103 concerns eyes and visual perception.

Baṣāʾir in the next verse concerns insights and signs providing grounds for knowledge and guidance.

Their proximity may inform reflection, but does not automatically make abṣār mean minds or thoughts in Al-Anʿām 6:103.

Each derivative should retain its own contribution.

8. The sun not overtaking the moon: celestial order

Yā Sīn 36:40

لَا الشَّمْسُ يَنْبَغِي لَهَا أَنْ تُدْرِكَ الْقَمَرَ وَلَا اللَّيْلُ سَابِقُ النَّهَارِ وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

lā al-shamsu yanbaghī lahā an tudrika al-qamara wa-lā al-laylu sābiqu al-nahāri wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūna

“It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does night outstrip day; each swims in an orbit.”

Tudrika is attributed to the sun, with the moon as object.

Its basic meaning is reaching something.

It shares the semantic family of pursuit and catching up, but has a different subject matter.

Here the sun and moon appear within creation’s order.

Catching up and outstripping

The verse continues wa-lā al-laylu sābiqu al-nahāri.

Sabq is from S B Q and concerns preceding or outstripping.

Idrāk can concern catching something in motion.

The concepts are close in motion: overtaking something may require first catching it.

They are not always synonyms.

Reaching something need not mean moving ahead of it; precedence can also be temporal or concern rank.

Yā Sīn 36:40 places both expressions within the ordering of sun, moon, night, and day.

Does the verse deny a physical collision?

Tudrika lexically concerns reaching.

Whether it means simultaneous occurrence, joining, catching up in a cycle, or another celestial relationship requires the surrounding verses and whole construction.

D R K does not itself mean two bodies physically colliding.

“The sun does not collide with the moon” substitutes a particular interpretation for the verb’s direct meaning.

Nor does this word alone yield a specific astronomical model of orbits, distances, or motion.

“Overtake the moon” preserves the verse’s linguistic image in the base translation.

9. Tadāraka: joining, successive arrival, and timely remedy

Al-Aʿrāf 7:38: groups reaching one another

Al-Aʿrāf 7:38 describes groups entering the Fire successively.

It then says:

“حَتَّىٰ إِذَا ادَّارَكُوا فِيهَا جَمِيعًا”

Until they all reach one another and gather there.

Iddārakū is Form VI, originally tadārakū, with tāʾ assimilated into dāl.

The groups’ joining one another fits the context.

The verse does not require simultaneous entry. Successive entry leads to their gathering in one situation.

Later groups then speak about earlier groups, holding them responsible for their misguidance.

This concerns leaders’ and followers’ responsibility, but iddārakū does not mean misleading or following.

The verb simply describes groups reaching one another.

Al-Qalam 68:49: favor reaches Jonah

Al-Qalam 68:49

لَوْلَا أَنْ تَدَارَكَهُ نِعْمَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ لَنُبِذَ بِالْعَرَاءِ وَهُوَ مَذْمُومٌ

lawlā an tadārakahu niʿmatun min rabbihi la-nubidha bi-al-ʿarāʾi wa-huwa madhmūmun

“Had favor from his Lord not reached him, he would certainly have been cast onto the bare shore while blameworthy.”

Tadārakahu is Form VI.

Niʿmatun is its subject, and the object pronoun -hu refers to Jonah.

The direct construction describes the Lord’s favor reaching Jonah.

Contextually this arrival changes an otherwise possible outcome.

The counterfactual lawlā indicates that without that favor, an outcome qualified by madhmūm, blameworthy, would have followed.

Tadāruk here therefore connects with rescue and timely intervention.

Does tadāruk mean forgiveness?

In Jonah’s story, divine favor connects with mercy and an improved condition.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:143 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:144 also describe his glorification and its consequence.

Al-Qalam 68:50 states that he was chosen and placed among the righteous.

Tadārakahu itself does not mean forgiveness or repentance.

It describes reaching or intervening in time; favor, mercy, and the result follow from other parts of the verses.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 also states that Jonah was cast onto the bare shore while ill.

Al-Qalam 68:49 should therefore not imply he was never cast there. The counterfactual concerns the blameworthy condition that would have accompanied that outcome without favor.

This matters to the verses’ relationship, while tadāruk retains reaching and timely intervention.

Al-Qurṭubī: being cast ashore ill versus blameworthy

Al-Naml 27:66: bal iddāraka ʿilmuhum fī al-ākhirati

Al-Naml 27:66

بَلِ ادَّارَكَ عِلْمُهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ بَلْ هُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِنْهَا بَلْ هُمْ مِنْهَا عَمُونَ

bali iddāraka ʿilmuhum fī al-ākhirati bal hum fī shakkin minhā bal hum minhā ʿamūna

“Rather, their knowledge concerning the Hereafter has reached its limit; rather, they doubt it; rather, they are blind to it.” This follows one analysis; two others and reading differences are explained below.

This is among the root’s linguistically most difficult Qur’anic uses.

Iddāraka derives from tadāraka; Ḥafṣ reads it with tāʾ assimilated into dāl.

Lexicographers and commentators disagree about its function and meaning here.

Al-Naml 27:65 discusses not knowing the unseen or the time of resurrection.

Al-Naml 27:66 then uses three rhetorical transitions to address knowledge, doubt, and blindness concerning the Hereafter.

Context is decisive for interpreting iddāraka.

First interpretation: their knowledge has come to an end

One interpretation takes iddāraka ʿilmuhum as knowledge concerning the Hereafter reaching its limit, leaving them unable to apprehend its reality.

This has been understood to fit the following doubt and blindness.

The expression can thus reproach their inability to know the Hereafter.

Tadāruk fundamentally concerns reaching and joining. Knowledge’s ending results from this construction and context, rather than being the word’s meaning everywhere.

Second interpretation: knowledge reaches them only in the Hereafter

Ibn Fāris and other lexicographers and commentators explain it as knowledge reaching its possessors in the Hereafter.

They recognize its reality when it becomes manifest, after the opportunity to benefit from that recognition has passed.

This concerns knowledge arriving too late, rather than the word itself meaning knowledge’s destruction.

It is closely connected to basic reaching, while requiring the context to distinguish present ignorance from recognition when the Hereafter occurs.

Third interpretation: has their knowledge reached the Hereafter?

Another reading has bal adraka ʿilmuhum.

Some explanations take it as a reproachful question or irony: has their knowledge really attained the Hereafter’s truth?

The answer is negative; subsequent doubt and blindness expose their inability.

The reading and interpretive variation prevent one lexical equivalent alone from fixing the sentence’s meaning.

Linguistic conclusion

All three interpretations connect D R K with reaching, attaining, or a process’s endpoint.

Its subject here is knowledge rather than bodily motion.

Iddāraka should not therefore describe one human group catching another here, as was appropriate in Al-Aʿrāf 7:38.

A different verbal subject produces a different use.

Root research should preserve these interpretations and established reading differences. A base translation may select a preferred interpretation without concealing real variation or dismissing alternatives without evidence.

Al-Qurṭubī: readings and analyses of iddāraka ʿilmuhum

10. Al-darki al-asfali: the lowest level

Al-Nisāʾ 4:145

إِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ فِي الدَّرْكِ الْأَسْفَلِ مِنَ النَّارِ وَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُمْ نَصِيرًا

inna al-munāfiqīna fī al-darki al-asfali mina al-nāri wa-lan tajida lahum naṣīran

“The hypocrites are in the lowest level of the Fire, and you will never find a helper for them.”

Al-dark is a noun qualified by al-asfal.

Dictionaries record darak for the lower part or depth of something, such as a well or deep place.

Darakāt also describes levels beneath one another.

This branch connects semantically with reaching a place’s lower part or depth.

Dark and darajah

Lexicographers describe a tendency distinguishing darajāt from darakāt.

Darajāt more often concerns upward ranks, while darakāt concerns downward levels.

Al-Rāghib explains this especially with Paradise and the Fire.

It should not become an absolute rule for all Arabic.

Darajah and dark belong to different roots: D R J and D R K.

Both concern levels and positions, but their direction and use differ by context.

Al-asfal in Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 explicitly indicates the lowest position.

“The lowest level” therefore fits the complete expression.

Does dark asfal specify how many levels the Fire has?

The verse describes the Fire’s lowest level.

It does not give the total number of levels.

Neither dark nor asfal proves a fixed number of levels or a particular physical structure.

Nor should a low level simply become an earthly architectural plan.

The verse depicts a very low position and severe outcome for hypocrites. Its actual nature requires independent evidence about the Hereafter.

The judgment’s scope concerning hypocrites

The verse uses al-munāfiqīn, hypocrites.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:146 immediately describes those who repent, reform, hold firmly to God, and make their religion sincere.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 should not be isolated as if no member of this group could return or reform.

Nor may anyone showing weakness, doubt, interpretive disagreement, or misconduct be called a hypocrite without evidence and assigned this outcome.

Dark lexically concerns a level or depth. Identifying hypocrisy and responsibility belongs to independent study of hypocrisy, faith, and repentance.

The forms dark and darak

Dictionaries record both dark and darak for a lower level.

Reading traditions also differ over the vowel on rāʾ here.

These do not create two independent roots.

Both concern a low level, with al-asfal clarifying its direction.

11. D R K and neighboring roots

D R K and L Ḥ Q: catching up and joining

Catching up is among idrāk’s closest meanings.

Dictionaries repeatedly explain dark through liḥāq.

Both can describe one person catching another.

Qur’anic D R K also covers death reaching someone, eyes perceiving, groups converging, and a lower level.

“Joining” cannot translate them all.

Nor should liḥāq be restricted to bodily motion in all Arabic. The families require comparison across their uses.

D R K and B L GH: reaching and attaining

Balagha concerns reaching a limit, destination, or stage.

Adraka al-ghulāmu and expressions of bulūgh overlap concerning human development.

Balagha may emphasize a specified limit, while idrāk often concerns one thing reaching another or attaining an object.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule.

The Qur’an uses each in different constructions.

Study of B L GH

D R K and B Ṣ R: apprehension and sight

Al-Anʿām 6:103 places these families in one sentence.

Abṣār concerns eyes or visual capacity; tudriku is the verb of apprehending.

They should not be treated as one word.

Sight can provide apprehension, but D R K is not restricted to sight.

B Ṣ R itself also has sensory and insight-related Qur’anic uses.

Both families’ contributions matter to interpreting the verse.

Study of B Ṣ R

D R K and R ʾ Y: apprehension and vision

Ruʾyah concerns seeing and, in some uses, mental perception or judgment.

Idrāk can likewise operate within sensory or cognitive fields.

They may overlap, but context is needed before identifying every vision with complete apprehension or every apprehension with vision alone.

This matters particularly to debates about seeing God.

A later theological dispute should not predetermine either root’s meaning.

Study of R ʾ Y

D R K and ʿ L M: attainment and knowledge

ʿIlm concerns knowing and knowledge.

Idrāk can sometimes concern apprehending or coming to know.

Many Qur’anic D R K occurrences, however, do not concern mental awareness.

Death or drowning reaching someone cannot mean knowing death or drowning.

Al-Naml 27:66 also has two independent words: knowledge is the subject, while the verb describes its attainment or culmination.

Modern Persian idrāk often approaches understanding, but cannot define the whole Qur’anic family narrowly through that sense.

Study of ʿ L M

D R K and Ḥ W Ṭ: apprehension and encompassing

Iḥāṭah concerns surrounding and encompassing.

Idrāk can sometimes approach complete attainment of an object.

They are not identical in every construction.

Some analyses explain idrāk in Al-Anʿām 6:103 through encompassing; that cannot become its necessary meaning everywhere.

Death overtaking a person in Al-Nisāʾ 4:78 is not the same as God’s knowledge encompassing everything elsewhere.

Eyes’ apprehension and encompassing knowledge also require distinction by object and kind of action.

Study of Ḥ W Ṭ

D R K and D R J: depth and levels

These roots differ only in their third consonant.

One D R K branch concerns a lower part or level.

D R J concerns steps and distinctions of rank.

Arabic sometimes contrasts darajāt and darakāt in upward and downward directions.

They are nevertheless distinct lexical families.

Not every D R K use means descending, nor every D R J use ascending.

That would fail to fit visual perception, death’s arrival, and istidrāj.

Study of D R J

D R K and D R Y: apprehending and knowing

Their cognitive associations can cause confusion in Persian or Arabic.

D R K ends in kāf; D R Y ends in yāʾ.

Qur’anic verbal forms of D R Y, such as yadrī and adrāka, concern knowing and informing. The verbal noun dirāyah itself does not occur in that form in the Qur’an.

D R K may also approach cognition in some contexts but remains an independent family.

Similar Persian senses of understanding and knowing do not make the Arabic roots identical.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

Persian equivalents should follow the subject, object, and verbal construction in each Qur’anic use.

Adraka with events reaching someone

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:78 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:100:

“Overtakes you” and “overtakes him.”

In Yūnus 10:90:

“Drowning overtook him” or “drowning engulfed him.”

These preserve reaching.

“Death understands you” or “Pharaoh understood drowning” would not fit these contexts.

Darakan and mudrakūna

Darakan in Ṭā Hā 20:77 concerns pursuers catching up.

“Do not fear being overtaken” makes the sense clear.

La-mudrakūna in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:61 concerns being caught by pursuers, in the companions’ speech.

“We will certainly be overtaken” is natural.

This expectation should not become a definitive statement that the event occurs.

Tudrikuhu and yudriku in Al-Anʿām 6:103

A suitable equivalent for both is:

“Apprehends.”

The complete rendering is:

“Eyes do not apprehend Him, while He apprehends eyes.”

This preserves the verse’s reciprocal structure.

“Encompasses” merits consideration in some lexical explanations, but should not replace every use as the root’s definitive meaning.

Tudrika in Yā Sīn 36:40

“Overtake the moon.”

This preserves the image without adding a physical collision or particular astronomical model.

Iddārakū in Al-Aʿrāf 7:38

“They reached one another” or “they all gathered.”

The groups’ convergence matters here, rather than mental perception or correcting an error.

Tadārakahu in Al-Qalam 68:49

“Reached him” or “intervened for him in time.”

Favor’s arrival here connects with Jonah’s rescue and changed outcome.

“Came to his aid” is also contextually appropriate, provided help and rescue are understood to arise from the whole sentence.

Iddāraka in Al-Naml 27:66

This use requires particular care.

Depending on reading and analysis, it can concern knowledge ending or reaching its limit, recognition arriving too late in the Hereafter, or a reproachful question about attaining its truth.

The final equivalent should follow the base reading and context while preserving established differences in the explanation.

Al-darki al-asfali in Al-Nisāʾ 4:145

“The lowest level” or “the lowest rank.”

Both convey the expression’s meaning.

Neither supplies the number of levels or their geometrical and bodily nature.

13. Conclusion

Arabic D R K concerns reaching or attaining something.

This may be physical, temporal, sensory, or cognitive; it also extends to people or events joining, timely remedy, and reaching a place’s lower part.

Early Arabic uses include catching quarry, human maturity, fruit ripening, visual perception, accessing water in wells, and intervening in a crisis.

The Qur’an has twelve occurrences in eleven verses.

Death overtakes people in Al-Nisāʾ 4:78 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:100; drowning overtakes Pharaoh in Yūnus 10:90; Ṭā Hā 20:77 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:61 concern pursuers catching up.

Al-Anʿām 6:103 connects idrāk with eyes, without settling all questions of vision and encompassing apart from independent study.

Yā Sīn 36:40 describes the sun not overtaking the moon within celestial order.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:38 concerns groups joining; Al-Qalam 68:49 concerns divine favor reaching Jonah, contextually connected with rescue and a changed outcome.

Al-Naml 27:66 concerns knowledge and the Hereafter, with reading and interpretive differences that should remain visible.

Dark in Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 concerns the lowest level. Related to the wider family, it cannot restrict the whole root to descent.

Modern Persian understanding conveys only part of this semantic field. Qur’anic idrāk may concern reaching, overtaking, attaining, joining, or a place’s depth rather than understanding alone.

Proposed final root definition: D R K concerns reaching and attaining. It can appear in pursuit and movement, events overtaking someone, sensory or cognitive apprehension, joining, and timely intervention. The dark branch concerns a lower part or level. Each derivative’s meaning follows its object, morphology, and context, rather than reducing the whole family to mental understanding or descent. Brief root meaning: Reaching and attaining; sensory or cognitive apprehension, joining and timely intervention; dark also denotes a lower level.
