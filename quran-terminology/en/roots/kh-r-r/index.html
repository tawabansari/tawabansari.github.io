Falling down, collapsing, and falling to the ground: from bodily falls to prostration and the human response to truth

The Arabic root خ ر ر concerns falling and movement from a higher position downward. The verb خَرَّ يَخِرُّ is used when a person falls to the ground, a stone tumbles from a height, or a building collapses.

In early Arabic, this family also concerned sound. خَرِير denotes the sound of flowing water, blowing wind, or a bird’s moving wings. Some lexicographers connect falling and vigorous movement with the sound they produce.

However, falling does not necessarily mean breaking, destruction, or making a loud noise. One person may fall involuntarily; another may deliberately lower themselves to prostrate or bow. Both involve movement from a higher to a lower position, but the cause and significance differ.

The Qur’an uses this root in different situations. Moses falls unconscious when his Lord manifests Himself to the mountain. In Joseph’s story, his family falls before him in prostration. Believers are also described as falling in prostration on hearing divine signs. The same root describes a collapsing roof, falling mountains, and a fall illustrating the condition of someone who associates partners with The God.

One important occurrence is Al-Furqān 25:73: the servants of the Entirely Merciful are described as not falling upon their Lord’s signs “deaf and blind” when reminded of them. This wording shows that outward response alone does not express everything about faith and acceptance; listening and understanding also matter.

We must therefore distinguish falling itself from prostration, bowing, unconsciousness, collapse, and conscious acceptance. These may accompany خُرُور, but none is a complete definition of the root.

1. Root Forms and Semantic Core

The root خ ر ر occurs twelve times in twelve Qur’anic verses. Every occurrence is a form of the unaugmented triliteral verb خَرَّ, appearing in the perfect or imperfect.

The Qur’anic forms are خَرَّ, فَخَرَّ, وَخَرَّ, خَرُّوا, وَخَرُّوا, يَخِرُّونَ, وَيَخِرُّونَ, يَخِرُّوا, and تَخِرُّ.

Attached conjunctions and prefixes do not create independent derivatives. The difference between خَرَّ and خَرُّوا likewise concerns person and number rather than a change in the root’s meaning.

These forms occur in Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, Yūsuf 12:100, Al-Naḥl 16:26, Al-Isrāʾ 17:107, Al-Isrāʾ 17:109, Maryam 19:58, Maryam 19:90, Al-Ḥajj 22:31, Al-Furqān 25:73, Al-Sajdah 32:15, Sabaʾ 34:14, and Ṣād 38:24.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve Form I verb occurrences

Arabic also records other derivatives in this family and related forms discussed alongside them in accounts of sound:

  • خَرَّ ـ يَخِرُّ / يَخُرُّ: fell, dropped, or came down from a height.
  • خَرّ and خُرُور: falling; in some usages, a forceful flow accompanied by sound.
  • خَرِير: the sound of flowing water, wind, or movement producing sound through contact or flow.
  • خَرْخَرَة: a continuous or repeated sound, such as an animal’s breathing or the movement of certain objects; this belongs to the independent quadriliteral خ ر خ ر and is discussed for its connection with sound, not as a direct derivative of خ ر ر.
  • خَرَّار: something that frequently falls, moves, or makes a sound; in some early usages, a description of a person or group entering from somewhere.
  • خَرَّارَة: a flowing spring; also a device that produces sound when moved.
  • خَارّ: someone or something falling.
  • خُرُور: a sound made by certain animals in sleep or while breathing.
  • أَخَرَّ: caused another to fall.
  • اِنْخَرَّ: fell or became slack.
  • خُرّ: a physical opening or passage into which something goes down, such as the grain inlet of a millstone.
  • خَرِير: in another branch, a depression between two areas of elevated ground.

These forms are recorded in Arabic lexical sources. However, apart from the various inflections of خَرَّ, no other derivative of this family occurs in the Qur’an.

Semantic Core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one underlying principle for this entry:

اضْطِرَابٌ وَسُقُوطٌ مَعَ صَوْتٍ

iḍṭirābun wa-suqūṭun maʿa ṣawtin

That is, disturbance or agitated movement and falling, accompanied by sound.

He explains this through a person falling, water flowing, the sound of water, and something falling from a height.

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

خَرَّ يَخِرُّ إِذَا سَقَطَ مِنْ عُلُوٍّ

kharra yakhirru idhā saqaṭa min ʿuluwwin

In other words, خَرَّ is used when something falls from a height.

These explanations share a semantic field: something moves downward or falls from its previous position. In some usages, this movement involves sound and force.

Sound, however, should not be made a necessary component of every occurrence.

For example, in خَرُّوا سُجَّدًا the Qur’an describes falling in prostration; the verb itself does not say that a sound was heard during the prostration.

Likewise, خَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا describes Moses falling in a state of ṣaʿq, rather than necessarily reporting the sound of his body striking the ground.

Falling and downward movement can therefore be considered the clearest center of the Qur’anic usages, while agitation and sound are recognized features of certain lexical usages.

This distinction prevents the root’s physical imagery from turning into details the verse itself does not state.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, volume 2, page 149: falling, movement, and sound

2. Early Usages: Falling, Flowing Water, and the Sound of Movement

A Person or Stone Falling

In Arabic, خَرَّ الرَّجُلُ describes a person falling to the ground.

خَرَّ الْحَجَرُ مِنَ الْجَبَلِ means that a stone fell from the mountain.

Movement from a higher to a lower position is prominent in these usages.

The cause is unspecified. A person may come down because of a blow, illness, slipping, or a deliberate decision.

A stone may detach from a height through erosion, pressure, or the breaking of its support.

Thus خَرَّ itself means neither being struck nor being ill, and does not establish whether the movement is deliberate.

These factors are identified from the sentence’s construction and context.

A Poetic Witness Attributed to Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī

In a verse attributed to him, Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī says of a sword:

بِهِ أَدَعُ الْكَمِيَّ عَلَى يَدَيْهِ
يَخِرُّ تَخَالُهُ نَسْرًا قَشِيبًا

bihi adaʿu al-kamiyya ʿalā yadayhi / yakhirru takhāluhu nasran qashīban

Translation:

“With it [the sword] I leave the armored warrior falling upon his hands, as though he were a vulture that has eaten poison.”

Here يَخِرُّ describes the warrior falling.

The poet depicts a fighter falling onto his hands after a blow.

The second hemistich compares him to a vulture affected by poisoned food.

قَشِيب here concerns a bird that has eaten food mixed with poison.

The image therefore involves falling and losing the ability to stand. However, يَخِرُّ itself does not mean being poisoned or wounded by a sword.

Sword and poison are elements of the poetic image.

Abū Khirāsh is known as a Hudhalī poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse is quoted in Maqāyīs al-Lughah, but its precise date is not established independently by its attribution.

It should therefore not be presented without qualification as a securely pre-Qur’anic witness.

Nevertheless, its use of يَخِرُّ agrees fully with the verb’s recorded early Arabic meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Abū Khirāsh’s witness and qashīb

خَرِيرُ الْمَاءِ: The Sound of Flowing Water

خَرِير is a familiar word in this family.

In Arabic, خَرِيرُ الْمَاءِ denotes the sound produced by flowing water.

For example, water moving rapidly among stones or pouring from a height into a lower place may produce a continuous sound.

Lexical sources also record خَرَّ الْمَاءُ for a forceful flow of water.

The early lexicographer Ibn al-Aʿrābī explains this usage in terms of the strength of the flow.

Falling and sound must nevertheless be distinguished.

In خَرَّ الْحَجَرُ, a stone’s movement and fall may be prominent.

In خَرِيرُ الْمَاءِ, the sound of flowing water is central.

These usages belong to one family, but each derivative highlights a different aspect.

Accordingly, خَرُّوا سُجَّدًا should not be translated as “they made the sound of prostration.” In that construction the verb concerns falling down.

خَرَّارَة: A Flowing Spring

Lexical sources use عَيْنٌ خَرَّارَةٌ for a spring whose water flows audibly.

خَرَّارَة is also recorded for a wooden device that produces sound when its string is pulled or it is spun.

These two usages show that the family’s sound-related branch is not limited to water.

The sound of movement or contact can concern different objects.

This similarity in sound does not establish that all derivatives of خ ر ر were originally names of sound-producing instruments.

Falling and sound are both recorded meanings. Establishing their exact historical relationship requires evidence beyond a shared image.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: flowing water, sound, and kharrārah

A Depression Between Areas of Higher Ground

Early Arabic also records خَرِير for a depression between two areas of elevated ground.

Ibn Fāris places this usage alongside the name for the opening through which grain enters a millstone.

Both examples suggest downward movement or entry into a hollow.

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah reads:

بِأَخِرَّةِ الثَّلَبُوتِ يَرْبَأُ فَوْقَهَا
قَفْرَ الْمَرَاقِبِ خَوْفُهَا آرَامُهَا

bi-akhirrati al-thalabūti yarbaʾu fawqahā / qafra al-marāqibi khawfuhā ārāmuhā

Approximate translation, taking account of the wild ass described in the poem:

“In the depressions of Al-Thalabūt, that wild ass keeps watch above the high, deserted lookout places; it fears the cairns marking the road.”

In one recorded reading, أَخِرَّة is the plural of a term for depressions in the ground. The subject of يَرْبَأُ is the wild ass described in the poem. آرَام is the plural of إِرَم, stone cairns marking a road; the animal fears hunters concealed behind them.

Commentaries on the ode also record different readings of this word, including أَحِزَّة.

This witness should therefore not be treated as conclusive, undisputed evidence for خَرِير in pre-Islamic poetry.

Independently of this variation, lexical sources clearly record خَرِير for an extended depression between areas of higher ground.

Ibn al-Anbārī’s Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-Sabʿ al-Ṭiwāl: the akhirrah variant, wild-ass context, and ārām

This usage must not become the primary meaning of the Qur’anic verb خَرَّ: the Qur’anic passages concern movement and falling, rather than the name of a type of terrain.

3. Involuntary Falling and Collapse in the Qur’an

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143: Moses Falls in a State of Ṣaʿq

وَلَمَّا جَاءَ مُوسَىٰ لِمِيقَاتِنَا وَكَلَّمَهُ رَبُّهُ قَالَ رَبِّ أَرِنِي أَنْظُرْ إِلَيْكَ قَالَ لَنْ تَرَانِي وَلَٰكِنِ انْظُرْ إِلَى الْجَبَلِ فَإِنِ اسْتَقَرَّ مَكَانَهُ فَسَوْفَ تَرَانِي فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّىٰ رَبُّهُ لِلْجَبَلِ جَعَلَهُ دَكًّا وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا فَلَمَّا أَفَاقَ قَالَ سُبْحَانَكَ تُبْتُ إِلَيْكَ وَأَنَا أَوَّلُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-lammā jāʾa mūsā li-mīqātinā wa-kallamahu rabbuhu qāla rabbi arinī anẓur ilayka qāla lan tarānī wa-lākini unẓur ilā al-jabali fa-ini istaqarra makānahu fa-sawfa tarānī fa-lammā tajallā rabbuhu li-al-jabali jaʿalahu dakkan wa-kharra mūsā ṣaʿiqan fa-lammā afāqa qāla subḥānaka tubtu ilayka wa-anā awwalu al-muʾminīna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When Moses came to Our appointed meeting and his Lord spoke to him, he said: My Lord, show Yourself to me so that I may look at You. He said: You will not see Me; but look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, you will see Me. When his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He made it crushed, and Moses fell unconscious. When he recovered consciousness, he said: Glory be to You; I turn back to You, and I am the first of the believers.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143

Here وَخَرَّ concerns Moses.

It is followed by صَعِقًا.

The two words belong to different roots.

خَرَّ expresses the movement of falling, while صَعِقًا describes Moses’ state as he falls.

The subsequent فَلَمَّا أَفَاقَ also shows that he recovered consciousness from a condition like a faint.

In this verse, therefore, Moses’ fall is accompanied by loss of consciousness.

This differs from deliberate prostration.

Although both may involve the body moving downward, in one a person consciously prostrates, whereas in the other he falls in a state of ṣaʿq.

Thus وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا should not be translated as “Moses prostrated” while overlooking صَعِقًا and أَفَاقَ.

Nor does خَرَّ alone mean death. Some falls may occur at death, but this verse explicitly states Moses’ recovery of consciousness.

Al-Naḥl 16:26: A Roof Collapses upon Its Owners

قَدْ مَكَرَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ فَأَتَى اللَّهُ بُنْيَانَهُمْ مِنَ الْقَوَاعِدِ فَخَرَّ عَلَيْهِمُ السَّقْفُ مِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ وَأَتَاهُمُ الْعَذَابُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

qad makara alladhīna min qablihim fa-atā allāhu bunyānahum mina al-qawāʿidi fa-kharra ʿalayhimu al-saqfu min fawqihim wa-atāhumu al-ʿadhābu min ḥaythu lā yashʿurūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those before them also plotted; then The God struck their building at its foundations, and the roof collapsed upon them from above, while punishment came upon them from where they were unaware.”

Al-Naḥl 16:26

Here فَخَرَّ concerns a roof.

The subject is السَّقْفُ, while عَلَيْهِمْ identifies those upon whom the roof falls.

مِنْ فَوْقِهِمْ clarifies the direction: from above them.

This is a clear example of the root’s physical meaning.

The roof occupied a higher position and then fell.

The verse begins with the plotting of people who lived before the addressees. It then describes their building being undermined at its foundations and the roof collapsing.

This image suggests that the structure on which they relied loses its stability, and its consequences reach them themselves.

However, خَرَّ itself does not mean the failure of a plot or the destruction of political power. Such concepts come from the whole verse and its building image.

The sentence also does not identify a particular building, city, or people by name.

Without independent evidence, all historical details of this collapse cannot therefore be determined.

The image of an actual building fits the meaning of collapse and can also explain the outcome of plotting. The possibility of a figurative reading should not, by itself, lead us to rule out the image’s physical reality in advance.

Sabaʾ 34:14: Solomon Falls after His Death

فَلَمَّا قَضَيْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَوْتَ مَا دَلَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَوْتِهِ إِلَّا دَابَّةُ الْأَرْضِ تَأْكُلُ مِنْسَأَتَهُ فَلَمَّا خَرَّ تَبَيَّنَتِ الْجِنُّ أَنْ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ الْغَيْبَ مَا لَبِثُوا فِي الْعَذَابِ الْمُهِينِ

fa-lammā qaḍaynā ʿalayhi al-mawta mā dallahum ʿalā mawtihi illā dābbatu al-arḍi taʾkulu minsaʾatahu fa-lammā kharra tabayyanati al-jinnu an law kānū yaʿlamūna al-ghayba mā labithū fī al-ʿadhābi al-muhīni

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When We decreed death for him, nothing made them aware of his death except a creature of the earth eating his staff. When he fell, it became clear to the jinn that, had they known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating punishment.”

Sabaʾ 34:14

Here خَرَّ concerns Solomon’s fall.

Death and falling must nevertheless be distinguished.

The beginning of the verse states Solomon’s death.

It then mentions a creature eating his staff.

The fall follows in فَلَمَّا خَرَّ.

This sequence shows that death and falling are related but different events in this context.

خَرَّ should therefore not be translated directly as “he died”: death has already been stated independently.

Early exegetical accounts explain that Solomon remained leaning on his staff after death; when it was damaged from within, his body fell.

This explanation fits the sequence. However, the exact length of time, the species eating the staff, and the configuration of Solomon’s location cannot be extracted from خَرَّ itself without evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: Solomon’s death, staff, and fall

In this verse, Solomon’s fall provides the occasion for revealing the limits of the jinn’s knowledge.

But خَرَّ does not mean ignorance of the unseen. Their lack of knowledge is conveyed by the verse’s other clauses.

4. Deliberate Falling: Prostration, Bowing, and Responding to the Signs

Yūsuf 12:100: Falling in Prostration before Joseph

وَرَفَعَ أَبَوَيْهِ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ وَخَرُّوا لَهُ سُجَّدًا وَقَالَ يَا أَبَتِ هَٰذَا تَأْوِيلُ رُؤْيَايَ مِنْ قَبْلُ قَدْ جَعَلَهَا رَبِّي حَقًّا وَقَدْ أَحْسَنَ بِي إِذْ أَخْرَجَنِي مِنَ السِّجْنِ وَجَاءَ بِكُمْ مِنَ الْبَدْوِ مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ نَزَغَ الشَّيْطَانُ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِي إِنَّ رَبِّي لَطِيفٌ لِمَا يَشَاءُ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

wa-rafaʿa abawayhi ʿalā al-ʿarshi wa-kharrū lahu sujjadan wa-qāla yā abati hādhā taʾwīlu ruʾyāya min qablu qad jaʿalahā rabbī ḥaqqan wa-qad aḥsana bī idh akhrajanī mina al-sijni wa-jāʾa bikum mina al-badwi min baʿdi an nazagha al-shayṭānu baynī wa-bayna ikhwatī inna rabbī laṭīfun li-mā yashāʾu innahu huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīmu

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He raised his parents onto the throne, and they fell in prostration before him. He said: My father, this is the fulfillment of my earlier dream; my Lord has made it true. He has been good to me, bringing me out of prison and bringing you from the desert after Satan stirred discord between me and my brothers. My Lord is subtle in what He wills; He is indeed the Knowing, the Wise.”

Yūsuf 12:100

Here خَرُّوا concerns those who fall in prostration before Joseph.

خَرُّوا expresses the downward movement, while سُجَّدًا specifies their state during it.

Grammatically, سُجَّدًا is a circumstantial qualifier.

The sentence therefore means more than “they fell”: they fell in a state of prostration.

Does خَرُّوا Alone Mean Worship?

No.

Here the verb concerns physical movement downward.

سُجَّدًا specifies the posture, and لَهُ relates this behavior to Joseph.

Early exegetical sources offer different explanations of this prostration. Some describe bowing or obeisance as a mark of respect; others discuss a bodily prostration resembling the familiar posture.

These sources also distinguish respectful prostration from worship.

The Qur’anic wording itself, however, does not specify the angle of bowing, whether the forehead touches the ground, or the complete customary procedure.

What it explicitly states is falling for Joseph in a state of prostration.

This behavior also relates to the dream at the beginning of the surah. In Yūsuf 12:4, Joseph recounts seeing the sun, moon, and eleven stars prostrating before him.

In Yūsuf 12:100, he himself identifies this as the fulfillment of that earlier dream.

Thus خَرُّوا لَهُ سُجَّدًا cannot be detached from the story and have all its details determined solely by the later technical meaning of an act of worship.

At the same time, the wording clearly relates the prostration to Joseph. لَهُ should not be stripped of its meaning or referred to someone else without evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpretations of prostration in Joseph’s story

Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:109: Falling upon the Chins

قُلْ آمِنُوا بِهِ أَوْ لَا تُؤْمِنُوا إِنَّ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ إِذَا يُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ يَخِرُّونَ لِلْأَذْقَانِ سُجَّدًا

qul āminū bihi aw lā tuʾminū inna alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma min qablihi idhā yutlā ʿalayhim yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni sujjadan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Say: Believe in it or do not believe. Those who were given knowledge before it, when it is recited to them, fall upon their chins in prostration.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:107

Here يَخِرُّونَ describes the response of people who fall in prostration on hearing the recitation.

لِلْأَذْقَانِ refers to the chins.

This expression emphasizes the face coming down in the image of prostration, but does not alone establish every detail of bodily posture.

سُجَّدًا also expresses the person’s state while falling.

The same verb appears again in Al-Isrāʾ 17:109:

وَيَخِرُّونَ لِلْأَذْقَانِ يَبْكُونَ وَيَزِيدُهُمْ خُشُوعًا

wa-yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni yabkūna wa-yazīduhum khushūʿan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They fall upon their chins, weeping, and it increases them in humility.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:109

Here يَبْكُونَ and خُشُوعًا concern weeping and humility.

Lowering the body, weeping, and humility thus come together in one image.

But يَخِرُّونَ itself means neither weeping nor humility.

This distinction matters: a person may physically prostrate while lacking the inward state of someone who prostrates through understanding and being moved.

Here the Qur’an describes people who have already received knowledge and in whom the recitation produces such a response.

This relationship between awareness, being moved, and behavior comes from the verses together, rather than the independent meaning of خُرُور.

Maryam 19:58: Prophets Falling while Weeping

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ مِنْ ذُرِّيَّةِ آدَمَ وَمِمَّنْ حَمَلْنَا مَعَ نُوحٍ وَمِنْ ذُرِّيَّةِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْرَائِيلَ وَمِمَّنْ هَدَيْنَا وَاجْتَبَيْنَا إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ خَرُّوا سُجَّدًا وَبُكِيًّا

ulāʾika alladhīna anʿama allāhu ʿalayhim mina al-nabiyyīna min dhurriyyati ādama wa-mimman ḥamalnā maʿa nūḥin wa-min dhurriyyati ibrāhīma wa-isrāʾīla wa-mimman hadaynā wa-ijtabaynā idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātu al-raḥmāni kharrū sujjadan wa-bukiyyan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those are the prophets upon whom The God bestowed favor: from Adam’s descendants, from those We carried with Noah, from Abraham’s and Israel’s descendants, and from those We guided and chose. When the signs of the Entirely Merciful were recited to them, they fell in prostration, weeping.”

Maryam 19:58

Here خَرُّوا is accompanied by سُجَّدًا وَبُكِيًّا.

سُجَّدًا expresses prostration, while بُكِيًّا expresses weeping.

The verse speaks of a group of prophets and guided people, describing their response when the signs of the Entirely Merciful are recited.

خَرُّوا itself, however, does not mean to weep.

Nor does this verse establish that every listener to the Qur’an must weep aloud on every occasion for their response to be valid.

It describes the behavior of a particular group. Establishing general requirements of worship is a separate matter.

Al-Sajdah 32:15: Falling, Glorification, and Freedom from Arrogance

إِنَّمَا يُؤْمِنُ بِآيَاتِنَا الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِّرُوا بِهَا خَرُّوا سُجَّدًا وَسَبَّحُوا بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ وَهُمْ لَا يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ

innamā yuʾminu bi-āyātinā alladhīna idhā dhukkirū bihā kharrū sujjadan wa-sabbaḥū bi-ḥamdi rabbihim wa-hum lā yastakbirūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Only those believe in Our signs who, when reminded of them, fall in prostration and glorify their Lord with praise of Him, without acting arrogantly.”

Al-Sajdah 32:15

Here خَرُّوا accompanies prostration, glorification, and the absence of arrogance.

Their combination shows falling to be a praised response to the reminder of the signs in this context.

خَرُّوا alone, however, does not mean glorifying or refraining from arrogance.

The Qur’an expresses those features in separate words.

Outward prostration and inward humility are therefore connected, but one should not be treated as the complete equivalent of the other without evidence.

Ṣād 38:24: David Falls in a Bowing Posture

قَالَ لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِ وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْخُلَطَاءِ لَيَبْغِي بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَقَلِيلٌ مَا هُمْ وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ

qāla la-qad ẓalamaka bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājihi wa-inna kathīran mina al-khulaṭāʾi la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti wa-qalīlun mā hum wa-ẓanna dāwūdu annamā fatannāhu fa-istaghfara rabbahu wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāba

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He said: He has certainly wronged you by asking to add your ewe to his ewes. Many partners wrong one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds, and they are few. David understood that We had tested him; he asked his Lord’s forgiveness, fell in a bowing posture, and turned back to Him.”

Ṣād 38:24

This verse has particular lexical importance.

In the earlier occurrences, خَرُّوا accompanied سُجَّدًا; here the wording is وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا.

خُرُور therefore cannot be defined as exclusively prostration.

The same verb can accompany رَاكِعًا. Early exegetical sources, including Al-Ṭabarī, also explain رَاكِعًا here as prostrating; that interpretation concerns the semantic range of ر ك ع and does not make خَرَّ an exclusive word for prostration.

Al-Ṭabarī: explaining rākiʿan as prostration

رَاكِعًا belongs to ر ك ع and specifies David’s state.

أَنَابَ belongs to another root and expresses returning and turning toward.

Asking forgiveness, falling in a bowing posture, and turning back are connected here, but remain three distinct concepts.

This example shows that the basic meaning of خَرَّ remains coming down; the accompanying word clarifies the exact state.

5. Falling in the Qur’an’s Warning Images

Maryam 19:90: Mountains Falling

تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ وَتَنْشَقُّ الْأَرْضُ وَتَخِرُّ الْجِبَالُ هَدًّا

takādu al-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna minhu wa-tanshaqqu al-arḍu wa-takhirru al-jibālu haddan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The heavens almost split apart because of it, the earth breaks open, and the mountains fall in collapse.”

Maryam 19:90

This verse follows the condemnation of attributing a child to the Entirely Merciful.

In this image, the heavens, earth, and mountains are described in terms of collapse and disintegration.

تَخِرُّ is a third-person feminine singular imperfect verb with the nonhuman plural subject الْجِبَالُ.

هَدًّا belongs to ه د د and concerns collapsing and breaking down.

Thus تَخِرُّ expresses the mountains’ downward movement, while هَدًّا emphasizes the force of their collapse.

However, the verse begins with تَكَادُ: “almost” or “are close to.”

Without considering that construction, the verse should not be read as reporting an actual, completed collapse of all mountains at the time of speaking.

It uses an exceptionally forceful image to express the enormity of an improper attribution to The God.

The image alone does not report a particular geological event.

Al-Ḥajj 22:31: Falling from the Sky in a Simile about Associating Partners

حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ وَمَنْ يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَكَأَنَّمَا خَرَّ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ أَوْ تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي مَكَانٍ سَحِيقٍ

ḥunafāʾa li-allāhi ghayra mushrikīna bihi wa-man yushrik bi-allāhi fa-ka-annamā kharra mina al-samāʾi fa-takhṭafuhu al-ṭayru aw tahwī bihi al-rīḥu fī makānin saḥīqin

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Being upright in devotion to The God, associating nothing with Him. Whoever associates partners with The God is as though he had fallen from the sky, and birds snatch him away or the wind casts him into a far-distant place.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:31

Here خَرَّ appears in a simile.

فَكَأَنَّمَا shows that the condition of someone who associates partners is compared to falling from the sky.

خَرَّ itself means falling from a height; مِنَ السَّمَاءِ identifies the starting point.

Two further images follow: being snatched by birds and being cast far away by the wind.

These are imagined consequences of the fall within the verse’s image, rather than independent meanings of خ ر ر.

The verse also does not say that everyone who associates partners physically falls from the sky.

The simile illustrates a spiritual condition and a condition of belief.

Here falling becomes connected with losing a secure position and exposure to dispersal and destruction.

This explanation must nevertheless remain a contextual reflection.

خَرَّ itself does not mean associating partners, going astray, or losing faith. Those concepts come from the subject and construction of the simile.

6. Al-Furqān 25:73: Not Falling upon the Signs Deaf and Blind

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِّرُوا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ لَمْ يَخِرُّوا عَلَيْهَا صُمًّا وَعُمْيَانًا

wa-alladhīna idhā dhukkirū bi-āyāti rabbihim lam yakhirrū ʿalayhā ṣumman wa-ʿumyānan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And those who, when reminded of their Lord’s signs, do not fall upon them deaf and blind.”

Al-Furqān 25:73

This verse is particularly important for understanding خ ر ر and its relationship to human awareness.

It occurs among the qualities of the servants of the Entirely Merciful.

Unlike the passages about prostration, it uses the negative construction لَمْ يَخِرُّوا.

Crucially, this negation must not be understood apart from صُمًّا وَعُمْيَانًا.

Does the Verse Reject Prostration or Falling Itself?

Two approaches have been discussed in analyzing this expression.

In one reading, the verse does not say that the servants of the Entirely Merciful never fall down; it says that they do not engage with the signs in a state of deafness and blindness.

In this analysis, صُمًّا وَعُمْيَانًا is the censured state being negated.

In another reading, خُرُور may function idiomatically to describe their manner of responding to the signs, without requiring an actual bodily movement on every occasion.

Both readings agree on one point: the verse does not praise engagement without listening, seeing, or understanding.

Al-Ṭabarī’s commentary also explains the verse through understanding and apprehending the signs.

Other reports emphasize that a person should not merely repeat others’ behavior out of habit and without knowledge.

The phrase therefore cannot simply mean “believers do not prostrate.”

That reading conflicts with verses such as Al-Sajdah 32:15 and Maryam 19:58, which describe falling in prostration in response to the signs.

Conversely, we should not automatically conclude that Al-Furqān 25:73 necessarily commands one particular form of bodily prostration.

Its clear concern is the quality of the human response to the signs.

Al-Ṭabarī: understanding the signs, idiomatic usage, and avoiding unknowing imitation

صُمًّا وَعُمْيَانًا: A Person’s State before the Signs

صُمًّا belongs to ص م م, and عُمْيَانًا to ع م ي.

Here deafness and blindness can also express inattention or failure to receive and recognize truth.

This expression must not become a negative judgment of people with hearing or visual disabilities.

The subject is behavior when reminded of the signs, rather than people’s moral worth on the basis of physical ability.

In this context, someone whose hearing is physically intact but who does not understand, or does not wish to understand, may be compared behaviorally to someone who does not hear.

Conversely, a physically deaf person may carefully learn the signs through other means and reflect upon them.

The verse’s criticism therefore concerns lack of insight and apprehension, rather than bodily impairment.

Understanding and Practical Response

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:107, those given knowledge fall in prostration when the signs are recited.

In Al-Furqān 25:73, the servants of the Entirely Merciful do not fall upon the signs deaf and blind.

These two usages can be read together.

In the first, awareness and being moved accompany falling down.

In the second, a response without understanding or insight is not presented as praiseworthy.

This connection does not establish that every person must thoughtlessly repeat someone else’s outward movement before every verse.

Nor does this verse condemn simply asking questions or requesting an explanation of meaning.

On the contrary, its emphasis on attentive listening and seeing relates to understanding what is said.

For example, someone may follow others’ behavior during recitation without knowing the verse’s subject or the responsibility it raises.

Such behavior is not necessarily the response expected here of the servants of the Entirely Merciful.

Another person may ask about a verse’s meaning, compare it with other verses, and then act upon it consciously.

This process accords with understanding and insight.

Nevertheless, لَمْ يَخِرُّوا عَلَيْهَا صُمًّا وَعُمْيَانًا does not mean that these servants may accept a command only after fully grasping every detail and reason behind it.

The verse concerns not being deaf and blind to a reminder; it does not require unlimited knowledge.

7. Distinctions from Related Roots

خ ر ر (KH R R, Khurūr) and س ق ط (S Q Ṭ, Suqūṭ): Falling

سَقَطَ concerns falling or becoming detached from a previous position.

خَرَّ also expresses falling.

Lexical sources, however, explain خَرَّ through downward movement and sometimes its resulting sound.

سَقَطَ has a broad field that can include falling, being lost, or becoming detached.

This difference must not become absolute: both verbs can describe the same event in some situations.

For example, Solomon’s fall in Sabaʾ 34:14 can be rendered in Persian with either an expression for “falling” or “falling down.”

In passages about prostration, however, “they fell in prostration” is more natural in both imagery and wording.

خ ر ر (KH R R, Khurūr) and ه و ي (H W Y, Huwiyy): Falling and Downward Movement

In some Arabic usages, هَوَىٰ denotes falling or movement from a height downward.

Al-Ḥajj 22:31 contains both fields: first falling with خَرَّ, then being cast by the wind with تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ.

Their combination shows that both verbs concern downward movement or removal from a previous position.

تَهْوِي nevertheless does not simply mean خَرَّ itself.

The verse describes two different events, and each verb has its own role.

ه و ي also has other Arabic semantic branches, including inclination and desire; its full range cannot therefore be equated with خ ر ر.

Study of H W Y

خ ر ر (KH R R, Khurūr) and س ج د (S J D, Sujūd): Falling and Prostration

This is one of the most important distinctions in the root’s Qur’anic usage.

خَرَّ expresses the movement of falling.

سَجَدَ concerns prostration and a posture of submission in the relevant usage.

They appear together in Yūsuf 12:100, Maryam 19:58, and Al-Sajdah 32:15.

Syntactically, therefore, falling and prostration are not one word.

خَرُّوا سُجَّدًا means that they fell in a state of prostration.

The distinction also becomes clear in Ṣād 38:24, where the same verb accompanies رَاكِعًا.

خَرَّ therefore cannot be rendered directly as “prostrated” in every verse.

Study of S J D

خ ر ر (KH R R, Khurūr) and ر ك ع (R K ʿ, Rukūʿ): Coming Down and Bowing

رُكُوع concerns bowing or bending.

In Ṣād 38:24, خَرَّ and رَاكِعًا appear together.

This combination shows that downward movement can also be described with a bowing posture.

خَرَّ itself does not mean bowing; nor is bowing always identical to falling or a sudden drop.

Accordingly, a proposed rendering of وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا is “he fell in a bowing posture”; the exact range of رُكُوع should be examined in its own study.

Study of R K ʿ

خ ر ر (KH R R, Khurūr) and ص ع ق (Ṣ ʿ Q, Ṣaʿq): Falling and Unconsciousness

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, خَرَّ and صَعِقًا appear together.

خَرَّ expresses falling, while صَعِقًا expresses the accompanying state.

A person may fall without losing consciousness or fall while unconscious.

The two concepts are therefore not identical.

Here صَعِقًا together with أَفَاقَ shows that Moses fell unconscious and then recovered consciousness.

Study of Ṣ ʿ Q

خ ر ر (KH R R, Khurūr) and ه د د (H D D, Hadd): Falling and Collapse

Maryam 19:90 pairs تَخِرُّ with هَدًّا.

خَرَّ expresses downward movement; هَدّ concerns breaking down and collapsing.

Something may fall without being completely crushed, or break where it stands without a major fall.

In the image of Maryam 19:90, the two concepts combine to convey the force of the mountains’ collapse.

هَدًّا must therefore not be treated as the independent meaning of تَخِرُّ.

Study of H D D

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

The most suitable general Persian equivalent of خ ر ر is forū oftādan, “falling down.”

It works for both people and physical objects, conveying downward movement without adding a cause or outcome.

Soqūṭ kardan, “falling,” is also accurate, but may sound more formal or restricted in some sentences.

Forū rīkhtan, “collapsing,” suits a building, roof, or mountain, but is unnatural for all human occurrences.

“Falling in prostration” or “falling in a bowing posture” is appropriate where the verse itself states prostration or bowing.

Brief Root Meaning and Meanings of the Qur’anic Forms

وَخَرَّ — Al-Aʿrāf 7:143

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Form I active perfect, third-person masculine singular.

Brief root meaning: Falling down.

Meaning here: Moses fell to the ground in a state of ṣaʿq and unconsciousness.

وَخَرُّوا — Yūsuf 12:100

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Falling down or coming down from a standing position.

Meaning here: Joseph’s family fell before him in a state of prostration.

فَخَرَّ — Al-Naḥl 16:26

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine singular.

Brief root meaning: Falling and collapsing.

Meaning here: The building’s roof collapsed upon them from above.

يَخِرُّونَ and وَيَخِرُّونَ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:109

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active imperfect, third-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Falling down.

Meaning in these verses: They fall upon their chins when the signs are recited: in prostration in the first verse, and with weeping and increased humility in the second.

خَرُّوا — Maryam 19:58 and Al-Sajdah 32:15

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine plural.

Brief root meaning: Falling down.

Meaning in these verses: They fell in prostration when reminded of divine signs or when those signs were recited.

تَخِرُّ — Maryam 19:90

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active imperfect, third-person feminine singular, with the nonhuman plural subject الْجِبَالُ, “the mountains.”

Brief root meaning: Falling and collapsing.

Meaning here: The mountains collapse in the verse’s immense warning image.

خَرَّ — Al-Ḥajj 22:31

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine singular.

Brief root meaning: Falling from a height.

Meaning here: Falling from the sky, used to illustrate the condition of someone who associates partners with The God.

يَخِرُّوا — Al-Furqān 25:73

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active imperfect, third-person masculine plural; jussive after لَمْ, with the final nūn omitted.

Brief root meaning: Falling down.

Meaning here: When reminded of the signs, the servants of the Entirely Merciful do not fall upon them deaf and blind: their response is not detached from understanding and insight.

خَرَّ — Sabaʾ 34:14

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine singular.

Brief root meaning: Falling down.

Meaning here: Solomon fell after his death and the damage to his staff.

وَخَرَّ — Ṣād 38:24

Root: خ ر ر (KH R R).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine singular.

Brief root meaning: Falling down.

Meaning here: After asking forgiveness, David fell in a bowing posture.

These usages share a brief root meaning, but the state, direction, cause, and result of movement are identified through each verse’s accompanying words and constructions.

Translations That Restrict the Meaning

Rendering خَرَّ as “prostrated” in every verse would misrepresent the collapsing roof, Solomon’s fall, and Moses falling in a state of ṣaʿq.

Always rendering it as “fell with a loud noise” would impose a feature of certain lexical usages on all Qur’anic occurrences.

Always rendering it as “was destroyed” would erase the distinctions between falling, breaking, and destruction.

Translating Al-Furqān 25:73 simply as “they did not prostrate” would displace the main role of صُمًّا وَعُمْيَانًا.

Falling down should therefore remain the basic meaning; each verse’s particular equivalent should follow its subject, accompanying state, and context.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root خ ر ر concerns falling and movement from a higher position downward. Early witnesses also connect this movement with agitation and sound, while خَرِير describes the sound of flowing water or moving objects.

Sound or agitation is nevertheless not essential to every Qur’anic occurrence.

The Qur’an uses this root twelve times, always in forms of the unaugmented triliteral verb خَرَّ.

Falling is sometimes involuntary, as with Moses in a state of ṣaʿq, Solomon’s fall, or a roof collapsing. Elsewhere it occurs as a conscious response to the signs, in prostration or bowing. Images of falling also convey the outcome of associating partners or the enormity of an improper attribution to The God.

In Al-Furqān 25:73, the Qur’an describes people who do not fall upon their Lord’s signs deaf and blind when reminded of them. This usage shows that outward behavior alone does not establish the human response to the signs; the quality of listening and understanding matters too.

Prostration, bowing, unconsciousness, destruction, and misguidance are nevertheless not fixed meanings of خَرَّ. Each becomes connected with falling in its particular usage.

Final definition: The root خ ر ر denotes falling and movement from a higher position downward, sometimes accompanied in early usages by agitation and sound. In the Qur’an, خُرُور can describe a bodily fall, collapse, or a person lowering themselves in prostration or bowing. The kind of movement, whether deliberate or involuntary, and its ethical or devotional significance come from accompanying words and each verse’s context, rather than the root’s independent meaning. Brief root meaning: Falling down and moving downward; in other lexical branches, flowing and its sound.
