Root w-ṭ-ʾ — Treading, trampling, preparing, and agreement

The Arabic root w-ṭ-ʾ concerns placing a foot on something, pressing it underfoot, and affecting the surface beneath it. Related uses include traversing land, subjecting someone to pressure, leveling and preparing a place, and bringing things into agreement.

The root occurs in five Qur’anic verses. One concerns treading a place that angers opponents; another, land not yet trodden. Al-Fatḥ addresses people being overrun and potentially harmed unknowingly. Al-Tawbah uses yuwāṭiʾū for matching the number of months, while waṭʾan in Al-Muzzammil concerns the effect and firmness of rising at night.

These uses extend beyond walking, without requiring every meaning to reproduce a physical image. Treading, exerting pressure, agreeing, and having a firm effect are related applications whose precise meanings depend on form and context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Treading and pressing underfoot

In early Arabic, waṭiʾa al-shayʾa meant setting foot on something, pressing it underfoot, or treading upon it.

It could describe ordinary walking or a foot or hoof pressing on the ground or another object.

Waṭiʾa al-arḍa means “he trod the ground.”

Waṭiʾahu bi-qadamihi means “he stepped on him or it with his foot.”

With a road or land as its object, traversing or treading is prominent. With a person subjected to feet or moving mounts, it can convey trampling and injury.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā says:

وَطِئْتَهُمُ وَطْءَ الْبَعِيرِ الْمُقَيَّدِ

waṭiʾtahumu waṭʾa al-baʿīri al-muqayyadi

“You trampled them as a fettered camel treads.”

The half-line belongs to a poem describing its subject’s power and military strength.

The surrounding passage describes troops, horses, and spears.

Waṭiʾtahum here is not ordinary walking: the poet compares overpowering and pressing the enemy to the heavy tread of a camel whose movement is restricted.

The verb can thus extend from physical foot contact and pressure to overpowering and oppressing another.

This half-line is transmitted in the ode attributed to Al-Aʿshā beginning “A-tarḥalu min Laylā wa-lammā tazawwadi.” Attribution alone does not independently establish the date and transmission of every word.

The witness supports the use of waṭʾ for physical pressure and for dominance over people in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It does not make every use mean warfare or defeating an enemy. The military meaning arises from the poem’s subject and the verb’s object.

Leveling and preparing

Another branch appears in waṭṭaʾa and tawṭiʾah.

Waṭṭaʾa al-arḍa means leveling or preparing ground for walking or use.

Waṭṭaʾa al-firāsha means preparing and softening a bed.

Waṭīʾ describes something soft, smooth, or easy to sit or move upon.

Ground without troublesome stones or irregularities, for example, is easier to walk on.

A well-prepared bed places less pressure and discomfort on the body.

The connection with foot or bodily contact is intelligible: the subject is preparing something to make treading or resting upon it easier.

Arabic sources also use this family for an agreeable, gentle person.

Rajulun waṭīʾu al-khuluq describes someone easy to associate with and mild in conduct.

Smooth ground offers an image for ease of companionship.

This image should not become a certain historical derivation for every form. Gentleness, level ground, and trampling an enemy remain distinct applications.

Muwāṭaʾah: correspondence and agreement

Another branch appears in wāṭaʾa and muwāṭaʾah.

Wāṭaʾahu ʿalā al-amr means agreeing or coordinating with someone about a matter.

Tawāṭaʾa al-qawm describes people agreeing on an issue.

An illustrative connection with treading is that two people move in coordination, as though their steps follow the same path.

This helps explain a semantic relationship; it does not prove that agreement historically originated in stepping into someone else’s footprints.

Lexicons directly record muwāṭaʾah as correspondence and agreement, and the Qur’an uses this branch.

Muwāṭaʾah does not invariably mean secret agreement or collusion in wrongdoing.

People can coordinate a good action or agree on a wrongful one.

Agreement is the meaning of this branch; its moral value depends on the subject and conduct involved.

Limits of the historical account

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period supports treading, pressure, and trampling.

Leveling, softening, preparing, and agreeing are also well attested in classical Arabic.

The available evidence does not establish the order in which every branch emerged.

They cannot all be derived with certainty from one original image.

Foot contact and pressure characterize the material core, but each derivative must also be examined on its own terms.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important Arabic forms include:

Waṭiʾa: he trod on, pressed underfoot, or set foot upon something.

Yaṭaʾu: he treads, tramples, or presses.

Waṭʾ: treading and pressing underfoot; in extended uses, the resulting pressure or effect.

Waṭʾah: a footstep or its place, pressure of the foot, or figuratively a heavy blow or pressure.

Wāṭiʾ: someone or something that steps on or tramples a surface.

Mawṭiʾ: a place of treading, where a foot is set.

Mawṭūʾ: something trodden or placed underfoot.

Waṭṭaʾa: he leveled, softened, or prepared; in some uses, he made something ready for a task.

Tawṭiʾah: preparation or making the way or conditions ready.

Waṭīʾ: soft, level, or easy to tread, sit on, or use.

Wiṭāʾ: something laid beneath the feet or body; a bedding or covering placed below.

Wāṭaʾa: he agreed or corresponded with another in a matter.

Muwāṭaʾah: agreement or correspondence.

Tawāṭaʾa: he agreed or coordinated with another.

Tawāṭuʾ: agreement among people on a matter; in contemporary Persian it often denotes collusion.

Arabic also uses waṭʾ for sexual intercourse. This relates to the bodily-contact branch, but none of the six Qur’anic occurrences concerns intercourse.

Its technical legal usage should therefore not be transferred to these verses.

Forms actually occurring in the Qur’an

The root occurs six times in five verses.

The forms are:

Li-yuwāṭiʾū in Al-Tawbah 9:37, from Form III.

Yaṭaʾūna and mawṭiʾan in Al-Tawbah 9:120: a Form I verb and a place noun.

Taṭaʾūhā in Al-Aḥzāb 33:27: Form I with a feminine object pronoun.

Taṭaʾūhum in Al-Fatḥ 48:25: Form I with a masculine plural object pronoun.

Waṭʾan in Al-Muzzammil 73:6: the Form I verbal noun.

The Form II verb waṭṭaʾa and the forms tawaṭṭaʾa and tawāṭaʾa do not occur in the Qur’an.

Nor do the nouns tawṭiʾah and tawāṭuʾ.

Semantic core

For clarity, the family can be discussed under four related semantic headings.

First, treading and trampling: contact between a foot and the ground or something else, potentially involving pressure and an effect.

Second, leveling and preparing; third, agreement and correspondence. These appear in derived forms and particular usages.

Fourth, firmness, heaviness, or effect: interpretations especially relevant to waṭʾan in Al-Muzzammil.

These headings are related, but a vague definition such as “having an effect” cannot replace them all.

Not every movement, effect, or agreement is expressed by w-ṭ-ʾ.

3. Treading land and places

Al-Tawbah 9:120 — Treading in God’s way

مَا كَانَ لِأَهْلِ الْمَدِينَةِ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهُمْ مِنَ الْأَعْرَابِ أَنْ يَتَخَلَّفُوا عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَرْغَبُوا بِأَنْفُسِهِمْ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ لَا يُصِيبُهُمْ ظَمَأٌ وَلَا نَصَبٌ وَلَا مَخْمَصَةٌ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَطَئُونَ مَوْطِئًا يَغِيظُ الْكُفَّارَ وَلَا يَنَالُونَ مِنْ عَدُوٍّ نَيْلًا إِلَّا كُتِبَ لَهُمْ بِهِ عَمَلٌ صَالِحٌ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُضِيعُ أَجْرَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

mā kāna li-ahli al-madīnati wa-man ḥawlahum mina al-aʿrābi an yatakhallafū ʿan rasūli Allāhi wa-lā yarghabū bi-anfusihim ʿan nafsihi dhālika bi-annahum lā yuṣībuhum ẓamaʾun wa-lā naṣabun wa-lā makhmaṣatun fī sabīli Allāhi wa-lā yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan yaghīẓu al-kuffāra wa-lā yanālūna min ʿaduwwin naylan illā kutiba lahum bihi ʿamalun ṣāliḥun inna Allāha lā yuḍīʿu ajra al-muḥsinīn

Al-Tawbah 9:120

“It was not for the people of Medina and the Bedouins around them to remain behind God’s Messenger or prefer their own lives to his. For no thirst, fatigue, or hunger afflicts them in God’s way, nor do they tread any place that enrages the disbelievers, nor gain anything against an enemy, without a righteous deed being recorded for them thereby. God does not let the reward of those who do good go to waste.”

Two forms from one root appear together:

يَطَئُونَ

yaṭaʾūna

Al-Tawbah 9:120

مَوْطِئًا

mawṭiʾan

Al-Tawbah 9:120

The first is a verb referring to treading.

The second names the place where a foot is set.

Their pairing makes the connection between action and location clear.

Yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan here means treading a place where their presence and movement anger the opponents.

Mawṭiʾ: a place of treading

Mawṭiʾ is morphologically a place noun.

It denotes where the action of waṭʾ occurs.

Here mawṭiʾan is indefinite within a negative construction:

وَلَا يَطَئُونَ مَوْطِئًا

wa-lā yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan

Al-Tawbah 9:120

The statement is therefore not confined to one particular place.

Any place meeting the following conditions falls within its scope.

Mawṭiʾ itself does not mean battlefield, enemy territory, or conquered land.

These may be contextual instances, but its lexical meaning is a place of treading.

The role of yaghīẓu al-kuffār

The following clause qualifies the place: treading there enrages the disbelievers.

This feature comes from the qualifying clause, not the root itself.

Mawṭiʾ alone does not mean angering opponents.

Nor does every movement or presence become righteous merely because someone dislikes it.

The verse concerns moving in God’s way and accompanying the Messenger through hardship.

Thirst, fatigue, hunger, treading difficult places, and confronting an enemy belong within this setting.

The action’s value follows from its purpose and circumstances.

Al-Tawbah 9:121 continues with spending in God’s way and traversing a valley.

This continuity suggests that even difficulties and steps taken in fulfilling responsibility do not escape God’s regard.

The root itself does not mean striving, reward, or righteous action.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 — Land not yet trodden

وَأَوْرَثَكُمْ أَرْضَهُمْ وَدِيَارَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ وَأَرْضًا لَمْ تَطَئُوهَا ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرًا

wa-awrathakum arḍahum wa-diyārahum wa-amwālahum wa-arḍan lam taṭaʾūhā wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:27

“And He made you inherit their land, homes, and possessions, and land you had not trodden. God has power over all things.”

Taṭaʾūhā here is a Form I verb.

Lam negates the verb and gives it past reference.

The pronoun hā refers to arḍan, “land.”

Arḍan lam taṭaʾūhā thus means land they had not trodden until then.

This fits the root’s material meaning.

Does it mean only physical foot contact?

Lexically, waṭiʾa al-arḍa concerns treading the ground.

In ordinary language, setting foot in a land can also convey reaching, entering, or being present there.

“We have not yet set foot in that land” need not refer solely to skin touching soil.

It means that we have not gone or been there.

This familiar meaning suits Al-Aḥzāb 33:27.

Taṭaʾūhā alone does not establish conquest, permanent private ownership, or a particular geographical identification.

It describes land not previously trodden by the addressees.

Inheritance is expressed by the separate verb awrathakum.

Awrathakum and lam taṭaʾūhā therefore contribute different meanings.

One concerns transfer by inheritance; the other describes the addressees’ previous relationship to the land.

Does waṭʾ always mean military conquest?

In a military context, treading enemy territory can imply military entry and control.

That sense follows from the circumstances and other expressions in the sentence.

This verse, for example, speaks of land, homes, and possessions inherited in a conflict setting.

Elsewhere someone can tread land without any warfare or seizure being involved.

Rendering waṭʾ as “conquering” everywhere would unjustifiably narrow its range.

4. Al-Fatḥ 48:25 — Overrunning people and unintended injury

هُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَالْهَدْيَ مَعْكُوفًا أَنْ يَبْلُغَ مَحِلَّهُ ۚ وَلَوْلَا رِجَالٌ مُؤْمِنُونَ وَنِسَاءٌ مُؤْمِنَاتٌ لَمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ أَنْ تَطَئُوهُمْ فَتُصِيبَكُمْ مِنْهُمْ مَعَرَّةٌ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ ۖ لِيُدْخِلَ اللَّهُ فِي رَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا لَعَذَّبْنَا الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

humu alladhīna kafarū wa-ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-al-hadya maʿkūfan an yablugha maḥillahu wa-lawlā rijālun muʾminūna wa-nisāʾun muʾminātun lam taʿlamūhum an taṭaʾūhum fa-tuṣībakum minhum maʿarratun bi-ghayri ʿilmin li-yudkhila Allāhu fī raḥmatihi man yashāʾu law tazayyalū la-ʿadhdhabnā alladhīna kafarū minhum ʿadhāban alīmā

Al-Fatḥ 48:25

“They are the ones who disbelieved and barred you from the Sacred Mosque, detaining the sacrificial offering from reaching its place. Were it not for believing men and believing women whom you did not know, whom you might overrun, thus incurring an adverse consequence because of them without knowledge, [you would not have been restrained]—so that God might admit into His mercy whomever He wills. Had they been separated, We would have punished those among them who disbelieved with a painful punishment.”

Taṭaʾūhum is from the same verb waṭiʾa.

Unlike Al-Aḥzāb 33:27, its object is not land.

Hum refers to believing men and women among the opposing group whose identity was unknown to the addressees.

This difference is decisive.

Treading land is an ordinary action.

Overrunning people during conflict can injure or kill them.

From treading to causing injury

The poetic witness attributed to Al-Aʿshā also uses waṭʾ for pressure and domination over an enemy.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 applies this semantic capacity to a sensitive situation.

Had fighting begun, unidentified people among the opponents might have been harmed.

An taṭaʾūhum cannot therefore be restricted to feet touching their bodies.

Here overrunning can include injury caused by military movement and armed confrontation.

The verb alone does not specify the injury’s exact form or severity.

Death, wounds, or other consequences must be considered through the circumstances and the rest of the sentence. The bracketed clause in the translation supplies an understood response to the conditional; it is not an independently stated Arabic clause.

The importance of lam taʿlamūhum

The Qur’an says of those men and women:

لَمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ

lam taʿlamūhum

Al-Fatḥ 48:25

That is, “you did not know them.”

This qualification is crucial.

The verse concerns people present among the opponents whose identity was unknown to the addressees.

The danger of harming them is therefore connected with lack of knowledge.

The same verse also contains bi-ghayri ʿilm, “without knowledge.”

These qualifications highlight knowing people’s identities and considering the consequences of military action.

Someone’s presence among opponents should not automatically imply agreement with their conduct or permission to harm them.

Even the possibility of unintended injury to unidentified people matters to the decision about confrontation.

What does maʿarrah express?

In fa-tuṣībakum minhum maʿarrah, the separate word maʿarrah concerns harm, blame, an adverse consequence, or liability arising from conduct.

Lexical explanations include sin, reproach, injury, and responsibility resulting from harm.

These meanings should not all be extracted from taṭaʾūhum itself.

Waṭʾ expresses overrunning or injuring in the situation described.

Maʿarrah expresses its adverse consequence.

The words perform different roles.

Does the verse concern only deliberate injury?

No. Lam taʿlamūhum and bi-ghayri ʿilm show that harm arising from failure to recognize people is at issue.

Unintended injury is not consequently insignificant.

In social life, someone may lack an intention to harm innocent people yet expose them to danger through an ill-considered decision.

Intention, knowledge, preventability, and actual consequences should remain distinct.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 establishes the importance of this distinction through its context.

It should not, without examining other relevant verses, be turned into a complete theory governing every military situation.

This use shows how movement and bodily pressure can become human injury, and why identifying those exposed to it matters.

5. Al-Tawbah 9:37 — Matching the number of months

إِنَّمَا النَّسِيءُ زِيَادَةٌ فِي الْكُفْرِ ۖ يُضَلُّ بِهِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يُحِلُّونَهُ عَامًا وَيُحَرِّمُونَهُ عَامًا لِيُوَاطِئُوا عِدَّةَ مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ فَيُحِلُّوا مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ ۚ زُيِّنَ لَهُمْ سُوءُ أَعْمَالِهِمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْكَافِرِينَ

innamā al-nasīʾu ziyādatun fī al-kufri yuḍallu bihi alladhīna kafarū yuḥillūnahu ʿāman wa-yuḥarrimūnahu ʿāman li-yuwāṭiʾū ʿiddata mā ḥarrama Allāhu fa-yuḥillū mā ḥarrama Allāhu zuyyina lahum sūʾu aʿmālihim wa-Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-kāfirīn

Al-Tawbah 9:37

“Postponement is only an increase in disbelief, by which those who disbelieve are led astray. They permit it one year and prohibit it another, to match the number God has made inviolable, thereby permitting what God has forbidden. Their evil deeds have been made attractive to them. God does not guide the disbelieving people.”

Li-yuwāṭiʾū is a Form III verb.

Its base is wāṭaʾa, used for agreement or correspondence with something.

The initial li- expresses the purpose of the action.

ʿIddata is the object of the matching: the number or count.

Li-yuwāṭiʾū ʿiddata mā ḥarrama Allāhu thus concerns matching the number of what God made inviolable.

Agreement in number, not in the substance of the command

Al-Tawbah 9:36 states that there are twelve months, four of them inviolable.

Al-Tawbah 9:37 describes shifting the months’ status, suspending inviolability at one time and establishing it at another.

The number could appear to be preserved while a month that should remain inviolable was treated as permissible.

For example, one could nominally preserve four inviolable months while substituting another month for an appointed one.

The number remains, but the command has been altered.

Li-yuwāṭiʾū here concerns this numerical matching.

Agreement is not necessarily obedience

Outward conformity to a number does not by itself constitute adherence to a command.

Someone can preserve an instruction’s appearance while changing its subject or timing so that the result contradicts its purpose.

The Qur’an itself states the result:

فَيُحِلُّوا مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ

fa-yuḥillū mā ḥarrama Allāhu

Al-Tawbah 9:37

“Thereby permitting what God has forbidden.”

The wrong lies in changing the ruling and its consequence, not in the root w-ṭ-ʾ itself.

Agreement can concern something right or wrong.

Does yuwāṭiʾū mean collusion?

In modern Persian, tawāṭuʾ commonly denotes secret agreement to do wrong.

Arabic muwāṭaʾah has the broader meaning of agreement and correspondence.

The verb in Al-Tawbah 9:37 does not necessarily mean a secret meeting or conspiracy.

Its object is ʿiddah, the count.

“Match” is consequently more precise here than “collude.”

Form III alone also does not establish two people participating with equal agency in an agreement.

The construction accommodates correspondence, and its object identifies the number of months as what is being matched.

Relationship to the material sense

Correspondence can be illustrated through footsteps following a shared path.

Muwāṭaʾah in this verse nevertheless no longer describes physical treading.

No one is stepping on months.

The issue is matching a count.

Translation should express this established derivative meaning, not create an unnatural image of stepping on the number of months to preserve an assumed original image.

6. Al-Muzzammil 73:6 — Rising at night and its effect

إِنَّ نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ هِيَ أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا وَأَقْوَمُ قِيلًا

inna nāshiʾata al-layli hiya ashaddu waṭʾan wa-aqwamu qīlā

Al-Muzzammil 73:6

“Rising at night is indeed firmer in effect and more upright in speech.”

Waṭʾan here is a Form I verbal noun.

Its contextual sense differs from ordinary treading on the ground.

The verse concerns rising and reading the Qur’an at night, pairing waṭʾan with aqwamu qīlan.

The surrounding verses help explain it.

The preceding verses

Al-Muzzammil 73:2 commands Muhammad to rise for part of the night.

Al-Muzzammil 73:3 specifies half the night or somewhat less.

Al-Muzzammil 73:4 permits adding to that amount and commands measured Qur’anic recitation. Reduction was mentioned in Al-Muzzammil 73:3.

Al-Muzzammil 73:5 says that God will cast a weighty word upon Muhammad.

Al-Muzzammil 73:6 then discusses rising at night and firmness of speech.

Al-Muzzammil 73:7 recalls his extended activity during the day.

This sequence places Al-Muzzammil 73:6 in the context of rising at night and attending to the Qur’an.

The precise sense of waṭʾan also requires lexical and reading-based analysis.

Waṭʾan: pressure, heaviness, and firmness

In the Ḥafṣ reading, waṭʾan has fatḥah on wāw and sukūn on ṭāʾ.

It is connected to the verbal noun of waṭiʾa.

In material usage, waṭʾ can concern foot pressure and the effect left upon the ground.

With night rising, this range can convey firmness, deeper effect, or the heaviness and difficulty of rising.

Two considerations should be distinguished.

Rising at night can be physically harder because it interrupts a time normally devoted to sleep and rest.

Attending to the Qur’an in nighttime quiet can also have a more lasting effect on attention, understanding, and remembrance.

Both aspects occur in lexical and exegetical explanations.

Waṭʾan alone does not establish that the verse concerns physical pressure on the ground or a prescribed position of the feet in worship.

The context concerns night rising, the Qur’an, and speech, rather than instruction in foot placement.

The readings waṭʾan and wiṭāʾan

Another recognized reading is:

وِطَاءً

wiṭāʾan

With kasrah on wāw and a long ā, this form relates to the verbal noun muwāṭaʾah.

Correspondence is prominent in this reading.

It can denote coordination of tongue, hearing, attention, and heart during Qur’anic reading.

In nighttime quiet, a person may follow their speech more attentively and be less distracted by daily affairs.

Spoken words can then agree more closely with inward attention and understanding.

This connects with the following phrase wa-aqwamu qīlan.

Distinguishing the readings

Waṭʾan has been explained through pressure, firmness, and stronger effect. Agreement has also been discussed under this reading; these interpretations should not be treated as exclusive compartments.

Wiṭāʾan more directly foregrounds correspondence among attention and speech.

The meanings are related.

Steady attention can accompany agreement between speech and comprehension.

The morphological distinction should be preserved without claiming that all interpretations are mutually exclusive.

Waṭʾan is analyzed as the Form I verbal noun, with an explanatory connection to treading and pressure.

Wiṭāʾan is analyzed in relation to muwāṭaʾah and agreement.

For the base rendering of waṭʾan, a supported contextual equivalent can be chosen and alternatives explained separately, without combining every interpretation into an unwieldy translation.

Aqwamu qīlan: more upright speech

The second part reads aqwamu qīlan.

Aqwamu derives from q-w-m and here concerns being more upright, firm, or correct.

Qīlan belongs to q-w-l, the family of saying and speech; it is distinct from q-y-l, the root associated with midday rest.

The verse also presents night rising as more conducive to firm speech.

In the context of measured Qur’anic recitation, this can relate to greater attention, more deliberate reading, and clearer understanding.

It should not be taken to mean inventing new speech or receiving revelation exclusively at night.

The preceding verses address Qur’anic reading and night rising.

Firmness of speech is understood within that context.

Is rising at night equally easy for everyone?

Al-Muzzammil 73:20 recognizes differing human circumstances.

It mentions illness, traveling in search of God’s bounty, and fighting in His way.

It then directs people to read what is manageable of the Qur’an.

The significance of night rising does not negate differing capacities and circumstances.

Illness, demanding work, or other responsibilities may prevent someone from remaining awake as long as another.

Human worth cannot consequently be measured solely by hours spent awake at night.

This reflection derives from the chapter as a whole, not from waṭʾan alone.

A suitable translation

For waṭʾan, one may write:

“Rising at night is indeed firmer in effect and more upright in speech.”

This conveys firmness and stronger effect.

Explanation should also retain heaviness and difficulty as recognized interpretations, and wiṭāʾan as a reading foregrounding agreement between attention and speech.

These legitimate distinctions should not be erased merely to simplify the article.

7. Comparison with related roots

W-ṭ-ʾ compared with d-w-s

D-w-s concerns treading and trampling.

It is close to the physical branch of w-ṭ-ʾ.

W-ṭ-ʾ additionally includes preparing, agreement, and the description of night rising.

“Trampling” suits some uses but does not cover the entire range.

Lam taṭaʾūhā in Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 means not having trodden the land, rather than not having trampled it destructively.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:25, the same verb concerns overrunning and harming people.

The object clarifies the difference.

W-ṭ-ʾ compared with kh-ṭ-w

Kh-ṭ-w concerns steps and stepping.

Its derivative khuṭuwāt occurs in the Qur’an.

Khuṭwah emphasizes a step or stage of movement.

Waṭʾ primarily concerns placing a foot on a surface and the contact or pressure involved.

These accompany each other in ordinary walking without being identical.

The Qur’anic phrase khuṭuwāti al-shayṭān has contextual significance beyond physical steps, but this does not authorize transferring all of that significance to w-ṭ-ʾ.

W-ṭ-ʾ compared with d-kh-l

Dakhala concerns entering a place or domain.

Waṭiʾa concerns setting foot on land or a surface.

Both can occur in one event.

Entering a land normally also involves treading its ground.

The verbs nevertheless differ.

Lam taṭaʾūhā in Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 describes land the addressees had not yet trodden.

The expression can imply not having gone or been there, although the root’s material sense concerns contact and treading.

Mawṭiʾ compared with mawṭin

Mawṭiʾ derives from w-ṭ-ʾ.

Mawṭin derives from the separate root w-ṭ-n.

The words resemble one another in form and meaning but are distinct.

Mawṭiʾ is a place of treading.

Mawṭin can denote a residence, a place of presence, or the scene or situation of an event.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 contains mawṭiʾan.

Al-Tawbah 9:25 contains mawāṭina from w-ṭ-n.

One letter can change a word’s root and semantic range.

Mawṭiʾan in Al-Tawbah 9:120 should not automatically be rendered as homeland, native land, or permanent residence.

Muwāṭaʾah compared with wifāq

W-f-q also concerns compatibility and agreement.

Arabic muwāṭaʾah likewise denotes correspondence or agreement.

Li-yuwāṭiʾū in Al-Tawbah 9:37 concerns matching the number of months.

The distinction should not be turned into an absolute rule.

“Agreement” can appropriately render muwāṭaʾah, while the material branch of w-ṭ-ʾ remains associated with foot pressure and treading.

Agreement alone does not establish that its subject is right or wrong.

Waṭʾ compared with thiql

Al-Muzzammil 73:5 mentions qawlan thaqīlan, “a weighty word.”

Al-Muzzammil 73:6 contains ashaddu waṭʾan.

Their proximity matters when considering heaviness and powerful effect.

Thiql concerns weight and heaviness, whereas material waṭʾ concerns foot contact and pressure.

Both may approach difficulty and pressure within a shared image.

This does not make waṭʾ synonymous with thiql in every use.

In Al-Tawbah 9:37 the same root concerns correspondence, not heaviness.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Several equivalents are needed, selected according to form and context. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

Al-Tawbah 9:37 — Li-yuwāṭiʾū

“To match the number God has made inviolable.”

“Match” is preferable to “collude” here because the object is the number of months.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 — Yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan

“Nor do they tread any place that enrages the disbelievers.”

“Tread” renders yaṭaʾūna, “place” renders mawṭiʾan, and the negative meaning comes from wa-lā, not from the verb itself.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 — Lam taṭaʾūhā

“And land you had not trodden.”

This preserves treading without adding a geographical identification or military conquest to the verb.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 — An taṭaʾūhum

“Lest you overrun them.”

Explanation should clarify that in conflict this can mean injuring or inadvertently killing people. Lack of knowledge comes from lam taʿlamūhum and bi-ghayri ʿilm, not from taṭaʾūhum alone.

“Trample” retains the physical image; “harm” makes the contextual meaning more explicit.

Al-Muzzammil 73:6 — Ashaddu waṭʾan

“Rising at night is indeed firmer in effect and more upright in speech.”

Waṭʾan should not be translated simply as “treading” here.

Explanation should discuss pressure, difficulty, stronger effect, and wiṭāʾan as foregrounding agreement between attention and speech.

Outside the Qur’an, waṭʾ may mean treading, pressing underfoot, or, in warfare, trampling.

Tawṭiʾah can mean preparation or making conditions ready.

Muwāṭaʾah means agreement or correspondence.

Waṭīʾ can describe a bed as soft and comfortable, or a person as gentle and easy to associate with.

No single equivalent should be imposed on every derivative.

9. Conclusion

W-ṭ-ʾ concerns treading, foot pressure, and pressing underfoot.

The poem attributed to Al-Aʿshā illustrates heavy pressure and dominance over an enemy in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Arabic also records leveling, softening, preparing, and agreement within this family.

The root occurs six times in five Qur’anic verses.

Yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan in Al-Tawbah 9:120 concerns treading a place.

Lam taṭaʾūhā in Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 describes land not previously trodden by the addressees.

Taṭaʾūhum in Al-Fatḥ 48:25 concerns the risk of overrunning and injuring unidentified people.

Li-yuwāṭiʾū in Al-Tawbah 9:37 belongs to agreement and matching the number of months.

Waṭʾan in Al-Muzzammil 73:6 is explained through the firmness, effect, or difficulty of night rising. Wiṭāʾan foregrounds correspondence between attention and speech, without making that interpretation wholly exclusive to this reading.

The root has several actual branches that cannot all be restricted to physical walking.

The family can be defined as follows:

Treading: placing and pressing a foot on the ground or something else, extending to traversing, trampling, and injury.

Preparing: softening, leveling, or preparing a surface or conditions for use or action.

Agreement: correspondence between things or parties concerning a particular matter.

Effect and firmness: pressure, heaviness, or stronger effect, relevant to explaining waṭʾan in Al-Muzzammil.

Treading, trampling, and harming are not identical. Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 concerns land; Al-Fatḥ 48:25 concerns people potentially harmed unknowingly.

Agreement does not establish rightness. Al-Tawbah 9:37 describes outward numerical correspondence while the divine ruling is altered.

Al-Muzzammil 73:6 uses the root to describe the effect and firmness of night rising without requiring a physical manner of stepping to be imposed on the verse.

Forqan should therefore choose treading in movement contexts, overrunning or trampling for people subjected to pressure, agreement for muwāṭaʾah, and an equivalent suited to effect and firmness for waṭʾan in Al-Muzzammil.

The physical core is treading and foot pressure; each derivative’s Qur’anic meaning is determined by its form and the subject of its verse.
