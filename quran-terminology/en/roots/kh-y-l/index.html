From images appearing in the mind and horses’ showy movement to imagining, supposition, swagger, and arrogance: three Qur’anic branches of one lexical family

The Arabic root KH Y L concerns a form or appearance becoming manifest, conception and supposition, and movement accompanied by display. Khayāl denotes a form or image appearing in dreams, the mind, or before the eyes; takhyīl concerns producing such an appearance or impression. Ikhtiyāl commonly means ostentation and arrogance through which someone displays their superiority.

Khayl is also the familiar name for horses. Early explanations propose a connection between this name and showy, swaggering movement. This must not be treated as established historical etymology: the animal’s name may have a history not fully recoverable from later lexicographers’ explanations.

Mental image, outward appearance, animal movement, and human conceit are related but distinct concepts.

Conceiving something is not necessarily lying or deception. A conception may be accurate or mistaken. A horse can also move beautifully and strikingly without its natural behavior constituting moral arrogance.

By contrast, someone who belittles others and displays superiority through conduct and appearance can be called mukhtāl.

KH Y L occurs nine times in nine Qur’anic verses: five instances of khayl, horses or mounted forces; one yukhayyalu in Moses’s encounter with the magicians; and three mukhtāl condemning arrogant display.

A single English equivalent cannot represent every derivative. Understanding the semantic fields nevertheless clarifies differences between appearance, reality, supposition, and claims of superiority.

1. Forms and semantic fields

KH Y L is a triliteral hollow root with medial yāʾ. Its second consonant is yāʾ, which becomes alif in certain inflected forms.

Important recorded forms and derivatives include:

  • خَالَ ـ يَخَالُ: to suppose, think something is so, or form a conception of it.
  • خَيْل، خِيلَة، خَيَلان، مَخَالَة، مَخِيلَة: verbal nouns and nouns concerning supposition, conception, and appearance.
  • خَيَال: a form, figure, or image appearing in the mind, a dream, or before the eyes.
  • خَيَالَة: the appearance or form of something; in some uses, an appearing figure or image.
  • خَيَّلَ ـ يُخَيِّلُ: to produce an image in another’s perception, or make something appear so that they conceive it in that way.
  • تَخْيِيل: producing an image, appearance, or impression.
  • تَخَيَّلَ: to conceive something in a particular form in the mind or before the eyes.
  • مُخَيَّل: something presented in a particular form or appearance.
  • مُخِيل: something appearing to indicate another matter; in some uses, obscure or doubtful.
  • خَيْل: horses, a collective designation for them.
  • خُيُول: a recorded plural of khayl.
  • خَيَّالَة: horsemen or owners of horses in military and social use.
  • خِيلَاء، خُيَلَاء: ostentation, swagger, arrogance, and displaying superiority.
  • اِخْتَالَ ـ يَخْتَالُ: to behave with swagger and conceit.
  • اِخْتِيَال: ostentation and movement accompanied by arrogance.
  • مُخْتَال: an arrogant, ostentatious person.
  • خَائِل: in some uses, someone marked by swagger; in another setting, someone who supposes.
  • أَخْيَل: a name for a colorful bird; also, in some uses, a description concerning appearance and movement.
  • مَخِيلَة: in natural description, a cloud showing signs of rain.
  • أَخَالَتِ السَّمَاءُ: the sky clouded over and showed signs of rain.

Arabic sources also record khāl for a skin mark, maternal uncle, cloud, and banner. These identically written forms cannot all be historically assigned to this root without independent examination.

Khāl meaning maternal uncle is discussed under KH W L, and must not be equated with khāla, to suppose, merely because they look alike.

Only three groups from this broad family occur in the Qur’an:

Khayl, yukhayyalu, and mukhtāl.

Khāla meaning to suppose, the noun khayāl, the verbal noun khuyalāʾ, and the independent verb ikhtāla do not occur there, although the derivative mukhtāl does.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nine occurrences in nine verses and nine surahs

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes:

الْخَاءُ وَالْيَاءُ وَاللَّامُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى حَرَكَةٍ فِي تَلَوُّنٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-lāmu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ḥarakatin fī talawwunin

Translation:

“Khāʾ, yāʾ, and lām have one underlying principle indicating movement accompanied by changing appearance.”

Ibn Fāris thus does not restrict khayāl to a completely static, motionless image.

In his account, an image may arise in the mind or a dream and appear in different forms.

He then discusses khayāl as a form or figure, connecting it with what someone imagines in sleep.

He also transmits an account deriving the name khayl from horses’ swaggering, showy movement.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, his definition of the semantic field and his attempt to connect its uses.

Second, historically proving that all these words actually developed from that initial image.

The second requires further historical evidence.

Movement accompanied by changing appearance is therefore a useful framework for possible connections, but should not become the definitive meaning of every derivative.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: movement with changing appearance and the account of khayl

2. Khayāl: form, appearance, and supposition

Khāla al-shayʾ: he supposed something was so

«خَالَ الشَّيْءَ يَخَالُهُ» means to suppose or think.

Lisān al-ʿArab mentions verbal nouns including khayl, khīlah, khayalān, and makhīlah for this use.

Someone seeing something at a distance and supposing it is a person can use this family to describe the impression.

It may really be a person, or a tree trunk, shadow, or another object resembling one from afar.

Khāla therefore does not necessarily make the conception false.

A supposition may correspond to reality or fail to do so.

Khiltuhu sometimes takes two objects, like other verbs of supposition.

Grammatically, it can therefore relate something observed to the conception formed about it.

A pre-Islamic witness from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

Ṭarafah’s Muʿallaqah says:

إِذَا الْقَوْمُ قَالُوا مَنْ فَتًى خِلْتُ أَنَّنِي
عُنِيتُ فَلَمْ أَكْسَلْ وَلَمْ أَتَبَلَّدِ

idhā al-qawmu qālū man fatan khiltu annanī / ʿunītu fa-lam aksal wa-lam ataballadi

Translation:

“When the people said, ‘Who is the young champion?’, I supposed they meant me; I neither grew idle nor remained dull and helpless.”

Khiltu belongs to this root.

The poet does not say they necessarily named him. He inferred from their words that he was intended.

Khāla can thus concern someone’s interpretation of others’ words or circumstances.

The poet’s supposition accompanies his readiness to undertake a responsibility.

The verb itself means neither courage nor readiness; those qualities come from the rest of the line.

The witness demonstrates KH Y L as supposition and conception in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, independently of Qur’anic explanations.

Khayāl in dreams, the mind, and before the eyes

Early Arabic uses khayāl for a form or image appearing in dreams, the mind, or before the eyes.

Lisān al-ʿArab includes a shadow, someone’s reflection in a mirror, and an image seen during sleep.

Their relationships to external reality differ.

A mirror image reflects something actually present.

A shadow can arise when an object blocks light.

An image seen in sleep need not correspond to an entity present at that moment.

Khayāl therefore does not mean every false or unreal thing.

It may represent something real, a memory or mental image, or a mistaken appearance in perception.

The shared meaning is a form or appearance arising before perception or the mind.

Early explanations also use «تَخَيَّلَ لَهُ الشَّيْءُ» when something appears to someone in a certain form.

The appearance of a form must nevertheless be distinguished from the accuracy of the judgment about it.

Khayāl: an erected figure for frightening animals

One important early use is khayāl as a figure erected to keep animals away.

Al-Aṣmaʿī describes placing wood in the ground and draping clothing over it so that it looks human from a distance.

A wolf or another animal might mistake it for a person standing there and avoid approaching.

This resembles a modern agricultural scarecrow.

Two different realities are involved:

First, the actual wood and clothing placed in the ground.

Second, the animal’s perceived image, which may be taken for a human.

A real object can therefore produce a misleading impression without the physical object being nonexistent.

This distinction helps explain yukhayyalu ilayhi in Moses’s story.

A poetic witness from Ibn Aḥmar

A poem attributed to Ibn Aḥmar says:

فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّى مَا تَجَلَّى مِنَ الدُّجَى
وَشَمَّرَ صَعْلٌ كَالْخَيَالِ الْمُخَيَّلِ

fa-lammā tajallā mā tajallā mina al-dujā / wa-shammara ṣaʿlun ka-al-khayāli al-mukhayyali

Translation of the sense:

“When what was to clear of the darkness had cleared, and a small-headed creature hastened along, like an erected artificial figure.”

An entity described as ṣaʿl is compared to al-khayāl al-mukhayyal. Ṣaʿl describes a small head; shammara concerns movement and haste. The entity being compared must not be identified with the artificial figure itself.

The comparison concerns the constructed figure erected in the ground.

Ibn Aḥmar al-Bāhilī is a mukhaḍram poet, having lived before and after Islam; Ibn Sallām places him among the Islamic poets. The date of this particular line is unknown. Attribution alone establishes neither a pre-Qur’anic nor a post-Qur’anic date for it.

Khayāl is thus not only an inward mental occurrence. An external object can also bear the name because of its form and appearance.

Lisān al-ʿArab: khayāl, the artificial figure, and Ibn Aḥmar’s witness

3. Makhīlah: a sign producing an expectation

A cloud promising rain

Makhīlah denotes a cloud showing signs of rainfall. Lexically, makhīlah with fatḥah on the mīm names the cloud; mukhīlah with ḍammah can describe a sky ready for rain.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

السَّحَابَةُ الْمُخِيلَةُ

al-saḥābatu al-mukhīlatu

A cloud that leads an observer to expect rain.

Akhālat al-samāʾ and takhayyalat al-samāʾ also describe the sky clouding over and showing signs of rain.

Rain has not necessarily fallen yet.

The observer sees signs such as clouds, a darkened sky, or lightning and considers rainfall likely.

It may rain, or the cloud may pass without rain.

Makhīlah can thus denote a sign producing an expectation in the mind.

This clarifies the connection between outward appearance and inward supposition.

It does not make every expectation of rain mistaken, or makhīlah inherently deceptive.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Gharīb al-Ḥadīth: makhīlah and mukhīlah

Appearance versus reality

Khayāl and makhīlah allow three situations to be distinguished:

First, something exists and is accurately perceived.

Second, something exists but is mistaken for something else because of distance, form, or viewing conditions.

Third, someone has an image in sleep or the mind that need not correspond to an external body at that moment.

KH Y L can describe all three.

Khayāl must therefore not be equated from the outset with unreality.

Appearance and reality require separate examination in each context.

4. Khayl: horses and the display of movement

Khayl names a group of horses

Khayl is the familiar Arabic name for horses.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْخَيْلُ: الْفُرْسَانُ

al-khaylu al-fursānu

Ibn Sīdah explains:

جَمَاعَةُ الْأَفْرَاسِ لَا وَاحِدَ لَهُ مِنْ لَفْظِهِ

jamāʿatu al-afrāsi lā wāḥida lahu min lafẓihi

That is, “A group of horses, with no singular of the same lexical form.”

Khayl is therefore a collective noun.

Faras normally names one horse; khayl describes horses collectively.

Military usage can extend the word from horses to their riders, since horse and rider form a practical unit in movement or battle.

Khuyūl is also recorded as a plural of khayl.

Khayl lexically names animals, without itself judging their owner’s moral worth.

A horse can serve travel, work, transport, defense, racing, or adornment.

Was khayl named from ikhtiyāl?

Ibn Fāris transmits Al-Aṣmaʿī’s account in which Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ is asked why khayl received that name.

A young Bedouin replies:

لِاخْتِيَالِهَا

li-ikhtiyālihā

That is, “Because of their swaggering, showy movement.”

Ibn Fāris finds this consistent with his proposed principle of movement and changing appearance.

It is understandable as an observation of horse behavior.

In walking, running, or display, a horse can hold its head and neck high, take prominent steps, and look striking.

Describing this movement must nevertheless be distinguished from proving the name’s historical origin.

The account is an early lexical explanation, rather than definitive evidence of the word’s first emergence.

A horse’s natural behavior is not moral arrogance either.

Someone may compare its movement to a swaggering person without establishing that the animal intends to belittle others or claim moral superiority.

A pre-Islamic witness from ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah says:

هَلَّا سَأَلْتِ الْخَيْلَ يَا ابْنَةَ مَالِكٍ
إِنْ كُنْتِ جَاهِلَةً بِمَا لَمْ تَعْلَمِي

hallā saʾalti al-khayla yā ibnata mālikin / in kunti jāhilatan bi-mā lam taʿlamī

It continues:

يُخْبِرْكِ مَنْ شَهِدَ الْوَقِيعَةَ أَنَّنِي
أَغْشَى الْوَغَى وَأَعِفُّ عِنْدَ الْمَغْنَمِ

yukhbirki man shahida al-waqīʿata annanī / aghshā al-waghā wa-aʿiffu ʿinda al-maghnami

Translation:

“Daughter of Mālik, if you do not know, why not ask the horsemen about what you do not know? Whoever witnessed the battle will tell you that I enter the fighting and show restraint when spoils are taken.”

Khayl literally denotes horses, but early commentators explain that the riders and participants in battle are intended.

Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-Sabʿ al-Ṭiwāl al-Jāhiliyyāt explains saʾalti al-khayl as asking their riders and horsemen.

The animal’s name thus extends to those riding it who can report the event.

This helps explain Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, where khayl can likewise describe mounted forces.

Versions of ʿAntarah’s Dīwān differ in certain phrases, but the use of khayl and its connection with horsemen are attested in early commentaries.

This does not establish horses literally speaking to humans. Asking khayl is a literary expression for asking human witnesses present in battle.

Text of Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-Sabʿ: khayl as horsemen

5. Ikhtiyāl: from showy movement to conceit

Khuyalāʾ and ikhtiyāl

Khuyalāʾ and ikhtiyāl concern swagger, ostentation, and arrogance.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains mukhtāl as an arrogant, ostentatious person.

Arrogance here is not merely a hidden feeling. Conduct and appearance commonly display it too.

Someone may walk, speak, or treat others in a way that presents themselves as superior and others as inferior.

Ikhtiyāl can thus concern movement and the outward display of conceit.

Beautiful movement must nevertheless be distinguished from arrogant behavior.

Someone may walk upright, dress well, or enjoy legitimate success without intending to belittle others.

Conversely, someone simply dressed may belittle others through conduct and judgment.

Ikhtiyāl must therefore not be restricted to clothing, head posture, or a particular gait.

An early rajaz witness concerning swagger

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits this rajaz:

يَمْشِي مِنَ الْخَيْلَةِ يَوْمَ الْوِرْدِ
بَغْيًا كَمَا يَمْشِي وَلِيُّ الْعَهْدِ

yamshī mina al-khaylati yawma al-wirdi / baghyan ka-mā yamshī waliyyu al-ʿahdi

Translation:

“At the watering place, he walks in swagger and insolence, as a crown prince walks displaying superiority.”

Khaylah concerns swagger and ostentation here.

Baghyan further clarifies the negative coloring of the conduct.

The witness does more than neutrally describe steps: swagger and insolence are present in its imagery. Nevertheless, the walker’s identity is unclear from this brief quotation, so no definite human’s or animal’s moral intention should be assigned without further evidence.

The lexical report does not identify the poet or precisely date the rajaz.

It is evidence of early usage, but must not be assigned to a particular pre-Islamic poet without evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the anonymous rajaz concerning al-khaylah

Ostentation versus possessing real ability

Someone may genuinely possess greater knowledge, ability, wealth, or skill in a particular field.

Possessing these qualities is not itself ikhtiyāl.

Ikhtiyāl concerns becoming conceited through such advantages and displaying superiority before others.

Someone may also claim nonexistent ability or knowledge and use that invented claim to belittle others.

Ikhtiyāl can accompany real or claimed advantages; in both cases, the moral issue concerns conduct and attitudes toward oneself and others.

Mukhtāl must therefore not simply mean a capable person, a well-dressed person, or a confident individual.

6. Yukhayyalu: the appearance of movement in Moses’s story

قَالَ بَلْ أَلْقُوا فَإِذَا حِبَالُهُمْ وَعِصِيُّهُمْ يُخَيَّلُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ سِحْرِهِمْ أَنَّهَا تَسْعَىٰ

qāla bal alqū fa-idhā ḥibāluhum wa-ʿiṣiyyuhum yukhayyalu ilayhi min siḥrihim annahā tasʿā

Proposed translation:

“He said: Rather, you throw. Then suddenly their ropes and staffs, through their magic, were made to appear to him as though they were moving.”

Ṭā Hā 20:66

Yukhayyalu is a passive imperfect verb of Form II.

Its active form is khayyala, and its verbal noun takhyīl.

In the Qur’anic expression, something appears to Moses in a particular way.

Ilayhi identifies the person receiving that appearance.

Min siḥrihim identifies its source or cause in the verse’s context.

Annahā tasʿā states the content of the impression produced for him.

Takhyīl here therefore concerns producing or experiencing an image in perception.

Were the staffs and ropes actually moving?

The verse reports movement appearing in Moses’s perception.

Yukhayyalu alone does not explain the phenomenon’s physical mechanism.

In a deceptive phenomenon, an object may remain completely still while the observer thinks it moves.

Alternatively, something may really move while the observer misunderstands its nature or the cause of motion.

Either situation can involve a difference between what actually happens and what appears in perception.

Ṭā Hā 20:66 explicitly emphasizes takhyīl without technically explaining the magicians’ method.

Some early exegetical reports mention materials used to move the ropes and staffs. These are proposed mechanisms, rather than the Qur’an’s explicit wording.

Al-Ṭabarī: perceptual appearance and reports of the magicians’ method

The connection with Al-Aʿrāf 7:116

Al-Aʿrāf 7:116 says:

سَحَرُوا أَعْيُنَ النَّاسِ وَاسْتَرْهَبُوهُمْ وَجَاءُوا بِسِحْرٍ عَظِيمٍ

saḥarū aʿyuna al-nāsi wa-istarhabūhum wa-jāʾū bi-siḥrin ʿaẓīmin

Proposed translation:

“They bewitched the people’s eyes, frightened them, and brought mighty magic.”

This clarifies visual perception and fear within the same confrontation.

Ṭā Hā 20:66 describes magic affecting how the ropes and staffs appeared.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:116 concerns its effect on the people’s eyes and their fear.

Together, the verses involve more than objects changing position: spectators’ perception and responses matter too.

They do not establish that every Qur’anic phenomenon of magic has exactly the same mechanism.

Nor is every mistaken image or visual error necessarily magic.

Takhyīl and discerning truth

Later in the story, Moses is commanded to throw what is in his hand.

Ṭā Hā 20:69 calls what the magicians made kaydu sāḥir, a magician’s device.

This connection distinguishes deceptive appearance from the truth subsequently revealed.

Takhyīl is nevertheless not lexically limited to magic.

Someone can conceive something without deliberate deception. Another may intentionally influence perception through images or performance.

Takhyīl concerns an appearance arising; its accuracy or deceptiveness is established through reality and context.

7. Qur’anic khayl: horses, resources, and cavalry

Adorned horses among worldly advantages: Āl ʿImrān 3:14

Āl ʿImrān 3:14 says:

وَالْخَيْلِ الْمُسَوَّمَةِ وَالْأَنْعَامِ وَالْحَرْثِ

wa-al-khayli al-musawwamati wa-al-anʿāmi wa-al-ḥarthi

Proposed translation of the relevant passage:

“And marked, adorned horses, livestock, and cultivated land.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:14

Khayl appears among things whose love has been made attractive to people.

Al-musawwamah belongs to S W M. Lexical and exegetical explanations associate it with marked, beautiful, distinguished, or prepared horses.

Khayl itself means horses, rather than adornment.

The verse lists women, children, precious wealth, horses, livestock, and cultivated land together.

These are different realities that can attract human affection.

The list does not make owning or liking horses inherently sinful.

The verse continues with worldly enjoyment and the better destination with God.

The issue is the place of worldly advantages relative to human destiny, rather than declaring them all forbidden.

Khayl in defensive preparedness: Al-Anfāl 8:60

وَأَعِدُّوا لَهُمْ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ مِنْ قُوَّةٍ وَمِنْ رِبَاطِ الْخَيْلِ

wa-aʿiddū lahum mā istaṭaʿtum min quwwatin wa-min ribāṭi al-khayli

Proposed translation of the relevant passage:

“Prepare against them whatever strength you can, including force and horses kept ready.”

Al-Anfāl 8:60

The verse uses ribāṭ al-khayl.

Ribāṭ belongs to R B Ṭ, concerning binding, keeping, and maintaining readiness.

Horses are part of the familiar means of military preparedness in this context.

Khayl must not itself become war or weapons in translation. It names the animal, while the expression and context establish its military role.

The verse also begins with mā istaṭaʿtum min quwwah: preparedness is not restricted to horses.

Naming horses does not require every period’s defensive equipment to consist of that animal or invalidate other legitimate, effective means.

Al-Anfāl 8:61 also discusses the other side inclining toward peace.

Preparedness must therefore be read alongside Qur’anic principles concerning warfare, peace, covenants, and justice.

Lexically, the conclusion is specific: khayl means horses here; ribāṭ describes keeping them ready.

Horses, mules, and donkeys: riding and adornment in Al-Naḥl 16:8

وَالْخَيْلَ وَالْبِغَالَ وَالْحَمِيرَ لِتَرْكَبُوهَا وَزِينَةً وَيَخْلُقُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-al-khayla wa-al-bighāla wa-al-ḥamīra li-tarkabūhā wa-zīnatan wa-yakhluqu mā lā taʿlamūna

Proposed translation:

“And horses, mules, and donkeys, for you to ride and as adornment; and He creates what you do not know.”

Al-Naḥl 16:8

Khayl accompanies two other animal groups.

Bighāl means mules, and ḥamīr donkeys.

The three separate names show that khayl is not a generic designation for all riding animals here: it denotes horses.

Two benefits are mentioned:

لِتَرْكَبُوهَا

li-tarkabūhā

and

زِينَةً

wa-zīnatan

Riding and adornment.

The Qur’an thus presents these animals’ practical usefulness and beauty within one context.

Mentioning riding and adornment does not itself exclude every other benefit.

Being an adornment is also different from the owner’s arrogance.

A horse may be beautiful and well adorned without its keeper necessarily being mukhtāl.

Conversely, someone may use a horse or any other possession to belittle people and display superiority.

That conduct requires independent moral assessment.

Bi-khaylika wa-rajilika: cavalry and infantry in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64

Addressing Satan, the verse says:

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُمْ بِصَوْتِكَ وَأَجْلِبْ عَلَيْهِمْ بِخَيْلِكَ وَرَجِلِكَ

wa-istafziz mani istaṭaʿta minhum bi-ṣawtika wa-ajlib ʿalayhim bi-khaylika wa-rajilika

Proposed translation of the relevant passage:

“Stir up whomever you can among them with your voice, and assail them with your cavalry and infantry.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64

Khayl appears alongside rajil.

Rajil here describes infantry, contrasting with mounted forces.

This contrast is familiar in Arabic military language.

Bi-khaylika can therefore be translated “with your horsemen” or “with your cavalry.”

Does the verse describe Satan’s actual physical horses, or use an image to describe mobilizing his forces and instruments?

Voice, cavalry, infantry, participation in wealth and children, and promising appear together.

The ending also says:

وَمَا يَعِدُهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ إِلَّا غُرُورًا

wa-mā yaʿiduhumu al-shayṭānu illā ghurūran

The broader subject is thus the means of enticing and misleading people.

Cavalry and infantry can in this context image the mobilization of every means of enticement.

This fits the familiar language of armies and warfare.

It does not establish physical cavalry units belonging to Satan that humans could observe like an ordinary army.

Conversely, figurative explanation must not dissolve khayl into any unspecified force.

Its basic meaning remains horses or horsemen, with the address understood through its context.

Al-Ṭabarī: cavalry and infantry in the address to Satan

Min khaylin wa-lā rikāb: horses and riding camels in Al-Ḥashr 59:6

فَمَا أَوْجَفْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ خَيْلٍ وَلَا رِكَابٍ

fa-mā awjaftum ʿalayhi min khaylin wa-lā rikābin

Proposed translation of the relevant passage:

“You urged neither horses nor riding camels into motion to obtain it.”

Al-Ḥashr 59:6

Khayl appears alongside rikāb.

Early lexical explanations identify rikāb in this expression as riding camels.

Two familiar groups of mounts thus appear in a setting of movement and operations.

Awjaftum belongs to W J F and concerns swift movement and driving mounts onward.

The context distinguishes property obtained through an ordinary military expedition from property reaching the Messenger under other circumstances.

Khayl itself means neither spoils nor fayʾ.

Those matters are established through the complete verse and its independent words.

Nor does the verse make every movement on horseback necessarily military or aggressive.

Khayl names the animal here; context supplies the military role.

Al-Ṭabarī: rikāb as camels and the absence of a military expedition

8. Qur’anic mukhtāl: conceit and others’ rights

Arrogance alongside social responsibility: Al-Nisāʾ 4:36

A passage of Al-Nisāʾ 4:36 ends:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ مَنْ كَانَ مُخْتَالًا فَخُورًا

inna allāha lā yuḥibbu man kāna mukhtālan fakhūran

Proposed translation:

“The God does not love anyone arrogant in display and boastful.”

The preceding passage concerns worshipping God and kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, neighbors, companions, and travelers.

Condemning mukhtāl and fakhūr therefore occurs within human and social responsibilities.

This connection matters.

Someone who considers themselves superior may disregard the rights of needy or less powerful people.

They might ignore a poor neighbor or refuse assistance they consider beneath their status.

These are illustrative instances. Mukhtāl does not itself mean neglecting an orphan or neighbor.

Its lexical meaning concerns conceit and ostentatious display.

Mukhtāl versus fakhūr

Mukhtālan fakhūran contains two independent descriptions.

Mukhtāl belongs to KH Y L and concerns ostentation and arrogance.

Fakhūr belongs to F KH R and concerns boasting and parading one’s advantages.

The behaviors can coincide without being identical.

Someone may display superiority through movement, conduct, and outward bearing, or boast verbally about wealth, lineage, position, and achievements.

Superiority is present in either, but its means of expression may differ.

Translation must not omit either word in a way that erases their distinction.

Do not walk upon the earth exultantly: Luqmān 31:18

وَلَا تُصَعِّرْ خَدَّكَ لِلنَّاسِ وَلَا تَمْشِ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَحًا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūrin

Proposed translation:

“Do not turn your cheek away from people contemptuously, nor walk upon the earth in conceited exultation. The God does not love anyone arrogant in display and boastful.”

Luqmān 31:18

Mukhtāl is placed alongside two behaviors.

First, turning one’s face away from people in a blameworthy manner.

Second, walking with maraḥ.

Lexical sources associate maraḥ with exultation, excessive joy, and frivolous or arrogant behavior.

Maraḥ nevertheless belongs to another root and must not be equated with ikhtiyāl.

Conceit can become visible in how someone treats people and walks.

Not every calm, well-presented, or confident manner of walking is ikhtiyāl.

The conduct condemned here is contemptuous behavior and displaying superiority.

The following verse, Luqmān 31:19, discusses moderation in walking and lowering one’s voice.

The connection concerns regulating conduct toward others, rather than prescribing one outward form for every walk.

Mukhtāl and responses to advantage and deprivation: Al-Ḥadīd 57:23

لِكَيْلَا تَأْسَوْا عَلَىٰ مَا فَاتَكُمْ وَلَا تَفْرَحُوا بِمَا آتَاكُمْ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

li-kaylā taʾsaw ʿalā mā fātakum wa-lā tafraḥū bi-mā ātākum wa-allāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūrin

Proposed translation:

“So that you do not suffer consuming grief over what escaped you, nor become conceitedly exultant over what you were given. The God does not love anyone arrogant in display and boastful.”

Al-Ḥadīd 57:23

Mukhtāl and fakhūr concern responses to advantage and deprivation here.

The verse mentions what escapes a person and what they are given.

It then condemns ostentation and boasting.

This connection highlights how someone responds to blessings and resources.

After gaining wealth, position, or another advantage, someone may consider themselves superior and parade it before others.

This does not make every joy at receiving a blessing inherently wrong.

Yūnus 10:58 also invites rejoicing in God’s bounty and mercy.

Legitimate joy thus differs from exultation accompanied by conceit.

Loss can also cause natural sorrow. Al-Ḥadīd’s context discourages attaching one’s worth and identity to impermanent advantages.

Mukhtāl here therefore concerns arrogance and display that can arise from advantages, rather than property itself or joy as a human experience.

9. Differences from related roots

KH Y L (khayāl) compared with Ẓ N N (ẓann)

Ẓann concerns supposition and judgment about something.

Khāla can likewise mean to suppose.

Khayāl, however, also encompasses form and appearance.

Someone may conceive something mentally without yet judging its external reality definitively.

Ẓann can concern judgment and estimating whether something is true.

Khayāl and ẓann are therefore close in some uses but not complete synonyms.

Yukhayyalu in Ṭā Hā 20:66 concerns movement appearing in Moses’s perception, rather than merely an abstract supposition.

Study of Ẓ N N

KH Y L (takhyīl) compared with W H M (wahm)

Wahm in familiar Arabic uses concerns a conception or supposition that may be mistaken.

Takhyīl can also produce a false perceptual image.

They are nevertheless not identical.

Takhyīl concerns an image or appearance arising or being produced.

Wahm emphasizes a mental impression, particularly when it does not correspond to reality.

Not every takhyīl is necessarily a false wahm, although the magicians’ story involves deceptive perception.

KH Y L (mukhtāl) compared with K B R (takabbur)

Kibr in the Qur’an concerns conceit and superiority.

Mukhtāl describes someone displaying such superiority through conduct and appearance.

Ikhtiyāl can foreground the behavioral, demonstrative aspect of arrogance.

Someone may harbor arrogance without openly displaying it in every situation.

Ikhtiyāl, by contrast, commonly involves behavior and display.

These concepts are not wholly separate. Mukhtāl can be understood as an outward expression of arrogance.

Study of K B R

KH Y L (mukhtāl) compared with F KH R (fakhr)

Fakhr concerns parading one’s advantages and superiority.

Mukhtāl also makes a display.

Boasting can especially appear through speech and claims, while ikhtiyāl can appear through movement and conduct.

The two descriptions occur together in three Qur’anic verses.

Translation should therefore retain both: “arrogant in display and boastful.”

KH Y L (mukhtāl) compared with M R Ḥ (maraḥ)

Maraḥ appears alongside the condemnation of mukhtāl in Luqmān 31:18.

It can concern exultation and frivolous or excessive behavior.

Ikhtiyāl concerns ostentation and displaying superiority.

Joy and exultation may foster arrogant conduct, but not every joy is ikhtiyāl.

Someone may also belittle others harshly or coldly without joy or exultation.

Maraḥ and ikhtiyāl can therefore coincide in conduct without being lexically identical.

KH Y L (khayl) compared with F R S (faras)

Faras names a horse.

Khayl is a collective noun for horses.

Military use can also extend khayl to mounted forces.

Both concern horses, but differ in form and usage.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, khayl stands alongside rajil, foregrounding mounted forces.

In Al-Naḥl 16:8, it accompanies mules and donkeys, identifying the animals themselves.

KH Y L (khayl) compared with R K B (rukūb)

Rukūb concerns riding.

Khayl names horses.

Someone may ride a horse, but not every means of riding is a horse.

Al-Naḥl 16:8 independently names horses, mules, and donkeys.

Khayl must therefore not mean all vehicles, although horses were a means of transport in that society.

KH Y L (khayāl) compared with Ṣ W R (ṣūrah)

Ṣūrah concerns something’s shape and form.

Khayāl can likewise concern form and appearance.

In perceptual uses, however, khayāl emphasizes what appears in the mind or before the eyes.

Ṭā Hā 20:66 concerns more than the ropes’ and staffs’ shape: their movement appears to Moses as though they are hastening.

Takhyīl can thus extend beyond a static shape to the appearance of movement or a condition.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

No single short equivalent of KH Y L conveys every Qur’anic use without ambiguity.

The basic equivalent for khayl is horses.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, its contrast with rajil makes cavalry or horsemen more natural.

In Ṭā Hā 20:66, yukhayyalu can mean “it was made to appear” or “it seemed to him.”

These preserve the difference between external reality and perceived impression without adding the magicians’ precise method.

In its three occurrences, mukhtāl can mean arrogantly ostentatious or conceited.

“Ostentatious” alone may be too broad, since not every attempt to attract attention involves arrogance and belittling others.

Combining display with superiority in a word box therefore provides greater precision.

Brief root meaning

The appearance or conception of something in the mind or before the eyes; movement accompanied by display. Specific derivatives denote horses, producing an impression, and arrogant ostentation.

The family’s possible shared meaning must not replace each derivative’s clear meaning.

Khayl in Al-Naḥl 16:8 does not mean a mental image; mukhtāl in Luqmān 31:18 does not name a horse or rider.

Nor does yukhayyalu in Ṭā Hā 20:66 make every act of imagining falsehood or magic.

Conclusion

KH Y L comprises several related semantic fields in Arabic.

In one branch, khayāl denotes a form or appearance arising in the mind, sleep, or before the eyes. Khāla means to suppose, while takhyīl concerns producing a conception or appearance.

In another, khayl names horses collectively. Early lexicographers propose a connection with horses’ showy movement, but further evidence is needed before treating that explanation as the name’s definitive historical origin.

In the moral branch, ikhtiyāl and mukhtāl concern human ostentation and conceit.

All three branches occur in the Qur’an.

Khayl appears five times concerning horses and horsemen: among worldly advantages, as a means of riding and adornment, or as part of military strength and resources.

Yukhayyalu appears once in Moses’s story, describing how the magicians’ ropes and staffs appeared to move.

Mukhtāl appears three times condemning arrogance and boasting, particularly concerning others’ rights, social conduct, and responses to worldly advantages.

Appearance, conception, showy movement, and ostentation are related fields, but differ in meaning and moral assessment.

A conception can be accurate or mistaken. A horse’s beauty and movement are not inherently blameworthy. Possessing blessings does not necessarily mean arrogance.

Mukhtāl condemns conceit and displaying superiority before others.

Yukhayyalu in Moses’s story concerns the distinction between perceived appearance and the reality to be recognized.

This family must therefore not be reduced to a single moral or philosophical meaning in Forqan’s root studies. Each derivative requires its own linguistic field and Qur’anic context, preserving defensible connections without inventing unproven historical relationships. Brief root meaning: The appearance or conception of something in the mind or before the eyes; movement accompanied by display. Specific derivatives denote horses, producing an impression, and arrogant ostentation.
