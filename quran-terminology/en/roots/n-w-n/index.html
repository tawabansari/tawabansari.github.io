Fish, the letter nūn, other lexical uses, and Qur’anic dhā al-nūn

The Arabic root N W N is known through nūn, whose early meanings include ḥūt, a fish. Some sources use it for a large fish. The Qur’an employs it in dhā al-nūn to identify Jonah.

Nūn is not only a fish name. It is also a letter name, and dictionaries record an inkwell, a sword blade, and other uses. Identical word forms alone do not establish one historical origin for every meaning.

The lexical root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87. Al-Qalam also opens with نٓ, but ordinary morphological analysis treats this as a disconnected letter, not a second occurrence of the noun meaning fish. Both passages need discussion without merging their root counts.

1. The main meaning in early dictionaries

One lexical definition states:

النُّونُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالنُّونُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ، وَالنُّونُ: الْحُوتُ.

al-nūnu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-nūnu kalimatun wāḥidatun wa-al-nūnu al-ḥūtu

Meaning: “Nūn, wāw, and nūn form one lexical item; nūn means ḥūt.”

The explanation then identifies Dhū al-Nūn as a sword name among some Arabs and tentatively relates that naming to a resemblance to a fish.

Unlike many root definitions, this does not introduce an abstract concept such as movement, height, gathering, or light. It directly names a particular creature: a fish.

Another explanation distinguishes the letter name from the fish name and states:

وَالنُّونُ: الْحُوتُ الْعَظِيمُ.

wa-al-nūnu al-ḥūtu al-ʿaẓīmu

Meaning: “Nūn is a large fish.”

“Fish” is thus an established Arabic meaning, and some dictionaries record “large fish.” The latter does not determine the animal’s precise species, size, or certain classification as a marine mammal.

Does nūn necessarily mean a whale?

Persian translations sometimes use “whale” for ḥūt in Jonah’s story. The choice requires care.

Ḥūt is a broad Arabic fish name. Nūn is likewise explained as ḥūt and sometimes ḥūt ʿaẓīm.

“Large fish” is therefore a more cautious lexical equivalent. The word alone does not establish whether the creature would be classified today as a whale, shark, or another large marine animal.

A root study should first preserve the established Arabic meaning rather than replace it with a guess about species.

2. Lexical forms and derivatives

Nūn: a fish name

Nūn is a noun, not a verb occurring in different Qur’anic conjugations.

The noun’s lexical definition states:

النُّونُ: الْحُوتُ، وَالْجَمْعُ أَنْوَانٌ وَنِينَانٌ.

al-nūnu al-ḥūtu wa-al-jamʿu anwānun wa-nīnānun

Meaning: “Nūn means fish; its plurals are anwān and nīnān.”

The two familiar plurals are أَنْوَان and نِينَان.

A morphological explanation reconstructs نِينَان as originally نِوْنَان, with wāw becoming yāʾ under the influence of the first nūn’s kasrah.

This recognizes the medial wāw in the word’s structure. Reconstructing the plural alone, however, does not establish the fish name’s historical origin.

Nūnah: a small fish or a particular use for fish

Some Arabic uses record nūnah as a fish.

One lexical transmission cites a poem from Arabic speakers of Qays containing al-nūnah. Those speakers explained that they meant a fish.

The word thus belongs to the aquatic-animal field in this use.

Its final tāʾ should not be taken without evidence as indicating a female fish; nor must every use necessarily mean a small fish.

Nūnah: a chin dimple

Another recorded meaning is a small hollow or dimple in a child’s chin.

Arabic dictionaries record this sense.

One can imagine a resemblance to curved or hollow shapes, but deriving the name from a fish, the letter’s shape, or another source requires independent historical evidence.

Visual similarity alone should not become a certain etymology.

Nūn: a sword blade

Some early dictionaries also record nūn for a sword blade or cutting edge.

Dhū al-Nūn and Dhū al-Nūnayn are also recorded as names of particular swords.

A general name for part of a sword must remain distinct from a sword’s proper name.

Dhū al-Nūn in Jonah’s story does not mean a sword bearer. The same construction can refer to different things in different settings.

Nawwana and tanwīn: from letter name to grammatical term

نَوَّنَ الاسمَ means to give a noun tanwīn.

Tanwīn concerns a vowelless n sound pronounced at the end of some nouns without an ordinary written nūn letter there.

This use derives from the letter name, not from fish.

The emphatic nūn, feminine-plural nūn, protective nūn, and plural nūn likewise concern a letter and its grammatical role, not semantic derivatives of the fish name.

These grammatical structures occur frequently in the Qur’an, but a word containing nūn does not thereby derive from N W N.

3. Early Arabic and poetic witnesses

Dhū al-Nūn: a sword name in pre-Islamic Arabic

One lexical witness concerns a sword named in reports of tribal warfare.

One account places Dhū al-Nūn with Mālik ibn Zuhayr. After his death, Ḥamal ibn Badr took it; al-Ḥārith ibn Zuhayr later obtained it.

A line attributed to al-Ḥārith ibn Zuhayr says:

وَيُخْبِرُهُمْ مَكَانَ النُّونِ مِنِّي
وَمَا أُعْطِيتُهُ عَرَقَ الْخِلَالِ

wa-yukhbiruhum makāna al-nūni minnī / wa-mā uʿṭītuhu ʿaraqa al-khilāli

“He tells them of Nūn’s place with me; I was not given it as the reward of friendship.”

The sense is that others will learn how the sword Nūn came into the poet’s hands: through confrontation and taking it, rather than friendship or a gift.

The witness matters in several ways.

First, nūn here is a sword’s proper name, not a fish.

Second, lexical explanations connect the naming with its shape, but this does not establish the name’s precise history.

Third, the people and event belong to accounts of pre-Islamic Arabian tribes. Transmissions differ about the sword’s original owner and some verses’ attribution. Another account names Dhū al-Nūn as al-Ḥārith ibn Ẓālim’s sword.

The witness supports nūn as a sword name in Arabic literary tradition without making every historical detail equally certain.

Dhū al-Nūnayn: a sword with two curved parts

Lexical material records Dhū al-Nūnayn for a sword with a broad blade and curved parts.

The name is explained as applying to a particular blade shape.

Nūn thus relates to a shape or particular sword part in some settings.

This does not establish that nūn originally meant curvature or a bow-like shape, or that the fish name necessarily came from it.

That conclusion would require independent etymological evidence.

Nūnah in a verse witness

A lexical transmission from Arabic speakers of Qays cites a verse about a bucket and water. The full bucket is likened to ʿayn al-nūnah.

The transmitter explains that those speakers understood nūnah as fish. Another transmission has al-mūlah instead of al-nūnah.

The variant matters: the witness’s wording was not uniform.

Its composition date and poet are also unclear. It cannot confidently be called pre-Islamic poetry, although it records usage reported from Arabic speakers.

What the witnesses establish

Nūn was not limited to fish; it also named objects, particularly swords.

For the general fish name, the present examination has not found a pre-Islamic poetic witness whose wording and attribution are sufficiently certain to reconstruct the word’s emergence.

That limitation should not be filled by invented accounts of fish shapes, curved swords, or the letter’s form. An established dictionary meaning differs from proof of its historical origin.

4. Qur’anic wa-dhā al-nūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87

وَذَا النُّونِ إِذْ ذَهَبَ مُغَاضِبًا فَظَنَّ أَنْ لَنْ نَقْدِرَ عَلَيْهِ فَنَادَىٰ فِي الظُّلُمَاتِ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنْتَ سُبْحَانَكَ إِنِّي كُنْتُ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-dhā al-nūni idh dhahaba mughāḍiban fa-ẓanna an lan naqdira ʿalayhi fa-nādā fī al-ẓulumāti an lā ilāha illā anta subḥānaka innī kuntu mina al-ẓālimīna

A close rendering:

“And the companion of the fish, when he departed in anger and thought We would not constrain him; then he called in the darknesses: There is no deity but You. Glory be to You; I have indeed been among the wrongdoers.”

Dhā al-nūn refers to Jonah. Interpretive explanations likewise identify it as ṣāḥib al-ḥūt and the person as Jonah.

Morphology and syntax

Wa-dhā al-nūn contains two main words.

Dhā is the accusative form of dhū, one of the traditionally named five nouns. Here it means someone possessing or associated with something.

Al-nūni is a definite genitive noun from N W N, the genitive complement of dhā.

Dhā is therefore not the demonstrative “this.” Nor should Arabic dhū be confused with Persian do, “two.”

The expression means “the companion of the fish,” not “two fish” or “this fish.”

Dhā is accusative and connects with the preceding remembrance of prophets. An understood udhkur gives the sense “remember also the companion of the fish.”

Qur’anic morphological analysis treats al-nūn as a masculine genitive noun.

Why is Jonah called the companion of the fish?

The passage should be read with the other verses about Jonah.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:139, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 say:

وَإِنَّ يُونُسَ لَمِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ
إِذْ أَبَقَ إِلَى الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ
فَسَاهَمَ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُدْحَضِينَ
فَالْتَقَمَهُ الْحُوتُ وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ

wa-inna yūnusa la-mina al-mursalīna / idh abaqa ilā al-fulki al-mashḥūni / fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīna / fa-iltaqamahu al-ḥūtu wa-huwa mulīmun

“Jonah was indeed among the messengers, when he fled to the laden ship; he shared in the drawing of lots and was among those who lost. The fish swallowed him while he was blameworthy.”

The verses explicitly name Jonah and then describe al-ḥūt swallowing him.

Al-Qalam 68:48 also says:

فَاصْبِرْ لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ وَلَا تَكُنْ كَصَاحِبِ الْحُوتِ إِذْ نَادَىٰ وَهُوَ مَكْظُومٌ

fa-iṣbir li-ḥukmi rabbika wa-lā takun ka-ṣāḥibi al-ḥūti idh nādā wa-huwa makẓūmun

“Remain steadfast for your Lord’s judgment, and do not be like the companion of the fish when he called, filled with suppressed distress.”

Three expressions can now be compared:

  • Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87: ذَا النُّونِ.
  • Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142: الْحُوتُ.
  • Al-Qalam 68:48: صَاحِبِ الْحُوتِ.

This is particularly clear Qur’anic evidence for nūn’s meaning.

One verse calls the person the companion of nūn; another names Jonah and describes ḥūt swallowing him; a third calls the same figure the companion of ḥūt.

The Qur’an itself thus connects nūn in dhā al-nūn with the creature called ḥūt elsewhere.

This does not require external narratives and agrees with the recorded lexical meaning.

Does dhū al-nūn mean the fish’s owner?

Dhū can express ownership but is not restricted to it. It can also denote association, attachment, or a particular relationship.

The story does not say Jonah owned or raised the fish.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 instead explicitly says the fish swallowed him.

“Companion of the fish” identifies Jonah through the event relating him to it, rather than establishing ownership.

Even with a clear lexical meaning, the relationship within a construction depends on context.

5. Nūn in Jonah’s story: meaning and interpretation

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87 includes Jonah’s angry departure, his supposition, calling in the darknesses, and admitting wrongdoing.

The next verse, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:88, says:

فَاسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُ وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْغَمِّ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نُنْجِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

fa-istajabnā lahu wa-najjaynāhu mina al-ghammi wa-kadhālika nunjī al-muʾminīna

“We answered him and delivered him from distress; thus We deliver the believers.” (Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:88)

The verses allow a separate study of error, acknowledgment, return, and deliverance. None is the lexical meaning of nūn.

Fa-ẓanna an lan naqdira ʿalayhi also has lexical and interpretive discussion. Naqdira ʿalayhi is explained here as constraining or placing under pressure, with other explanations also recorded. This belongs to Q D R, not N W N.

Study of Q D R

Darknesses and narrative details

Familiar interpretations sometimes identify the darknesses as night, sea, and the fish’s interior.

Al-ẓulumāt itself does not name those three separately.

These verses likewise do not specify the animal’s scientific classification, size, Jonah’s exact time inside it, or how he survived.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:144 says:

لَلَبِثَ فِي بَطْنِهِ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ يُبْعَثُونَ

la-labitha fī baṭnihi ilā yawmi yubʿathūna

“He would have remained in its belly until the day they are raised.”

This answers a condition beginning in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:143. It should not become a statement that Jonah actually remained inside until resurrection.

The next verse, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145, describes him being cast out.

The Qur’an’s account must therefore remain distinct from details supplied by later narratives.

Nor does nūn itself establish that the fish necessarily symbolizes a people, government, prison, or mental condition. Such readings require independent evidence and cannot silently replace the familiar direct meaning.

6. Nūn wa-al-qalam: a disconnected letter or a fish name?

Al-Qalam 68:1

نٓ وَالْقَلَمِ وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ

nūn wa-al-qalami wa-mā yasṭurūna

“Nūn. By the pen and what they write.”

The text begins with one letter: نٓ.

This differs visibly from al-nūni in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87.

Al-Anbiyāʾ has a complete noun with a definite article within a construct phrase; al-Qalam opens with a separate letter.

Views transmitted in early interpretations

Early interpretations transmit several views of the opening.

Some call it a fish name; certain reports describe a huge fish supporting the earth.

Others understand an inkwell and relate it to the pen and writing.

Other explanations include a surah name, part of a divine name, or one of the disconnected letters.

The reports establish historical disagreement, but disagreement does not make every view equally supported.

Linguistic assessment of نٓ

One lexical argument observes that if a fish or inkwell were meant, one would expect the complete word نون, rather than the separate letter ن.

The argument rests on the distinction between writing a word and a letter.

Other surahs opening with separate letters, such as صٓ, قٓ, يسٓ, and حمٓ, provide an internal Qur’anic parallel for treating نٓ among the disconnected letters.

Qur’anic morphological indexing likewise classifies نٓ in Al-Qalam 68:1 as a disconnected letter, not a fish noun.

Linguistic evidence favors reading نٓ as the name of the letter nūn.

That does not answer every question about the purpose of disconnected letters.

One can more confidently identify the opening’s form as a letter, while avoiding certainty about why that letter was chosen or its precise message without evidence.

Does nūn refer to an inkwell and pen?

Dictionaries do record nūn as an inkwell, so the lexical explanation has a precedent.

Its position beside al-qalam and yasṭurūn may suggest the possibility.

Proximity alone does not establish that نٓ abbreviates an inkwell.

That would require evidence for such use of a separate ن and for that meaning within the construction.

The examined evidence is insufficient. The disconnected-letter analysis better fits the written form and other surah openings.

Reports of a fish supporting the earth

Some interpretive reports identify the opening nūn with a vast fish carrying the earth on its back.

Al-Qalam 68:1 does not itself provide that explanation.

Nor do other verses explicitly report that the earth rests on a fish or describe the universe that way.

Such a report cannot establish the definition of N W N.

A cosmological narrative should not be inserted into the lexical meaning of fish and then used to interpret al-Qalam or Jonah’s story.

7. Nūn and ḥūt: two words in one semantic field

Ḥūt is the closest Qur’anic word to nūn.

N W N and Ḥ W T are separate roots but refer to the same creature in Jonah’s story.

Ḥūt occurs elsewhere, including Moses and his companion in Al-Kahf 18:61 and Al-Kahf 18:63, and the fish story in Al-Aʿrāf 7:163.

Those passages use ḥūt, not nūn.

Their occurrences cannot be morphologically counted under N W N.

Nor do two different words necessarily establish two different biological species.

In Jonah’s story, the Qur’an uses both for the same fish.

Nūn may be a more particular or literary fish name, with some sources stressing size, but the Qur’anic evidence does not establish a precise zoological boundary between it and ḥūt.

8. Distinctions from similar words

N W N and N W Y

Nawā, from N W Y, occurs in Al-Anʿām 6:95:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ

inna allāha fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā

“The God is the splitter of grain and fruit stones.”

Nawā here denotes a fruit stone. Its root differs from N W N; sharing the first two radicals does not merge their meanings.

N W N and N H Y

Nahā belongs to N H Y, concerning preventing and forbidding.

Although nūn is a letter name and nahy a verbal or behavioral act, they have no direct morphological relationship.

N W N and Jonah’s name

Yūnus is the prophet’s proper name, occurring in several Qur’anic verses.

It should not be indexed as a derivative of N W N.

Their relationship is narrative: Jonah is the person called dhā al-nūn.

A partial resemblance in sound does not establish one etymology.

Nūn and Nineveh

Nineveh is a historical place associated with Jonah in material outside the Qur’an.

The name does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be derived from N W N without morphological and historical evidence.

The historical geography of Jonah’s people is a separate study, not part of defining nūn.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Fish” is the best equivalent for al-nūn within Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87.

Thus:

وَذَا النُّونِ

wa-dhā al-nūni

A natural rendering is:

“And the companion of the fish …”

The word explanation may note that some early dictionaries also use nūn for a large fish.

Adding “whale,” “enormous fish,” “miraculous fish,” or a species name to the direct translation exceeds what the word certainly conveys.

For Al-Qalam 68:1, retaining “Nūn” is more suitable: the letter name is at issue, while “fish” and “inkwell” lack sufficient support here.

The two forms should therefore remain distinct in Forqan’s explanations:

النُّونِ — Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87FishThe fish associated with Jonah
نٓ — Al-Qalam 68:1The letter name nūnA disconnected letter opening the surah

The second row is a different kind of linguistic unit and should not count as a second occurrence of the same lexical root.

10. Conclusion

The best-known nominal meaning of nūn in the Arabic family N W N is fish. Some early dictionaries use “large fish,” which does not determine a precise biological species.

Anwān and nīnān are recorded plurals. Nūnah has several uses, including fish and a chin dimple.

Nūn also named a letter, an inkwell, a sword blade, and particular swords. These are recorded uses, but their full historical relationship is uncertain.

The Qur’an uses the lexical root once, in dhā al-nūn in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87. Comparison with al-ḥūt in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 and ṣāḥib al-ḥūt in Al-Qalam 68:48 identifies the fish associated with Jonah.

Al-Qalam opens with نٓ, whose form and structure better fit a disconnected letter. Turning it into a fish, inkwell, or cosmic being requires evidence not established by the form and context alone.

A concise definition for Forqan:

N W N: nūn names a fish in established lexical use, sometimes a large fish. Recorded uses also include the letter name, an inkwell, a sword blade, and sword names, without a demonstrated common historical origin for all. Qur’anic al-nūn in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87 concerns Jonah’s fish; نٓ in Al-Qalam 68:1 is examined as a disconnected letter, not a second fish noun.
