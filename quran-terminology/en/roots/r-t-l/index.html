Order and coordination of parts, clarity and distinctness of speech, measured reading, and the Qur’an’s gradual presentation

Early Arabic R T L concerns coordination and order among the parts of a whole, arranging them clearly and distinctly, and expressing speech in a measured, clear manner.

In tangible usage, teeth that are regular, well arranged, and appropriately spaced are described as رَتِل or مُرَتَّل. In speech, رَتَّلَ الْكَلَامَ means speaking deliberately, making the parts of speech clear, and delivering it in an orderly, recognizable manner.

The uses are semantically connected: just as regularly arranged teeth do not overlap and each has its place, measured speech does not merge words and letters so that they become difficult to distinguish.

This comparison must not become an invented etymological picture. What is attested is ratal concerning the arrangement of teeth and tartīl concerning speech. Determining which use came first historically requires further evidence.

One early lexical definition says:

الرَّتَلُ: تَنْسِيقُ الشَّيْءِ

al-ratalu: tansīqu al-shayʾ

That is: “Ratal is the coordination and arrangement of something.”

Another lexical definition is:

الرَّتَلُ: اتِّسَاقُ الشَّيْءِ وَانْتِظَامُهُ عَلَى اسْتِقَامَةٍ

al-ratalu: ittisāqu al-shayʾi wa-intiẓāmuhu ʿalā istiqāmah

That is: “Ratal is the coherence and regular arrangement of something along an orderly course.”

These definitions matter: tartīl is not originally just the name of a particular melody for Qur’anic reading. Order, clarity, distinct parts, and avoiding haste that makes speech unintelligible belong to its semantic field.

R T L occurs four times in two Qur’anic verses: Al-Furqān 25:32 and Al-Muzzammil 73:4. Each contains a Form II verb and its verbal noun.

The key point is that the verses do not describe an entirely identical action despite sharing a root. Al-Muzzammil 73:4 commands the prophet to perform tartīl of the Qur’an, while in Al-Furqān 25:32 The God says وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا.

The root’s common meaning must therefore be retained while examining the difference between the addressee’s reading and The God’s tartīl of the Qur’an.

1. Qur’anic forms and constructions

VerseQur’anic formGrammatical formContextual sense
Al-Furqān 25:32وَرَتَّلْنَاهُForm II past verb, first-person pluralWe presented it with order and clarity
Al-Furqān 25:32تَرْتِيلًاAccusative Form II verbal nounA clear, measured arrangement
Al-Muzzammil 73:4وَرَتِّلِForm II imperative, masculine singularRead clearly and deliberately
Al-Muzzammil 73:4تَرْتِيلًاAccusative Form II verbal nounClear, measured reading

In both verses, تَرْتِيلًا is a cognate accusative: a verbal noun from the same verb that emphasizes the action itself.

The basic verb رَتِلَ, active participle مُرَتِّل, and passive participle مُرَتَّل do not occur in the Qur’an. All Qur’anic occurrences thus belong to the Form II verb and its verbal noun.

2. Basic meaning in early dictionaries

Coordination and order among parts

One early definition is tansīq al-shayʾ: arranging something in coordination.

Tansīq here concerns parts placed in a proportionate order. Order does not always mean complete contact between parts; appropriate spacing can itself contribute to the whole’s arrangement and coordination.

The description of teeth makes this particularly clear.

ثَغْرٌ رَتِلٌ describes teeth that are well arranged, even, and appropriately spaced.

Three related Arabic explanations are recorded: well-arranged teeth, spaced teeth, and teeth that do not overlap.

Ratal must therefore not be reduced to being tightly packed together.

In some uses, the prominent feature is orderly parts that remain separate and distinguishable.

This matters especially for tartīl of speech: orderly speech does not run letters and words together unintelligibly without separation.

Brightness and beauty of teeth

Ratal also describes the whiteness and brightness of teeth.

Whiteness and كَثْرَةُ مَائِهَا are also recorded in this family. The latter expression, in Arabic descriptive language, concerns freshness, brilliance, and a pleasing appearance of teeth.

Ratal describing teeth thus involves more than dry geometrical arrangement: beauty and visible proportion also matter.

This does not establish that tartīl of the Qur’an necessarily means beautiful reading with musical melody.

Beautiful teeth and clear speech belong to different settings. Proportion and discernible parts provide a defensible lexical connection, rather than transferring every feature from one instance to the other.

Orderly, clear speech

رَتَّلْتُ الْكَلَامَ is used when someone avoids haste in speaking and arranges the speech well.

رَتَّلَ الْكَلَامَ is also explained as composing speech well, making it clear, and taking time in its delivery.

Lexical explanations bring these three features together: good composition, clarity, and avoiding haste.

They are related but not complete synonyms.

Speech may have orderly sentences yet be delivered so fast and indistinctly that a listener cannot recognize its parts.

Conversely, someone may speak very slowly but pronounce words inaccurately or speak incoherently.

Tartīl is therefore more than slowness: it is reading or speaking while preserving order, clarity, and distinct parts.

This follows from the combined lexical definitions, rather than a later technical term in Qur’anic recitation.

3. Arabic forms and derivatives

رَتِلَ: being orderly and well arranged

رَتِلَ is a basic Form I verb for the parts of something, especially teeth, being or becoming regular and well arranged.

The recorded construction رَتِلَ الثَّغْرُ concerns teeth set regularly and proportionately in place.

رَتَل is its verbal noun, and رَتِل its descriptive form.

This verb does not occur in the Qur’an.

رَتَل and رَتِل: order and proportion

Ratal principally concerns coordination and a pleasing arrangement.

Ratil can also describe well-arranged teeth or speech.

The forms are recorded with different vowels. Those differences must not be overlooked.

Later usage also applies رَتْل to vehicles or people moving in succession.

This modern usage has an intelligible connection with orderly succession, but must not serve as ancient evidence for Qur’anic meaning. Contemporary dictionaries also mark the vehicle-group sense as newly coined.

رَتَّلَ: arranging and expressing clearly

رَتَّلَ belongs to Form II.

رَتَّلَ الْكَلَامَ concerns arranging speech well and delivering it clearly.

The Qur’an uses the verb both for what the prophet should do with the Qur’an and for what The God has done with it.

This difference shows that the verb cannot be confined to an identical vocal activity in both verses.

Its verbal noun is تَرْتِيل, the root’s only nominal derivative in the Qur’an.

مُرَتِّل and مُرَتَّل

مُرَتِّل is the Form II active participle: someone who performs tartīl in speech or reading.

مُرَتَّل is the corresponding passive participle: something expressed or arranged with tartīl.

Both forms are established in Arabic but do not themselves occur in the Qur’an.

ثَغْرٌ مُرَتَّلٌ is also recorded for well-arranged, appropriately spaced teeth.

The passive participle does not necessarily mean someone deliberately arranged the teeth by hand; it can describe their resulting state and orderly appearance.

تَرَتَّلَ: speaking deliberately

تَرَتَّلَ is Form V and describes someone who speaks without haste, pronouncing words clearly and deliberately.

يَتَرَتَّلُ فِي كَلَامِهِ occurs beside يَتَرَسَّلُ in Arabic usage.

Tarassul here concerns calm, unhurried speech, rather than sending a prophet or messenger.

تَرَتَّلَ does not occur in the Qur’an.

رُتَيْلَى and رُتَيْلَاء: a small creature’s name

رُتَيْلَاء is attested in early Arabic as the name of a venomous creature.

Other dictionaries record رُتَيْلَى and رُتَيْلَاء for a small creature, including a spider.

Its inclusion under R T L does not itself establish that the creature was named for regular legs or orderly movement.

Such an explanation would require independent evidence about the name’s history.

The word does not occur in the Qur’an.

Other less frequent uses

الرَّتَلُ وَالرَّتِلُ also describe something pleasing and good.

مَاءٌ رَتِلٌ denotes cold water.

This use is recorded in lexical transmission, but its precise historical connection with order and coordination is unclear.

Some sources record رَأْتَلَة for a gait in which the body sways unevenly, while treating رَأْبَلَة as the more familiar form. It should therefore not be assigned categorically to R T L without caution.

Order and coordination, and their application to teeth and speech, have clearer support overall. Rare uses should be recorded without reshaping the central definition into an unsupported theory about the origin of every meaning.

4. Early Arabic poetic witnesses

Well-arranged teeth in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

One principal witness occurs in a poem by al-Aʿshā:

تُجْرِي السِّوَاكَ بِالْبَنَانِ عَلَىٰ
أَلْمَى كَأَطْرَافِ السَّيَالِ رَتِلْ

tujrī al-siwāka bi-al-banāni ʿalā / almā ka-aṭrāfi al-sayāli ratil

Meaning: “With her fingers she runs the tooth-stick over a mouth with pleasingly dark lips and regular teeth, like the tips of sayāl branches.”

Ratil here concerns the beauty and arrangement of teeth.

The witness illustrates the root outside Qur’anic usage, describing a tangible state.

It concerns neither reading a text nor vocal tartīl. The teeth are described as ratil because of their appearance and arrangement.

One lexical transmission quotes the verse anonymously, while al-Aʿshā’s collected poetry and literary and grammatical transmissions attribute it to him.

Al-Aʿshā is a prominent pre-Islamic poet. The witness therefore matters for pre-Qur’anic usage, while its transmitted wording and attribution must be retained with appropriate care.

Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī’s witness: ثَغْرٌ رَتِل

A verse attributed to Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī is quoted in descriptions of teeth:

وَمُبَدَّدٍ رَتِلٍ كَأَنَّ الـ
ـنَّحْلَ عَسَّلَ فِيهِ بَارِدُ

wa-mubaddadin ratilin ka-anna al-naḥla ʿassala fīhi bārid

Approximate meaning: “And [a mouth] with spaced, well-arranged teeth, as though bees had laid honey in it; [a mouth] cool.”

Ratil describes teeth set regularly beside one another with appropriate gaps.

The lexical explanation connects ratil with gaps that prevent teeth from overlapping.

This accords with “a pleasing arrangement” in other early dictionaries.

Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī is regarded as a pre-Islamic poet. Because this verse is transmitted as evidence about teeth, its literary details should not be reconstructed beyond the text and its explanation.

What the two witnesses contribute to understanding tartīl

The two witnesses establish that early Arabic R T L described the orderly beauty of parts before becoming a familiar term for Qur’anic reading.

They do not establish that tartīl of the Qur’an simply means placing verses from different surahs beside one another.

Nor do they establish that tartīl necessarily means prolonging the voice or reading melodiously.

The poetry clearly supports order, proportion, and distinct parts. The application to speech must be assessed through independent lexical evidence and Qur’anic context.

5. وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا: the command to read in a measured manner

Al-Muzzammil 73:4

أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا

aw zid ʿalayhi wa-rattili al-qurʾāna tartīlā

A close translation:

“Or add to it, and read the Qur’an clearly and deliberately.”

This verse follows the command to rise during part of the night.

Al-Muzzammil 73:1, Al-Muzzammil 73:2, and Al-Muzzammil 73:3 address the person called al-muzzammil and command rising for part of the night, specifying half of it or a little less. Adding to it follows in Al-Muzzammil 73:4.

The command وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا then appears.

The immediate context therefore concerns how to read the Qur’an during the night vigil, rather than merely ordering verses to compile a book.

Morphology and syntax

وَرَتِّلِ consists of the conjunction وَ and the imperative رَتِّلْ.

The verb is second-person masculine singular, Form II. Its final kasrah in connected recitation resolves the meeting of two consonants without vowels when joined to الْقُرْآنَ.

الْقُرْآنَ is the verb’s object.

تَرْتِيلًا is an accusative verbal noun serving as a cognate accusative.

The sentence both states the action and emphasizes its performance through the same verb’s verbal noun.

Grammatically, the command addresses one person. Extending it to others or determining a general obligation requires separate examination of the relevant verses; رَتِّلْ itself is not plural.

Early explanations of tartīl in this verse

One early exegetical explanation understands the verse as reading clearly and avoiding haste.

One transmission describes a clear reading.

Another describes reading the Qur’an’s parts successively and deliberately.

بَيِّنْهُ بَيَانًا, “make it clear,” also appears in several transmissions.

Despite different wording, these explanations share one point: the Qur’an should be read so that its parts are clear and distinguishable.

Lexical and grammatical explanations also note that haste can cause letters to run into one another and speech to lose clarity.

Tartīl here therefore directly concerns preserving speech’s structure and clarity.

Does tartīl mean only reading slowly?

No.

Slow reading may help achieve tartīl, but it is not a complete definition.

Reading very slowly while pronouncing letters and words indistinctly does not fulfill the word’s requirement of clarity.

Conversely, unnaturally separated, disconnected words may destroy the sentence’s continuity of meaning.

Early definitions balance deliberation, clarity, proportion, and avoiding haste that blends speech together.

Clear, measured reading is therefore more precise than simply “reading slowly.”

Does tartīl necessarily mean reading with a beautiful melody?

The root alone establishes no such requirement.

Clear, measured reading may accompany a pleasing voice and suitable melody. Vocal beauty and tartīl’s lexical meaning are nevertheless different subjects.

Early definitions foreground orderly, clear speech rather than restricting tartīl to a particular melody or song.

Translating رَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ as “sing the Qur’an beautifully” therefore loses part of the meaning and substitutes a feature that the verb itself does not require.

Is tartīl identical with technical tajwīd rules?

In later recitation disciplines, tartīl denotes a mode of reading in which deliberation, clarity, pronunciation rules, and pauses matter.

This technical use accords with part of the older meaning.

It does not establish that every later tajwīd classification, specified prolongation, recitation level, or detailed teaching rule is contained within the word itself.

Some rules result from research and technical codification in recitation studies. Their relation to the Qur’anic command requires independent evidence.

This does not establish that using later terminology necessarily contradicts the Qur’an. The point is that a word’s lexical definition is not identical with a technical discipline’s complete system of rules and terms.

Contemporary teaching sometimes identifies tartīl as a recitation level alongside ḥadr and tadwīr. This helps explain technical terminology but should not replace the root’s Qur’anic meaning.

Tartīl and understanding meaning

Is clear Qur’anic reading related to understanding it?

Yes, the relationship is defensible, but it must be expressed precisely.

Reading with clear letters and words, without excessive haste, can provide more opportunity to attend to meaning.

Al-Muzzammil 73:5 says:

إِنَّا سَنُلْقِي عَلَيْكَ قَوْلًا ثَقِيلًا

innā sa-nulqī ʿalayka qawlan thaqīlā

“We shall soon cast a weighty utterance upon you.”

And in Al-Muzzammil 73:6:

إِنَّ نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ هِيَ أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا وَأَقْوَمُ قِيلًا

inna nāshiʾata al-layli hiya ashaddu waṭʾan wa-aqwamu qīlā

“Rising at night is indeed firmer in impact and more upright in speech.”

The connection places the command of tartīl within receiving and reading a significant, weighty utterance.

Tartīl and tadabbur are not complete synonyms, however.

Tadabbur belongs to D B R and concerns reflecting on the verses. Tartīl in Al-Muzzammil 73:4 concerns the manner of reading.

Suitable tartīl can support attention and understanding, but رَتَّلَ alone should not be translated “interpret” or “reflect on the meaning.”

The surah’s final verse

Al-Muzzammil 73:20 says:

فَاقْرَءُوا مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ

fa-iqraʾū mā tayassara mina al-qurʾān

“So read what is manageable for you of the Qur’an.”

Together with the account of the prophet and some companions standing at night, this shows that Qur’anic reading is an actual subject of the surah’s context.

Reading clearly and deliberately therefore has direct support in Al-Muzzammil 73:4.

This also prevents reducing tartīl here to rearranging verses from different surahs by topic: the context foregrounds reading.

6. وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا: The God’s tartīl of the Qur’an

Al-Furqān 25:32

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ عَلَيْهِ الْقُرْآنُ جُمْلَةً وَاحِدَةً ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنُثَبِّتَ بِهِ فُؤَادَكَ ۖ وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lawlā nuzzila ʿalayhi al-qurʾānu jumlatan wāḥidatan kadhālika li-nuthabbita bihi fuʾādaka wa-rattalnāhu tartīlā

A translation close to the construction and context:

“Those who disbelieved said: ‘Why was the Qur’an not sent down upon him all at once?’ Thus, to strengthen your heart through it; and We presented it with tartīl.”

Unlike Al-Muzzammil 73:4, this verse does not command a reader.

The God is the subject of رَتَّلْنَا, and the pronoun هُ refers to the Qur’an.

The action expressed by rattala concerning the Qur’an must therefore be examined within this context.

The opponents’ question as the key to context

The verse begins with the objection that the Qur’an was not revealed all at once:

لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ عَلَيْهِ الْقُرْآنُ جُمْلَةً وَاحِدَةً

lawlā nuzzila ʿalayhi al-qurʾānu jumlatan wāḥidah

“Why was the Qur’an not sent down upon him all at once?”

The objection concerns the manner and sequence of revelation.

The reply states that this manner strengthens the prophet’s heart.

Then it says:

وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا

wa-rattalnāhu tartīlā

“And We presented it with tartīl.”

This connection relates tartīl to the Qur’an’s presentation and revelation.

Taking the verb merely as “We read the Qur’an with a beautiful voice” leaves its relationship to “Why was it not revealed all at once?” unclear.

The context therefore supports a measured presentation, part by part, with order and clarity.

This does not exclude reading entirely. The Qur’an also attributes reading or reciting revelation to The God, as in Al-Qiyāmah 75:18. The issue is which aspect of tartīl is prominent in Al-Furqān 25:32.

First view: gradual presentation and revelation

One early explanation connects tartīl here with teaching the Qur’an part by part and its revelation in separate stages.

One transmission describes revelation at separate times.

Another describes one or more verses being revealed successively in response to different questions and events.

Other early explanations also use بعضه على إثر بعض: one part following another.

This reading directly relates to the opening objection about revelation all at once.

Second view: clarification and orderly arrangement

Some early interpretations explain وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا as clarifying and explaining the Qur’an.

One explanation uses فَسَّرْنَاهُ تَفْسِيرًا.

Others speak of clarifying and setting out the Qur’an in detail.

These fit the lexical meaning of making speech’s parts clear and distinct.

“Clarifying and detailing” as an explanation of tartīl must nevertheless be distinguished from tafsīr as a detailed verse-by-verse commentary in a book.

Explaining rattalnāhu through fassarnāhu does not make R T L equivalent to fassara in every use.

Assessing the two readings

Both have lexical and contextual support.

The first attends to the sequence and gradualness of presentation.

The second foregrounds the clarity and order of what is presented.

They need not be competing readings in this verse.

The root concerns order and clear parts, while the revelation context places that order and clarity within a presentation in stages.

A more comprehensive reading is therefore:

The God presented the Qur’an deliberately, clearly, and part by part, rather than as a disordered, hurried, or single delivery.

The independent meaning of رَتَّلَ must still be distinguished from gradual revelation as a contextual conclusion. Rattala alone does not always mean “sent down”; the complete sentence establishes that connection in Al-Furqān 25:32.

Supporting witness: Al-Isrāʾ 17:106

وَقُرْآنًا فَرَقْنَاهُ لِتَقْرَأَهُ عَلَى النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ مُكْثٍ وَنَزَّلْنَاهُ تَنْزِيلًا

wa-qurʾānan faraqnāhu li-taqraʾahu ʿalā al-nāsi ʿalā mukthin wa-nazzalnāhu tanzīlā

“And a Qur’an We divided into portions, so that you might read it to people deliberately; and We sent it down, a sending down.”

This verse is particularly significant for examining Al-Furqān 25:32.

It places two subjects together: the Qur’an in portions and its deliberate reading to people.

The connection further clarifies presentation in stages alongside reception and reading.

فَرَقْنَاهُ belongs to F R Q and نَزَّلْنَاهُ to N Z L. All three verbs must not be treated as complete synonyms.

Farq emphasizes separating, sending down concerns revelation, and tartīl emphasizes orderly, clear presentation.

Together, they can express different aspects of how the Qur’an is presented.

Further witness: Al-Qiyāmah 75:16, Al-Qiyāmah 75:17, Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:19

Al-Qiyāmah 75:16 says:

لَا تُحَرِّكْ بِهِ لِسَانَكَ لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِ

lā tuḥarrik bihi lisānaka li-taʿjala bih

“Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it.”

Then, in Al-Qiyāmah 75:17, Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:19:

إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْآنَهُ ۝ فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَاهُ فَاتَّبِعْ قُرْآنَهُ ۝ ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا بَيَانَهُ

inna ʿalaynā jamʿahu wa-qurʾānah / fa-idhā qaraʾnāhu fa-ittabiʿ qurʾānah / thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānah

“Its gathering and reading are upon Us. Then, when We have read it, follow its reading. Then its clarification is upon Us.”

The verses address haste in receiving or reading, as well as gathering, recitation, and clarification.

This helps explain tartīl: the Qur’an connects haste, reading, and clarification.

Qurʾān, bayān, and tartīl remain different words; conceptual relationships do not make their roots identical.

Connection with Al-Furqān 25:33

The next verse says:

وَلَا يَأْتُونَكَ بِمَثَلٍ إِلَّا جِئْنَاكَ بِالْحَقِّ وَأَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا

wa-lā yaʾtūnaka bi-mathalin illā jiʾnāka bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-aḥsana tafsīrā

“They bring you no example without Our bringing you the truth and a better clarification.”

This verse also matters.

Its connection with Al-Furqān 25:32 places the Qur’an’s presentation amid life’s questions, objections, and events, with clear answers accompanying it.

Gradual revelation is thus more than distributing a text across different times: it concerns strengthening the prophet’s heart and providing clear replies.

This contextual message must not become the independent meaning of R T L.

7. Does tartīl mean gathering verses on one topic from different surahs?

The question arises from the meanings of coordination and orderly arrangement.

It might be argued that because tartīl concerns order among parts, tartīl of the Qur’an means placing related verses from different surahs together to construct a thematic collection.

That can be a useful method of Qur’anic research. Gathering related verses, comparing words, and reading the Qur’an through the Qur’an permit a broader examination of concepts.

This research method cannot, however, be treated as tartīl’s direct and exclusive meaning.

First, Al-Muzzammil 73:4 concerns a night vigil and Qur’anic reading. Lexical evidence likewise connects tartīl of speech with clear, measured delivery.

Second, The God is the subject in Al-Furqān 25:32, and the issue is presentation and revelation. No person is commanded there to reclassify dispersed verses from an already complete book.

Third, neither verse expressly mentions thematic collections, selecting words with a shared root, or rearranging surahs.

Tartīl may therefore have a practical, suggestive relationship with orderly study of verses without that becoming a necessary etymological relationship.

Reading the Qur’an through the Qur’an can rest on independent Qur’anic evidence; establishing it does not require stretching tartīl beyond what the language supports.

8. Tartīl compared with reading, recitation, clarification, and reflection

R T L and Q R A: tartīl and qirāʾah

قَرَأَ belongs to Q R A and expresses reading and recitation in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an is the object of rattil in Al-Muzzammil 73:4, while Al-Muzzammil 73:20 contains the command فَاقْرَءُوا.

The verbs concern a related activity but do not emphasize an identical aspect.

Qirāʾah expresses reading itself.

Tartīl here specifies the manner: clear, measured, and preserving distinct parts.

Tartīl of the Qur’an in this use is a form of reading, but not every reading necessarily exhibits tartīl’s desired qualities.

Study of Q R A

R T L and T L W: tartīl and tilāwah

تَلَا belongs to T L W, whose principal semantic fields include following and coming after something.

Qur’anic tilāwah also concerns reading verses.

Tartīl belongs to the separate root R T L and emphasizes clarity and orderly delivery in speech.

The two may coincide in one act without sharing an identical range of meanings.

Their proximity also does not establish that both necessarily involve a particular song or melody.

Study of T L W

R T L and B Y N: tartīl and clarification

Bayān concerns revealing and making clear.

Early lexical and exegetical explanations repeatedly connect tartīl with clarification.

This is understandable: measured, clear speech makes its parts easier to recognize and receive.

Clarification can nevertheless occur without vocal reading—for example, through writing, a gesture, or an independent explanation.

Tartīl in Al-Muzzammil 73:4, by contrast, concerns Qur’anic reading.

Al-Furqān 25:32 also combines clarification with the order and sequence of presentation.

Tartīl must therefore not simply be translated “interpret” in every setting.

Study of B Y N

R T L and F R Q: order and distinct parts

فَرَقَ belongs to F R Q and concerns separating or distinguishing.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:106 describes the Qur’an through فَرَقْنَاهُ.

R T L also involves distinct parts alongside their order.

Farq and ratal are not identical concepts, however.

Something can be divided into many parts that remain disordered.

Conversely, a whole can be orderly without an act of division occurring on every occasion.

The Qur’an’s presentation in portions thus relates to tartīl, but rattala itself must not be turned into faraqa.

Study of F R Q

R T L and R T B: tartīl and arrangement

These roots are semantically close in modern Persian and Arabic.

Tartīb belongs to R T B and concerns order and the placement of parts together.

Tartīl belongs to R T L.

Both can concern an orderly whole without sharing the same root.

In speech, tartīl additionally relates to clear delivery and avoiding haste.

Rendering tartīl as “arrange” in every verse therefore does not convey its full meaning.

R T L and D B R: tartīl and reflecting on the Qur’an

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82 says:

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ

a-fa-lā yatadabbarūna al-qurʾān

“Do they not reflect on the Qur’an?”

Muḥammad 47:24 poses the same question.

Tadabbur concerns reflection and examining speech’s implications and consequences.

Tartīl can provide an opportunity for such reflection, but the roots remain lexically different.

Someone may read the Qur’an clearly and deliberately without giving its meaning much thought.

Conversely, someone may reflect through studying the written Qur’an and comparing verses without reciting aloud at that moment.

Their relationship is real and useful, but neither should be reduced completely to the other.

Study of D B R

R T L and H DH R M: measured and hurried reading

Arabic hadhramah describes very rapid, successive reading or speech that may reduce clarity.

Recitation sources also contrast it with measured reading.

Semantically, such haste can conflict with tartīl’s qualities.

This does not make all fast reading unintelligible or worthless. Preserving clear, distinct speech remains the principal lexical concern.

9. Two existing Persian Forqan translations

The existing Persian Forqan texts contain two renderings of Al-Furqān 25:32. The excerpts below are translated from Persian for comparison.

The Persian translation on the surah page, at Al-Furqān 25:32, reads:

Original Persian verse translation: Al-Furqān 25:32

“And We read it slowly and continuously.”

The Persian article “Naskh in the Qur’an: Challenging the Claim That Its Verses Can Be Set Aside” instead reads:

Naskh in the Qur’an: original Persian article

“And We arranged it with tartīl.”

The renderings emphasize different aspects of the root.

The first favors calm, continuous reading.

The second attends to the Qur’an’s order and arrangement.

In the context of Al-Furqān 25:32, the second has stronger contextual support through the question about revelation all at once. “Arranging” alone, however, does not convey the full clarity and deliberateness of tartīl.

The first retains reading but may lead a reader to restrict the verse to The God’s vocal recitation.

This difference in the existing Persian texts merits translation review. The present study records its lexical and contextual implications and keeps proposals distinct from quotations of existing texts.

Proposed rendering of Al-Muzzammil 73:4

“Or add to it, and read the Qur’an clearly and deliberately.”

Reading is established by the context and the word Qur’an, while “clearly and deliberately” conveys tartīl’s distinctive quality.

“Read with tartīl” also respects the wording, but needs explanation for readers unfamiliar with the term.

Proposed rendering of Al-Furqān 25:32

The final choice should follow an independent review of the complete verse’s translation. At the lexical-study stage, the following is defensible:

“Those who disbelieved said: ‘Why was the Qur’an not sent down upon him all at once?’ Thus, to strengthen your heart through it; and We presented it with tartīl.”

Retaining tartīl at this stage avoids reducing all its disputed meaning to a single interpretive phrase.

An explanatory box can clarify that, in this context, tartīl concerns an orderly, clear presentation of the Qur’an in stages.

If the term is unpacked in the final translation, “We presented it clearly and deliberately” also merits consideration. “In stages” and “gradually,” however, come from the revelation context; the verb alone does not invariably mean “revealed gradually.”

In both verses, tartīl should not become exclusively singing, melody, mere slowness, or thematic rearrangement of verses.

11. Conclusion

Early Arabic R T L concerns order, coordination, proportion, and the clear structure of a whole.

In tangible use, thaghr ratil describes well-arranged, regular, appropriately spaced teeth. Al-Aʿshā and Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī provide early poetic evidence.

For speech, رَتَّلَ الْكَلَامَ concerns clear, orderly, measured expression whose parts are not obscured through haste or blending together.

The root occurs four times in two Qur’anic verses.

Al-Muzzammil 73:4 commands one addressee within reading during a night vigil. Clear, measured reading that preserves order and distinct parts is suitable.

In Al-Furqān 25:32, The God attributes tartīl to Himself. The opponents’ question about revelation all at once foregrounds how the Qur’an is presented. Early explanations likewise discuss revelation in portions, clarification, and detailing.

Order and clarity remain shared in both verses, but their subject and application differ: the manner of reading in one, and the Qur’an’s presentation and arrangement in the other.

Tartīl cannot be restricted to a musical technique, the complete system of later tajwīd rules, or an exclusive method of thematic rearrangement. These are related subjects whose authority requires independent evidence.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

R T L concerns order, coordination, and clearly distinct parts of a whole. In speech, tartīl means orderly, clear, measured expression or reading, avoiding haste that blends the parts together. Al-Muzzammil 73:4 applies this to reading the Qur’an; Al-Furqān 25:32, in context, applies it to the Qur’an’s clear, orderly presentation in stages.
