The Arabic root ح ر ض has two important, apparently different uses. حَرَض describes someone whose strength has been worn away by illness, grief, exhaustion, or severe weakness, bringing them close to destruction. By contrast, حَرَّضَ means urging and encouraging someone to do something, seeking to strengthen their motivation to act.

Both uses occur in the Qur’an.

In Yūsuf 12:85, Joseph’s brothers tell their father that his continual remembrance of Joseph will bring him to حَرَض: such severe wasting and weakness that he approaches death.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65, the imperative حَرِّضْ addresses the Prophet. Both verses concern urging believers to fight.

Lexicons offer different explanations of the relationship between the meanings. Some explain taḥrīḍ as urging someone out of lassitude and weakness; others describe encouragement accompanied by warning about the consequences of abandoning a responsibility.

But the semantic proximity does not establish the precise historical origin of حَرَّضَ with certainty.

The same family includes names for plants and substances, including حُرْض for a washing plant and إِحْرِيض for safflower or its coloring material. Ibn Fāris treats this branch separately from weakness and wasting.

The root ح ر ض occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses: once as حَرَضًا and twice as the imperative حَرِّضِ.

Understanding it therefore requires distinguishing human wasting, the act of urging another, and the independent plant uses. None should replace another without contextual evidence.

1. Forms and derivatives

The root ح ر ض consists of ḥāʾ, rāʾ, and ḍād. It is a sound, regular triliteral Arabic root.

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • حَرِضَ ـ يَحْرَضُ: Became severely wasted, ill, or weak; approached death.
  • حَرُضَ ـ يَحْرُضُ: Became weak and wasted, or entered a state of incapacity.
  • حَرَضَ ـ يَحْرِضُ / يَحْرُضُ: In some early uses, became ruined or wasted; in a transitive construction, ruined something.
  • حَرَض: Severe wasting and incapacity; also a description of someone brought close to death by illness or grief.
  • حَرِض: Weak, wasted, and afflicted by illness or incapacity.
  • حَارِض: A weak, wasted person; in another use, someone considered ineffective in ability or conduct.
  • حَرِيض: Afflicted by severe weakness and wasting.
  • حَرَاضَة و حُرُوض: Weakness, wasting, and ruin.
  • أَحْرَاض: A plural for weak people or those disparaged as ineffective in early usage.
  • حُرْضَان: With ḍammah on ḥāʾ: a plural for weak people, and in another use a singular description of a weak, incapacitated male or female camel. These uses should be distinguished.
  • أَحْرَضَ ـ يُحْرِضُ: Made someone wasted or weak, or brought them close to destruction.
  • إِحْرَاض: Causing severe weakness and wasting.
  • مُحْرَض: Someone brought to weakness and wasting by illness, grief, or another cause.
  • حَرَّضَ ـ يُحَرِّضُ: Urged and encouraged another to act.
  • تَحْرِيض: Urging, earnest encouragement, and strengthening motivation to act.
  • حَارَضَ ـ يُحَارِضُ: In recorded uses, pursued an action earnestly and persistently; in another, competed in a game involving drawing arrows.
  • مُحَارَضَة: Persistent pursuit of an action, or competition in particular early uses.
  • حُرْض / حُرُض: A plant used for washing, particularly that known as أُشْنَان.
  • مِحْرَضَة: A container for keeping or using the washing substance حُرْض.
  • حَرَّاض: Someone working with ḥurḍ; in another use, someone heating or preparing gypsum.
  • إِحْرِيض: Safflower or its coloring material.
  • مُحَرَّض: In one use, a wasted, incapacitated person; in another, a garment dyed with safflower.
  • حُرْضَة: In early social usage, the person responsible for mixing and handling arrows in a drawing game.

Their presence in lexicons does not mean that all occur in the Qur’an.

The actual Qur’anic forms are:

VerseFormMorphologyContextual meaning
Al-Nisāʾ 4:84وَحَرِّضِForm II imperativeUrge and encourage
Al-Anfāl 8:65حَرِّضِForm II imperativeUrge to fight
Yūsuf 12:85حَرَضًاNoun or verbal noun used descriptivelyWasted and close to death

وَحَرِّضِ and حَرِّضِ are the same verb, with the conjunction وَ attached only in the former.

The final kasrah in حَرِّضِ is an incidental pronunciation in connected speech, avoiding two adjacent vowelless consonants before الْمُؤْمِنِينَ. The underlying imperative is حَرِّضْ; the kasrah is not a different verbal pattern or mood.

حَرَضًا in Yūsuf 12:85 is not Form II and must not be equated with حَرِّضْ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences, two Form II imperatives and one noun

2. Semantic centers in early Arabic

Two bases in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two bases for ح ر ض.

The first concerns a plant and related substances; the second concerns ruin, wasting, weakness, and approaching destruction.

Of the second he writes:

دَلِيلُ الذَّهَابِ وَالتَّلَفِ وَالْهَلَاكِ وَالضَّعْفِ

dalīlu al-dhahābi wa-al-talafi wa-al-halāki wa-al-ḍaʿfi

That is: “Indicating disappearance, ruin, destruction, and weakness.”

He then identifies الحَرَض as someone on the verge of destruction.

This illuminates the principal semantic field of حَرَضًا in Jacob’s story.

Ibn Fāris importantly places the plant name حُرْض in an independent branch.

There is therefore no basis for claiming, without evidence, that weakness must derive from the plant name, or that the plant name necessarily derives from wasting and illness.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the plant branch and the ruin-and-weakness branch

حَرَض: wasting close to destruction

Kitāb al-ʿAyn relates الحَرَض to someone whom grief has so worn away that they are close to perishing.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also uses حَرَض for someone suffering severe illness and weakness.

Lisān al-ʿArab, reporting Abū ʿUbaydah, explains that grief or love may so wear someone away that their strength diminishes.

The word therefore does not mean simply being sad.

A person may grieve while retaining physical and practical ability.

The use under discussion refers to a more severe stage: wasting and a condition whose continuation may lead to destruction.

Lexicographers accordingly explain it through دَنَف, ضَنًى, and approaching death.

These concern illness, wasting, and severe incapacity.

But حرض should not yield a particular medical diagnosis in today’s terminology.

It describes a general condition of wasting and proximity to destruction, rather than naming a specific disease.

Is حَرَض death itself?

In lexicons, حرض sometimes comes very close to destruction.

More precisely, however, it can refer to someone still alive but severely wasted and on the verge of perishing.

This matters in Yūsuf 12:85, since حَرَضًا and مِنَ الْهَالِكِينَ are separated by أَوْ.

Context thus allows a distinction between wasting close to death and death itself.

The concepts are linguistically very close, but not identical.

A poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam attribute this verse to Imruʾ al-Qays:

أَرَى الْمَرْءَ ذَا الْأَذْوَادِ يُصْبِحُ مُحْرَضًا
كَإِحْرَاضِ بَكْرٍ فِي الدِّيَارِ مَرِيضِ

arā al-marʾa dhā al-adhwādi yuṣbiḥu muḥraḍan / ka-iḥrāḍi bakrin fī al-diyāri marīḍi

That is: “I see the man who owns herds of camels become wasted, like a sick young camel wasting in the dwelling places.”

Here مُحْرَضًا concerns wasting and incapacity.

The poet sets possessions and economic strength against illness and wasting.

Someone may own many camels yet gain no benefit from that strength and property when faced with weakness and illness.

In the second half, a sick young camel provides a concrete example of incapacity.

The witness shows the ح ر ض family used poetically for declining strength and approaching complete incapacity.

Sources report both مُحْرَضًا and مُحْرِضًا.

The attribution to Imruʾ al-Qays is recorded in lexicons, but lexical transmission alone does not securely date the verse’s composition.

Al-Ṭabarī: the witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and the description of wasting

Al-ʿArjī: wasting through attachment

Al-ʿArjī, the Umayyad-era poet, says:

إِنِّي امْرُؤٌ لَجَّ بِي حُبٌّ فَأَحْرَضَنِي
حَتَّى بَلِيتُ وَحَتَّى شَفَّنِي السَّقَمُ

innī imruʾun lajja bī ḥubbun fa-aḥraḍanī / ḥattā balītu wa-ḥattā shaffanī al-saqamu

That is: “Love has clung to me and worn me away, until I wasted and illness sapped my strength.”

أَحْرَضَنِي concerns the wasting effect of grief and attachment.

The poet describes his inner condition through its consequence of diminished strength and wasting.

This approaches حَرَضًا in Jacob’s story, where attachment and grief likewise relate to a person’s wasting.

Al-ʿArjī’s poetry nevertheless belongs to the Islamic period; it is not a pre-Islamic witness.

Both لَجَّ بِي حُبٌّ and جَدَّ بِي حُبٌّ are transmitted. The former is recorded in Lisān al-ʿArab and an edition of the poet’s collection.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: Al-ʿArjī’s poem and the lajja/jadda variants

حَرَض in descriptions of incapacitated people

Lexicons also record حَرَض for someone considered very weak in ability or effectiveness.

Some early social uses even applied it disparagingly.

For example, حَرَض and أَحْرَاض were sometimes applied to people who did not fight, lacked weapons, or were considered ineffective and of little worth by others.

This shows incapacity extending from physical condition to a social judgment about people.

Such descriptions are not necessarily sound moral judgments of human worth.

Physical weakness, illness, or inability to fight does not itself remove a person’s human value.

Lexical research should therefore record early disparaging uses without endorsing the associated social judgments.

حُرْض: a washing plant

Another branch is حُرْض or حُرُض.

Lexicons record it for the plant known as أُشْنَان.

The plant or material obtained from it was used for washing, particularly cleaning the hands.

مِحْرَضَة was a container for keeping or using that substance.

Some sources also record حُرْض for gypsum and حَرَّاض for someone preparing it through heating.

These uses belonged to early material life and occupations.

None, however, occurs in the Qur’an.

حَرَضًا in Yūsuf 12:85 should therefore not be interpreted as a washing plant or gypsum.

إِحْرِيض: safflower and its coloring material

Lexicons record إِحْرِيض for safflower and its coloring material.

ثَوْبٌ مُحَرَّضٌ in one use denotes a garment dyed with safflower.

This differs from human wasting and urging people to fight.

The forms show why derivatives sharing ح ر ض cannot all be reduced, without examination, to one semantic field.

Nor should modern uses of particular chemicals be assigned to this Arabic branch simply because their names resemble it.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and other lexicons: ḥurḍān, ush-nān, safflower, and related occupations

3. حَرَضًا in Jacob’s story

Yūsuf 12:85: wasting close to death

Arabic verse:

قَالُوا تَاللَّهِ تَفْتَأُ تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ حَتَّىٰ تَكُونَ حَرَضًا أَوْ تَكُونَ مِنَ الْهَالِكِينَ

qālū ta-Allāhi taftaʾu tadhkuru yūsufa ḥattā takūna ḥaraḍan aw takūna mina al-hālikīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They said: By The God, you keep remembering Joseph until you become so wasted and weak that you approach death, or become one of those who perish.”

Here حَرَضًا is a noun or verbal noun used descriptively.

تَكُونَ حَرَضًا means reaching a state of severe wasting and incapacity.

The addressee is Jacob.

The speakers are his children, commenting on his continual remembrance of Joseph.

The role of تَفْتَأُ

In تَاللَّهِ تَفْتَأُ تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ, تَفْتَأُ belongs to ف ت أ.

The oath construction and sense of continuation imply an omitted negative.

The point is that Jacob continues remembering Joseph and does not cease.

Continual remembrance and grief are thus present in the sentence.

But this comes from تَفْتَأُ and the oath construction, not from حَرَضًا itself.

حَرَضًا expresses the wasting consequence that the speakers fear or warn against if the situation continues.

حَرَضًا and الْهَالِكِينَ

The verse names two outcomes joined by أَوْ:

حَرَضًا and مِنَ الْهَالِكِينَ.

This difference should not disappear in translation.

حَرَض here concerns severe wasting and approaching death.

الْهَالِكِينَ comes from ه ل ك and refers to those who perish.

The speech can therefore be understood as continued grief bringing Jacob either to severe incapacity or to death and destruction.

Translating حَرَضًا simply as dead would remove the difference from the second expression.

Conversely, merely sad would fail to convey the intensity of wasting.

“Wasted and close to death” is more precise.

Jacob’s grief in context

The preceding verse, Yūsuf 12:84, describes Jacob’s grief and his eyes becoming white.

In Yūsuf 12:85, his children therefore comment on the effects of continued grief.

But حَرَضًا alone does not establish that Jacob suffered a particular disease.

The word neither supplies a medical diagnosis nor determines the precise extent of physical or psychological harm.

His children’s speech must also be recognized as their speech.

What they say about their father differs from the Qur’an endorsing every part of their judgment.

Jacob’s response

In Yūsuf 12:86, Jacob says that he presents his distress and grief only to God, and knows from God what his children do not know.

This response matters for understanding the preceding verse.

Jacob does not deny his grief, but expresses it in relation to God.

حَرَضًا therefore does not establish that all intense grief is automatically sinful or indicates lack of faith.

The account presents wasting and grief alongside Jacob’s hope and knowledge.

Nor does the verse establish that every grieving person must approach death.

حرض here describes a very severe condition that Jacob’s children raise in connection with continued grief.

Al-Ṭabarī: the omitted negative, descriptive verbal noun, and distinction from perishing

4. حَرِّضْ: urging action

The meaning of Form II

حَرِّضْ is a Form II verb.

In Qur’anic usage, it means urging, encouraging, and strengthening people’s motivation to act.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains التَّحْرِيض through التَّحْضِيض and الْحَثّ.

Al-Jawharī likewise relates urging to fight to encouragement and arousal toward it.

The direct meaning is therefore the act of urging and strengthening motivation to act, without itself guaranteeing a response or success.

Its object must be determined from the sentence.

Someone may be encouraged to do right or incited toward wrongdoing.

The verb itself does not determine the object’s moral value or legitimacy.

Both Qur’anic occurrences of حَرِّضْ concern fighting.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:84: personal responsibility and urging believers

Arabic verse:

فَقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا تُكَلَّفُ إِلَّا نَفْسَكَ وَحَرِّضِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَكُفَّ بَأْسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَاللَّهُ أَشَدُّ بَأْسًا وَأَشَدُّ تَنْكِيلًا

fa-qātil fī sabīli Allāhi lā tukallafu illā nafsaka wa-ḥarriḍi al-muʾminīna ʿasā Allāhu an yakuffa baʾsa alladhīna kafarū wa-Allāhu ashaddu baʾsan wa-ashaddu tankīlan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“So fight in the way of The God; you are charged with responsibility only for yourself; and urge the believers. Perhaps The God will restrain the fighting power of those who have disbelieved; and The God is stronger in might and more severe in chastisement.”

Three matters should be distinguished here.

First is the command فَقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ.

Second is لَا تُكَلَّفُ إِلَّا نَفْسَكَ, specifying the addressee’s responsibility.

Third is وَحَرِّضِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ, concerning urging other believers.

Here urging relates to fighting because the first command establishes the subject.

But urging others must be distinguished from deciding in their place.

لَا تُكَلَّفُ إِلَّا نَفْسَكَ shows that the Prophet’s responsibility is not equated with each believer’s responsibility.

حَرِّضْ alone therefore does not mean forcing people to fight.

This lexical distinction likewise cannot establish every ruling about participation in war or governmental authority; those require independent examination of the relevant verses.

The aim stated later in the verse

The same verse continues:

عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَكُفَّ بَأْسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

ʿasā Allāhu an yakuffa baʾsa alladhīna kafarū

Urging believers thus occurs in a context concerned with restraining the enemy’s force and aggression.

This aim should be preserved in understanding the verse.

But ح ر ض itself does not name defense, fighting, or jihad.

Taḥrīḍ denotes urging; its object and aim come from other parts of the sentence.

Here urging to fight belongs to a particular situation.

A command in this context does not establish that anyone may incite violence against ideological opponents at any time.

That generalization does not follow from the verb’s meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: the Prophet’s responsibility and urging believers to fight

Urging and coercion

In direct usage, حَرِّضْ means encouraging and urging.

أَكْرَهَ comes from ك ر ه and concerns compelling another against their will.

The concepts are not identical in Arabic.

Someone may encourage another through reasons, reminders of responsibility, or strengthened motivation without removing their will.

But encouragement may also accompany pressure, threats, or deception in some circumstances.

Taḥrīḍ alone therefore does not make every method of encouragement permissible.

Qur’anic research must assess legitimate methods of invitation and social responsibility in light of other verses.

Al-Anfāl 8:65: urging to fight and the role of perseverance

Arabic verse:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ حَرِّضِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ عَلَى الْقِتَالِ إِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ عِشْرُونَ صَابِرُونَ يَغْلِبُوا مِائَتَيْنِ وَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ مِائَةٌ يَغْلِبُوا أَلْفًا مِنَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu ḥarriḍi al-muʾminīna ʿalā al-qitāli in yakun minkum ʿishrūna ṣābirūna yaghlibū miʾatayni wa-in yakun minkum miʾatun yaghlibū alfan mina alladhīna kafarū bi-annahum qawmun lā yafqahūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“O Prophet, urge the believers to fight. If twenty among you are steadfast, they will overcome two hundred; and if a hundred of you, they will overcome a thousand of those who have disbelieved, because they are a people who do not understand.”

Unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:84, this verse places the object of urging directly after the verb:

عَلَى الْقِتَالِ

ʿalā al-qitāli

The phrase is therefore clear: the Prophet is commanded to urge believers to fight.

الْقِتَال comes from ق ت ل and concerns armed fighting.

حَرِّضْ itself does not mean fighting; it means urging others toward that action.

The distinction is essential in translation.

Urging and perseverance

The verse continues with عِشْرُونَ صَابِرُونَ.

Urging believers is thus related to their perseverance against an enemy.

In context, the urging is more than arousing a passing emotion: it strengthens readiness and perseverance in difficult circumstances.

But the full concept of patience and perseverance should not be merged into ح ر ض.

Patience and perseverance belong to the independent ص ب ر family, which has a broad Qur’anic range.

Urging is the command here; perseverance is a quality specified later for the confrontation.

Ability and the numbers of forces

Al-Anfāl 8:65 states numerical ratios between steadfast believers and an opposing group.

But the next verse, Al-Anfāl 8:66, describes a lightening and God’s knowledge of weakness among the believers, stating a different ratio.

This connection matters.

Al-Anfāl 8:65 should not be separated from Al-Anfāl 8:66 and made into an unchanging numerical rule about every war.

Early commentaries also differ in how they understand these ratios. Some examine obligation and lightening; others emphasize encouragement and promised victory.

ح ر ض itself cannot resolve that legal and exegetical disagreement.

What is lexically clear is the command to urge believers to fight.

Numerical ratios, lightening, and people’s responsibilities require examination across the verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: urging and its relationship to the following verse’s lightening

Does urging mean guaranteed victory?

No.

حَرِّضْ means urging and encouraging.

The verb alone does not guarantee the outcome.

Someone may be urged to act yet not do so because of circumstances, inability, or a different decision.

A highly motivated group may likewise enter battle without winning.

Al-Anfāl 8:65 states promises and ratios of victory later in the verse; these do not derive from taḥrīḍ itself.

The root should therefore not be defined as though taḥrīḍ inherently meant victory or certain empowerment.

5. The relationship between حَرَض and تَحْرِيض

How does wasting relate to urging?

At first sight, the root’s two Qur’anic uses differ.

In Yūsuf 12:85, حَرَض is a state of severe weakness and wasting.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65, حَرِّضْ means urging action.

Early lexicographers proposed several explanations of the relationship.

In one, urging may relate to bringing someone out of lassitude and weakness: rousing them so that they do not remain inactive and incapable.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī also suggests that taḥrīḍ originally concerned removing or averting a state of ḥaraḍ.

He compares the formation with verbs whose pattern expresses removing a particular state or thing.

In another explanation, Al-Zajjāj describes encouragement that also makes a person aware of the harmful consequences of abandoning the action.

Here the relationship to ḥaraḍ concerns the possible wasting or ruin of someone who fails in the responsibility.

These explanations should not be confused with a certain history of derivation.

Ibn Fāris himself reports the relationship between taḥrīḍ and weakness or destruction as a view proposed by some lexicographers.

It therefore cannot be asserted without evidence that حَرَّضَ must originally have meant rescuing someone from illness before coming to mean encouragement.

What established Arabic usage clearly shows is the difference between the direct meanings of the two forms.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Zajjāj, and Ibn Fāris: semantic explanations linking taḥrīḍ with ḥaraḍ

Is urging the same as threatening?

Some lexical explanations include making someone aware of the consequences of abandoning a responsibility as part of taḥrīḍ.

But this does not mean that every act of urging must include a threat.

Urging may work through reminders of a goal, strengthened motivation, explanation of an action’s importance, or encouragement to persevere.

In some situations, the speaker may also use a warning.

A threat may accompany urging in particular circumstances, but is not the word’s fixed, exclusive meaning; recalling a consequence need not itself constitute a threat.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65, “urge” or “encourage” is more suitable than replacing the verb with “threaten” without justification.

Does حَرَض in Yūsuf simply mean lack of motivation?

No.

Jacob is associated with wasting through grief and attachment in the story.

But the verse does not say he had merely lost the motivation to act.

حَرَضًا here concerns wasting and severe weakness.

By contrast, taḥrīḍ in the other verses concerns urging action.

The senses can be studied within one lexical family, but neither should fully replace the other.

6. Differences from related roots

Ḥ R Ḍ and Ḥ TH TH: urging and prompting haste

Arabic حَثَّ concerns encouraging, setting in motion, and hastening.

حَرَّضَ can likewise mean urging someone to act.

Lexical explanations repeatedly explain taḥrīḍ through حَثّ.

But ح ث ث also has a field of swift, persistent movement.

By contrast, ح ر ض includes the branch of wasting, incapacity, and approaching destruction.

The roots are thus close in encouragement to act but not identical.

Study of Ḥ TH TH

Ḥ R Ḍ and Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ: urging and encouragement

حَضَّ comes from ح ض ض and concerns encouraging or urging an action.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains taḥrīḍ through taḥḍīḍ.

The Qur’an uses ح ض ض for encouraging the feeding of the needy, including Al-Māʿūn 107:3 and Al-Fajr 89:18.

By contrast, حَرِّضْ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65 concerns fighting.

This shows that the object of encouragement comes from context.

The act of urging itself is not confined to one particular action.

Ḥ R Ḍ and Ḥ R Ṣ: urging and intense concern

حَرِصَ, with ṣād, comes from ح ر ص.

That family concerns strong desire, concern, and effort to obtain something.

حَرَض, with ḍād, concerns weakness and wasting in its Qur’anic use in Yūsuf.

حَرَّضَ concerns urging another.

حِرْص and حَرَض must therefore not be confused because their consonants resemble one another.

حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:128 belongs to ح ر ص, not ح ر ض.

Study of Ḥ R Ṣ

Ḥ R Ḍ and Ḍ ʿ F: wasting and weakness

ضَعْف concerns incapacity and diminished strength.

حَرَض can also express severe weakness.

But the wasting use usually has greater intensity, concerning a condition close to ruin or death.

Not every weak person is therefore necessarily ḥaraḍ.

Al-Anfāl 8:66 uses ضَعْفًا, from the independent ض ع ف family.

Weakness in the following verse should not cause حَرِّضْ in Al-Anfāl 8:65 to be translated as weaken.

Ḥ R Ḍ and H L K: wasting and destruction

هَلَكَ concerns perishing and ceasing to exist.

حَرَض can refer to someone approaching death.

Both families occur in Yūsuf 12:85.

Their co-occurrence shows nearby semantic fields, but the sentence itself distinguishes severe wasting from perishing.

Translation should preserve that distinction.

Study of H L K

Ḥ R Ḍ and M R Ḍ: wasting and illness

مَرَض concerns illness.

حَرَض can describe someone wasted by illness or grief.

But not every illness reaches the condition of ḥaraḍ.

Yūsuf 12:85 concerns very severe wasting and weakness.

“Ill” alone is therefore not a complete equivalent of حَرَضًا.

Study of M R Ḍ

Ḥ R Ḍ and Ḥ Z N: grief and its wasting effect

حُزْن concerns grief.

حَرَض in Jacob’s story can be the wasting result of continued grief.

But grief and wasting are not identical.

Someone may be sad without reaching severe incapacity.

Conversely, ḥaraḍ may arise from illness or another cause and need not always concern grief.

Grief should therefore not become the word’s fixed definition.

Study of Ḥ Z N

Ḥ R Ḍ and Q T L: urging and fighting

قِتَال comes from ق ت ل and concerns fighting.

حَرِّضْ in its two Qur’anic occurrences commands the urging of believers.

Al-Anfāl 8:65 explicitly names the object as الْقِتَال.

The two roots thus stand together in one sentence without having the same meaning.

Someone may encourage another to fight, but the encouragement itself is not the fighting.

Study of Q T L

Ḥ R Ḍ and J H D: urging action and exerting effort

جَهَدَ and its derivatives concern effort and the expenditure of strength.

حَرَّضَ means urging another to act.

In some circumstances, someone may encourage another toward jihad or difficult exertion.

But jihad does not name every act of urging, just as taḥrīḍ does not mean exerting strength in every field.

Al-Furqān 25:52 also describes striving with the Qur’an; not every use of jihad should be restricted to warfare.

7. Challenges in understanding and semantic development

Does taḥrīḍ always have a negative meaning?

In contemporary Persian, taḥrīk and taḥrīḍ sometimes concern incitement to violence, hatred, or crime.

But this negative use is not the entire historical meaning of the root.

Arabic حَرَّضَ concerns urging and encouragement; its moral value depends on the object and method.

Both Qur’anic verbal occurrences concern fighting.

But that fact does not establish that Arabic taḥrīḍ is used only for warfare or violence.

Is urging to fight the same as forcing belief?

No.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65, the object is fighting within the verses’ particular contexts.

The command does not mean forcing opponents to adopt a belief.

The Qur’an denies compulsion in religion in Al-Baqarah 2:256 and speaks of not transgressing in fighting in Al-Baqarah 2:190.

A verb for urging believers in a wartime situation should therefore not yield unlimited authority to impose beliefs on others.

Conversely, these verses do speak of fighting; their military subject should not be changed into purely intellectual effort.

The precise approach preserves their direct subject while assessing the legitimacy and limits of fighting across the Qur’an.

Does urging always produce successful action?

No.

Taḥrīḍ is the act of urging and encouraging.

Someone may accept or reject the invitation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 also distinguishes the Prophet’s personal responsibility from urging believers.

The word itself therefore cannot guarantee obedience or victory.

Does حَرَضًا in Yūsuf mean insanity?

Some early exegetical explanations also speak of impaired understanding in explaining ḥaraḍ.

But the broader, clearer meaning is wasting and severe weakness.

Jacob’s children’s speech should not yield a conclusive diagnosis of their father’s mental or intellectual condition.

The next verse shows Jacob speaking of knowledge and hope that his children do not possess.

“Becoming insane” is therefore unsuitable for the direct translation of حَرَضًا.

Should حَرَضًا be translated as death?

حرض approaches death, but differs from هالكين in Yūsuf 12:85.

Rendering both expressions as dying would erase the distinction.

“Wasted and close to death” is therefore more precise.

Must taḥrīḍ historically mean treating weakness?

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī proposes this relationship as a possible explanation.

Ibn Fāris likewise reports some lexicographers’ view connecting taḥrīḍ with wasting, rather than conclusively proving the verb’s history.

The relationship is therefore better retained as a semantic explanation.

A root study should not construct a certain narrative in which ḥaraḍ first meant illness, then treating illness, then encouragement to fight.

That historical sequence requires independent evidence.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For حَرَضًا in Yūsuf 12:85, the most suitable basic equivalent is “wasted and close to death.”

A natural rendering within the sentence is:

“Until you become so wasted and weak that you approach death.”

This preserves the intensity of weakness while distinguishing it from actual death.

“Ill” alone is too general.

“Insane” is also only one exegetical explanation of the severity of impairment and should not replace the principal meaning.

For حَرِّضْ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:84, “urge” or “encourage” is suitable.

For حَرِّضِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ عَلَى الْقِتَالِ in Al-Anfāl 8:65, “urge the believers to fight” is direct and clear.

Persian bar-angīkhtan is suitable because it expresses prompting and strengthening motivation without prespecifying its form and method.

Forqan’s word boxes should retain two distinct fields.

Brief root meaning: “Severe wasting and weakness to the verge of destruction; in Form II, urging and prompting action.”

Meaning in Yūsuf 12:85: “Someone whose strength has been worn away by severe wasting and grief, approaching death.”

Meaning in Al-Nisāʾ 4:84: “Urging and encouraging believers to participate in the fighting discussed in the verse.”

Meaning in Al-Anfāl 8:65: “Encouraging and strengthening believers’ motivation for fighting and perseverance in the confrontation discussed.”

Direct verse translations should not include explanations of the verb’s possible origin, the ush-nān washing plant, or safflower coloring material.

These belong to the research article, rather than the direct rendering of Qur’anic sentences.

9. Conclusion

Arabic ح ر ض has two important fields: severe wasting and incapacity, and urging action.

حَرَض describes someone whose strength has been worn away by illness, grief, or another cause to the verge of death.

حَرَّضَ in its established use means encouraging and urging another to act.

Lexicographers offer different explanations of their relationship, but the precise historical origin should not be presented as certain without evidence.

Arabic sources also record a branch concerning a washing plant and safflower coloring material. Ibn Fāris distinguishes it from weakness and destruction.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an.

In Yūsuf 12:85, حَرَضًا expresses wasting and approaching death. It is close to الْهَالِكِينَ, but context distinguishes it from actual death.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65, حَرِّضْ commands the urging of believers. Its object is fighting in these verses, but the verb itself does not mean fighting.

Thus, حَرَض expresses severe wasting and incapacity, whereas تَحْرِيض is the act of urging and strengthening motivation to act.

Translation should keep these senses distinct.

حَرَضًا cannot diagnose a particular illness or mental disorder in Jacob; nor can حَرِّضْ authorize unlimited violence, coerced belief, or warfare against every opponent.

The verb means urging. The object’s legitimacy, the limits of responsibility, and the method of action must be known from context and other verses.

For Forqan, “wasted and close to death” in Yūsuf 12:85 and “urge” in Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65 accurately preserve the words’ differences in form and use.
