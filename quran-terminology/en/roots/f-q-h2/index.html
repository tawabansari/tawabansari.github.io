Understanding meaning, grasping intent, and informed recognition: beyond the technical term for religious jurisprudence

The root in early Arabic

The three-letter Arabic root ف ق ه denotes understanding, grasping the meaning of speech or a matter, and recognizing its intent. فَقِهَ الشَّيْءَ means that someone understood something and grasped its meaning; فَقِهَ عَنْهُ means that they understood what another person meant.

In the word's early usage, the object of understanding could be someone's speech, an explanation of a problem, a situation that arose, or a reality requiring attention and recognition. Fiqh was not originally the name of a legal system or a body of religious rulings.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imru’ al-Qays reads:

لَا يَفْقَهُ الْقَوْمُ فِيهِ كُلَّ مَنْطِقِهِمْ
إِلَّا سِرَارًا تَخَالُ الصَّوْتَ مَرْدُودَا

Lā yafqahu l-qawmu fīhi kulla manṭiqihim / illā sirāran takhālu ṣ-ṣawta mardūdā.

“The people there do not fully understand one another's speech except in whispers, as though the sound were returning.”

The verse describes a place in terms of sound and conversation. Yafqahu here concerns understanding speech, not knowing rulings or possessing specialized religious learning.

The verse is recorded in the poetry collection attributed to Imru’ al-Qays. Since pre-Islamic poetry has reached us through later transmission, attribution alone does not conclusively establish the date of an individual verse. With that limitation, this example accords with the general meaning of understanding speech recorded in Arabic lexical sources and clearly present in the Qur’an.

Other usages recorded by Arabic lexicons include understanding, awareness, and perceptiveness. The expression فَحْلٌ فَقِيهٌ, for a male animal skilled in mating, illustrates faqīh in the sense of capable or adept. This lexical report should not be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without independent evidence, but it shows that the recorded semantic range of the family was not restricted to religious learning.

No original physical image has been established with sufficient certainty to serve as the historical source of every use of this root. Explanations such as “opening up a closed meaning” may aid understanding, but should not be presented as the root's proven original meaning.

Forms of the root and its semantic core

Known Arabic forms include فَقِهَ (faqiha), يَفْقَهُ (yafqahu), فِقْه (fiqh), فَقِيه (faqīh), فُقَهَاء (fuqahāʾ), فَقُهَ (faquha), فَقَاهَة (faqāhah), تَفَقَّهَ (tafaqqaha), and فَقَّهَ (faqqaha). Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The root occurs twenty times in twenty Qur’anic verses: nineteen times in the basic verb form and once in Form V. The attested forms are يَفْقَهُونَ, يَفْقَهُوهُ, نَفْقَهُ, تَفْقَهُونَ, يَفْقَهُوا, and لِيَتَفَقَّهُوا.

The nouns fiqh, faqīh, and fuqahāʾ do not occur in the Qur’anic text. An examination of Qur’anic meaning should therefore begin with the verbs actually present in the verses, rather than titles and classifications that became widespread later.

A defensible semantic core is grasping the meaning or intent of something.

Hearing speech does not yet establish that its intent has been understood. Someone may recognize the words without understanding their relationship, the message, or the reality being indicated. Fiqh can express this grasp of meaning.

It should not, however, be claimed that fiqh necessarily denotes a deeper level than every kind of ordinary understanding in all Arabic usages. Sometimes it means simply understanding speech; sometimes recognizing the relationship between signs and reality; sometimes an understanding that affects judgment and behavior. The degree of depth emerges from the verb's object and context.

Fiqh in understanding speech and human communication

One of the clearest usages occurs in Shu‘ayb's exchange with his people:

قَالُوا يَا شُعَيْبُ مَا نَفْقَهُ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا تَقُولُ وَإِنَّا لَنَرَاكَ فِينَا ضَعِيفًا وَلَوْلَا رَهْطُكَ لَرَجَمْنَاكَ وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْنَا بِعَزِيزٍ

Qālū yā Shuʿaybu mā nafqahu kathīran mimmā taqūlu wa-innā la-narāka fīnā ḍaʿīfan wa-lawlā rahṭuka la-rajamnāka wa-mā anta ʿalaynā bi-ʿazīz.

“They said: Shu‘ayb, we do not understand much of what you say, and we see you as weak among us. Were it not for your kin, we would stone you, and you are not held in honor among us.”

Hud 11:91

مَا نَفْقَهُ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا تَقُولُ means not understanding much of what is said. The verb's immediate object is Shu‘ayb's speech, but the context shows that the issue goes beyond a simple linguistic exchange. After declaring that they do not understand, his people use social standing and power to threaten him.

Nafqahu alone does not establish that they were necessarily linguistically incapable, or that they consciously denied everything Shu‘ayb said. Such possibilities must be assessed against their behavior and speech throughout the surrounding verses.

Another usage occurs in Moses' prayer:

يَفْقَهُوا قَوْلِي

Yafqahū qawlī.

“So that they may understand my speech.”

Ta-Ha 20:28

In the preceding verse, Ta-Ha 20:27, Moses asks The God to loosen the knot in his tongue. He then states the purpose: that his audience may understand him.

Here, fiqh directly concerns understanding speech. Clear expression and the listener's understanding are connected, but fiqh itself means neither oratorical skill nor removal of a speech difficulty; those matters emerge from the prayer as a whole.

Another example occurs in the story of Dhu al-Qarnayn:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ بَيْنَ السَّدَّيْنِ وَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمَا قَوْمًا لَا يَكَادُونَ يَفْقَهُونَ قَوْلًا

Ḥattā idhā balagha bayna s-saddayni wajada min dūnihimā qawman lā yakādūna yafqahūna qawlā.

“Until, when he reached the place between the two barriers, he found on this side of them a people who could scarcely understand speech.”

Al-Kahf 18:93

لَا يَكَادُونَ يَفْقَهُونَ قَوْلًا expresses severe difficulty understanding speech. A language difference or communication difficulty may be an instance of this. The verse itself, however, does not say that these people were unintelligent, uncivilized, or incapable of thinking.

The distinction matters: not understanding another person's speech does not always indicate ignorance or moral corruption. Sometimes the barrier lies in language, expression, or the possibility of communication.

An-Nisa 4:78 likewise uses لَا يَكَادُونَ يَفْقَهُونَ حَدِيثًا concerning people who attribute favorable and adverse events in a particular way. The subject is their failure to grasp speech and the explanation relevant to that situation properly.

These usages show that fiqh can concern understanding speech without necessarily having a technical or specialized meaning.

Fiqh in understanding the signs and recognizing the world

The Qur’an does not use this root only for human speech. Divine signs, signs of creation, and realities encountered by people are also objects of understanding.

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَكُمْ مِنْ نَفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ فَمُسْتَقَرٌّ وَمُسْتَوْدَعٌ قَدْ فَصَّلْنَا الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَفْقَهُونَ

Wa-huwa lladhī anshaʾakum min nafsin wāḥidatin fa-mustaqarrun wa-mustawdaʿun qad faṣṣalnā l-āyāti li-qawmin yafqahūn.

“It is He who brought you into being from a single self; then a place of settlement and a place of deposit. We have indeed set out the signs in detail for people who understand.”

Al-An’am 6:98

The verse concerns human emergence from a single self and its relationship to settlement and being entrusted. It ends by connecting the detailed exposition of the signs with yafqahūna.

Understanding here is more than merely hearing a report; people must attend to the signs presented and grasp their meaning. That these signs concern creation and life follows from the verse's content, not from an inherently biological meaning in the root fiqh.

Al-An’am 6:65 likewise speaks of presenting the signs in different ways so that the audience may understand, after mentioning punishment, division into factions, and making some taste the violence of others. Fiqh can therefore concern recognizing social consequences and the relationship between human behavior and its results.

Another verse reveals the limits of human understanding:

تُسَبِّحُ لَهُ السَّمَاوَاتُ السَّبْعُ وَالْأَرْضُ وَمَنْ فِيهِنَّ وَإِنْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ إِلَّا يُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِهِ وَلَٰكِنْ لَا تَفْقَهُونَ تَسْبِيحَهُمْ إِنَّهُ كَانَ حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا

Tusabbiḥu lahu s-samāwātu s-sabʿu wa-l-arḍu wa-man fīhinna wa-in min shayʾin illā yusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdihi wa-lākin lā tafqahūna tasbīḥahum innahu kāna ḥalīman ghafūrā.

“The seven heavens, the earth, and whoever is in them glorify Him. There is nothing that does not glorify Him with praise, but you do not understand their glorification. He is indeed Forbearing, Forgiving.”

Al-Isra 17:44

This verse is especially significant for understanding the root's scope. People may encounter a phenomenon without grasping its reality or manner. The Qur’an affirms the glorification of created things while stating that humans do not understand it.

Lā tafqahūna is not, by itself, moral condemnation here. The verse speaks of a limit to human understanding concerning a reality whose occurrence the Qur’an affirms.

The root thus applies when someone is to understand speech, when they examine a sign, and when they cannot grasp a reality.

The shared verb does not establish a single cause for all these failures to understand.

Fiqh, the heart, and human responsibility toward truth

In some verses, failure to understand accompanies heedlessness, turning away, and the closing of access to truth.

وَلَقَدْ ذَرَأْنَا لِجَهَنَّمَ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا أُولَٰئِكَ كَالْأَنْعَامِ بَلْ هُمْ أَضَلُّ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْغَافِلُونَ

Wa-laqad dharaʾnā li-jahannama kathīran mina l-jinni wa-l-insi lahum qulūbun lā yafqahūna bihā wa-lahum aʿyunun lā yubṣirūna bihā wa-lahum ādhānun lā yasmaʿūna bihā ulāʾika ka-l-anʿāmi bal hum aḍallu ulāʾika humu l-ghāfilūn.

“We have indeed brought forth for Hell many of the jinn and humankind. They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear. They are like livestock—rather, further astray. Those are the heedless.”

Al-A’raf 7:179

The verse distinguishes possessing a faculty from using it. Having eyes does not, on its own, guarantee seeing truth; having ears likewise does not mean hearing and accepting every message. Within the same construction, the heart is connected with fiqh.

This usage places understanding within a wider network than sensory perception alone. The heart in this context concerns recognition and grasping meaning; that relationship should not, without independent evidence, be used to draw conclusions about the biological structure of the brain or heart.

Al-An’am 6:25, Al-Isra 17:46, and Al-Kahf 18:57 also connect coverings over the heart and heaviness in the ears with failure to understand. In Al-Kahf 18:57, the context itself mentions turning away from the signs after being reminded and forgetting previous deeds. The condition therefore cannot be explained solely as an innate or imposed inability; human behavior also has a place in the description.

At-Tawbah 9:87, At-Tawbah 9:127, and Al-Munafiqun 63:3 likewise connect failure to understand with the heart's condition. The last says:

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ آمَنُوا ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا فَطُبِعَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ فَهُمْ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

Dhālika bi-annahum āmanū thumma kafarū fa-ṭubiʿa ʿalā qulūbihim fa-hum lā yafqahūn.

“That is because they believed and then disbelieved, so their hearts were sealed, and they do not understand.”

Al-Munafiqun 63:3

The sequence matters: belief, then disbelief, then sealing of the heart and failure to understand. In this context, inability to understand is not detached from the course of behavior described.

Al-Isra 17:44, by contrast, showed that not every failure to understand means disbelief or heedlessness. Lā yafqahūna should therefore not receive one fixed explanation in every verse.

Failing to understand consequences and the reality of power

In several verses, fiqh concerns correctly recognizing a situation and the consequences of conduct.

In Al-Anfal 8:65, failure to understand describes a group in the context of combat and steadfastness. In At-Tawbah 9:81, those displeased about setting out in the heat encounter a warning about the Fire's greater heat. In Al-Hashr 59:13, some people fear their opponents more than The God, and this condition is attributed to their lack of understanding.

In Al-Munafiqun 63:7, this relationship appears in an economic and social decision:

هُمُ الَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ لَا تُنْفِقُوا عَلَىٰ مَنْ عِنْدَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ حَتَّىٰ يَنْفَضُّوا وَلِلَّهِ خَزَائِنُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

Humu lladhīna yaqūlūna lā tunfiqū ʿalā man ʿinda rasūli llāhi ḥattā yanfaḍḍū wa-li-llāhi khazāʾinu s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-lākinna l-munāfiqīna lā yafqahūn.

“They are the ones who say: Do not spend on those with the Messenger of The God until they disperse. Yet to The God belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, but the hypocrites do not understand.”

Al-Munafiqun 63:7

This group imagines it can disperse others by cutting off financial support. The Qur’an's response recalls The God's comprehensive ownership of the treasuries of the heavens and earth.

Their failure to understand is not simply ignorance of a few words' meanings; they misjudge a reality relevant to their conduct.

Al-Fath 48:15 also qualifies the denial of understanding with “a little.” Even when describing people whose behavior is condemned, the verse therefore does not necessarily declare an absolute failure to understand anything.

Together, these usages show that fiqh can concern correctly recognizing intent, reality, and consequences. These domains, however, are determined by each verse's context and do not constitute an independent definition of the root.

Acquiring understanding in religion: understanding religion or specializing in rulings?

The root's only Qur’anic occurrence in Form V is in At-Tawbah:

وَمَا كَانَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ لِيَنْفِرُوا كَافَّةً فَلَوْلَا نَفَرَ مِنْ كُلِّ فِرْقَةٍ مِنْهُمْ طَائِفَةٌ لِيَتَفَقَّهُوا فِي الدِّينِ وَلِيُنْذِرُوا قَوْمَهُمْ إِذَا رَجَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَحْذَرُونَ

Wa-mā kāna l-muʾminūna li-yanfirū kāffatan fa-lawlā nafara min kulli firqatin minhum ṭāʾifatun li-yatafaqqahū fī d-dīni wa-li-yundhirū qawmahum idhā rajaʿū ilayhim laʿallahum yaḥdharūn.

“It is not for the believers to go forth all together. Why, then, should a party from every group of them not go forth to gain understanding in religion and warn their people when they return to them, so that they may take heed?”

At-Tawbah 9:122

Li-yatafaqqahū belongs to Form V. Here, this construction denotes engaging in the acquisition of understanding and making an effort to learn. Whereas yafqahu can express the presence or absence of understanding, yatafaqqahu here concerns the process of acquiring it.

Fī d-dīn specifies the object of that effort: understanding religion.

There are two recognized readings of the antecedents of li-yatafaqqahū and li-yundhirū. In one, the pronouns refer to the party that goes forth: they travel to gain understanding and warn their people after returning. In the other, in light of the context of mobilization, some go while others remain to learn religion and convey what they have learned to those who return.

In terms of the immediate continuity of the wording, reference to the departing group is clearer. The context of distributing responsibility between those departing and those remaining, however, explains the emergence of the second reading.

In both readings, acquiring understanding of religion and conveying it to others is central. The verse connects learning with warning. The aim is that people benefit from what is learned and take heed regarding their conduct and its consequences.

Does acquiring understanding in religion, however, mean specializing in jurisprudential rulings in today's familiar sense?

The verse itself contains no such restriction.

Dīn is a broad Qur’anic term and cannot, without examination, be restricted to the jurisprudential questions of a particular school. Nor is tafaqquh here the name of a legal institution, a religious office, or a legislative apparatus.

Understanding divine commands and limits can form part of acquiring understanding in religion, but the expression is not restricted to practical rulings. Recognizing the signs, human responsibility, guidance, warning, truth and falsehood, and the consequences of deeds also relates to the purpose stated in the verse.

In later periods, fiqh gradually became the name of a specialized discipline concerning practical rulings. That technical usage can be recognized in its own setting, but it cannot determine the root's entire Qur’anic semantic range.

For this reason, لِيَتَفَقَّهُوا فِي الدِّينِ is better rendered “so that they may gain understanding in religion” or “so that they may understand religion properly”, rather than being restricted from the outset to “so that they may learn jurisprudential rulings.”

Nor does the verse, by itself, grant a group of learners authority to legislate independently of revelation or immunity from error. The task stated is to acquire understanding and give warning.

Differences from related words

ف ق ه relates to roots such as ع ل م, ف ه م, ع ق ل, and د ب ر within the field of knowledge and understanding meaning. These words, however, do not have identical Qur’anic usages.

ع ل م (ʿilm): Concerns knowing and awareness. In many verses, its objects are knowledge, realities, and matters of which someone is aware. Fiqh can likewise express a kind of knowledge, but usages such as understanding speech emphasize grasping meaning and intent. This is a difference of tendency, since ʿilm and fiqh overlap in Arabic.

ف ه م (fahm): The closest word to the general meaning of fiqh. In Al-Anbiya 21:79, concerning David and Solomon's judgment, فَهَّمْنَا refers to causing Solomon to understand the matter. This does not establish an absolute rule that fahm is always superficial and fiqh always deep. Both can convey understanding meaning; their precise difference must be examined in construction and context.

ع ق ل (ʿaql): This root occurs in the Qur’an in verbal forms and is connected with exercising thought and judgment. Fiqh and ʿaql can operate together in a cognitive process, but are not lexically identical. Nor should a root image for ʿaql be used, without evidence, to construct definitive stages in the emergence of fiqh.

د ب ر (tadabbur): Qur’anic tadabbur concerns attentive consideration of speech and reflection upon it. It may lead to better understanding, but fiqh and tadabbur are not entirely synonymous names for the same act.

The comparison shows that the Qur’an expresses human cognition through different words. Their relationships should be understood from actual verse usages, rather than a fixed hierarchy that necessarily places each word above another.

Suggested renderings for Forqan

For the basic verb faqiha and its Qur’anic forms, “understand,” “grasp,” and “comprehend” are natural primary equivalents.

In verses about speech, such as Hud 11:91, Ta-Ha 20:28, and Al-Kahf 18:93, “understand speech” or “grasp the intent of speech” is suitable.

In verses about signs, reality, or the consequences of behavior, “grasp,” “understand correctly,” or, where context warrants, “recognize” may be clearer.

In Al-Isra 17:44, لَا تَفْقَهُونَ تَسْبِيحَهُمْ can be rendered “you do not understand their glorification,” since the subject is a reality whose manner is not clearly accessible to humans.

For لِيَتَفَقَّهُوا فِي الدِّينِ in At-Tawbah 9:122, “so that they may gain understanding in religion” or “so that they may understand religion properly” is suitable. “Gain a deep understanding” can also be an acceptable explanation of the aim of tafaqquh, but should not imply that fiqh inherently means deep, specialized understanding in every verse.

Negative forms such as لَا يَفْقَهُونَ and مَا نَفْقَهُ should likewise be translated contextually. “They do not understand” suffices in some verses; elsewhere “they do not grasp” or “they do not understand correctly” better conveys the intended distinction. Adding “they do not want to understand” is justified only when the context supports conscious conduct and turning away.

Conclusion

At its foundation, ف ق ه concerns understanding and grasping meaning or intent. Its Qur’anic usages extend from understanding human speech to grasping signs, recognizing reality, and assessing situations and behavioral consequences.

The root is not restricted to a specialized field in the Qur’an. Shu‘ayb's people use it concerning understanding his words; Moses asks that his speech be understood; the Qur’an describes people who understand the signs, and humans who do not understand the glorification of created things.

Failure to understand does not have a single cause either. Sometimes the barrier concerns communication and language, sometimes the limits of human knowledge, and sometimes the turning away and heedlessness explained in the verses' context.

Acquiring understanding in religion in At-Tawbah 9:122 concerns striving to understand religion and conveying awareness to others. Restricting it to denominational jurisprudence projects a later technical meaning back onto the Qur’an.

The resulting definition for Forqan is:

ف ق ه: Understanding and grasping the meaning or intent of speech, a sign, or a matter that becomes an object of knowledge; the degree and quality of understanding are determined by context. In Form V, engaging in acquiring such understanding.

This definition preserves the root's general meaning while allowing its varied Qur’anic usages to be understood without imposing a narrow post-Qur’anic technical sense.
