Core sense: infertility and failure to produce offspring or an expected yield; extended uses include unfruitfulness, bringing no benefit, and severe difficulty or constriction that resists relief.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root «ع ق م» concerns infertility, failure to reproduce, severity, constriction, and absence of an expected yield.

Early Arabic uses «عُقْم» and «عَقْم» for infertility. «عَقِيم» describes a woman or man who does not produce children. «عَقُمَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ» means that a woman was infertile or did not conceive; «رَحِمٌ عَقِيمٌ» describes a womb in which pregnancy does not occur.

Early lexicons sometimes explain it as «هَزْمَةٌ تَقَعُ فِي الرَّحِمِ», a depression or impairment in the womb preventing conception.

This is an old lexical explanation, not a medical diagnosis of every instance. The word «عقيم» does not identify its cause.

The family also includes «عَقَام» for severe, difficult-to-treat illness. «دَاءٌ عُقَامٌ» describes an illness regarded as incurable or exceptionally resistant to treatment.

«حَرْبٌ عَقَامٌ» describes a severe, merciless war, foregrounding intense conflict and injury.

«رِيحٌ عَقِيمٌ» describes wind that brings no rain or expected beneficial yield, contrasted in lexical usage with rain-bearing and fertilizing winds.

«يَوْمٌ عَقِيمٌ» can describe a harsh, unfruitful day that brings the people concerned no benefit or relief.

The connections do not justify reducing all uses to an invented definition. Human infertility concerns children; barren wind concerns beneficial yield; severe war foregrounds intensity and injury.

Some lexical explanations also associate «عَقْم» with cutting or interruption. Interrupted reproduction offers a possible semantic comparison, but does not establish the historical origin of every branch.

Two important fields should therefore remain visible: infertility and failure to yield an expected outcome; and severity, constriction, and difficulty resisting relief or a desired result.

In the Quran, infertility and its extensions to wind and a day are central.

2. Evidence from early Arabic

An early example is attributed to al-Aʿshā:

تَلْوِي بِعِذْقٍ خِصَابٍ كُلَّمَا خَطَرَتْ
عَنْ فَرْجِ مَعْقُومَةٍ لَمْ تَتْبَعْ رَبْعًا

talwī bi-ʿidhqi khiṣābin kullamā khaṭarat / ʿan farji maʿqūmatin lam tatbaʿ rabʿā

“Whenever she swishes her tail, she swings what resembles a cluster of khiṣāb dates away from the opening of a barren she-camel that has not followed a young calf.”

«مَعْقُومَة» here describes a barren she-camel.

«رَبْع» denotes a young camel. In the transmitted wording, the she-camel is the subject of «لَمْ تَتْبَعْ رَبْعًا»: she has not followed a calf. It does not literally say that no calf followed her.

The description concerns the animal's reproduction, not total inability to move or general incapacity.

Infertility is a specific condition and should not be generalized to every function of a living being.

Lexicons attribute the line to al-Aʿshā. Its precise date and all textual variants have not been independently established here. It attests early «معقومة» without establishing every historical detail of the scene.

Not producing a comparable offspring

Another example is attributed to Abū Dahbal al-Jumaḥī, an early Islamic poet:

عُقِمَ النِّسَاءُ فَلَنْ يَلِدْنَ شَبِيهَهُ
إِنَّ النِّسَاءَ بِمِثْلِهِ عُقْمُ

ʿuqima al-nisāʾu fa-lan yalidna shabīhahu / inna al-nisāʾa bi-mithlihi ʿuqmu

“Women are as though barren of his like; they will not bear his equal—for women are barren of one like him.”

The poet uses infertility as an image of praise.

He does not claim that all women have physically become infertile; his subject is so exceptional that no equal is born.

A bodily meaning becomes a literary expression.

The example shows that the root can describe failure to produce a comparable example.

The line is attributed variously to Abū Dahbal al-Jumaḥī and al-Ḥazīn al-Laythī, both belonging to the Islamic period. It is useful early Arabic evidence, not an independent pre-Islamic witness.

Severity and constriction

Early lexicons record «حَرْبٌ عَقَامٌ» and «دَاءٌ عُقَامٌ».

The former is a severe war with extensive injury and killing, in which people show little mercy.

The latter is a severe, intractable illness.

Neither directly describes human or animal infertility. Severity and lack of relief or a favorable outcome connect them.

These expressions do not name every kind of difficulty; they emphasize a condition from which escape or recovery is difficult.

Further lexical branches

Some sources use «عُقْم» for a red garment and for patterned cloth.

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah uses «عَقْمًا وَرَقْمًا» of woven pattern and color.

The recorded family is therefore not limited to infertility.

«مَعَاقِم» denotes bodily joints, including those of horses. «اعْتِقَام» can mean digging a small test shaft within a well as water is approached, or continuing to dig downward.

Lexical explanations propose links with severity, constriction, or complexity. Patterned cloth, joints, and infertility need not be forced into one original image.

These branches show the breadth of Arabic; Quranic «عقيم» must be understood through its own usage and context.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms include «عَقِمَ», «عَقُمَ», «عُقِمَ», «عُقْم», «عَقْم», «عَقِيم», «عَقِيمَة», «مَعْقُوم», «عُقَمَاء», «عَقَائِم», «عَقَام», «تَعْقِيم», «مَعَاقِم», and «اعْتِقَام».

They do not all have the same role or meaning.

«عَقِمَ», «عَقُمَ», and «عُقِمَ»: reported verbal forms in the reproductive branch, concerning infertility or not conceiving; lexicons record differing vocalizations.

«عُقْم» and «عَقْم»: infertility and not producing children.

«عَقِيم»: someone or something that does not produce offspring or an expected yield.

«عَقِيمَة»: a feminine form recorded in some lexical uses.

«مَعْقُوم»: a person or animal described as infertile; extending it to every impaired function requires evidence for that particular usage.

«عُقَمَاء» and «عَقَائِم»: plural forms in the infertility branch.

«عَقَام» and «عُقَام»: in some uses infertile; elsewhere, severe illness or war.

«تَعْقِيم»: a modern term used for reproductive sterilization and, in another technical field, sterilization of materials; these specialist meanings should be kept distinct.

«مَعَاقِم»: bodily joints and connections in older usage.

«اعْتِقَام»: digging a small shaft within a well or continuing downward; also entering upon a matter, and in other recorded uses, overcoming.

Shared consonants do not require one equivalent for every form.

Quranic ʿaqīm and modern sterilization

Modern reproductive terminology can use sterilization for an intervention intended to remove fertility.

In hygiene, the term is also used for microbial sterilization; it is not an exact synonym of every disinfection procedure.

Neither specialist sense defines Quranic «عَقِيم».

In the Quran, the adjective describes absent offspring or an expected yield.

It does not itself specify surgery, medication, injury, or another procedure.

Modern microbial sterilization should not be read into the verses.

The Quranic form

The Quran uses only the adjective «عَقِيم» from this root.

It occurs four times in four verses:

  • «عَقِيمٍ» in Al-Ḥajj 22:55, describing a day.
  • «عَقِيمًا» in Al-Shūrā 42:50, describing a person without offspring.
  • «عَقِيمٌ» in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, describing Abraham's wife.
  • «الْعَقِيمَ» in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41, describing wind.

These are one word, differing in definiteness and grammatical case.

No verb from this root occurs in the Quran.

The Quranic study should therefore focus on the adjective, without presenting other lexical derivatives or modern terms as Quranic forms.

4. Infertility among differing reproductive circumstances

Al-Shūrā provides a direct bodily use of ʿaqīm.

The preceding verse says:

لِلَّهِ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۚ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ ۚ يَهَبُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ إِنَاثًا وَيَهَبُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ الذُّكُورَ

li-Allāhi mulku al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi yakhluqu mā yashāʾu yahabu li-man yashāʾu ināthan wa-yahabu li-man yashāʾu al-dhukūr

Al-Shūrā 42:49

Al-Shūrā 42:49

“To The God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. He creates what He wills; He grants daughters to whom He wills and grants sons to whom He wills.”

It continues:

أَوْ يُزَوِّجُهُمْ ذُكْرَانًا وَإِنَاثًا ۖ وَيَجْعَلُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ عَقِيمًا ۚ إِنَّهُ عَلِيمٌ قَدِيرٌ

aw yuzawwijuhum dhukrānan wa-ināthan wa-yajʿalu man yashāʾu ʿaqīman innahu ʿalīmun qadīr

Al-Shūrā 42:50

Al-Shūrā 42:50

“Or He gives them sons and daughters together, and makes whom He wills infertile. Indeed, He is Knowing, Capable.”

The two verses describe different circumstances of having children.

One person may receive daughters, another sons, another both.

Another may have no children.

Here «عَقِيمًا» means infertile: someone who does not produce children.

Is it restricted to women?

The verse says «مَنْ يَشَاءُ».

«مَنْ» refers to a person and does not inherently restrict the referent to either sex.

The adjective should not therefore be limited to infertile women here.

Both «رَجُلٌ عَقِيمٌ» and «امْرَأَةٌ عَقِيمٌ» are established Arabic expressions.

It can concern a man or woman.

A couple's childlessness does not by itself establish which partner or circumstances are involved.

The word alone identifies neither the medical cause nor a responsible person.

Does infertility imply lesser human worth?

Having and not having children are set alongside each other within creation and divine will.

The passage does not say that parents are morally superior or that infertility punishes a sin.

A family may have several children yet neglect their upbringing and rights.

Another may have none yet contribute substantially through service, education, assistance, and good work.

The Quran connects moral worth with faith, reverence, and righteous action, not the number of children.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 identifies those most mindful of The God as most honored before Him.

ʿAqīm should not become a label for humiliating a childless person.

It describes reproduction, not human dignity or worth.

Does it necessarily mean permanent infertility?

Ordinarily it describes someone who does not produce children.

The description alone does not establish a cause or whether circumstances can change.

A description at a particular time is not by itself a judgment about the whole future.

Nor does that mean the condition must change.

Neither outcome follows conclusively from the word's form alone.

Al-Shūrā 42:50 concerns differing reproductive circumstances, not medical mechanisms or duration.

“Infertile” is appropriate; adding “forever” requires independent evidence.

5. An elderly, infertile woman and the announcement of a child

The other human occurrence belongs to Abraham's account.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28, the divine envoys announce a knowledgeable boy to Abraham.

The Quran then says:

فَأَقْبَلَتِ امْرَأَتُهُ فِي صَرَّةٍ فَصَكَّتْ وَجْهَهَا وَقَالَتْ عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ

fa-aqbalati imraʾatuhu fī ṣarratin fa-ṣakkat wajhahā wa-qālat ʿajūzun ʿaqīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

“His wife approached with a cry, struck her face, and said: An elderly, infertile woman!”

Two descriptions appear together:

«عَجُوزٌ» and «عَقِيمٌ».

The first concerns old age; the second infertility.

They should not be merged.

Old age and infertility are distinct lexical descriptions; neither word alone supplies every detail of the other condition.

Abraham's wife applies both to herself here.

Why both descriptions?

The announcement surprised her: she was elderly and understood herself to be infertile.

Hūd 11:72 records a related statement:

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا ۖ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عَجِيبٌ

qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿajīb

Hūd 11:72

Hūd 11:72

“She said: How astonishing! Shall I bear a child when I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man? This is indeed astonishing.”

This verse foregrounds both spouses' old age.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 also has her call herself «عقيم».

Infertility and age are thus distinct features that occur together in their circumstances.

ʿAqīm alone does not specify advanced age, nor does ʿajūz lexically mean infertile.

The reply to Abraham's wife

The next verse says:

قَالُوا كَذَٰلِكِ قَالَ رَبُّكِ ۖ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْحَكِيمُ الْعَلِيمُ

qālū kadhāliki qāla rabbuki innahu huwa al-ḥakīmu al-ʿalīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:30

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:30

“They said: Thus has your Lord said. Indeed, He is the Wise, the Knowing.”

The reply rests on the Lord's declaration, knowledge, and wisdom.

Her known condition will not prevent the announced gift.

The verse does not explain its bodily mechanism.

No particular uterine obstruction or bodily change can be inferred from this sentence alone.

Such details require separate evidence.

The word means infertile; childbearing contrary to expectation is learned from the account as a whole.

Comparison with ʿāqir in Zechariah's account

The Quran describes Zechariah's wife with «عَاقِر».

Āl ʿImrān 3:40 says «وَامْرَأَتِي عَاقِرٌ».

Maryam 19:5 and Maryam 19:8 use the same adjective in the accusative.

ʿĀqir belongs to ʿ Q R; ʿaqīm to ʿ Q M.

Both can describe human infertility.

That overlap does not merge the roots.

The choice of two words alone does not establish distinct medical types for the two women.

Such a medical or historical classification needs independent evidence.

Both are described as infertile in accounts announcing a child; each lexical family still merits separate study.

6. Wind without a beneficial yield

Another Quranic occurrence describes wind:

وَفِي عَادٍ إِذْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ

wa-fī ʿādin idh arsalnā ʿalayhimu al-rīḥa al-ʿaqīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41

“And in ʿĀd, when We sent upon them the barren wind.”

«الْعَقِيمَ» qualifies «الرِّيحَ».

Wind does not bear children like a human or animal.

The infertility image is transferred to a different subject.

Arabic uses barren wind for wind bringing no rain or beneficial yield, in contrast with rain-bearing or fertilizing winds.

The retained image is failure to produce the expected yield.

Fertilizing winds

Al-Ḥijr says:

وَأَرْسَلْنَا الرِّيَاحَ لَوَاقِحَ فَأَنْزَلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَسْقَيْنَاكُمُوهُ وَمَا أَنْتُمْ لَهُ بِخَازِنِينَ

wa-arsalnā al-riyāḥa lawāqiḥa fa-anzalnā mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-asqaynākumūhu wa-mā antum lahu bi-khāzinīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:22

Al-Ḥijr 15:22

“We sent the winds fertilizing, then sent down water from the sky and gave it to you to drink; you are not its keepers.”

«لَوَاقِحَ» belongs to L Q Ḥ and concerns fertility and yielding an outcome.

It contrasts with «الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ».

The winds described as lawāqiḥ are associated with fertility and rain, while the wind sent against ʿĀd is called ʿaqīm.

The Quran does not describe all winds alike.

This contrast does not scientifically establish that barren wind has absolutely no effect on pollen, clouds, or movement of materials.

Here it describes wind that brings ʿĀd no beneficial yield.

An account of every physical property goes beyond the word's lexical meaning.

Is it merely rainless wind?

The next verse adds:

مَا تَذَرُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ إِلَّا جَعَلَتْهُ كَالرَّمِيمِ

mā tadharu min shayʾin atat ʿalayhi illā jaʿalathu ka-al-ramīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42

“It left nothing it came upon without making it like decayed remains.”

The wind is not merely rainless; it has destructive power in ʿĀd's account.

That destruction is explicit in the actions and imagery of Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42.

ʿAqīm foregrounds unfruitfulness and absence of beneficial fertility or yield.

One wind may bring useful rain.

Another may bring none but move dust.

A third may damage houses, trees, and other objects.

These situations differ.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:42 both name the wind barren and describe its destruction.

Rendering the adjective alone as “devastating storm” is inadequate because part of that interpretation comes from the following verse.

A literal Persian “infertile wind” may also sound unfamiliar without explanation of the metaphor.

“Unfruitful wind” makes the sense clearer.

Does it name a wind direction?

Some early reports identify it with a directional wind such as the west wind.

The verse itself names no geographical direction.

A direction should therefore not enter the lexical definition of ʿaqīm.

Other verses on ʿĀd use descriptions such as «صَرْصَر» and «عَاتِيَة».

They express other features and belong to their own roots.

Here ʿaqīm concerns absence of beneficial yield; intensity and destruction follow from the wider passages.

7. The punishment of a barren day

Al-Ḥajj contains the remaining occurrence:

وَلَا يَزَالُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي مِرْيَةٍ مِنْهُ حَتَّىٰ تَأْتِيَهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً أَوْ يَأْتِيَهُمْ عَذَابُ يَوْمٍ عَقِيمٍ

wa-lā yazālu alladhīna kafarū fī miryatin minhu ḥattā taʾtiyahumu al-sāʿatu baghtatan aw yaʾtiyahum ʿadhābu yawmin ʿaqīm

Al-Ḥajj 22:55

Al-Ḥajj 22:55

“Those who disbelieved will remain in doubt about it until the Hour comes upon them suddenly, or the punishment of a barren day comes upon them.”

«عَقِيمٍ» qualifies «يَوْمٍ».

A day does not bear offspring; the image is again transferred to another subject.

A barren day can mean a harsh, unfruitful day bringing the people concerned no benefit or relief.

Here the adjective accompanies punishment.

This is not an ordinary unproductive working day; punishment reaches them.

A day without a following night?

Some early lexical and interpretive explanations identify it with the Day of Resurrection, without another day and night in the ordinary earthly sequence.

Others identify it with a severe battle day, especially Badr, bringing the defeated group no benefit or relief.

Both explanations draw on absent fruitfulness or a favorable sequel.

Neither referent should simply be made the word's fixed lexical meaning.

Al-Ḥajj 22:55 places two expressions together:

تَأْتِيَهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً

taʾtiyahumu al-sāʿatu baghtah

Al-Ḥajj 22:55

And:

يَأْتِيَهُمْ عَذَابُ يَوْمٍ عَقِيمٍ

yaʾtiyahum ʿadhābu yawmin ʿaqīm

Al-Ḥajj 22:55

They are joined by «أَوْ».

The wording distinguishes the Hour's sudden arrival from the arrival of a barren day's punishment.

The conjunction alone does not settle the referent. Some interpreters distinguish an earthly day from the Hour; others distinguish the Hour's arrival from its punishment while taking the day as the Resurrection.

Nor does the wording alone prove a particular historical battle.

The verse names no battle, location, or date.

Unfruitfulness here

For humans, ʿaqīm concerns not producing children.

For wind, it concerns absence of a beneficial yield.

For a day, the comparable image is failure to bring the people concerned benefit or relief.

Someone might repeatedly be warned about a truth yet refuse to examine it.

When confronted with the outcome of that conduct, the earlier situation may no longer be recoverable.

That day can then be harsh and unfruitful for that person.

This illustration fits the verse's description of people remaining in doubt until a decisive event reaches them.

It does not imply that all human beings are deprived of every good on that day.

The verse concerns those who disbelieved and are reached by punishment.

Al-Ḥajj 22:56 describes divine judgment and the faithful's outcome; Al-Ḥajj 22:57 states the outcome of those who disbelieved and denied the signs.

Unfruitfulness here relates to punishment and the group concerned, not a denial of every benefit for every being.

8. Infertility, barren wind, and a barren day

The four occurrences concern three settings:

Human reproduction.

Wind and its environmental effects.

A day and its consequences for people.

The two human occurrences directly mean infertility.

Wind and day use an extended sense.

A shared image is failure to yield an expected outcome.

For humans, that outcome is offspring.

For wind, beneficial yield can include fertility and rain.

For the day, the people concerned receive punishment rather than benefit and relief.

The semantic connection should not be stretched without limits.

A day without financial success is not automatically a barren day in this Quranic sense.

Nor does every rainless wind have the destructive character of the wind sent against ʿĀd.

The shared meaning and particular features of each instance should remain distinct.

Does it always mean complete destruction?

Neither human occurrence means the person is dead.

An infertile woman may possess other bodily and human capacities.

A barren wind may be powerful: absence of one beneficial function does not mean absence of all force or effects.

In ʿĀd's account, it has destructive power.

A barren day is not devoid of events; it brings the relevant people no benefit or relief in the context of punishment.

Thus being barren does not mean being absolutely ineffective, but lacking a particular kind of yield or outcome.

This distinction matters for translation and lexical study.

9. Comparison with related roots

ʿ Q M and ʿ Q R

The roots share their first two consonants and have closely related human senses.

Both ʿaqīm and ʿāqir can describe a woman or man without offspring.

Quranic ʿaqīm describes Abraham's wife and, in Al-Shūrā 42:50, people without children.

ʿĀqir describes Zechariah's wife.

ʿ Q R also has a branch concerning wounding and hamstringing animals.

Quranic ʿ Q M concerns infertility and barren wind and day.

They overlap in infertility without forming one lexical family.

One should not assert, without evidence, that one always means congenital infertility and the other an acquired condition.

ʿ Q M and L Q Ḥ

L Q Ḥ concerns fertility, pregnancy, and conditions for reproduction.

Al-Ḥijr 15:22 uses «الرِّيَاحَ لَوَاقِحَ».

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41 uses «الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ».

Wind can thus be described in relation to fertility or absence of beneficial yield.

The roots are not simply labels for good and bad wind.

Laqāḥ concerns fertility and ʿuqm infertility; each wind's value and consequences follow from its features and context.

ʿ Q M and J D B

Jadb concerns dry land and lack of rain or yield.

Barren wind can likewise be associated with absent rain and benefit.

The words are not identical.

Jadb can describe the land or a dry year; ʿaqīm's principal branch concerns infertility and absent offspring or yield.

Barren wind may contribute to dry, unproductive surroundings, but wind and the land's condition are different subjects.

ʿ Q M and J R Z

J R Z includes vegetation-free land, where plant cover has disappeared or nothing grows.

Al-Kahf 18:8 uses «صَعِيدًا جُرُزًا».

Quranic ʿaqīm describes people, wind, and a day, not land.

Vegetation-free land may relate to lack of agricultural yield, but that does not make juruz and ʿaqīm synonymous everywhere.

ʿ Q M and Q Ṭ ʿ

Q Ṭ ʿ concerns cutting and separation.

Some explanations associate ʿuqm with interruption of a connection or process.

Quranic ʿaqīm does not mean cutting a physical object.

An infertile woman does not necessarily have a cut in a particular organ.

Nor is a barren wind necessarily a wind that cuts something.

Interrupted yield or continuity can help explain some uses without defining every derivative directly.

ʿ Q M and Ṣ R Ṣ R

Ṣarṣar describes wind in the Quranic account of ʿĀd.

It belongs to another root and concerns the wind's severe, afflicting character.

ʿAqīm in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41 belongs to the same account but supplies another description.

One wind can be intense and destructive as well as barren in beneficial yield.

Intensity should not be inserted into ʿaqīm so that the differing descriptions collapse.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

All occurrences use ʿaqīm, but the equivalent should fit each subject.

«عَقِيمًا» — Al-Shūrā 42:50

Root: ʿ Q M

Word type: An adjective on «فَعِيل», accusative in «يَجْعَلُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ عَقِيمًا».

Brief meaning: Infertility and failure to produce offspring or an expected yield.

Meaning here: A person who does not produce children.

Proposed translation:

وَيَجْعَلُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ عَقِيمًا

wa-yajʿalu man yashāʾu ʿaqīmā

Al-Shūrā 42:50

“And makes whom He wills infertile.”

This includes men and women without adding a sex restriction.

«عَقِيمٌ» — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

Word type: Indefinite nominative adjective qualifying «عَجُوزٌ».

Brief meaning: Infertility and not producing children.

Meaning here: A woman who does not bear children.

Proposed translation:

عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ

ʿajūzun ʿaqīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

“An elderly, infertile woman!”

Age and infertility remain separate descriptions.

«الْعَقِيمَ» — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41

Word type: Definite accusative adjective qualifying «الرِّيحَ».

Brief meaning: Infertility; failure to bring expected fertility or yield.

Meaning here: Wind that brings no expected beneficial fertility or yield.

Proposed translation:

الرِّيحَ الْعَقِيمَ

al-rīḥa al-ʿaqīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41

“The barren wind” or “the unfruitful wind.”

In Persian, “unfruitful wind” is more immediately clear, while “barren wind” preserves the original image and can be explained briefly.

The direct translation should not be restricted to “devastating storm”; destruction is explicit in the next verse.

«عَقِيمٍ» — Al-Ḥajj 22:55

Word type: Indefinite genitive adjective qualifying «يَوْمٍ».

Brief meaning: Infertility, unfruitfulness, and absence of beneficial yield.

Meaning here: A harsh, barren day bringing the relevant group no benefit or relief.

Proposed translation:

عَذَابُ يَوْمٍ عَقِيمٍ

ʿadhābu yawmin ʿaqīm

Al-Ḥajj 22:55

“The punishment of a barren day.”

This proposal retains the image of unfruitfulness; it does not by itself alter the installed verse translation.

“Barren day” preserves the broader adjective without necessarily identifying a battle or specifying all details of the Resurrection.

Brief meaning for Forqan word boxes

A summary can be:

“Infertility and failure to produce offspring or an expected yield; in other branches, severity and constriction.”

The contextual meaning should be separate:

In Al-Shūrā 42:50, an infertile person.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, an infertile woman.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41, a barren, unfruitful wind.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:55, a harsh, barren day.

One adjective can preserve a semantic connection while taking different applications with different subjects.

Conclusion

The root concerns infertility, absent reproduction, severity, and lack of an expected yield.

Human ʿaqīm describes a woman or man without offspring. It does not determine cause, duration, or human worth.

Other branches include severe war, intractable illness, constriction, joints, digging, and patterned cloth. They should not be reduced to one historical origin without evidence.

The Quran uses only ʿaqīm.

Al-Shūrā 42:50 places infertility alongside differing reproductive circumstances.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, Abraham's wife describes herself as elderly and infertile after the announcement of a child.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41, the wind does not bring expected beneficial fertility; the next verse describes its destruction.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:55, the day brings punishment to the group concerned, without benefit or relief for them.

Barrenness does not mean absence of every capacity or effect. An infertile woman has other capacities; barren wind can destroy; a barren day can contain many events without bringing the people concerned benefit.

The shared Quranic image is infertility and absence of an expected yield: offspring for people, beneficial fertility for wind, and benefit or relief for those facing the day of punishment.

“Infertile” for people, “barren wind” for Al-Dhāriyāt 51:41, and “barren day” for Al-Ḥajj 22:55 preserve that connection without importing unstated medical causes, physical mechanisms, or historical and eschatological details.
