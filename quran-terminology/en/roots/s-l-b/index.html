The Arabic root S L B concerns taking or snatching something from another, especially swiftly, through dominance, or against the holder's will. Salaba means “snatched” or “took from someone”; salb names the act, while salab can name what is taken. Maslūb describes someone or something subjected to such taking.

This root has been used for taking clothing, weapons, property, and other things. Extended uses concern deprivation of reason, ability, loved ones, or resources. Salūb describes a mother that has lost her young; shajaratun salīb describes a tree stripped of leaves and branches. This family also describes palms without fruit.

The Quran uses the root only once, as yaslubhumu in Al-Ḥajj 22:73. If a fly snatches something from the false deities, the verse says, they cannot retrieve it from the fly.

The example questions the creative capacity and real power of those invoked apart from The God.

It is important to distinguish ordinary taking, snatching something from another, and retrieving it. Lexical meaning must also be distinguished from ethical or legal judgment. Whether an act is theft, usurpation, a wartime action, or a natural deprivation depends on its circumstances and context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Salb: Taking swiftly or through dominance

Arabic lexicons define al-salb through taking swiftly and snatching something away.

One lexical definition of this family states:

«السِّينُ وَاللَّامُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ أَخْذُ الشَّيْءِ بِخِفَّةٍ وَاخْتِطَافٍ»

al-sīnu wa-al-lāmu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa akhdhu al-shayʾi bi-khiffatin wa-ikhtiṭāf

“Sīn, lām, and bāʾ have one central sense: taking something deftly and snatching it away.”

This highlights taking something and a manner of taking involving speed or snatching.

Salabahu thawbahu means taking or snatching someone's garment from them.

The construction identifies both the person deprived and the thing removed from their possession.

Salb is therefore more than an object's changing location. It relates taking to deprivation.

Moving one's own clothing from one place to another is not, merely as movement, salb. Taking another's garment against their wishes can be described by it.

Some uses emphasize force and dominance; others emphasize speed and sudden snatching.

Not every salb necessarily involves blows, physical violence, or overt resistance.

Something may be deftly taken from another's control without a direct physical struggle.

A defensible basic definition is therefore:

Taking or snatching something from another so that it leaves the previous holder's control or reach.

Speed, dominance, and compulsion are familiar features of such taking, but their degree and form depend on context.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Taʾabbaṭa Sharran says:

سَلَبْتَ سِلَاحِي بَائِسًا وَشَتَمْتَنِي
فَيَا خَيْرَ مَسْلُوبٍ وَيَا شَرَّ سَالِبِ

salabta silāḥī bāʾisan wa-shatamtanī / fa-yā khayra maslūbin wa-yā sharra sālib

“You took my weapons while I was distressed and helpless, and insulted me; what a worthy person was despoiled, and what an evil despoiler!”

Salabta expresses taking and snatching.

Silāḥī identifies what was taken from the poet: his weapons.

Maslūb describes the person subjected to taking; sālib identifies the one who takes.

The witness clearly presents three elements: the taker, the person deprived, and the object taken.

The object is a weapon, a warrior's means of defense and exercise of power.

Losing a weapon can leave someone vulnerable, but vulnerability is not part of salb's direct meaning. It follows from the object and the person's situation.

“The best of those despoiled” and “the worst of despoilers” also judge the two parties. The act is condemned here, but that evaluation comes from the poet's complete statement.

The verse appears in the collection attributed to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran and in early grammatical works. The reading and grammatical analysis of bāʾisan vary, but salabta silāḥī and the contrast between maslūb and sālib remain clear in its familiar transmission.

Taʾabbaṭa Sharran is a pre-Islamic poet. Attribution to him alone does not conclusively date the composition of this particular verse.

The witness strongly supports the usage within poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period without establishing when the root first arose.

Salab: What is taken from a warrior

Early Arabic also uses salab for clothing, weapons, and equipment taken from a defeated or slain combatant.

Here it names the objects taken from their former owner, rather than only the act of taking.

A sword, garment, armor, or other accompanying equipment could fall under this designation.

Salb as the act and salab as what is taken must therefore be distinguished in vocalization and usage, even though both belong to the same family.

The lexical meaning does not itself decide whether taking such property is legitimate.

Who has a right to take another's possessions, or how people's belongings should be treated in war, is an ethical and legal question that this definition alone cannot answer.

The military use should not be generalized to every form. Al-Ḥajj 22:73 describes a fly taking something, not a warrior taking spoils.

Salb and the covering of trees

Lexicons record al-salab for bark or covering removed from trees.

Shajaratun salīb describes a tree stripped of its leaves and branches. Related forms also describe fruitless palms; these specific uses should be distinguished.

Something formerly belonging to the tree's natural covering or growth is now absent.

The connection with taking and deprivation is intelligible: the tree no longer has its earlier leaves or branches.

This does not mean that a person or creature always forcibly snatched them away.

Leaves may fall because of seasonal change or other natural causes.

Extended uses can thus emphasize the resulting loss even without a human taker.

Salūb: A mother that has lost her young

Nāqatun salūb specifically means a she-camel that has lost her young. Salūb is also recorded of a woman who has lost her child; nāqah itself does not mean every female animal.

The loss may involve the young dying or being delivered before full development.

Aslabat al-nāqatu is also recorded for such a condition.

Lexicons record a comparable use for a bereaved woman.

The sense extends from physically taking an object to deprivation of a child or loss of a loved being.

It emphasizes loss and deprivation without determining the cause of death, personal responsibility, or whether the event was intentional.

Maslūb al-ʿaql: Loss of judgment

Salabahu ʿaqlahu and maslūb al-ʿaql are also known Arabic expressions.

Reason is not a physical object someone can remove by hand.

The meaning is that understanding, discernment, or judgment has been taken away or impaired.

Salb can therefore concern nonmaterial human capacities.

This does not make every lapse of concentration, heedlessness, or error of judgment a deprivation of reason.

Each state must be assessed according to its actual degree and context.

Technical logic and philosophy also use salb opposite ījāb for negation or denying a predication. This specialized extension should not be imported into Quranic yaslubhumu without independent historical evidence.

2. Quranic forms and derivatives

The root occurs only once in the Quran, in Al-Ḥajj 22:73.

Its Quranic form is:

Yaslubhumu

It is formed from the basic triliteral verb salaba yaslubu.

Yaslub is a third-person masculine singular imperfect verb, made jussive by conditional in.

The pronoun hum refers to those from whom the fly takes something.

Salaba can take two objects: the person or entity deprived, and the thing taken.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:73, hum is the first object and shayʾan the second.

Al-dhubāb is the subject.

The construction therefore identifies the fly as the taker and something as being taken from the deities in question.

Arabic has other forms of this family:

Salaba: Took or snatched something from another.

Salb: The act of taking or snatching. Salab: What is taken, such as clothing and weapons; salab is also recorded as a verbal noun in some lexical accounts.

Sālib: Active participle; the taker or snatcher.

Maslūb: Passive participle; someone or something subjected to taking.

Salīb: Deprived; someone or something stripped of what they had.

Istalaba: Snatched or swiftly took from another.

Istilāb: Snatching or sudden taking.

Sallāb: Someone who takes from others frequently or repeatedly.

Salūb and sālib: In certain uses, a female animal that has lost its young.

Tasallabat: In mourning contexts, a woman wearing mourning clothes and setting aside adornment.

These exact forms do not occur in the Quran. Salaba is nevertheless the citation form of the same basic verb whose imperfect yaslub occurs there; they should not be treated as unrelated morphological patterns.

The Quran has only yaslubhumu, whose meaning must be examined within its conditional construction and context.

3. Wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan: A fly taking something

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ ضُرِبَ مَثَلٌ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ لَنْ يَخْلُقُوا ذُبَابًا وَلَوِ اجْتَمَعُوا لَهُ وَإِنْ يَسْلُبْهُمُ الذُّبَابُ شَيْئًا لَا يَسْتَنْقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ ضَعُفَ الطَّالِبُ وَالْمَطْلُوبُ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ḍuriba mathalun fa-istamiʿū lahu inna alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāhi lan yakhluqū dhubāban wa-lawi ijtamaʿū lahu wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan lā yastanqidhūhu minhu ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-al-maṭlūb

Al-Ḥajj 22:73

“People, an example has been set forth, so listen to it. Those you invoke apart from The God will never create a fly, even if they gather together for it. If a fly snatches something from them, they cannot retrieve it from it. Weak are both the seeker and the sought.”

Salb occurs here within a clear, tangible example.

The example contrasts the Creator's real power with the inability of those invoked apart from The God.

The Quran places two incapacities together.

First, they cannot create even a fly.

Second, they cannot retrieve anything that the same fly might take from them.

A fly is small and familiar, yet even this little creature can take something the false deities cannot recover.

The meaning of yaslubhumu

In wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan, salb means snatching something from another.

Al-dhubāb is the subject: the fly performs the taking.

Hum identifies those from whom something is taken.

Shayʾan identifies what is taken.

The direct meaning is clear:

“If a fly snatches something from them.”

This accords with the early meaning of taking and snatching.

The verb does not imply that a fly makes human judgments about ownership or whether taking is rightful.

Salb describes the fly's taking, without assigning it the ethical or legal responsibility of a human thief.

Likewise, “snatching” can describe animals and natural events without implying human criminal intent.

What does the fly take?

The verse uses shayʾan.

It is indefinite and names no particular substance or possession.

Historical and exegetical explanations mention food, perfume, and fragrant substances placed on idols, from which flies took something.

Such examples fit the setting and familiar behavior of flies.

Food or a sweet substance might, for example, rest on a statue or object receiving offerings, and a fly might take some.

The Quran itself does not specify honey, food, oil, or perfume here.

Shayʾan should therefore not be restricted in translation to any one of these examples.

“Something” is the most precise equivalent.

The indefinite word also preserves the example's breadth: the false deities cannot retrieve what a fly has taken from them.

The verse's conditional construction

The clause begins with in.

This conditional particle makes yaslub jussive.

This part of the verse thus presents a hypothetical situation:

If a fly takes something from them, they cannot recover it.

The construction does not report one particular event happening at that moment.

Their inability in the hypothetical situation is nevertheless part of the verse's argument.

Translation should preserve the condition, rather than turn it into the report “a fly took something from them.”

Taking and inability to retrieve

Yaslubhumu is followed by:

«لَا يَسْتَنْقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ».

lā yastanqidhūhu minhu

Al-Ḥajj 22:73

They cannot retrieve it from the fly.

Yastanqidhūhu belongs to N Q DH and concerns rescuing or extracting something from a situation of confinement or lost access.

Here it means recovering what the fly took.

The two actions are distinct.

Salb describes taking or snatching something away.

Istinqādh here concerns recovering and freeing it from the taker's control. Form X does not inherently restrict it to an attempt without success; the sentence negates their ability to recover it.

The two verbs point in opposite directions within one relationship.

The fly takes something; the false deities cannot recover it.

The question is therefore not only preventing the taking, but also remedying it and restoring what was taken.

The weakness of seeker and sought

The verse ends:

«ضَعُفَ الطَّالِبُ وَالْمَطْلُوبُ».

ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-al-maṭlūb

Al-Ḥajj 22:73

Ṭālib means seeker or requester; maṭlūb is whoever or whatever is sought or requested.

Several interpretations of their referents have been proposed.

In a reading closely connected to the immediate context, the seeker is the powerless deity expected to recover what the fly took, and the sought is the fly from which it is demanded.

Another reading identifies the seeker as the worshipper asking something of the false deity, and the sought as that deity.

Both can be considered linguistically; the immediate connection with the fly's taking and the inability to retrieve supports the first reading.

The basic translation can nevertheless retain “seeker and sought,” leaving identification to contextual explanation.

The central message remains clear: something unable to create a tiny creature or recover what was taken from it cannot occupy the position of independent divine power.

4. Salb in Quranic relationships: Taking, ability, and assigning equals

Taking something is different from creating it

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 places two important verbs together:

Yakhluqū and yaslubhumu.

Khalq here concerns creation.

Salb concerns taking or snatching something from another.

Their meanings differ.

Creating a being and taking something already present are different acts.

A person may move, pick up, or reshape something; such limited ability does not establish independent creative power or lordship.

The verse does not express the false deities' inability only through their failure to create: even recovering something from a fly is beyond them.

The example therefore challenges claims of their divine power from two directions.

The verse within Al-Ḥajj

Al-Ḥajj 22:71 describes worshipping things apart from The God for which no authority has been sent down and about which the worshippers have no knowledge.

Al-Ḥajj 22:72 describes some deniers' hostile response to clear verses.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 then presents the example of the false deities' inability.

Al-Ḥajj 22:74 says:

مَا قَدَرُوا اللَّهَ حَقَّ قَدْرِهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَقَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

mā qadarū Allāha ḥaqqa qadrihi inna Allāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz

Al-Ḥajj 22:74

“They have not esteemed The God as He deserves. Indeed, The God is Strong, Mighty.”

This sequence shows that the fly example is not merely identifying a zoological characteristic.

The main issue is recognizing the Creator's true standing and the inability of something assigned divine status.

On one side are deities unable to create a fly or recover something from it.

On the other, The God is described as qawiyyun ʿazīz.

The false deities' weakness is set against the strength and invincibility of The God.

Is the verse only about stone idols?

Manufactured idols clearly fit the example, since they cannot possess creative power or independent authority.

The verse nevertheless begins yā ayyuhā al-nās: people.

Alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāh likewise refers to those invoked apart from The God.

The message should therefore not be confined without evidence to one community or particular form of idolatry.

Conceptually, any claim assigning independent divine power to a being or authority must be examined for its truth.

This does not mean every respect for a person, request for help, or following of an authority automatically constitutes worship of false deities.

Using creatures' limited capacities must be distinguished from assigning them divine status; the distinction concerns what is believed about their power and position, not merely their role in a person's life.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 concerns the power and position attributed to other deities, rather than denying every limited capacity of created beings.

Is every salb an injustice?

The Quranic occurrence attributes salb to a fly.

The fly takes something in the verse's language, but this does not confer human moral responsibility on it.

Arabic also uses the family for a tree losing leaves, an animal losing its young, or a person losing a capacity.

The root does not therefore mean injustice in every use.

When a person wrongfully deprives another of property, freedom, or a legitimate opportunity, their conduct may be oppressive.

The condemnation follows from the person's rights, the manner of taking, and the circumstances, not merely the presence of S L B.

This distinction preserves lexical meaning while allowing ethical judgment to rest on evidence.

5. Comparison with related roots

S L B and ʾ KH DH: Snatching and taking

Akhdh concerns taking and has a wide semantic range.

Someone may receive a gift, take another's hand, pick something up with its owner's permission, or receive money.

The verb akhadha alone makes none of these actions salb or theft.

Salb primarily highlights snatching and removing something from its previous holder's control.

Every salb is a kind of taking, but not every taking is salb.

Yaslubhumu in Al-Ḥajj 22:73 gives a more pointed image of taking something away from the false deities than a general verb for taking.

S L B and S R Q: Snatching and covert theft

Saraqa concerns secretly taking another's property.

The concealed manner of taking is important in the clear sense of theft.

Salb concerns snatching and removing something from another's control, without necessarily requiring concealment.

Someone may snatch an object in full view of its owner. That can be salb without secrecy being its defining feature.

Covert theft may also occur quickly, but concealment plays an important role in identifying it.

The two roots can overlap in particular acts without being complete synonyms.

Yaslubhumu al-dhubāb likewise does not mean a fly commits the human crime of theft.

S L B and GH Ṣ B: Snatching and wrongful seizure

Ghaṣb concerns taking possession by force and without right.

Al-Kahf 18:79 describes a king forcibly taking people's ships through ghaṣban.

Salb can also involve force and dominance, but lexically focuses on snatching and taking from another.

The legal use of ghaṣb more explicitly highlights wrongful possession.

Both may describe one act, but not every salb should be assigned the legal category of ghaṣb without examining its circumstances.

S L B and KH Ṭ F: Snatching and sudden seizure

KH Ṭ F concerns swift, sudden snatching.

Al-Ḥajj 22:31 compares the one who assigns partners to The God to someone falling from the sky and being snatched by birds; it is not a generic description of a bird taking an unspecified object.

The image highlights the speed and suddenness of the taking.

Salb can likewise involve swift snatching, and one lexical definition explains it through ikhtiṭāf.

Its meaning is not limited to speed in every use.

Deprivation of property or ability may, for example, occur gradually and be described by the family in broader usage.

Khaṭf and salb overlap in snatching, but their differences in application should remain clear.

S L B and S L L: Snatching and drawing out

S L L concerns drawing something out from within something else.

The Quran uses sulālah in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12 and Al-Sajdah 32:8.

Al-Nūr 24:63 uses yatasallalūn for people slipping secretly out of a gathering.

This family highlights drawing out or departing, sometimes gradually or covertly.

Salb concerns taking something from another.

Drawing a sword from its sheath can be expressed through S L L. Snatching someone else's sword against their wishes additionally raises the sense of salb.

Drawing out and taking away from an owner or holder are different aspects.

S L B and N Q DH: Taking away and recovering

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 places these families in a direct relationship.

Yaslubhumu describes snatching something away.

Yastanqidhūhu describes recovering or rescuing it from its situation.

Salb removes something from control; istinqādh concerns restoring it.

The two are not merely alternative synonyms: their actions point in opposite directions within this sentence.

The contrast sharpens the image of the false deities' inability: they cannot create a fly, and if one takes something from them, they cannot recover it.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic Persian equivalent of S L B is “snatch away.”

In some contexts, “take from another,” “take forcibly,” or “remove from someone's control” also fit.

“Snatch” in Al-Ḥajj 22:73 conveys the basic meaning and suits the image of the fly.

For the phrase:

«وَإِنْ يَسْلُبْهُمُ الذُّبَابُ شَيْئًا»

wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan

Al-Ḥajj 22:73

The proposed rendering is:

“If a fly snatches something from them.”

It preserves the condition, subject, object taken, and relationship to those deprived.

“If a fly takes something from them” is also generally correct, but conveys less of the force and imagery of snatching.

“If a fly steals something from them” is intelligible, but may suggest human theft and criminal concealment beyond what the word states.

“Snatches” is preferable in that respect.

Other Arabic expressions can be rendered as follows:

  • Salaba al-shayʾa: Snatched something or took it from another.
  • Salabahu thawbahu: Took his garment from him.
  • Salb: Taking or snatching; salab: what is taken.
  • Sālib: A taker or snatcher.
  • Maslūb: A person deprived of something, or an object taken.
  • Istalaba: Snatched suddenly or took swiftly.
  • Istilāb: Snatching or sudden taking.
  • Salīb: Deprived; someone who has lost property or ability.
  • Nāqatun salūb: A she-camel that has lost her young.
  • Shajaratun salīb: A tree stripped of its leaves and branches.
  • Maslūb al-ʿaql: Someone whose ordinary judgment or awareness has been lost.

Technical salb, contrasted with ījāb, means negating a predication. It should not enter the translation of yaslubhumu.

Salb in this verse also does not mean destroying or annihilating what is taken.

Something may leave someone's control and still exist.

What the fly takes is the object of attempted recovery. The basic act is taking and removing from reach, not necessarily destroying the thing.

Conclusion

S L B concerns taking or snatching something from another, especially swiftly, through dominance, or against the previous holder's will.

Early Arabic applies it to clothes, weapons, and property. The verse attributed to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran contrasts sālib and maslūb in the taking of weapons.

Extended forms describe a tree losing leaves and branches, palms lacking fruit, loss of young, deprivation of ability, and impaired reason.

The root thus extends beyond physically taking an object to deprivation of something formerly present.

It occurs only once in the Quran, in Al-Ḥajj 22:73.

If a fly takes something from the false deities, they cannot retrieve it.

This is part of a broader argument about the inability of those invoked apart from The God.

They can neither create a fly nor recover something that a fly takes from them.

The taking is therefore more than a description of an animal's behavior: it supplies a tangible example exposing the false deities' powerlessness.

The word's lexical meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the verse's whole message. Salb means taking away; inability, creation, and criticism of assigning equals are understood from the complete verse and context.

The final definition is therefore:

S L B denotes taking or snatching something from another so that it leaves the former holder's control or reach. Extended uses can describe loss of property, young, covering, or ability. In Al-Ḥajj 22:73, it directly means a fly taking something away.

The family's distinctive feature is the relationship between taking and deprivation.

Something is taken and the other party no longer possesses it. Whether this happens forcibly, secretly, rightfully, wrongfully, or through a natural event must be determined from context.

For Forqan, “snatches” is the most suitable basic equivalent of yaslubhumu.

The Quranic example draws strength from its simplicity: a tiny creature can take something from what people imagine to be a deity, while that deity cannot recover it.

The image invites scrutiny of the real power of what people worship and invoke. How can something unable to create or even retrieve something from a fly occupy the place of Creator and Lord?

Al-Ḥajj 22:74 completes the argument by recalling the strength and invincibility of The God.
