Edge, margin and threshold: from the rim of a pit and the last sunlight to the danger of falling and the firmness of foundations in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH F W concerns an edge, margin or outer limit. Shafā names a boundary where something ends and another space begins, such as a well’s rim, a pit’s edge or a precipice. Related usages describe approaching an event, nearing an ending, or a small remainder.

Shafā al-biʾr is a well’s rim; shafā al-ḥufrah is a pit’s edge. Someone at the edge has not necessarily crossed it, though they may be very close.

Ashfā ʿalā al-halāk is recorded for approaching destruction or reaching its threshold. A spatial edge thus supplies an image for someone close to an event.

Shafā itself does not always mean danger, destruction or falling. It can name a vessel’s rim, a well’s edge or a phenomenon’s last visible portion. Danger depends on the place, situation and other wording.

The Quran uses this root twice, both as shafā: Āl ʿImrān 3:103 and Al-Tawbah 9:109.

The first depicts people at a fiery pit’s edge whom God rescued. The second depicts a structure founded on an unstable bank’s edge, followed by collapse.

The spatial meaning serves two images: danger and rescue, and an unstable foundation with its outcome.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Shafā: an edge or outer limit

Early lexicons define shafā through ḥarfu kulli shayʾin: the edge of something.

Ḥarf here means a side or outer boundary, not an alphabetic letter.

Consider a well opening at ground level. A person beside it stands at its edge; crossing that boundary may mean falling into the well.

The edge is where the surrounding ground meets the opening.

Shafā therefore names neither the well nor pit itself, but its boundary.

It does not specify depth, size or the stability of surrounding ground.

A well’s rim may be firm and guarded, or a pit’s margin unstable and dangerous. Both have edges.

The root should therefore not be defined as “a dangerous place” or “a place certain to collapse.”

Physically, shafā names an edge or outer limit; its safety or danger depends on the place.

From a spatial edge to a last remainder

Early Arabic also uses shafā for a small remainder.

For example, it can describe the last visible portion of the setting sun.

It is also recorded for the moon’s dwindling visible remainder as it approaches disappearance.

Mā baqiya minhu illā shafan means that only a little remains.

Here the boundary need not be a physical edge; it is the last amount or stage before a condition ends.

When only a little daylight remains, one is at the boundary between day’s end and night’s beginning.

This relates to a limit or ending, but does not restrict every use to small quantity.

Shafā ḥufrah concerns a pit’s edge, not a small amount of the pit.

An early poetic witness: the last sunlight

Al-ʿAjjaj, the well-known first-century AH poet of rajaz, says:

وَمَرْبَإٍ عَالٍ لِمَنْ تَشَرَّفَا
أَشْرَفْتُهُ بِلَا شَفًا أَوْ بِشَفَا

wa-marbaʾin ʿālin li-man tasharrafā / ashraftuhu bi-lā shafan aw bi-shafā

The sense is:

“A high lookout for one who seeks a view: I ascended it when the sun had disappeared, or when only a little remained.”

Marbaʾ means a high lookout.

Bi-lā shafan here describes the sun no longer being visible.

Bi-shafā refers to its last remaining light or visible portion.

Lexical sources transmit variants of the verb and other wording. Another version has awfaytuhu qabla shafan aw bi-shafā.

The readings connect shafā with the small remaining portion around sunset.

Al-ʿAjjaj belongs to the Umayyad period. This is early Arabic after revelation, not pre-Islamic poetry.

This study has not independently established a securely dated pre-Islamic poetic witness for shafā as an edge. Its clear Quranic and lexical usages establish the meaning without proving when it first arose.

A witness attributed to al-ʿAbbas ibn Mirdas

A collection of lexical questions attributed to Nafiʿ ibn al-Azraq and answers attributed to Ibn ʿAbbas assigns this line to al-ʿAbbas ibn Mirdas:

يُكَبُّ عَلَى شَفَا الْأَذْقَانِ كَبًّا
كَمَا زَلَقَ التَّخَتُّمُ عَنْ خُفَافِ

yukabbu ʿalā shafā al-adhqāni kabban / kamā zalaqa al-takhattumu ʿan khufāf

The relevant phrase means: “He is cast face down upon the edges of the chins.” The second hemistich presents a slipping image; because its wording varies, a definitive rendering of its details is not used as evidence.

Shafā al-adhqān concerns the edges of chins in this transmission, within the edge-and-margin field.

Al-ʿAbbas ibn Mirdas lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods.

This line has not been independently confirmed here from his collected poetry, and its wording varies. It should not be treated as securely pre-Islamic evidence.

It records an edge-of-a-body-part usage within a lexical tradition, without proving the line’s date or the expression’s general pre-Islamic circulation.

Approaching an ending or danger

Ashfā ʿalā al-shayʾ is recorded for approaching something or reaching its threshold.

Ashfā ʿalā al-mawt describes someone close to death.

They are not physically at a well’s edge; their condition is pictured as a boundary before another state.

Approaching an event and the event occurring are distinct stages.

Someone near death has not necessarily died and may survive or be rescued.

Likewise, nearing a task’s end does not mean having finished it.

Approach must not be equated with the outcome’s realization.

This matters directly in Āl ʿImrān 3:103: the addressees were at a fiery pit’s edge and were rescued.

Being at shafā does not itself mean falling.

Lexicons sometimes discuss ashfā and related threshold verbs under SH F W and SH F Y together. Their final radical is also disputed in some forms.

Similar verbs should therefore not all be assigned exclusively and certainly to SH F W without examining their structure.

2. Important related forms

SH F W is a final-weak root: its last radical is a weak consonant.

In shafā, the surface ending is alif.

Important related forms include:

Shafā: edge, margin or outer limit; also a small remainder.

Shafawān: the dual, two edges. Its wāw supports classification under SH F W.

Ashfāʾ: a plural meaning edges or margins.

Shufiyy and shifiyy: forms reported alongside the plurals of shafā in some lexical accounts; their spelling and classification are not uniform. Neither occurs in the Quran.

Shafati al-shamsu tashfū: the sun neared setting or only a little remained visible.

Shafā al-hilāl: the crescent appeared. Some lexicons record this appearance sense; its spelling and classification should not be confused with the separate usage for a dwindling remainder.

Ashfā ʿalā al-shayʾ: approached something or reached its threshold. Early accounts explain it with ashrafa ʿalā al-shayʾ and also associate its classification with SH F Y.

Ashfā ʿalā al-halāk: reached the brink of destruction.

Shafah: a lip. Its omitted radical has been analyzed as either wāw or hāʾ.

Shafawāt and shifāh matter in that discussion. The Quranic index places shafah under SH F H, so it should not be called an undisputed SH F W derivative.

Only the noun shafā occurs in the Quran under this root.

The threshold verbs, plurals and solar or lunar usages do not occur there in these forms and senses.

Shafā and shifāʾ also need distinction: their similar appearance does not give them the same Quranic meaning.

3. Āl ʿImrān 3:103: at the edge of a fiery pit

The Quran says:

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا ۚ وَاذْكُرُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذْ كُنْتُمْ أَعْدَاءً فَأَلَّفَ بَيْنَ قُلُوبِكُمْ فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ بِنِعْمَتِهِ إِخْوَانًا وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

wa-iʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli allāhi jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū wa-udhkurū niʿmata allāhi ʿalaykum idh kuntum aʿdāʾan fa-allafa bayna qulūbikum fa-aṣbaḥtum bi-niʿmatihi ikhwānan wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā kadhālika yubayyinu allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tahtadūn

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“Hold fast, all together, to the rope of The God, and do not divide. Remember The God’s favor upon you: when you were enemies, He joined your hearts, and through His favor you became brothers. You were at the edge of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it. Thus The God makes His signs clear to you, so that you may be guided.”

Shafā is combined here with ḥufratin mina al-nār.

Ḥufrah names a pit; al-nār names fire.

Shafā names neither pit nor fire, but the edge.

Standing at the edge versus falling in

The image places the addressees at a fiery pit’s edge.

Someone there has not necessarily fallen in.

They may nevertheless be close to falling: losing support or taking a wrong step could carry them into it.

The image evokes both immediate danger and possible rescue before falling.

The verse continues:

فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا

fa-anqadhakum minhā

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“Then He rescued you from it.”

Anqadha belongs to N Q DH and means rescued or brought out of danger.

The verb confirms that the situation is dangerous in this context.

That danger arises from the complete phrase and the rescue verb, not from shafā always meaning falling or destruction.

Someone led away from a fiery pit before falling can be called rescued even without having entered the fire.

Crossing the edge and falling into it would be a different event.

The verse depicts exposure to danger followed by rescue.

“You were in the fire” would therefore alter the precise meaning of being at its pit’s edge.

Enmity and joined hearts

The preceding clauses help explain the image.

The Quran says:

إِذْ كُنْتُمْ أَعْدَاءً فَأَلَّفَ بَيْنَ قُلُوبِكُمْ

idh kuntum aʿdāʾan fa-allafa bayna qulūbikum

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“When you were enemies, He joined your hearts.”

It then says:

فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ بِنِعْمَتِهِ إِخْوَانًا

fa-aṣbaḥtum bi-niʿmatihi ikhwānan

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“Through His favor you became brothers.”

The verse describes relationships transformed from enmity into fellowship.

Within this context, the fiery brink can evoke the destruction and disastrous outcome toward which enmity and division had brought them.

Long feuds, tribal resentment and bloodshed can imperil a community’s life, security and relationships.

This illustrates a social implication of the image, rather than replacing its reference to the Fire.

The verse should not be reduced to tribal war: it explicitly names a fiery pit, and fire also concerns the hereafter in the Quran.

The familiar exegetical reading concerns rescue from unbelief and the prospect of the Fire. Social destruction through enmity is a contextual implication, not a replacement for that reading or for the phrase’s literal imagery.

Lexically, shafā remains an edge.

Contextually, the edge conveys proximity to grave danger from which God rescued them.

Holding fast and avoiding renewed danger

The verse begins:

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا

wa-iʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli allāhi jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“Hold fast, all together, to the rope of The God, and do not divide.”

The command connects with the reminder of former enmity.

The Quran recalls rescue while calling them to maintain their bond with God and avoid division.

Āl ʿImrān 3:101 speaks of holding fast to God and being guided to a straight path.

Āl ʿImrān 3:102 commands reverence and remaining in submission.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 then addresses collective adherence, avoiding division and remembering rescue.

Āl ʿImrān 3:105 warns against becoming like those who divided and differed after clear signs came.

The image thus sits among verses about preserving guidance and avoiding renewed enmity.

This connection matters for the verse’s message.

People rescued from grave danger may return to their former condition if they resume the conduct that brought them near it.

Shafā itself nevertheless means neither division nor enmity.

Division comes from tafarraqū and enmity from aʿdāʾ. Shafā contributes the boundary image, made dangerous by the surrounding phrase.

4. Al-Tawbah 9:109: building on an unstable bank’s edge

The second occurrence is in Al-Tawbah.

أَفَمَنْ أَسَّسَ بُنْيَانَهُ عَلَىٰ تَقْوَىٰ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٍ خَيْرٌ أَمْ مَنْ أَسَّسَ بُنْيَانَهُ عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ فَانْهَارَ بِهِ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ

a-fa-man assasa bunyānahu ʿalā taqwā mina allāhi wa-riḍwānin khayrun am man assasa bunyānahu ʿalā shafā jurufin hārin fa-inhāra bihi fī nāri jahannama wa-allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“Is someone who founded his building on reverence for The God and His approval better, or someone who founded it on the edge of a crumbling bank, which collapsed with him into the fire of Hell? The God does not guide wrongdoing people.”

Shafā occurs in this phrase:

عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ

ʿalā shafā jurufin hār

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“On the edge of an unstable, crumbling bank.”

Three words must be distinguished.

Shafā is the edge.

Juruf describes an eroded bank, such as land beside a valley or river whose base has been washed away.

Hār describes something unstable and liable to collapse.

The building thus stands at the edge of unstable ground.

Why foundations matter

A building’s stability depends on more than beautiful walls, height or appearance.

If its ground is unstable and undermined, even a solid-looking building may collapse.

A firm foundation offers greater stability.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 uses this contrast.

One structure is founded on reverence and God’s approval.

The other stands on an unstable bank’s edge.

Instability comes from juruf hār, not shafā alone.

Shafā specifies the position of the foundation.

It should not be defined as an edge necessarily collapsing.

An edge may be firm or unstable; here its bank’s instability is expressly described.

The harmful mosque: religious appearance and harmful purpose

The preceding context is decisive.

Al-Tawbah 9:107 describes people taking a mosque for specified purposes:

وَالَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مَسْجِدًا ضِرَارًا وَكُفْرًا وَتَفْرِيقًا بَيْنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-alladhīna ittakhadhū masjidan ḍirāran wa-kufran wa-tafrīqan bayna al-muʾminīn

Al-Tawbah 9:107

“Those who took a mosque for harm, unbelief and division among the believers…”

The verse continues with providing a base for someone who had previously fought God and His Messenger.

It says they will swear they intended only good, while God testifies that they lie.

The issue is therefore more than constructing a building.

The building is outwardly a mosque, but its founders’ purposes contradict their claim to benevolence.

Al-Tawbah 9:108 forbids the Prophet to stand there and identifies a mosque founded on reverence from the first day as more deserving.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 portrays the difference through firm and unstable foundations.

A building’s religious name does not itself establish the rightness of its founders’ purposes or conduct.

A place can have a religious title or attractive appearance yet serve hostility, deception, corruption or illegitimate interests.

Appearance cannot replace the reality of conduct and purpose.

For example, a center supposedly serving people may actually be used for deception, division and exploitation; its good name does not justify that conduct.

This illustrates a contextual application, not a lexical meaning of shafā as deception, hypocrisy or corruption.

Those ideas arise from the preceding verses and the contrasting foundations.

Fa-inhāra bihi: from possible danger to depicted collapse

The verse continues:

فَانْهَارَ بِهِ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ

fa-inhāra bihi fī nāri jahannam

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“Then it collapsed with him into the fire of Hell.”

Inhāra belongs to H W R and concerns collapse.

It does not belong to SH F W.

The sentence moves from an edge-position to collapse.

The distinction matters.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, the addressees are at the pit’s edge and are rescued.

In Al-Tawbah 9:109, the structure is founded at an unstable edge and collapse follows.

Here collapse does not remain merely possible: the verse explicitly depicts it.

Grammatical analyses differ on the pronouns. Collapse may be attributed to the building, edge or bank, and bihi may refer to the structure or its builder.

Across these readings, the image remains an unstable foundation carrying what rests on it, or its owner, toward a destructive end.

The image figuratively expresses the outcome of the preceding people’s conduct and purposes; it need not report an observable earthly building collapse.

Reverence versus hypocrisy as a foundation

Two foundations are contrasted.

First:

عَلَىٰ تَقْوَىٰ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٍ

ʿalā taqwā mina allāhi wa-riḍwān

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“On reverence for God and His approval.”

Second:

عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ

ʿalā shafā jurufin hār

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“On the edge of an unstable bank.”

Reverence and seeking God’s approval are presented as the first basis for conduct.

The second is compared to unstable ground.

The contrast is firmness versus instability, but concerns more than a building’s durability.

The context associates one structure with reverence and goodness, the other with harm, unbelief and division.

Socially and morally, a sound foundation connects with sound purpose and conduct.

This offers a recognizable application to human life.

A person or institution may win trust through publicity, wealth and polished appearance, but outward success does not alter a foundation of deception and violated rights.

An activity founded on trustworthiness, justice and sound intention differs morally from deception even if its initial resources are modest.

The verse does not promise economic success for every good undertaking or immediate material collapse for every corrupt institution.

It expressly mentions the hereafter; collapse into Hell should not be reduced to earthly economic or political failure.

5. Two Quranic images: rescue and collapse

Comparing Āl ʿImrān 3:103 with Al-Tawbah 9:109 clarifies the semantic field.

Both place people or a structure beside great danger.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 says:

وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا

wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“You were at the edge of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it.”

Al-Tawbah 9:109 says:

عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ فَانْهَارَ بِهِ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ

ʿalā shafā jurufin hārin fa-inhāra bihi fī nāri jahannam

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“On the edge of a crumbling bank, which collapsed with him into the fire of Hell.”

The first edge indicates immediate danger, followed by rescue.

The second is the foundation site of a structure involved in collapse.

Shafā itself therefore means neither rescue nor falling.

It means edge in both; the verbs and contexts supply the outcomes.

Anqadha expresses rescue in one.

Inhāra expresses collapse in the other.

The root should not be defined as a stage at which destruction is already certain.

Someone at an edge may fall or be rescued.

Nor does it mean “the threshold of rescue,” since the second image ends in collapse.

Both depict the boundary of a dangerous situation; the realization of rescue or collapse comes from the other words and the verse’s development.

6. Distinctions from related roots

SH F W compared with SH F R

Shafīr belongs to SH F R and means an edge, such as a well’s rim or riverbank.

It overlaps with shafā in this sense.

The roots and morphological structures nevertheless differ.

Shafā belongs to SH F W; shafīr to SH F R.

Both may be translated “edge,” without merging their histories.

The latter family also includes shafrah for a blade’s cutting edge and shafr for the margin of certain body parts.

Sharpness is not a constant feature of shafā. A pit’s edge may be sharp or rounded, firm or loose; the word does not specify this.

SH F W compared with Ṭ R F

Ṭaraf means a side, extremity or outer part.

This is close to shafā.

Its various constructions cover directions, ends and relative positions broadly.

The Quranic shafā names a particular pit or bank edge adjoining another space.

Ṭaraf is thus a more general equivalent, while “edge” better preserves these two images.

Shafā is not restricted to precipices: solar remainders and other edges show a wider range.

SH F W compared with Ḥ R F

Early Arabic also uses ḥarf for a side or edge.

This explains the lexical definition ḥarfu kulli shayʾin.

Ḥarf also has other Quranic usages.

Al-Ḥajj 22:11 says:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَعْبُدُ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ حَرْفٍ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yaʿbudu allāha ʿalā ḥarf

Al-Ḥajj 22:11

“Among people is one who worships God on an edge.”

Its context concerns unstable, conditional faith.

Being on an edge can evoke instability and readiness to slip.

Ḥarf and shafā nevertheless belong to different roots, and their verses require separate study.

The concepts of unstable faith in Al-Ḥajj 22:11 are not all part of shafā’s lexical definition.

SH F W compared with SH F H

Shafah means a lip.

Al-Balad 90:9 says:

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:9

“And a tongue and two lips.”

A lip and an edge are physically related images because the lip borders the mouth.

Lexicographers report two familiar analyses of shafah’s structure.

Some identify the omitted radical as hāʾ, supported by shifāh and shufayhah.

Others identify wāw, citing shafawāt.

A historical connection between shafā and shafah cannot simply be ruled out, nor can they be declared one undisputed root family.

The Quranic classification places shafatayni under SH F H and shafā under SH F W.

Forqan therefore treats the Quranic families separately while explaining the structural disagreement where relevant.

SH F W compared with SH F Y

Shifāʾ belongs to SH F Y and concerns healing or recovery.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 says:

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

“When I become ill, He heals me.”

Al-Naḥl 16:69 also mentions a drink containing healing for people.

Shafā in Āl ʿImrān 3:103 and Al-Tawbah 9:109 means an edge.

Similar spelling does not give the words the same Quranic meaning.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 does not place its addressees “on the healing of a pit,” but on its edge.

Likewise, shafā juruf means the bank’s edge, not its healing.

Some lexicons discuss these families together because of structural proximity and variation in the final radical. Some also explain a connection through reaching a threshold.

Such an account does not by itself establish a single historical source meaning for healing and edge.

Their resemblance therefore does not make all their meanings shared.

SH F W compared with Q R B

Qurb denotes proximity or approaching.

Some figurative shafā usages also concern an event’s threshold.

Not every proximity is shafā.

Closeness to a friend or a short distance between houses need not involve a brink between conditions.

Ashfā ʿalā al-mawt concerns approaching death’s boundary rather than mere spatial or emotional closeness.

Threshold imagery thus presents a particular kind of proximity: nearing the boundary beyond which another state begins.

This is not an absolute rule for every derivative, but helps explain the Quranic images.

7. Morphology and translation proposals for Forqan

The Quranic noun shafā

Both occurrences are singular nouns.

Shafā is a maqṣūr noun, ending in alif with case inflection understood rather than audibly expressed.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103:

عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ

ʿalā shafā ḥufrah

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“On the edge of a pit.”

It follows the preposition ʿalā and is genitive.

Ḥufratin is its genitive complement.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 likewise says:

عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ

ʿalā shafā jurufin hār

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“On the edge of an unstable bank.”

Shafā again follows ʿalā and is in a construct with jurufin.

Hārin qualifies jurufin; it is not a derivative of shafā.

Both occurrences share the structure of being on something’s edge.

What differs is the thing whose edge is named.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 has a fiery pit.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 has an unstable bank liable to collapse.

Translation choices within the family

Shafā: “edge,” “margin” or “outer limit.”

Shafā al-biʾr: “the well’s rim.”

Shafā al-ḥufrah: “the pit’s edge.”

Shafā al-juruf: “the edge of an eroded bank.”

Shafawān: “two edges.”

Ashfāʾ: “edges” or “margins.”

Mā baqiya minhu illā shafan: “Only a little of it remains.”

Shafati al-shamsu tashfū: “The sun neared setting” or “only a little remained visible.”

Ashfā ʿalā al-halāk: “reached the brink of destruction.”

Translation must distinguish an actual edge from a figurative threshold.

“Edge” suits a pit or bank.

“Brink” or “threshold” suits approaching death or destruction.

“A little remaining” suits the final sunlight or a small quantity.

These senses should not be interchanged regardless of construction.

Proposed Quranic renderings

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا

wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“You were at the edge of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it.”

This preserves the edge-position and rescue separately. These proposals do not change the installed verse translations.

“You were about to fall into fire” conveys the image’s effect but adds “fall,” which is absent from this clause.

“The edge of a pit” is therefore more direct.

Al-Tawbah 9:109

عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ فَانْهَارَ بِهِ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ

ʿalā shafā jurufin hārin fa-inhāra bihi fī nāri jahannam

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“On the edge of a crumbling bank, which collapsed with him into the fire of Hell.”

“Edge” directly renders shafā.

“Bank” represents juruf; “crumbling” conveys hār.

“Collapsed” renders the separate verb inhāra.

These meanings should not be compressed into one word.

“Founded it on certain destruction” may suggest the parable’s outcome but removes the distinct edge, bank and collapse images.

“The edge of a crumbling bank” preserves the image and lets the continuation supply its outcome.

8. Conclusion

SH F W concerns edges, margins and outer limits in early Arabic.

Shafā names a boundary where something ends. Related usages concern the final remainder of a phenomenon or the threshold of death or destruction.

Early lexical accounts and al-ʿAjjaj’s poetry attest the last-sunlight usage. His poetry is post-Islamic, not a pre-Islamic witness. This study does not establish a securely dated earlier poetic usage; the Quran and early lexicons nevertheless clearly attest the edge meaning.

The root occurs twice in the Quran.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 depicts people rescued from a fiery pit’s edge, within former enmity, joined hearts, fellowship and avoiding division.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 depicts a structure at an unstable bank’s edge and its collapse, contrasting reverence and divine approval with harm, unbelief and division.

Shafā means edge in both, while outcomes differ.

One image ends in rescue, the other in collapse.

Danger, rescue and destruction are therefore not constant lexical meanings of shafā. The location and the rest of the verse determine the outcome.

Nor should shafā be equated with healing, shifāʾ, or the lip, shafah, without examining morphological differences.

A defensible core is an edge or outer limit, with related usages for a threshold or final remainder.

“Edge” is the most suitable common equivalent for the two Quranic occurrences.

The comparison also supports a reflection: reaching a dangerous brink does not mean the outcome has already occurred, as rescue precedes falling in the first image.

A structure founded unsoundly for harmful purposes may collapse despite a firm appearance; the second image depicts its end in fire.

One recalls joined hearts and release from enmity; the other condemns a religious-looking structure serving harm and division.

These conclusions come from the full verses and their connections, not from shafā itself meaning guidance, unity, hypocrisy or destruction.
