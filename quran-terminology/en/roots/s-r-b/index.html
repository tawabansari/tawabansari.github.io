Central meanings: going, flowing, and passing along a route, whether people and animals moving across land or water passing through a channel or opening. The noun sarab denotes a passage, including an underground one. Sarāb denotes a mirage: in the familiar desert image, something resembling flowing water or a water surface appears at a distance where that water is absent. Qur’anic uses concern someone going about by day, the fish’s passage through the sea, the mirage in a comparison with human deeds, and mountains becoming a mirage.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic family S R B concerns going, flowing, finding a way, and moving along a route.

Saraba fī al-arḍ means to go across the land or travel a route.

Saraba al-faḥlu concerns a male camel going out to pasture.

Sārib describes someone or something proceeding along its course.

Al-sarb in one usage denotes a road or route of movement.

Al-sarab is also recorded for a passage or underground way.

In another branch, sariba al-māʾu concerns water flowing or escaping through an opening.

These usages share a feature: something moves along a course or passes through a way.

Yet the movement, its route, and the thing moving must be distinguished.

A person or animal may proceed along a route.

Water may pass through an opening.

A way or passage may provide the course through which movement occurs.

These are related but not identical meanings.

First pre-Islamic example: an unrestrained camel in al-Akhnas ibn Shihāb’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet al-Akhnas ibn Shihāb al-Taghlibī says in a well-known poem:

أَرَى كُلَّ قَوْمٍ قَارَبُوا قَيْدَ فَحْلِهِمْ
وَنَحْنُ خَلَعْنَا قَيْدَهُ فَهْوَ سَارِبُ

arā kulla qawmin qārabū qayda faḥlihim / wa-naḥnu khalaʿnā qaydahu fa-huwa sāribu

Translation:

“I see every people keeping their male camel close-tethered; we have removed his tether, and he roams freely.”

Sārib here describes a male camel whose tether has been removed.

The poet speaks about his people’s way of life and standing.

In the poem’s image, other peoples keep their camel close for fear of attack or losing their livestock; this is a poetic boast, not an independent historical judgment about all peoples.

He boasts of his people’s strength and security, saying their camel is free to roam the pasture.

Here sārib does not mean hiding.

The camel has been released from restraint and proceeds along its course.

Nor does sārib itself mean brave or powerful.

Courage and strength belong to the poet’s boast about his people.

Sārib describes the camel’s movement.

This example shows that sārib describes a moving creature. Freedom from restraint is understood here from the removal of its tether, not as an essential component of every use of the word.

The verse is transmitted in al-Akhnas’s poem and early Arabic poetry collections. Its attribution is well established in that tradition, although, like other pre-Islamic poetry, its text reached us through later transmission.

Second pre-Islamic example: a flowing mirage in al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī says:

وَإِذَا الرِّكَابُ تَوَاكَلَتْ بَعْدَ السُّرَى
وَجَرَى السَّرَابُ عَلَى مُتُونِ الْجَدْجَدِ

wa-idhā al-rikābu tawākalat baʿda al-surā / wa-jarā al-sarābu ʿalā mutūni al-jadjadi

Translation:

“When the mounts grow sluggish after the night journey, and the mirage seems to flow across the expanses of hard, level ground.”

Al-sarāb names the phenomenon that resembles water from a distance.

Jarā expresses flowing.

Mutūn al-jadjad concerns expanses of hard, level ground.

The poet describes mounts weary after traveling through the night, while a mirage appears over the ground.

Sarāb here is not actual water.

Its appearance suggests something flowing across the surface.

This illustrates the word’s history: sarāb was a recognized optical phenomenon in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Its imagery also connects with motion and flow.

That image alone does not prove that sarāb historically developed from the verb saraba, to flow or proceed.

Lexical explanations also propose this connection, but explaining a semantic relationship differs from establishing historical origin.

A historically grounded definition

Early Arabic evidence reveals two important usages.

First, sārib describes a moving creature, freely roaming in the poetic example.

Second, sarāb names a water-like optical phenomenon appearing over the ground.

Lexical records also place paths, passages, flowing water, livestock movement, and moving groups within this family.

A defensible account is therefore:

The verbal branch of S R B concerns going or proceeding along a course; nouns also denote routes and passages. Sarāb names a mirage that, in the familiar desert image, resembles flowing water at a distance although that water is absent.

These connections are explicable, but the senses should not all be derived from one original image without adequate historical evidence.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Saraba yasrubu

This is the family’s basic triliteral verb.

It means to go, proceed, or move across the land.

سَرَبَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

saraba fī al-arḍ

That is: “He traveled across the land.”

سَرَبَ الْفَحْلُ

saraba al-faḥlu

That is: “The male camel set out toward pasture.”

Movement and going are prominent here.

The verb alone does not specify the person’s or animal’s speed or destination.

Surūb

The verbal noun of saraba in the sense of going or moving.

سَرَبَ سُرُوبًا

saraba surūban

That is: “He went or proceeded.”

Applied to livestock, it concerns their going out to pasture.

Sārib

The active participle of the basic verb.

It describes someone or something proceeding along a course.

Al-Akhnas’s poem calls the unrestrained camel sārib.

Al-Raʿd 13:10 uses this form for a person going about by day.

Sarb

One familiar meaning is a road or route of movement.

خَلِّ سَرْبَهُ

khalli sarbahu

That is: “Leave his way clear.”

The expression concerns allowing a person or animal to proceed along its way.

Sarb is also used in some contexts for grazing livestock.

The usages are related, but one names a route and the other a collection of animals.

Sarab

In relation to terrain, it denotes a passage that may extend underground or through a barrier.

Al-sarab is recorded for a cavity, underground passage, or animal passageway.

This is the noun used in Al-Kahf 18:61.

Saraban there concerns the fish’s movement in the sea.

Sirb

Arabic also uses this form for a group of animals, birds, or people.

سِرْبٌ مِنَ الطَّيْرِ

sirbun mina al-ṭayr

That is: “A flock of birds.”

سِرْبٌ مِنَ الظِّبَاءِ

sirbun mina al-ẓibāʾ

That is: “A herd of gazelles.”

It describes creatures moving together or forming a group.

It does not establish that every member always moves in the same direction at the same speed.

Sariba yasrabu

Applied to water, it concerns flowing or leaking.

سَرِبَ الْمَاءُ

sariba al-māʾu

That is: “The water flowed or leaked.”

سَرِبَتِ الْمَزَادَةُ

saribat al-mazādatu

This describes a waterskin or vessel from which water escapes through openings.

Movement here is connected with passage through a particular opening or channel.

Insaraba and tasarraba

These verbs are known for entering or moving through narrow ways and for liquid flowing or leaking.

اِنْسَرَبَ الْحَيَوَانُ فِي جُحْرِهِ

insaraba al-ḥayawānu fī juḥrihi

That is: “The animal entered its burrow.”

تَسَرَّبَ الْمَاءُ

tasarraba al-māʾu

That is: “The water leaked through an opening.”

Modern uses also concern penetration and gradual passage.

Those modern meanings should not be transferred to every early form without historical examination.

Sarraba and tasrīb

Applied to livestock, sarraba can mean sending them out in successive groups.

Tasrīb is its verbal noun.

In water-related usages, the verb is also recorded for putting water into a waterskin so that its leather and stitching swell and its openings close. This specific practice should be distinguished from the general modern sense of causing leakage.

These constructions concern directing or admitting something, but the verb alone does not identify what is being moved.

Masrab and masārib

Masrab denotes a route, passage, or place through which something passes.

Masārib is its plural.

Applied to water, it can denote a channel of flow.

Applied to animals, it can denote their tracks or routes.

The family also has uses concerning a narrow line of body hair.

These share the image of a course or line without referring to the same material object.

Sarāb

A name for a mirage, familiar in the desert as a distant appearance resembling water or a water surface.

This water-like type is familiar in hot environments over open, level ground; not every kind of mirage in modern optics is limited to this appearance or setting.

A person may believe there is water in the distance, then approach and discover none at that location.

Sarāb occurs in Al-Nūr 24:39 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:20.

The forms actually used in the Qur’an

S R B occurs four times in three principal forms:

  • Wa-sāribun in Al-Raʿd 13:10: an active participle.
  • Saraban in Al-Kahf 18:61: a noun denoting a passage.
  • Ka-sarābin in Al-Nūr 24:39: mirage preceded by the comparative kāf.
  • Sarāban in Al-Nabaʾ 78:20: mirage describing the mountains’ transformation.

Other Arabic forms, including surūb, tasarraba, insaraba, and masrab, do not occur in the Qur’an in those forms.

3. Movement, passage, and mirage

Three important usages in this family are semantically related.

First, movement along a course.

Second, the route or passage through which movement occurs.

Third, a mirage that can resemble water flowing across the ground.

Movement and its route

Someone walking across the land follows a course.

Water also follows a course as it flows.

Sometimes that course is visible, as with a path across a plain.

Sometimes it is hidden or confined, as with an underground cavity or an opening through which water leaks.

Movement and route are clearly connected in these uses.

Sārib does not name the route; it describes the creature moving.

Sarab in the passage sense names the route.

The mover and the place of movement must therefore be distinguished.

Mirage and the image of flowing water

Lexical explanations connect the name sarāb with movement and flow.

A mirage can look as though water is flowing over the ground.

This provides an intelligible relationship with saraba in the sense of flowing or proceeding.

Yet a mirage is not simply a type of movement.

It is an optical phenomenon.

Something appears water-like to the observer, although the expected water is absent.

Translating sarāb merely as “flow” would erase its defining visual feature in this usage.

Conversely, treating every use of S R B as optical deception would distort the movement and passage senses.

Movement and passage are independent uses of this family. Sarāb names a recognized phenomenon whose resemblance to flowing water can explain a semantic connection without establishing a definite historical derivation.

4. Wa-sāribun bi-al-nahār: going about by day

سَوَاءٌ مِنْكُمْ مَنْ أَسَرَّ الْقَوْلَ وَمَنْ جَهَرَ بِهِ وَمَنْ هُوَ مُسْتَخْفٍ بِاللَّيْلِ وَسَارِبٌ بِالنَّهَارِ

sawāʾun minkum man asarra al-qawla wa-man jahara bihi wa-man huwa mustakhfin bi-al-layli wa-sāribun bi-al-nahār

Al-Raʿd 13:10

Translation:

“Among you, whoever conceals speech and whoever declares it, whoever hides at night and goes about by day, are alike.”

Al-Raʿd 13:10

Wa-sāribun consists of an active participle preceded by a conjunction.

Bi-al-nahār specifies the time of the movement.

The verse places different human circumstances alongside one another.

Someone may conceal their speech.

Another may say it openly.

Someone may hide at night.

Another may go about in daylight.

What does sārib mean here?

Sārib describes someone going or proceeding along a course.

Al-Raʿd 13:10 places this movement in daylight.

Its contrast with mustakhfin bi-al-layl makes openly moving by day a clear contextual reading.

Al-Akhnas’s description of a freely roaming camel as sārib supports the movement sense.

But S R B itself does not mean being visible.

Visibility here follows from daytime movement contrasted with hiding at night.

Could sārib describe someone hiding?

Some early lexical explanations also propose someone hiding as a reading of sārib here.

They connect this with sarab as an underground passage and insaraba as entering a concealed way.

On that reading, sārib could denote someone concealing himself even during the day.

This possibility is not entirely unrelated to the family’s wider semantic range.

Yet mustakhfin bi-al-layl and sāribun bi-al-nahār stand alongside asarra al-qawla and jahara bihi.

This structure highlights the contrast between concealment and openness.

“Someone openly going about by day” is therefore the more fitting contextual reading.

Another lexical use alone is not sufficient reason to discard that clear contrast.

Hidden and manifest within divine knowledge

Al-Raʿd 13:9 presents The God as Knower of the unseen and the witnessed.

Al-Raʿd 13:10 then addresses concealing and declaring speech and human circumstances by night and day.

Sawāʾ here concerns their equality in relation to divine knowledge.

People may conceal something they say from others.

They may act privately without any other human knowing.

They may also speak and move openly before others.

Whether an action is hidden from or visible to other humans does not limit The God’s knowledge.

This follows from the verse as a whole and its context.

Sārib alone does not mean divine knowledge or the disclosure of all secrets.

Is hiding at night condemned?

That conclusion does not follow from this verse.

Concealing speech or doing something privately is not intrinsically sinful.

People may remain out of sight for privacy, security, or a legitimate purpose.

Likewise, moving openly by day is not intrinsically virtuous.

Al-Raʿd 13:10 primarily concerns equality before divine knowledge, rather than a moral judgment on every hidden or public act.

Sārib here is someone going about by day. The openness of the movement follows from the daytime setting and its contrast with hiding at night.

5. Saraban: the fish’s passage through the sea

فَلَمَّا بَلَغَا مَجْمَعَ بَيْنِهِمَا نَسِيَا حُوتَهُمَا فَاتَّخَذَ سَبِيلَهُ فِي الْبَحْرِ سَرَبًا

fa-lammā balaghā majmaʿa baynihimā nasiyā ḥūtahumā fa-ittakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri saraban

Al-Kahf 18:61

Translation:

“When they reached the meeting place of the two, they forgot their fish, and it took its way into the sea as through a passage.”

Al-Kahf 18:61

Saraban is an accusative noun.

It appears in the construction ittakhadha sabīlahu … saraban.

Sabīlahu denotes the way the fish takes.

Fī al-baḥr identifies where it moves.

Saraban describes the route as a passage.

Why does sarab mean a passage?

Al-sarab denotes a way that may extend underground or through something.

An animal may enter its burrow through a narrow way.

Water may pass through a channel.

Both involve a passage through which movement occurs.

Al-Kahf 18:61 employs this image for the fish’s route through the sea.

Saraban thus concerns the way or passage the fish takes in the water.

“As through a passage” can make the image clear in translation.

It does not establish that a tunnel with solid walls and a defined structure necessarily formed in the water.

“Passage” expresses the image of a way through.

Its precise physical nature is not fully specified by the word.

Sabīl and sarab

Two words concerning a way appear in the same sentence.

Sabīlahu belongs to S B L.

Saraban belongs to S R B.

They are semantically close but do not perform identical functions.

Sabīl identifies the fish’s course.

Sarab here characterizes that course as a passage.

A literal rendering such as “made its way in the sea a way” would not make the image clear to a Persian reader.

“Took its way in the sea as through a passage” better distinguishes the expressions.

Did the fish disappear into the water?

The verse says the fish took its way into the sea.

That movement may have taken it out of the travelers’ sight.

But saraban does not itself mean that the fish was destroyed or transformed into something else.

Nor does the word alone establish that the fish had been dead and then revived.

Those details are not stated in this verse.

Other proposals about the event require their own evidence.

Lexically, the clear point is the fish’s movement and its course in the sea.

Connection with Al-Kahf 18:63

Al-Kahf 18:63 returns to the fish through the account of Moses’ young companion.

It again says that the fish took its way into the sea.

This time the sentence ends with ʿajaban.

ʿAjab belongs to ʿ J B and concerns wonder.

The two verses describe the same event from different angles.

Al-Kahf 18:61 characterizes the fish’s route with saraban.

Al-Kahf 18:63 expresses wonder with ʿajaban.

Sarab therefore does not itself mean a miracle or wonder.

Wonder is conveyed by a separate word in the later verse.

The event’s role in the story

Al-Kahf 18:60 states Moses’ determination to reach the meeting place of the two seas.

In Al-Kahf 18:64, after the fish incident is recalled, Moses says that this is what they were seeking.

The two then retrace their steps.

Within the story, the fish’s movement thus becomes a sign identifying the place they sought.

But sarab does not lexically mean a guiding sign.

That function follows from the sequence of events.

Saraban in Al-Kahf 18:61 concerns the fish’s passage through the sea. The event’s wonder and its role in finding the intended location are understood from neighboring verses.

6. A mirage on a plain: imagined water in Al-Nūr 24:39

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَسَرَابٍ بِقِيعَةٍ يَحْسَبُهُ الظَّمْآنُ مَاءً حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَهُ لَمْ يَجِدْهُ شَيْئًا وَوَجَدَ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ فَوَفَّاهُ حِسَابَهُ وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ

wa-alladhīna kafarū aʿmāluhum ka-sarābin bi-qīʿatin yaḥsabuhu al-ẓamʾānu māʾan ḥattā idhā jāʾahu lam yajidhu shayʾan wa-wajada Allāha ʿindahu fa-waffāhu ḥisābahu wa-Allāhu sarīʿu al-ḥisāb

Al-Nūr 24:39

Translation:

“Those who disbelieved—their deeds are like a mirage on a plain that the thirsty person supposes to be water, until, reaching it, he finds it to be nothing, but finds The God there, who pays him his account in full; and The God is swift in accounting.”

Ka-sarābin combines the comparative kāf with the noun sarāb.

Bi-qīʿatin concerns open, level ground.

Al-ẓamʾān is the thirsty person.

Yaḥsabuhu expresses his supposition.

Māʾan is what he supposes it to be: water.

The image is clear: a thirsty person sees something water-like in the distance and expects to reach water.

What is a mirage?

A mirage is an optical phenomenon produced by the bending of light through air layers with differing refractive properties. In its familiar water-like form, often associated with temperature differences near the ground, a distant surface can appear watery or reflective.

The observer really does see something.

But what is seen is not the water the observer supposes it to be.

The optical phenomenon must therefore be distinguished from the mistaken interpretation.

Mirage does not mean that no real visual phenomenon has occurred.

The optical phenomenon is real; the water expected at that location is absent.

This distinction matters in Al-Nūr 24:39.

The thirsty person takes what he sees to be water.

On reaching the location, he does not find the expected water.

Why is the observer thirsty?

The image does not merely identify someone seeing a mirage.

It specifies al-ẓamʾān: someone thirsty and needing water.

Thirst explains the expectation and hope attached to the sight.

For this person, something resembling water is more than an ordinary view.

He may place his hopes in it and strive to reach it.

But arrival does not fulfill that expectation.

The image therefore concerns more than visual error: someone expects something beneficial and saving, yet fails to find it at the moment of need.

This follows from the complete image.

Sarāb alone does not mean hope, thirst, or disappointment.

Those ideas arise from the accompanying clauses.

Human deeds and mirage

The verse begins:

أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَسَرَابٍ

aʿmāluhum ka-sarāb

Al-Nūr 24:39

The comparison concerns the deeds of the group described as alladhīna kafarū.

The Qur’an thus compares their deeds to a mirage.

The point of comparison must be understood from what follows.

Someone expects to reach water but does not find what he anticipated.

Applied to deeds, this can describe people who imagine a value, result, or reward for their actions, only to meet a different reality at the reckoning.

The issue is not merely doing or not doing an action.

It is the relationship between what people expect from their conduct and what becomes evident when they are called to account.

Does this comparison erase every worldly effect of their deeds?

The comparison itself does not establish that.

Human actions can have real effects in this world.

A building may remain standing.

Accumulated wealth may pass to others.

A beneficial deed may really have helped someone.

Those worldly effects alone do not determine the outcome a person will find before The God.

Al-Nūr 24:39 links the mirage image with divine accounting.

Its point should not be expanded without evidence into a claim that every action of the people concerned had no material effect.

What the verse highlights is disappointed expectation before the reality of reckoning.

Finding The God there: confronting the account

After describing the thirsty person’s disappointment, the verse says he finds The God there and receives his account in full.

This connects the image to the reckoning.

Wa-wajada Allāha ʿindahu should not be interpreted without evidence as The God’s bodily presence beside a terrestrial mirage.

The following clause speaks of settling the account in full.

The person who hoped to reach something encounters an inescapable reality: the accounting of his conduct.

The ending highlights the difference between human expectation and the truth known to The God.

The manner of divine presence and accounting should not be derived from the physical mirage image.

The preceding and following verses

Al-Nūr 24:37 and Al-Nūr 24:38 describe people whom commerce does not distract from remembrance of The God, establishing ṣalāh, and giving zakāh, and mention the reward for their deeds.

Al-Nūr 24:39 then compares the deeds of those who disbelieve to a mirage.

Al-Nūr 24:40 presents another image: layers of darkness in a deep sea.

This passage uses light, seeing, not seeing, expectation, and reality to explain human circumstances.

The properties of the following darkness image should not all be transferred to sarāb here.

The mirage image concerns seeing apparent water and failing to find actual water.

The darkness of the deep sea is a separate image with its own wording and structure.

Sarāb in Al-Nūr 24:39 names an optical phenomenon whose resemblance to water allows mistaken expectation. The message about deeds and reckoning follows from the whole comparison and its context, not from the noun alone.

7. Mountains becoming a mirage in Al-Nabaʾ 78:20

وَسُيِّرَتِ الْجِبَالُ فَكَانَتْ سَرَابًا

wa-suyyirati al-jibālu fa-kānat sarāban

Al-Nabaʾ 78:20

Translation:

“The mountains are set in motion and become a mirage.”

Sarāban is the accusative predicate of kānat.

Al-jibāl, the mountains, are the subject under discussion.

Suyyirat belongs to S Y R and means being set in motion.

Fa-kānat sarāban describes the resulting state.

The verse connects the mountains’ movement with their becoming a mirage; the precise timing and physical stages cannot be determined from this wording alone.

What image does their becoming a mirage convey?

In human experience, mountains represent firmness and elevation above the land.

They have material mass and structure.

A mirage, however, appears from afar to be something whose expected material reality is not found on approach.

The mountains becoming a mirage conveys a profound transformation of their condition.

What was firm and prominent reaches a state unlike its familiar condition.

Here the mirage image connects with the loss of the mountains’ familiar solidity and appearance.

The noun’s meaning must still be distinguished from the event described concerning the mountains.

Sarāb does not itself mean crushing rock.

Other verses express crushing, scattering, and removing mountains through their own verbs and images.

Other verses about mountains

Ṭā Hā 20:105 speaks of mountains being swept away and scattered.

Ṭā Hā 20:106 describes the ground as a level, open expanse.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 describes mountains being crumbled.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 describes their becoming scattered dust.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:5 compares mountains to carded wool.

These verses help identify the range of Qur’anic images for the mountains’ transformation.

None should replace the meaning of sarāb in Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 without analysis.

Here suyyirat expresses being set in motion, and sarāban describes the resulting condition through the mirage image.

Do the mountains literally become an optical mirage?

This verse alone does not establish a specific scientific mechanism.

In present experience, mirage is an optical phenomenon arising under particular light and atmospheric conditions.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 describes transformed mountains amid momentous events.

It does not specify the exact physical process by which their rocks become a mirage.

The noun alone cannot support a theory about refraction, evaporating mountains, or their conversion into a particular form of energy.

Conversely, the image should not be reduced to a claim that mountains have always been imaginary.

Mountains presently have material reality.

The verse concerns their transformation.

It does not deny their current existence; it describes their reaching another condition in the event concerned.

Mirage in Al-Nūr and Al-Nabaʾ

In Al-Nūr 24:39, a thirsty person sees something water-like at a distance.

Mirage is central to a comparison involving deeds and human expectation.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:20, the mountains themselves become a mirage.

In the first verse, a familiar phenomenon explains a human situation.

In the second, it portrays the mountains’ transformation.

The phenomenon named is shared, but the subjects and implications differ.

Al-Nūr concerns disappointed expectation and reckoning.

Al-Nabaʾ concerns the loss of the mountains’ familiar condition as the world changes.

Sarāb retains its familiar meaning in both verses, while the point of the image and its message depend on each context.

8. Distinctions from nearby roots and words

S R B and S B L

Sabīl denotes a way or path.

Sarab also denotes a passage in one usage.

Both occur in the same sentence in Al-Kahf 18:61.

Sabīl identifies the fish’s route.

Sarab characterizes it as a passage here.

They are close in the semantic field of routes and passage.

The broader S R B family also includes movement, water flow, animal groups, and mirage.

Its whole range cannot therefore be reduced to sabīl.

S R B and S Y R

Sayr concerns going and moving.

Sārib likewise describes someone proceeding along a course in its clear movement sense.

The two overlap in movement.

S R B also has usages concerning passages, water flow, and mirage.

Both families appear in Al-Nabaʾ 78:20.

Suyyirat expresses the mountains being set in motion.

Sarāban describes the resulting state.

Despite related letters and movement imagery, the words do not mean the same thing.

S R B and S R Ḥ

S R Ḥ concerns release and sending forth, including sending livestock to pasture.

S R B is also used for livestock going to pasture.

The families are very close in this field.

Sārib describes the creature moving.

Verbal uses of S R Ḥ can concern releasing or sending that creature forth.

This is a tendency, not an absolute division across all uses; sāriḥ can also describe a grazing or moving animal.

S R B and N F Q

Nafaq in one Arabic use denotes an underground passage.

Sarab can also name an underground way.

The physical images are close.

The S R B family is not confined to underground settings; it includes wider uses for routes, movement, and water flow.

The passage in Al-Kahf 18:61 is in the sea.

An underground use elsewhere does not establish that the fish necessarily traveled beneath the seabed through soil.

Sarāb and āl

Āl in desert optical terminology denotes something close to a mirage.

Lexical records sometimes treat the two as synonymous.

Other explanations distinguish them.

One describes āl as appearing in morning light before intense heat, with distant objects appearing altered.

Sarāb in another description is especially a water-like appearance over the ground in daytime heat.

Early accounts do not completely agree on their precise times of appearance.

A fixed hour cannot therefore be made an essential part of sarāb’s lexical meaning.

The Qur’an uses sarāb; its familiar water-like appearance is central to the image under discussion.

S R B and S R B L

These families share initial letters but are distinguished in the Qur’anic morphological index.

S R B is triliteral.

S R B L is classified as quadriliteral in that index.

Qur’anic sarābīl denotes garments.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 mentions clothing protecting people from heat or the harm of battle.

Ibrāhīm 14:50 mentions garments in a scene of punishment.

Sirbāl in those passages does not mean a road, passage, or mirage.

Similar beginnings do not suffice to equate their roots and meanings.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The three Qur’anic forms in this family have real semantic differences.

Sārib concerns a person’s movement.

Sarab denotes a way or passage.

Sarāb names an optical phenomenon.

Translations should preserve these differences.

Important meanings in the family

Saraba yasrubu: went, proceeded, moved along a course.

Surūb: going or proceeding.

Sārib: proceeding, in motion.

Sarb: route or path; also grazing livestock in another usage.

Sarab: a passage that may extend underground or through something.

Sirb: a group of creatures.

Sariba al-māʾu: the water leaked or flowed.

Insaraba and tasarraba: passed through a passage or opening, entered, or leaked.

Sarraba: sent forth or dispatched in groups; in a specific waterskin usage, put water inside to swell and seal its stitching.

Masrab: route or passage.

Sarāb: mirage; in the familiar image, an appearance resembling water where the expected water is absent.

Renderings of the Qur’anic forms

In Al-Raʿd 13:10:

وَسَارِبٌ بِالنَّهَارِ

wa-sāribun bi-al-nahār

Al-Raʿd 13:10

“And goes about by day.”

In Al-Kahf 18:61:

فَاتَّخَذَ سَبِيلَهُ فِي الْبَحْرِ سَرَبًا

fa-ittakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri saraban

Al-Kahf 18:61

“It took its way into the sea as through a passage.”

Ka-sarābin in Al-Nūr 24:39 means “like a mirage.”

Sarāban in Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 means “a mirage,” describing the mountains’ transformed condition in fa-kānat sarāban.

These proposals convey the distinctions relevant to Forqan’s translations; they do not authorize changing the installed verse translations as part of this root study.

10. Conclusion

S R B concerns going, flowing, and passing along a route.

Sarab can name a way or passage.

Water-related uses also express flow and leakage.

In al-Akhnas’s pre-Islamic poem, sārib describes a camel released from its tether and roaming freely.

Al-Mutalammis’s poem uses sarāb for the familiar phenomenon appearing like water flowing over the ground.

These examples illustrate movement and mirage in early Arabic.

Lexical records include routes, underground passages, water flow, animal groups, and mirage within the family.

They should not all be assigned a single certain historical origin without evidence.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Raʿd 13:10, sārib describes a person going about in daylight, in the context of The God’s knowledge of hidden and manifest things.

In Al-Kahf 18:61, saraban concerns the fish’s passage through the sea. It denotes movement along a course without fully specifying the passage’s physical nature.

Al-Nūr 24:39 compares deeds to a mirage when people encounter a reckoning contrary to their expectations.

The image resembles distant water, but the expected water is absent.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 describes mountains being set in motion and becoming a mirage.

This portrays a transformation of their familiar state without the noun alone specifying a physical mechanism.

Three main uses must remain distinct: movement along a course; the passage through which it occurs; and mirage, which in the familiar image resembles water where the expected water is absent.

For Forqan, “going about” suits sārib, “a passage” or “a passage-like way” suits sarab, and “mirage” suits the other two occurrences.

The central distinction is between movement, its route, and the mirage phenomenon: related but different concepts.

Sarāb retains its familiar sense in both verses, while context determines the message: disappointed expectation and reckoning in Al-Nūr, and the mountains’ profound transformation in Al-Nabaʾ.

A shared root does not make all meanings identical, and the mirage image does not justify an unsupported scientific or philosophical theory about the reality of everything.
