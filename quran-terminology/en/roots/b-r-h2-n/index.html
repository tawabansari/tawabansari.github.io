The Qur’anic Root B R H N

Burhān: evidence that clarifies and establishes something

Burhān is clear, establishing evidence: something that carries a claim beyond mere assertion and provides grounds for its truth. In the Qur’an, such evidence is not always a verbal argument; it may also be a tangible sign.

This study treats B R H N under a four-consonant root heading, while acknowledging disagreement over whether the nūn is radical or additional and over the word’s historical formation. The Qur’an uses only the noun burhān from this family; no corresponding verb occurs.

The word occurs eight times in the Qur’an: Al-Baqarah 2:111, Al-Nisāʾ 4:174, Yūsuf 12:24, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:24, Al-Muʾminūn 23:117, Al-Naml 27:64, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32 and 28:75. Burhānakum, “your proof,” occurs four times, and the dual burhānāni occurs in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32.

The Qur’anic uses establish a reasonably clear semantic core: burhān takes a claim or message beyond mere assertion or supposition and provides grounds for its truth. Yet the Qur’an does not restrict burhān to a verbal argument. The two signs given to Moses are called “two proofs”; Joseph encounters “his Lord’s proof”; and in Al-Nisāʾ, a proof from the Lord comes to the people.

An initial definition can be stated as follows:

B R H N: clear, substantiating evidence that makes the truth of a claim, message, or position apparent. Burhān may take the form of evidence that can be presented, a manifest sign, or a proof from The God; it is not restricted to verbal argument.

Pre-Qur’anic Arabic and reports from early Islam

The evidence examined did not yield a pre-Qur’anic Arabic poem, proverb, contract, or everyday usage of burhān whose attribution and meaning were sufficiently secure. The Geʿez and Sabaic evidence discussed below belongs to other languages and does not fill that gap. Nor is there a single established physical image proven to be the source of all the Arabic uses: the connection with light is an etymological question, not a settled history of the word.

A report transmitted by Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī from the Prophet and recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim states:

وَالصَّدَقَةُ بُرْهَانٌ

Wa-l-ṣadaqatu burhān.

“And charity is evidence.”

Burhān is the predicate of al-ṣadaqah: an action is called evidence or a sign, rather than a verbally formulated syllogism. This is compatible with the breadth of the Qur’anic meaning, but it is not a pre-Islamic attestation. The report is attributed to the Prophet’s period and preserved in a later compilation; it cannot establish the word’s pre-Qur’anic antiquity or a sequence of development from “light” to “proof.”

Linguistic background: light, testimony, and an uncertain historical route

The historical formation of burhān has long been disputed. Some lexicographers regarded its nūn as radical and treated the word as quadriliteral; others considered the nūn additional and analyzed the word on the pattern fuʿlān. Proposed lexical connections include cutting and the decisive force of proof, as well as whiteness and brightness. These analyses should not be mistaken for an established historical derivation.

Related languages provide further material for comparison. In Geʿez, the closely resembling bərhān denotes light and brightness. Sabaic, an Ancient South Arabian language, attests brhn, provisionally interpreted as testimony or being attested. The specialist dictionary marks this interpretation as uncertain.

These comparisons suggest possible early connections with languages surrounding Arabic, but do not by themselves establish the direction or date of transmission. A semantic relationship between “becoming clear” and “clarifying evidence” is understandable: evidence brings something out of uncertainty and makes it clear.

The evidence should not, however, be used to reconstruct the word’s precise history with excessive confidence. It does not establish with certainty that the Qur’anic form was borrowed directly from Geʿez, or that “light” was necessarily the first stage underlying every later use. The similar Sabaic form must also be considered, but uncertainty about its meaning prevents it from serving as decisive evidence for a particular historical route.

“Brightness” may therefore form an important part of the word’s semantic background, while its Qur’anic definition must still be grounded in its uses within the Qur’an.

The verb barhana requires similar caution. It does not occur in the Qur’an. Some early lexicographers also regarded barhana, “to adduce proof,” as a later formation. We should therefore not reconstruct the Qur’anic noun’s meaning by working backward from that later verb.

Hātū burhānakum: a claim alone is not enough

One particularly clear Qur’anic construction occurs four times:

هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

Hātū burhānakum.

“Bring your proof.”

In Al-Baqarah, the issue is a claim to exclusive admission to the Garden:

وَقَالُوا لَنْ يَدْخُلَ الْجَنَّةَ إِلَّا مَنْ كَانَ هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَىٰ ۗ تِلْكَ أَمَانِيُّهُمْ ۗ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

Wa-qālū lan yadkhula al-jannata illā man kāna hūdan aw naṣārā; tilka amāniyyuhum; qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīna.

“They said, ‘No one will enter the Garden unless they are Jewish or Christian.’ Those are their wishes. Say: ‘Bring your proof, if you are truthful.’”

Al-Baqarah 2:111

The sequence clarifies the meaning. A claim is first made; the Qur’an then calls it amāniyyuhum, “their wishes,” and says:

هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

Hātū burhānakum.

Believing a claim or wishing it to be true is not itself proof. If the claim is true, something must be capable of establishing its truth.

The same construction occurs in connection with taking gods other than The God:

أَمِ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِهِ آلِهَةً ۖ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

Ami ittakhadhū min dūnihi ālihatan; qul hātū burhānakum.

“Or have they taken gods apart from Him? Say: ‘Bring your proof.’”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:24

And in Al-Naml:

أَإِلَٰهٌ مَعَ اللَّهِ ۚ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

A-ilāhun maʿa Allāhi; qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīna.

“Is there a god alongside The God? Say: ‘Bring your proof, if you are truthful.’”

Al-Naml 27:64

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75 repeats the same command in a scene of judgment.

In all these cases, the demand for burhānakum, “your proof,” is directed to those making the claim. The assertion has been made; what is now required is something that establishes it.

One of the word’s clearest distinctions is therefore:

Burhān is not the claim itself; it is the evidence that establishes the claim’s truth.

Lā burhāna lahu bihi: a claim without supporting evidence

Al-Muʾminūn presents the same relationship from the opposite direction:

وَمَنْ يَدْعُ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ لَا بُرْهَانَ لَهُ بِهِ فَإِنَّمَا حِسَابُهُ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِ

Wa-man yadʿu maʿa Allāhi ilāhan ākhara lā burhāna lahu bihi fa-innamā ḥisābuhu ʿinda rabbihi.

“Whoever invokes another god alongside The God, for which he has no proof, his reckoning is only with his Lord.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:117

Here no proof is produced. The Qur’an speaks of an absence of proof:

لَا بُرْهَانَ لَهُ بِهِ

Lā burhāna lahu bihi.

A claim may be made even though there is no proof for it.

This use shows that burhān is more than a tool of debate. The more fundamental question is whether evidence exists that establishes the truth of what is claimed.

Fa-dhānika burhānāni: a proof can be a tangible sign

If we had only hātū burhānakum, we might imagine that burhān meant a verbal argument alone. The account of Moses prevents that restriction.

After the signs of Moses’ staff and hand, the Qur’an says:

فَذَانِكَ بُرْهَانَانِ مِنْ رَبِّكَ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَلَإِهِ

Fa-dhānika burhānāni min rabbika ilā Firʿawna wa-malaʾihi.

“These, then, are two proofs from your Lord to Pharaoh and his chiefs.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32

Burhānāni is the dual of burhān.

The two proofs here are not two verbal arguments. The context concerns signs given to Moses, to be brought before Pharaoh and his chiefs.

A few verses later, when Moses comes to them, the Qur’an says:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَهُمْ مُوسَىٰ بِآيَاتِنَا بَيِّنَاتٍ

Fa-lammā jāʾahum Mūsā bi-āyātinā bayyinātin.

“When Moses came to them with Our clear signs…”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:36

This nearby passage shows that a sign can be called burhān in view of its function as proof.

We can draw a distinction in emphasis:

Āyah emphasizes that something is a sign;
burhān emphasizes its capacity to establish and disclose truth.

These are not complete synonyms, and not every āyah can automatically be called burhān. Al-Qaṣaṣ nevertheless shows that their semantic ranges can overlap.

Raʾā burhāna rabbihi: the proof Joseph perceived

The account of Joseph states:

وَلَقَدْ هَمَّتْ بِهِ وَهَمَّ بِهَا لَوْلَا أَنْ رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنَصْرِفَ عَنْهُ السُّوءَ وَالْفَحْشَاءَ

Wa-laqad hammat bihi wa-hamma bihā lawlā an raʾā burhāna rabbihi; kadhālika li-naṣrifa ʿanhu al-sūʾa wa-al-faḥshāʾa.

“She intended him, and he would have intended her had he not seen his Lord’s proof. Thus We acted to turn evil and indecency away from him.” This translation reflects one reading of the lawlā construction. Interpretations differ over the relationship between hamma bihā and the following condition; the present discussion concerns the nature of the proof.

Yūsuf 12:24

Here the verb raʾā, “saw,” occurs with burhān:

رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ

Raʾā burhāna rabbihi.

The expression shows that burhān is not only something a person presents to another in debate. A person may themselves encounter and perceive a proof from their Lord.

The verse does not, however, explain what that proof was. Nor does raʾā require us to understand it as physical light or a visible object. The Qur’an says that Joseph saw his Lord’s proof; further details should not be inserted without evidence.

The linguistic background of burhān and its association with brightness may make this expression more suggestive, but cannot replace the verse itself. That background does not establish that Joseph necessarily saw a particular light or radiance.

The secure limit of the claim is:

His Lord’s proof clarified the truth for Joseph at that moment and was connected with evil and indecency being turned away from him. The verse does not disclose its precise nature.

Burhānun min rabbikum and nūran mubīnan

Al-Nisāʾ contains a particularly noteworthy use:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ قَدْ جَاءَكُمْ بُرْهَانٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكُمْ نُورًا مُبِينًا

Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu qad jāʾakum burhānun min rabbikum wa-anzalnā ilaykum nūran mubīnan.

“O people, a proof has come to you from your Lord, and We have sent down to you a manifest light.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:174

In the examples examined earlier, people are required to bring their proof:

هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

Hātū burhānakum.

Here, however, the proof comes from the Lord:

جَاءَكُمْ بُرْهَانٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ

Jāʾakum burhānun min rabbikum.

Burhān need not therefore be a human construction or argument. It may be something The God makes available to people to clarify the truth.

The occurrence of burhān and nūran mubīnan, “a manifest light,” in one verse is also noteworthy, particularly in light of the linguistic background connecting burhān with brightness.

The Qur’an nevertheless uses two distinct expressions:

بُرْهَانٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ

Burhānun min rabbikum.

And:

نُورًا مُبِينًا

Nūran mubīnan.

This juxtaposition can highlight the closeness of establishing truth and making something clear. It does not by itself establish that burhān and nūr have exactly the same meaning, or that they must refer to the same thing.

What the verse securely states is that a proof has come from the Lord, alongside the sending down of “a manifest light.”

Burhān in the scene of judgment

In Al-Qaṣaṣ, the familiar command is heard again:

وَنَزَعْنَا مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ شَهِيدًا فَقُلْنَا هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ فَعَلِمُوا أَنَّ الْحَقَّ لِلَّهِ وَضَلَّ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

Wa-nazaʿnā min kulli ummatin shahīdan fa-qulnā hātū burhānakum fa-ʿalimū anna al-ḥaqqa li-Allāhi wa-ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūna.

“We shall draw out a witness from every community and say, ‘Bring your proof.’ Then they will know that the truth belongs to The God, and what they used to fabricate will be lost to them.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75

The verse begins with shahīdan, “a witness,” then demands burhānakum, “your proof,” and finally states:

فَعَلِمُوا أَنَّ الْحَقَّ لِلَّهِ

Fa-ʿalimū anna al-ḥaqqa li-Allāhi.

“Then they knew that the truth belongs to The God.”

This sequence clarifies the role of burhān. It belongs to a setting in which a claim must be tested against the truth.

Yet burhān, testimony, and truth are not synonyms. Burhān is what can substantiate the claim and establish its truth in that assessment.

From brightness to proof: a possible connection, not a compulsory definition

Evidence from related languages helps us consider the relationship between brightness and proof.

When something becomes clear, it emerges from obscurity; when a claim is subjected to proof, its truth or falsehood likewise ceases to be uncertain. In this sense, a semantic movement from brightness to clarifying evidence is readily understandable.

The Qur’anic uses repeatedly touch on this field:

Burhān establishes the truth of a claim;
the two signs of Moses are proofs;
Joseph perceives his Lord’s proof;
and in Al-Nisāʾ, proof stands alongside “a manifest light.”

Yet this connection must not be turned into the equation:

Burhān = light

Nūr, “light,” is an independent Qur’anic word, and burhān has its own semantic function. Brightness may contribute to its background and to how we understand its imagery, but its Qur’anic use concerns evidence and proof.

Qur’anic burhān and logical demonstration

In later periods, burhān became a technical term in logic for a particular kind of demonstrative reasoning that yields certainty.

That specialized meaning must not be imposed on the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses burhān much more broadly:

In Al-Baqarah, Al-Anbiyāʾ, Al-Naml, and Al-Qaṣaṣ, it is what a claimant must bring to establish a claim;

in Al-Muʾminūn, a claim may be made for which there is no proof;

in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32, the two signs of Moses are “two proofs”;

in Yūsuf, it is something Joseph perceives from his Lord;

and in Al-Nisāʾ, a proof comes from the Lord to the people.

The Qur’anic meaning cannot therefore be restricted to the particular structure of a logical syllogism. The later logical term is one specialized development of the word, not a definition of all its Qur’anic uses.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In English, “proof” is a useful rendering of burhān in many contexts. It should not be understood exclusively as a formal mathematical or philosophical demonstration. The explanation “clear evidence that establishes the truth” helps convey the range of the Arabic word in the Qur’an.

For:

هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

Hātū burhānakum.

“Bring your proof”

This rendering is natural and precise. “Bring your evidence” also conveys the meaning, while “clear, substantiating evidence” makes the function more explicit. “Evidence” is not necessarily a weaker translation in every context.

For:

لَا بُرْهَانَ لَهُ بِهِ

Lā burhāna lahu bihi.

One can say:

“He has no proof for it.”

For:

فَذَانِكَ بُرْهَانَانِ مِنْ رَبِّكَ

Fa-dhānika burhānāni min rabbika.

“These, then, are two proofs from your Lord”

This rendering is suitable, with the explanation that the two proofs in this context are the tangible signs given to Moses.

For:

رَأَىٰ بُرْهَانَ رَبِّهِ

Raʾā burhāna rabbihi.

“He saw his Lord’s proof”

This wording should be retained without adding an account of the proof’s nature to the translation.

And for:

قَدْ جَاءَكُمْ بُرْهَانٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ

Qad jāʾakum burhānun min rabbikum.

“A proof has come to you from your Lord”

This preserves both the Qur’anic construction and the word’s semantic range.

Conclusion

This study has treated B R H N under a four-consonant heading while retaining the disagreement over its historical formation. The Qur’an uses only the noun burhān from this family: it occurs eight times, with no corresponding Qur’anic verb.

Related languages provide material for historical comparison. The similar Geʿez form is connected with light and brightness, while Sabaic attests a comparable form provisionally interpreted as testimony or being attested. These data justify investigating a relationship between becoming clear and establishing truth, but do not conclusively establish how the word entered Arabic.

The Qur’an makes the word’s contextual meaning clearer. Burhān separates a claim from mere supposition or wish and provides grounds for its truth:

هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

Hātū burhānakum.

Yet burhān is not solely a verbal argument. The two signs of Moses are burhānāni, Joseph encounters burhāna rabbihi, and in Al-Nisāʾ a burhānun min rabbikum comes to the people.

The word’s possible historical connection with brightness should not lead us to translate burhān as “light” in the Qur’an. Al-Nisāʾ 4:174 itself places burhān alongside “a manifest light.” This proximity helps us understand the relationship between clarification and proof, but does not establish that the two words are identical in meaning.

A concise definition can be stated as follows:

B R H N: clear, substantiating evidence that establishes the truth of a claim, message, or position and distinguishes it from mere assertion, supposition, or fabrication. In the Qur’an, burhān may be evidence that can be presented, a tangible sign, or a proof from The God; it is therefore not restricted to verbal argument or the technical vocabulary of logic. Its possible historical connection with brightness may illuminate its semantic background, but burhān and nūr are not identical in Qur’anic usage.
