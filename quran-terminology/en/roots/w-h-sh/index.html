Root W Ḥ SH — Wild Animals, Unfamiliarity, and Loneliness

The Arabic root و ح ش is associated with the absence of familiarity and companionship. It is used in several settings: animals that are not tame or accustomed to people, places emptied of human inhabitants, and what a person feels without familiar company or in unfamiliar surroundings.

In the Quran, this root occurs only once, in the form الْوُحُوشُ, in a description of the great events of the Resurrection.

The important point is that وَحْش does not inherently mean a predatory, dangerous, or bloodthirsty animal. A gazelle can be wild without being predatory. In this use, the distinction concerns being unaccustomed to humans, rather than violence or predation.

1. The root in early Arabic

In early Arabic, وَحْش was used for animals unaccustomed to humans, especially those of the desert and open plains. Animals living with people or accustomed to their presence were, by contrast, considered tame and familiar.

An example appears in a poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, a poet of the pre-Islamic period:

كَأَنَّ عُيُونَ الْوَحْشِ حَوْلَ خِبَائِنَا

ka-anna ʿuyūna al-waḥshi ḥawla khibāʾinā

وَأَرْحُلَنَا الْجَزْعُ الَّذِي لَمْ يُثَقَّبِ

wa-arḥulanā al-jazʿu alladhī lam yuthaqqabi

“The eyes of the wild animals around our tents and baggage are like undrilled beads of black-and-white onyx.”

In this part of the poem, the poet describes hunting and the subsequent encampment. He compares the eyes of the hunted animals to beads of onyx with black and white markings.

Here الْوَحْش names animals living freely in the wild and hunted as game; it does not designate mythical beings or necessarily predators.

Gazelles and wild cattle are among the animals used to explain this image. In this usage, وَحْش thus includes herbivorous animals that flee from humans.

The verse is attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays in his collected poetry and literary works. It witnesses the word’s use in the poetic tradition associated with the pre-Islamic period, although a poet’s name alone cannot establish the precise date of every verse’s composition and transmission.

The semantic conclusion is clear: in early usage, وَحْش designated untamed animals unaccustomed to people; its meaning was not restricted to predators.

Arabic lexicons also record uses beyond animals: وَحْشَة for loneliness and unfamiliarity, مُوحِش for an empty, desolate place, and اسْتَوْحَشَ for feeling estranged or uneasy in the absence of familiar company.

These uses are related in meaning, but the available evidence does not establish which branch was historically first.

We cannot state with certainty that an untamed animal was first called وَحْش and that loneliness was later named from it, or conversely that an earlier notion of unfamiliarity was subsequently applied to animals.

What can be stated with greater confidence is this family’s established association with the absence of companionship, distance from familiarity, and being unaccustomed to others.

2. Forms and the semantic core

Several important forms of this family are recorded in Arabic.

وَحْش: An untamed animal unaccustomed to people. In some lexical uses, the word also concerns solitude and the emptiness of a place.

وُحُوش: The plural of وَحْش, meaning wild animals. This is the root’s only Quranic form.

وَحْشِيّ: An animal or something else unaccustomed to people or outside their familiar domestic environment, as in “wild ass.”

وَحْشَة: Loneliness, estrangement, unfamiliarity, or unease arising from the absence of familiar company.

اسْتَوْحَشَ: To feel lonely or estranged; to feel unfamiliar with or distant from someone or something.

تَوَحَّشَ: To become removed from familiar company and life; in another use, to become wild or live away from people.

أَوْحَشَ: Of a place, to become empty and uninhabited; in other uses, to cause loneliness or estrangement.

مُوحِش: A place or situation that is deserted, unfamiliar, or lonely because familiar company is absent.

These forms belong to the Arabic word family, but apart from الْوُحُوشُ, none occurs in the Quran under this root.

The semantic core

A defensible semantic core for this family is:

Distance from or absence of familiar company: in an animal’s relation to humans, in an empty place without companions, or in a person’s loneliness and estrangement.

This core helps explain how the different branches are related.

A wild animal is one not accustomed to human company.

A مُوحِش place lacks familiar human presence or has been emptied of its inhabitants.

A مُسْتَوْحِش person feels no familiarity or companionship and may be troubled by solitude or unfamiliar surroundings.

These three uses nevertheless do not mean exactly the same thing. An animal’s untamed condition, an empty place, and human loneliness are distinct situations sharing a semantic background.

Does وَحْش mean violence?

In modern Persian, “wild” is sometimes applied to a person who is extremely violent, cruel, or uncultivated.

That usage should not be projected back onto the meaning of وَحْش in the Quran.

The Arabic expression “wild ass” does not mean a predatory or cruel animal. It means an undomesticated ass living freely in the wild.

Modern expressions such as “savage behavior” or “human savagery” are therefore ethical and social uses of the word, rather than definitions applicable to all wild animals.

Likewise, a withdrawn person or someone who enjoys solitude is not violent or unethical merely for that reason. Fear or a lack of familiar company is not the same as predation or oppressive conduct.

3. Quranic usage

Al-Takwīr 81:5 — Wild animals gathered together

وَإِذَا الْوُحُوشُ حُشِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-wuḥūshu ḥushirat

Al-Takwīr 81:5

“And when the wild animals are gathered together.”

Here الْوُحُوشُ is the plural of وَحْش and refers to undomesticated animals.

حُشِرَتْ is a passive perfect verb from ح ش ر, expressing being gathered together.

The verse’s two main words thus belong to distinct roots:

الْوُحُوشُ comes from و ح ش and means wild animals.

حُشِرَتْ comes from ح ش ر and means “were gathered together.”

Their similarity in sound must not lead us to merge the roots or transfer one word’s meaning to the other.

Grammatically, الْوُحُوشُ is a nominative noun, while حُشِرَتْ is a feminine singular verb. Such agreement is familiar in Arabic with nonhuman plurals.

The passive construction also indicates that the agent of gathering is not explicitly named in this sentence.

The verse within Al-Takwīr

The verse stands among great events that transform the familiar order of the world.

Al-Takwīr 81:3 describes mountains being set in motion.

In Al-Takwīr 81:4, she-camels ten months pregnant, valuable to their owners, are abandoned.

In Al-Takwīr 81:5, wild animals are gathered together.

In Al-Takwīr 81:6, the seas undergo an immense transformation.

This sequence places the gathering of wild animals within a broader picture of the world’s transformation.

In ordinary life, wild animals often keep their distance from people, remain dispersed in their natural habitats, and sometimes flee from one another.

The verse, however, speaks of a time when these animals are gathered together.

The image would have been meaningful to an audience familiar with desert and plains animals. Creatures ordinarily dispersed and unaccustomed to human company are gathered in an extraordinary scene.

The verse itself does not explain how this gathering takes place.

Are the animals brought together in one place? Does this gathering belong to a particular stage of the Resurrection? Will all kinds of animals be present in the same condition?

الْوُحُوشُ حُشِرَتْ alone cannot answer these questions in detail.

What the verse explicitly states is the gathering of wild animals during that great event.

The connection with Al-Anʿām 6:38

Al-Anʿām 6:38 speaks of creatures on earth and birds, describing them as communities like human beings. It then states that they will be gathered to their Lord.

This verse is relevant to Al-Takwīr 81:5 because it shows that gathering in the Quran is not confined to humans.

Al-Takwīr 81:5 names a particular group as الْوُحُوشُ, whereas Al-Anʿām 6:38 speaks more broadly of terrestrial creatures and birds.

Mentioning wild animals therefore does not establish that only they will be gathered, to the exclusion of domestic animals or other living creatures.

Conversely, these verses do not authorize inventing unstated details about the presence, judgment, or fate of each kind of animal.

Gathering is stated in the Quran; a full account of its manner requires separate evidence.

4. Challenges in understanding and translation

Wild animals are not identical with predators

One limitation of some translations is rendering الْوُحُوشُ as “predatory animals.”

That rendering covers some examples but narrows the word’s scope.

Lions and wolves may be wild animals, but not every wild animal is a predator.

Gazelles, wild cattle, and wild asses are also undomesticated animals.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 uses السَّبُعُ, a word referring to a predatory animal.

This distinction shows that Arabic uses different expressions for predation and an undomesticated condition.

“Wild animals” is consequently more accurate than “predators” in Al-Takwīr 81:5: it preserves the undomesticated sense without excluding nonpredatory animals without cause.

الْوُحُوشُ does not mean frightened creatures

Because the related Persian word for dread is associated with fear today, a reader might suppose that الْوُحُوشُ means animals that have become terrified.

The word’s form does not support that reading.

الْوُحُوشُ names a category of animals rather than describing their momentary fear.

The great events described in the surah may be frightening to animals too, but their fear is not the lexical meaning of الْوُحُوشُ.

A discussion of their fear or flight requires contextual or independent evidence.

Is “wild” always opposed to “domestic”?

For animals, the distinction between wild and domestic is one of this family’s clearest uses.

It should not, however, be imposed on every meaning of the root.

Calling a place مُوحِش does not mean that it has failed to become domesticated like an animal.

The emphasis is on the absence of companionship and familiar human presence.

Similarly, a person’s dread or estrangement need not have anything to do with animals or nature.

“Wild as opposed to domestic” thus suits the animal branch, while the broader family concerns familiarity, companionship, and their absence.

5. Comparison with related roots

W Ḥ SH (وَحْش) compared with ʾ N S (أُنْس)

The root أ ن س is associated with companionship, familiarity, and feeling at ease with others, while also having other meanings and constructions.

In Arabic, اسْتِئْنَاس and اسْتِيحَاش can express opposite conditions: approaching familiarity and companionship, or withdrawing from them and feeling estranged.

This contrast helps explain وَحْش.

In the animal branch, a wild animal has not become accustomed to ordinary human presence and company.

Yet وَحْش should not simply be treated as the opposite of “human” as a species. The root أ ن س has various uses, not all of which mean a human being.

The central distinction in the animal usage concerns familiarity and its absence.

W Ḥ SH (وَحْش) compared with S B ʿ (سَبُع)

In its animal use, سَبُع refers to a predator.

وَحْش refers instead to an animal that is not domesticated or accustomed to people.

An animal can be both wild and predatory, as a lion is.

Another can be wild without being predatory, as a gazelle is.

These designations therefore overlap without being completely equivalent.

This distinction matters when translating Al-Takwīr 81:5: replacing “wild animals” with “predators” excludes nonpredatory animals from the verse’s scope.

W Ḥ SH (وَحْش) compared with D B B (دَبَب)

The root د ب ب concerns movement, and دَابَّة is used in the Quran for a moving creature.

This designation is broader than وَحْش because moving about does not depend on being wild or domestic.

An animal kept in a house or on a farm, for example, can also be a دَابَّة.

In Al-Anʿām 6:38, دَابَّة alongside birds introduces a broader range of living creatures.

In Al-Takwīr 81:5, الْوُحُوشُ refers to a more specific group.

“Moving creatures” is therefore not a precise equivalent for الْوُحُوشُ, just as “wild animals” cannot replace دَابَّة in every verse.

W Ḥ SH (وَحْش) compared with N ʿ M (نَعَم)

In Quranic usage, أَنْعَام denotes a familiar group of livestock used for food, clothing, carrying loads, and other human needs.

الْوُحُوشُ, by contrast, concerns animals that are undomesticated and unaccustomed to people.

These designations should not be made into an exhaustive classification of all living creatures.

Not all domestic animals necessarily belong under أَنْعَام, and all undomesticated creatures do not constitute a single zoological group.

The comparison clarifies two ways of naming: one identifies a familiar group of livestock, while the other concerns animals’ undomesticated condition and unfamiliarity with humans.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Takwīr 81:5, the most suitable rendering of الْوُحُوشُ is:

“Wild animals”

The proposed rendering of the full verse is therefore:

“And when the wild animals are gathered together.”

This rendering has several advantages.

First, it preserves the word’s basic meaning.

Second, unlike “predators,” it does not restrict the verse to hunting or carnivorous animals.

Third, unlike “frightened animals,” it does not substitute an emotion for the animals’ designation.

Fourth, the corresponding Persian expression is natural in contemporary Persian and clear to a general readership.

For Arabic derivatives absent from the Quran, equivalents should be chosen according to context.

Of an animal, وَحْشِيّ means “wild” or “undomesticated.”

Of a person, وَحْشَة may mean “loneliness,” “estrangement,” or “dread arising from solitude.”

Of a place, مُوحِش may mean “deserted,” “lonely and without company,” or “frightening,” depending on context.

اسْتَوْحَشَ may likewise be rendered “felt lonely” or “felt estranged.”

Preserving the root connection therefore does not require one identical equivalent in every use.

7. Conclusion

The root و ح ش concerns distance from familiarity and companionship. Its animal branch concerns an undomesticated condition, its spatial branch the absence of familiar human presence, and its human branch loneliness or estrangement.

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates the use of وَحْش for untamed animals, including hunted game, in language associated with the pre-Islamic period. The use was not limited to predators.

In the Quran, only الْوُحُوشُ occurs, in Al-Takwīr 81:5.

The verse speaks of wild animals being gathered amid the great events of the Resurrection. حُشِرَتْ belongs to the independent root ح ش ر; gathering must not be included in the definition of و ح ش itself.

The connection with Al-Anʿām 6:38 also shows that the Quran’s gathering of animals extends beyond the mention of wild animals in one verse. The manner of gathering and each group’s fate cannot, however, be determined without independent evidence.

The final definition is distance from or absence of familiarity and companionship: in animals, being undomesticated and unaccustomed to people; in other uses, lacking company or feeling lonely and estranged.

Within this family, being wild is not inherently a sign of violence, predation, or moral wrongdoing.

“Wild animals” is thus a precise and natural equivalent for الْوُحُوشُ in Al-Takwīr 81:5, consistent with the word’s historical and Quranic meaning.
