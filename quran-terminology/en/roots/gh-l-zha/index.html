Thickness, coarseness, hardness, firmness, and severity in conduct, covenants, and punishment

Arabic gh-l-ẓ concerns thickness, coarseness, and hardness. Physically, ghalīẓ describes something thick, substantial, or rough rather than thin and delicate. Related ghilẓa denotes hardness or sternness, ghilāẓ is the plural of ghalīẓ, and istaghlaẓa means becoming thick or stout.

The family extends beyond material objects to conduct, speech, social relationships, covenants, and severe suffering or punishment.

Cloth may be ghalīẓ, meaning thick and coarse; a person may be ghalīẓ in dealing harshly with others; a covenant may be ghalīẓ as a firm, serious commitment not to be taken lightly.

These uses share a semantic background, but each derivative takes its precise meaning from what it describes.

The Quran uses four principal formations: ghalīẓ and its plural ghilāẓ, ghilẓa, the imperative ughluẓ, and the verb istaghlaẓa.

They concern firm covenants, harsh conduct, severe punishment, angels, and a plant growing thick. They cannot all be reduced to violence, hard-heartedness, or strictness.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

Arabic ghilaẓ contrasts with riqqa.

Physically, riqqa concerns thinness and delicacy, while ghilaẓ expresses thickness and coarseness.

Consider thin cloth that transmits light and folds easily. Thick cloth may be several times more substantial and harder to fold.

The latter can be called thawbun ghalīẓun.

Rope, animal hide, tree trunks, and terrain can also be described with this root.

Ghalīẓ need not mean hard like stone. Something may be thick yet remain flexible or soft.

Thickness, hardness, roughness, and inflexibility should therefore be distinguished. They may coincide without being identical.

A Pre-Islamic Witness: Thick Camel Hide in Zuhayr's Poetry

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma includes:

غَلِيظٍ عَلَى مَجْذَى الْقُرَادِ كَأَنَّمَا

ghalīẓin ʿalā majdhā al-qurādi ka-annamā

بِجَانِبِ صَفْوَانٍ يَزِلُّ وَيَرْتَقِي

bi-jānibi ṣafwānin yazillu wa-yartaqī

Translation:

“Thick-skinned where the tick settles, as though it slips and climbs on the side of smooth stone.”

This part of the ode describes a powerful camel.

Ghalīẓ concerns the thickness of its hide.

Majdhā al-qurādi is where a tick attaches to the animal.

The hide is so thick and firm that the tick struggles to attach, as though moving over smooth rock without finding a secure hold.

Here ghalīẓ is entirely physical, concerning the hide's thickness and strength.

Neither broken covenants, ethical harshness, nor punishment is involved.

The verse appears in the collected poetry attributed to Zuhayr, in the ode beginning wa-yawma talāfaytu al-ṣibā an yafūtanī.

This attribution provides an important witness from the pre-Islamic poetic tradition for physical ghalīẓ, although attribution alone cannot securely date every individual verse.

Ghalīẓ Terrain

Lexical sources use arḍun ghalīẓatun for hard, rough, or uneven ground that is difficult to traverse or work.

Soft, level ground differs from ground with a hard, rough surface.

Ghilaẓ here concerns the terrain itself.

Such ground need not necessarily be stony: dictionaries also describe hard ground without many stones as ghalīẓ.

Surface roughness and hardness matter, rather than a particular kind of stone.

Harsh Conduct and Speech

Rajulun ghalīẓun describes a person harsh in conduct or speech.

Ghallaẓa ʿalayhi and aghlaẓa lahu fī al-qawli concern treating someone sternly or speaking harshly.

A manager might address an error through humiliation and aggression instead of a clear, respectful explanation.

Such conduct can illustrate harshness in speech.

Someone else may calmly and respectfully make a firm decision and refuse to abandon a clear right.

That person can be steadfast without necessarily being abusive or unjust.

Firmness and intensity in conduct are therefore not always violence or ill temper.

Hardness and Firmness in Nonmaterial Matters

Amrun ghalīẓun describes a difficult, weighty matter, while ʿahdun ghalīẓun describes a firm, emphatic covenant.

Physical thickness is no longer involved.

A covenant is not cloth or rope whose thickness can be measured. Ghalīẓ expresses the commitment's seriousness and firmness.

In punishment-related uses, the family describes intense, heavy suffering.

The Quran clearly displays this semantic extension.

The physical image alone does not prove that every ethical or social use arose from it at one specific historical moment. Early witnesses illuminate the material sense; Quranic uses establish broader meanings in Arabic at the time of revelation.

2. Forms and Derivatives

Recognized Arabic forms include:

  • Ghaluẓa / yaghluẓu: became thick, coarse, hard, or rough.
  • Ghilaẓ: thickness, coarseness, or hardness.
  • Ghilẓa: hardness, sternness, or severity in conduct or a situation.
  • Ghilāẓa: coarseness, roughness, or strictness.
  • Ghalīẓ: thick, stout, hard, harsh, or severe, according to its object.
  • Ghalīẓa: feminine of ghalīẓ.
  • Ghilāẓ: plural of ghalīẓ.
  • Ghallaẓa / yughalliẓu: made thicker or harder; more broadly, increased emphasis or severity.
  • Taghlīẓ: making thicker or harder; of covenants and oaths, reinforcing or emphasizing.
  • Aghlaẓa: acted or spoke sternly; in another use, found something thick.
  • Ighlāẓ: stern or firm treatment.
  • Istaghlaẓa / yastaghliẓu: became thick, stout, or strong.
  • Istighlāẓ: becoming thick or stout.
  • Mughallaẓ: thickened or hardened; of commitments, reinforced or intensified.

The Quran uses four principal formations.

Ghalīẓ and plural ghilāẓ describe covenants, punishment, the heart, and angels.

Ghilẓa concerns sternness and firmness in confrontation.

Ughluẓ is an imperative calling for stern treatment.

Istaghlaẓa describes a plant that has grown thick.

Morphological Distinctions

Ghalīẓ is an adjective on the faʿīl pattern, understood according to the noun it qualifies.

Ghilāẓ is its plural.

Ughluẓ is the imperative of the basic triliteral verb ghaluẓa. The Quran uses it in wa-ughluẓ ʿalayhim.

Its written resemblance to Form IV aghlaẓa must not obscure the distinction.

Istaghlaẓa is Form X and means becoming thick or stout in its Quranic occurrence.

Form X here does not mean requesting thickness, but acquiring that condition.

Morphology clarifies these possibilities; syntax and context determine the final meaning.

3. Quranic Uses

A. Mīthāqan Ghalīẓan: A Firm, Solemn Covenant

An-Nisaʾ 4:21

وَكَيْفَ تَأْخُذُونَهُ وَقَدْ أَفْضَىٰ بَعْضُكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا

wa-kayfa taʾkhudhūnahu wa-qad afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍin wa-akhadhna minkum mīthāqan ghalīẓā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:21

“And how could you take it, when you have gone in unto one another and they have taken from you a solemn covenant?”

Ghalīẓan qualifies mīthāqan.

Mīthāq belongs to w-th-q, concerning firmness and commitment.

Adding ghalīẓ emphasizes the covenant's importance, seriousness, and strength.

A mīthāq ghalīẓ is therefore not a physically thick or harsh covenant, but a serious, firm commitment that must not be treated lightly.

Why Is the Marriage Covenant Called Ghalīẓ?

An-Nisaʾ 4:20 describes men wishing to replace one wife with another and prohibits taking back wealth given to the wife.

An-Nisaʾ 4:21 explains the wrong through the spouses' intimate relationship and established covenant.

Akhadhna minkum shows women taking a covenant from men.

Marriage is not merely a privilege for one party: the man has also accepted responsibilities toward his wife.

A man may decide to separate after years of marriage. That decision does not itself authorize disregarding his wife's financial rights or using pressure and slander to recover what he gave her.

The marriage covenant's seriousness and associated rights must not become instruments of exploitation by either party.

Its firmness does not prohibit every separation. Other Quranic passages address separation and just conduct afterward.

Ghalīẓ here expresses serious commitment and weighty responsibility, not maintaining every relationship under all conditions and at any cost.

Forqan also examines this in its separate principle on marriage as a solemn covenant.

The Covenant of the Children of Israel

An-Nisaʾ 4:154 also uses mīthāqan ghalīẓan.

It concerns the covenant taken from the Children of Israel.

The expression is therefore not exclusive to marriage.

Ghalīẓ again emphasizes the commitment's firmness and importance.

Its particular content comes from the context of the Children of Israel, not the adjective alone.

An-Nisaʾ 4:155 then mentions their breaking the covenant. The connection underscores the serious responsibility to honour it.

The Covenant of the Prophets

Al-Ahzab 33:7

وَإِذْ أَخَذْنَا مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ مِيثَاقَهُمْ وَمِنْكَ وَمِنْ نُوحٍ وَإِبْرَاهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَى ابْنِ مَرْيَمَ وَأَخَذْنَا مِنْهُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا

wa-idh akhadhnā mina al-nabiyyīna mīthāqahum wa-minka wa-min Nūḥin wa-Ibrāhīma wa-Mūsā wa-ʿĪsā ibni Maryama wa-akhadhnā minhum mīthāqan ghalīẓā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:7

“And when We took from the Prophets their covenant, and from you, and from Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus son of Mary; and We took from them a solemn covenant.”

God is the agent taking the covenant.

Ghalīẓ again qualifies mīthāq.

The commitment differs from marriage: this is the Prophets' covenant.

The expression thus appears in three settings: marriage, the Children of Israel's covenant, and the Prophets' covenant.

Their common feature is firm, serious commitment.

This does not make their content, parties, or resulting rights identical.

Here ghilẓa describes the strength of commitment, not violence between the parties.

B. Ghalīẓ al-Qalb: Harshness in Human Conduct

Al ʿImran 3:159

فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ لِنْتَ لَهُمْ وَلَوْ كُنْتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَوَكِّلِينَ

fa-bi-mā raḥmatin mina Allāhi linta lahum wa-law kunta faẓẓan ghalīẓa al-qalbi la-nfaḍḍū min ḥawlika fa-ʿfu ʿanhum wa-staghfir lahum wa-shāwirhum fī al-amri fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā Allāhi inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-mutawakkilīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:159

“So by mercy from The God you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. So pardon them, ask forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter. Then when you have resolved, trust in The God. Indeed, The God loves those who trust in Him.”

Ghalīẓ al-qalb contrasts with gentleness and mercy.

Qalb here concerns the person's inner disposition. It does not describe physical heart thickness.

Ethically, it can describe hard-heartedness and lack of appropriate gentleness or flexibility toward others.

Faẓẓan and Ghalīẓ al-Qalb

The verse places two descriptions together.

Faẓẓ concerns roughness in conduct and speech.

Ghalīẓ al-qalb emphasizes a harsh disposition and inward response.

Someone may speak to others using humiliating language, making their outward harshness evident.

Another may avoid shouting yet be so hard-hearted that they allow no opportunity for correction and disregard others' difficulties.

These situations may coincide without being identical.

The verse uses distinct words; reducing both to “harsh” loses that distinction.

Their juxtaposition alone does not establish an absolute boundary between inward character and outward behaviour. Both can express aspects of harshness.

Gentle Treatment and Firm Decisions

The verse begins with Muhammad's mercy and gentleness, then mentions pardon, seeking forgiveness, and consultation.

It then commands trust in God after reaching a decision.

Gentleness and kindness therefore do not mean inability to decide.

A manager may respectfully examine an employee's error, listen, and offer an opportunity to improve, then reach a clear, firm decision.

This can combine kindness with steadfastness.

Another manager may humiliate others and refuse to hear their views to display power.

That is harshness capable of undermining trust and cooperation, rather than firmness of decision.

Al ʿImran 3:159 also states the consequence of harshness and hard-heartedness: people dispersing from around Muhammad.

Ghalīẓ al-qalb is blameworthy here, but that negative value must not be transferred to every use of gh-l-ẓ.

C. Wa-ughluẓ ʿAlayhim: Sternness in Confrontation

At-Tawba 9:73

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ جَاهِدِ الْكُفَّارَ وَالْمُنَافِقِينَ وَاغْلُظْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَمَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu jāhidi al-kuffāra wa-al-munāfiqīna wa-ughluẓ ʿalayhim wa-maʾwāhum jahannamu wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr

Al-Tawbah 9:73

“O Prophet, strive against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be stern with them. Their refuge is Hell—an evil destination.”

Ughluẓ is an imperative.

Muhammad is addressed, and ʿalayhim identifies the groups toward whom sternness is directed.

This is stern, firm treatment, not physical thickness.

The verb alone does not specify the action required.

Sternness may appear in speech, serious opposition, resistance to harmful conduct, or military confrontation in wartime.

The action must be understood from jāhid, the circumstances of those involved, and the relevant Quranic passages.

Ughluẓ alone should not be read as ordering killing, torture, or unlimited mistreatment of all religious opponents.

Quranic striving and confrontation also entail ethical limits and responsibilities.

Al-Baqara 2:190 prohibits transgression in war.

Al-Maʾida 5:8 forbids allowing hostility toward a group to displace justice.

Al-Mumtahana 60:8 does not prohibit kindness and justice toward those who have not fought believers or expelled them from their homes.

Sternness in a particular confrontation therefore does not authorize abandoning justice and human rights.

The same expression recurs in At-Tahrim 66:9.

It again concerns sternness and firmness toward the specified groups, without defining every action through the word alone.

How Does This Differ from Gentleness in Al ʿImran?

Al ʿImran 3:159 describes Muhammad's avoidance of harshness and hard-heartedness toward his companions.

At-Tawba 9:73 and At-Tahrim 66:9 command sternness in confronting specified groups.

These situations should not be deemed contradictory without considering their parties and circumstances.

Kindness toward those needing guidance, correction, and consultation can coexist with firmness against conduct requiring serious opposition.

This difference does not establish a rule of gentleness toward every believer and harshness toward every nonbeliever in all circumstances.

The Quran relates conduct to justice, responsibility, the circumstances of confrontation, and people's actions.

Ughluẓ must therefore be understood in its sentence and within Quranic limits.

D. Ghilẓatan: Firmness in Battle

At-Tawba 9:123

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُمْ مِنَ الْكُفَّارِ وَلْيَجِدُوا فِيكُمْ غِلْظَةً وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُتَّقِينَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū qātilū alladhīna yalūnakum mina al-kuffāri wa-l-yajidū fīkum ghilẓatan wa-ʿlamū anna Allāha maʿa al-muttaqīn

Al-Tawbah 9:123

“O you who have believed, fight those disbelievers who are near you, and let them find firmness in you. Know that The God is with those who are mindful of Him.”

Ghilẓa occurs in a context of war.

Qātilū commands military confrontation.

Wa-l-yajidū fīkum ghilẓatan calls for the opposing side to encounter firmness in the believers.

Here ghilẓa concerns steadfastness rather than displaying weakness in confrontation.

A group under attack might stand firm in defending life and security, preventing an opponent from exploiting fear or weakness.

This illustrates firmness in war.

It does not authorize combatants to commit any violence, transgression, or injustice they choose.

The verse ends by stating that God is with those who are mindful of Him.

Warfare and firmness are thus not detached from taqwā.

Ghilẓa itself does not mean victory, killing more people, or inflicting greater injury. Such outcomes cannot be imposed on the root without evidence.

E. Ghilāẓun Shidādun: Describing Angels

At-Tahrim 66:6

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا قُوا أَنْفُسَكُمْ وَأَهْلِيكُمْ نَارًا وَقُودُهَا النَّاسُ وَالْحِجَارَةُ عَلَيْهَا مَلَائِكَةٌ غِلَاظٌ شِدَادٌ لَا يَعْصُونَ اللَّهَ مَا أَمَرَهُمْ وَيَفْعَلُونَ مَا يُؤْمَرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū qū anfusakum wa-ahlīkum nāran waqūduhā al-nāsu wa-al-ḥijāratu ʿalayhā malāʾikatun ghilāẓun shidādun lā yaʿṣūna Allāha mā amarahum wa-yafʿalūna mā yuʾmarūn

Al-Taḥrīm 66:6

“O you who have believed, protect yourselves and your families from a fire whose fuel is people and stones. Over it are stern, strong angels who do not disobey The God in what He commands them, and who do what they are commanded.”

Ghilāẓ is the plural of ghalīẓ.

Shidād belongs to the separate root sh-d-d.

Both describe angels appointed over the fire.

Ghilẓa here concerns sternness and severity in carrying out their assignment.

The verse continues by affirming their obedience and performance of what they are commanded.

Their description belongs to the setting of assignment and obedience.

Ghilāẓ and Shidād

Ghilāẓ can emphasize their stern, firm, unyielding conduct in that assignment.

Shidād emphasizes strength and power.

The descriptions overlap but should not be treated as completely synonymous without examination.

Nor does their combination establish that one describes only inward character and the other only bodily strength.

Such a boundary would require independent evidence.

Ghilāẓun shidādun does not imply unjust or disobedient angels: the verse explicitly states their obedience.

Stern execution of an assignment differs from injustice or exceeding its limits.

The expression does not determine their bodily size, shape, or biology. It describes severity and performance rather than explaining physical nature.

F. ʿAdhābin Ghalīẓin: Severe, Weighty Punishment

Hud 11:58

وَلَمَّا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا نَجَّيْنَا هُودًا وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَعَهُ بِرَحْمَةٍ مِنَّا وَنَجَّيْنَاهُمْ مِنْ عَذَابٍ غَلِيظٍ

wa-lammā jāʾa amrunā najjaynā Hūdan wa-alladhīna āmanū maʿahu bi-raḥmatin minnā wa-najjaynāhum min ʿadhābin ghalīẓ

Hūd 11:58

“When Our command came, We saved Hud and those who believed with him through mercy from Us, and We saved them from a severe punishment.”

Ghalīẓ qualifies punishment.

It denotes severity and weight, not physical thickness.

The context concerns the rescue of Hud and his companions and the fate of ʿAd.

The severe punishment is therefore linked to ʿAd's fate in context; explanations differ on whether the final rescue clause specifically concerns worldly punishment or punishment in the hereafter.

The secure lexical point is its severity. Ghalīẓ alone identifies neither its time nor a particular kind of otherworldly punishment.

Other Uses of ʿAdhāb Ghalīẓ

Ibrahim 14:17 places severe punishment after a scene of someone struggling to swallow a drink, with death approaching from every side but not ending their life.

Luqman 31:24 describes brief enjoyment followed by being compelled into severe punishment.

Fussilat 41:50 describes someone claiming prosperity and mercy as their entitlement and doubting resurrection, then speaks of making disbelievers taste severe punishment.

Ghalīẓ emphasizes severity and weight in each case.

It does not alone specify punishment's duration, physical composition, or every stage of suffering.

It means severe or hard. Duration, kinds of suffering, causes, and outcomes depend on other parts of the passages.

Severity in this family is not confined to human conduct: a condition or event can itself be ghalīẓ.

4. Fa-staghlaẓa: A Plant Growing Thick and Firm

Al-Fath 48:29

مُحَمَّدٌ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ وَالَّذِينَ مَعَهُ أَشِدَّاءُ عَلَى الْكُفَّارِ رُحَمَاءُ بَيْنَهُمْ تَرَاهُمْ رُكَّعًا سُجَّدًا يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانًا سِيمَاهُمْ فِي وُجُوهِهِمْ مِنْ أَثَرِ السُّجُودِ ذَٰلِكَ مَثَلُهُمْ فِي التَّوْرَاةِ وَمَثَلُهُمْ فِي الْإِنْجِيلِ كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ فَآزَرَهُ فَاسْتَغْلَظَ فَاسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ يُعْجِبُ الزُّرَّاعَ لِيَغِيظَ بِهِمُ الْكُفَّارَ وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ مِنْهُمْ مَغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

Muḥammadun rasūlu Allāhi wa-alladhīna maʿahu ashiddaʾu ʿalā al-kuffāri ruḥamāʾu baynahum tarāhum rukkaʿan sujjadan yabtaghūna faḍlan mina Allāhi wa-riḍwānan sīmāhum fī wujūhihim min athari al-sujūdi dhālika mathaluhum fī al-tawrāti wa-mathaluhum fī al-injīli ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾahu fa-āzarahu fa-staghlaẓa fa-stawā ʿalā sūqihi yuʿjibu al-zurrāʿa li-yaghīẓa bihimu al-kuffāra waʿada Allāhu alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti minhum maghfiratan wa-ajran ʿaẓīmā

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Muhammad is the Messenger of The God. Those with him are firm against the disbelievers and merciful among themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, seeking bounty from The God and His approval. Their mark is on their faces from the effect of prostration. That is their description in the Torah; their description in the Gospel is like a crop that brings forth its shoot and strengthens it, then grows thick and stands upon its stalks, pleasing the growers, so that through them He enrages the disbelievers. The God has promised those among them who believe and do righteous deeds forgiveness and a great reward.”

Fa-staghlaẓa is Form X.

Unlike conduct or punishment uses, its subject is plant growth.

The plant first produces shoots and new branches or growth.

It then grows stronger and its stalks develop.

Istaghlaẓa describes its becoming thick and stout.

Fa-āzarahu and Fa-staghlaẓa

The verse uses two different verbs.

Fa-āzarahu belongs to ʾ-z-r and concerns strengthening and support.

Fa-staghlaẓa belongs to gh-l-ẓ and expresses becoming thick and stout.

The stages are related in outcome but not identical in meaning.

A plant begins with a slender stem. As shoots grow and its structure strengthens, the stalks may thicken and become better able to stand and bear its weight.

Strengthening and thickening are related features, but translating both simply as “grew strong” loses the distinction.

Fa-staghlaẓa contributes specifically the image of thickening.

Fa-stawā ʿAlā Sūqihi

After thickening, the verse says the plant stands firmly on its stalks.

Istawā belongs to another root and concerns establishment in a suitable condition.

In this image, thicker stalks relate to the plant's ability to stand firmly.

Standing on its stalks comes from the full expression; it is not the lexical meaning of istaghlaẓa itself.

Why Does the Plant Image Matter?

The verse shows that gh-l-ẓ retains physical thickness in the Quran.

For the plant, ghilẓa is neither blameworthy nor violent.

It forms part of healthy growth and increasing strength.

This prevents reducing the whole root to harshness, ill temper, or ethical sternness.

Context applies the image to the growth and firmness of the community accompanying Muhammad.

The verb directly means the plant growing thick; its relationship to the believing community emerges from the whole comparison.

Nor does the image make every group's numerical or political growth evidence of truth. The described community's worth rests on faith, righteous action, and the verse's other qualities, not thick stalks alone.

5. Distinctions from Related Roots

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with R-q-q: Thickness and Thinness

Arabic ghilaẓ contrasts with riqqa.

Physically, one expresses thickness and the other thinness.

A thick rope differs from a thin strand.

Ethically, the contrast can extend to sternness and gentleness.

A thin object is not necessarily kind, nor a thick object violent.

Physical and ethical uses need separate examination.

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with L-y-n: Hardness and Softness

Līn concerns softness and gentleness.

Al ʿImran 3:159 places linta lahum against ghalīẓ al-qalb.

Hard-heartedness clearly contrasts with gentle conduct in that context.

This does not make every softness commendable and every hardness blameworthy.

Some circumstances require gentleness to repair relationships; others require firmness to prevent injustice and protect rights.

Conduct's value depends on its object and circumstances.

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with F-ẓ-ẓ: Harsh Conduct and Harsh Disposition

Faẓāẓa concerns rough conduct and speech.

Ghilẓa has a wider field, describing bodies, covenants, punishment, and conduct.

They occur together in Al ʿImran 3:159.

Outward harshness and a harsh disposition may thus coincide.

Ghalīẓ cannot be reduced entirely to faẓẓ, since other Quranic uses do not fit that definition.

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with Q-s-w: Hardness and Hard-Heartedness

Quranic qaswa concerns the heart becoming hard and unreceptive to truth and reminders.

Al-Baqara 2:74 describes hearts like stones or harder.

This approaches ghalīẓ al-qalb in Al ʿImran 3:159.

The roots nevertheless remain distinct.

Ghilaẓ can also express physical thickness, severe punishment, and firm covenants.

Q-s-w in heart-related Quranic uses emphasizes hardness and unreceptiveness.

Not everything ghalīẓ is hard-hearted, nor does every qaswa mean physical thickness.

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with Sh-d-d: Hardness and Strength

Shidda concerns force, firmness, hardness, and strength.

The Quran uses this family widely.

At-Tahrim 66:6 describes angels as ghilāẓun shidādun.

The descriptions overlap, emphasizing the severity of their assignment and capacity to execute it.

Shidda does not always mean ghilẓa.

A force may be intense without anything becoming thick or rough.

Conversely, cloth may be thick without possessing great strength.

Thickness and force are distinguishable properties.

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with Th-kh-n: Thickness and Density

Arabic th-kh-n concerns thickness, density, and weight.

Physically, thakhīn and ghalīẓ can overlap.

Both may describe a thick object.

Quranic derivatives of th-kh-n also occur in particular military contexts.

Those military senses or outcomes should not be transferred to istaghlaẓa describing a plant.

Plant growth in Al-Fath 48:29 makes thickening clear.

Gh-l-ẓ Compared with Gh-l-b: Hardness and Prevailing

Gh-l-b concerns prevailing and victory.

Gh-l-ẓ concerns thickness, coarseness, and severity.

Someone may prevail through power and firmness, but victory is a separate outcome rather than the meaning of ghilẓa.

Ghilẓa in At-Tawba 9:123 occurs in war but does not itself mean victory.

Written resemblance or an association between hardness and power does not identify the roots.

6. Challenges of Understanding and Translation

Is Ghalīẓ Always Negative?

No.

Ghalīẓ al-qalb is blameworthy in Al ʿImran 3:159.

Mīthāq ghalīẓ in An-Nisaʾ 4:21 is a firm, serious covenant.

Istaghlaẓa in Al-Fath 48:29 belongs to plant growth.

Positive or negative value therefore comes from the object and context, not the root alone.

Is Mīthāq Ghalīẓ a Harsh Covenant?

No.

Here ghilẓa concerns firm, serious commitment.

“A harsh covenant” inappropriately substitutes a physical or behavioural image for the actual use.

Does Ughluẓ ʿAlayhim Mean Unlimited Mistreatment?

No.

The Quran uses it in striving and confrontation.

The action and its ethical limits require the relevant passages as a whole.

Sternness and firmness do not themselves authorize injustice, torture, betrayal, or violation of others' rights.

Does Ghilāẓ Shidād Mean Unjust Angels?

No.

It describes sternness in their assignment.

The same verse explicitly states their obedience.

Stern execution of divine command must not be equated with injustice or disobedience.

Is ʿAdhāb Ghalīẓ Physically Thick Punishment?

No.

Ghalīẓ describes the punishment's severity.

It alone determines neither size, shape, nor material composition.

Does Istaghlaẓa Mean Only Growing Strong?

In Al-Fath 48:29 it describes a plant growing thick and stout.

Strengthening also emerges from fa-āzarahu and the whole image.

Translating istaghlaẓa only as “grew strong” blurs its distinction from the preceding verb.

“Grew thick” or “became stout” is more precise.

Are Sternness and Kindness Incompatible?

No.

Al ʿImran 3:159 emphasizes Muhammad's gentleness and mercy toward his companions.

At-Tawba 9:73 and At-Tahrim 66:9 describe sternness toward specified groups.

Conduct should correspond to the situation, people involved, and responsibility.

Kindness does not abandon rights; firmness does not abandon justice.

7. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

Suggested equivalents for the Quranic forms:

Ghalīẓan in mīthāqan ghalīẓan: firm, solemn, or emphatically binding.

A suitable rendering: “a solemn covenant.”

Ghalīẓ al-qalb — Al ʿImran 3:159: hard-hearted.

Wa-ughluẓ ʿalayhim — At-Tawba 9:73 and At-Tahrim 66:9: and be stern with them.

Ghilẓatan — At-Tawba 9:123: firmness, sternness, or steadfastness in confrontation.

In the sentence: “And let them find firmness in you.”

Ghilāẓun — At-Tahrim 66:6: stern or severe in treatment.

ʿAdhābin ghalīẓin: severe punishment.

Fa-staghlaẓa — Al-Fath 48:29: then it grew thick, or became stout.

Non-Quranic equivalents likewise depend on context:

Ghaluẓa: became thick, coarse, or hard.

Ghilaẓ: thickness, coarseness, or hardness.

Ghilẓa: hardness, sternness, or firmness.

Ghalīẓ: thick, stout, coarse, hard, or severe.

Ghallaẓa: made thicker or harder; of a commitment, reinforced.

Taghlīẓ: intensifying, strengthening, or increasing hardness.

Aghlaẓa fī al-qawli: spoke sternly or harshly.

Istaghlaẓa: became thick and stout.

Mughallaẓ: thickened, hardened, or reinforced, according to use.

One fixed equivalent should not be imposed on the whole family.

Thick hide, firm covenants, hard hearts, steadfast confrontation, severe punishment, and thickening plants are related uses requiring their own suitable equivalents.

Conclusion

Arabic gh-l-ẓ concerns thickness, coarseness, and hardness.

Physically it describes stout hide, cloth, rope, plants, and other objects. The witness attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma illustrates thick camel hide.

Nonmaterial uses concern harsh conduct, firm commitment, and severe suffering or punishment.

The Quran displays four principal formations: ghalīẓ with plural ghilāẓ, ghilẓa, ughluẓ, and istaghlaẓa.

Mīthāq ghalīẓ describes marriage, the Children of Israel's covenant, and the Prophets' covenant, expressing firm, serious commitment.

Ghalīẓ al-qalb in Al ʿImran 3:159 concerns hard-heartedness and harsh conduct, contrasting with gentleness and mercy.

Ughluẓ ʿalayhim and ghilẓa express sternness and firmness in confrontation and war without themselves authorizing injustice or transgression.

Ghilāẓ shidād in At-Tahrim 66:6 describes angels carrying out their assignment sternly and firmly.

ʿAdhāb ghalīẓ expresses severe punishment, not physical thickness or composition.

Istaghlaẓa in Al-Fath 48:29 describes plant growth and thickening. Physical meaning remains present in the Quran, preventing reduction of the family to violence or ethical sternness.

The core field of gh-l-ẓ is thickness and coarseness rather than thinness and delicacy; broader uses convey severity, hardness, and firmness appropriate to what is described.

The root itself denotes neither virtue nor vice. Thickening plants may indicate healthy growth, firm covenants may be valuable, hard-heartedness may drive people apart, and firmness against aggression may protect rights.

Each derivative must therefore be understood through form and context. Ethical value and outcome do not come from the root alone.
