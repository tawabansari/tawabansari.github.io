The left side, ill omen, and an unhappy outcome: spatial direction, linguistic beliefs, and human destiny in the Quran

Early Arabic SH ʾ M has two established semantic fields: the left side and movement or position to the left; and ill omen, inauspiciousness, or an unhappy outcome—a condition associated with harm, loss, or an unwelcome consequence.

Both fields occur within one lexical family. Shuʾm contrasts with yumn, blessedness or good fortune, while the Y M N family also includes the right side. Mashʾama can denote the left side or an inauspicious condition.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets illustrates both uses. This connection does not prove that every meaning developed from one original image or that Arabs in every period considered the left inherently ominous. The evidence establishes spatial and evaluative senses in early language; the precise dating and transmission of each witness require separate examination.

The Quran uses only الْمَشْأَمَة from this root: twice in Al-Waqiʿah 56:9 and once in Al-Balad 90:19. Both verses use أَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَة to identify a group within judgment and the final outcome.

1. Lexical forms and core meanings

Important members of the family include:

  • شَأْمَة: the left side or leftward direction.
  • مَشْأَمَة: the left side; also inauspiciousness or an unfavorable condition.
  • شُؤْم: ill omen, inauspiciousness, or an unhappy outcome, contrasting with good fortune.
  • أَشْأَم: more inauspicious or deeply ill-fated; in spatial usage, left.
  • شُؤْمَى: a feminine form used for the left side or limb, such as the left hand.
  • مَشْؤُوم: someone or something considered inauspicious.
  • تَشَاؤُم: regarding something as an ill omen or expecting an unwelcome outcome from it.
  • شَأْم and شَآم: the geographical name al-Sham, connected with the leftward direction in lexical explanations.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran. Shuʾm, ashʾam, mashʾum, and tashaʾum do not occur in these forms. The Quranic derivative is mashʾama.

Mashʾama follows the pattern mafʿala, which can concern direction, location, or condition. Its contrast with maymana is the central Quranic clue.

The family should retain two branches in its definition: left in spatial usage, and inauspiciousness or an unhappy outcome in evaluative usage. Their cultural association is clear, but the available evidence does not securely establish which historically preceded the other.

2. Early Arabic evidence

The left side in al-Aʿsha's poetry

In a verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿsha, describing a wild bull resisting hunting dogs, we read:

فَأَنْحَى عَلَى شُؤْمَى يَدَيْهِ فَذَادَهَا
بِأَظْمَأَ مِنْ فَرْعِ الذُّؤَابَةِ أَسْحَمَا

fa-anḥā ʿalā shuʾmā yadayhi fa-dhādahā / bi-aẓmaʾa min farʿi al-dhuʾābati asḥamā

The image is of the animal turning toward its left foreleg and driving the dogs away with a dark, slender horn projecting from its head.

شُؤْمَى يَدَيْهِ refers to the animal's left forelimb. It specifies bodily position; it does not declare that limb genuinely ominous or predict an unhappy event.

The witness establishes a spatial use in early poetry. Context must therefore prevent every derivative from being reduced to ill omen or bad fortune.

An unhappy outcome in Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma's poetry

In his celebrated Muʿallaqa, the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr describes war's devastation:

فَتُنْتِجْ لَكُمْ غِلْمَانَ أَشْأَمَ كُلُّهُمْ
كَأَحْمَرِ عَادٍ ثُمَّ تُرْضِعْ فَتَفْطِمِ

fa-tuntij lakum ghilmāna ashʾama kulluhum / ka-aḥmari ʿādin thumma turḍiʿ fa-tafṭimi

Meaning: War will bear you ill-fated sons, each like “the red man of ʿAd,” then nurse and wean them. War is pictured as a creature bearing and raising an ominous generation. Commentators connect the comparison with the man who hamstrung the she-camel of Thamud; the reference to ʿAd in the transmitted verse should not be treated as an independently established historical identification.

Ashʾam concerns war's destructive consequences here. The poet describes generations and events bringing harm and calamity.

The example matters because inauspiciousness need not mean an unknown force or a causeless event. Even in early usage it can describe consequences of harmful conduct, such as starting a war.

Ashʾam therefore does not mean farther left here; it emphasizes harm and an unhappy outcome.

Ominous and favorable signs

An early poetic verse, variously attributed to al-Muraqqish or Khuzar ibn Lawdhan, reads:

فَإِذَا الْأَشَائِمُ كَالْأَيَامِنِ
وَالْأَيَامِنُ كَالْأَشَائِمْ

fa-idhā al-ashāʾimu ka-al-ayāmini / wa-al-ayāminu ka-al-ashāʾim

Meaning:

“Those considered ominous proved like those considered favorable, and those considered favorable like those considered ominous.”

Related verses concern amulets and signs to which people looked for protection or indications of good and harm.

The contrast between al-ashaʾim and al-ayamin illustrates opposing lexical families for things considered unfavorable and favorable.

The poem itself also challenges certainty about these signs. What is considered ominous may prove like something favorable, and vice versa.

The witness cannot establish that every pre-Islamic Arab shared the same beliefs about omens. It shows the vocabulary and the possibility of criticizing such beliefs within that linguistic setting.

What the early evidence establishes

The witnesses distinguish three uses: left as an actual position; inauspiciousness as a description of an unwelcome consequence; and treating something as an omen as a human judgment about signs.

Ill omen should therefore not always be reduced to superstition, nor should every spatial left be read as an unhappy outcome.

3. The left, inauspiciousness, and the name al-Sham

Some Arabic uses extend right and left beyond spatial direction into associations with favorable and unfavorable outcomes.

Yumn denotes blessedness or good fortune, opposed by shuʾm. Maymana and mashʾama can accordingly evoke both opposing sides and contrasting conditions or outcomes.

Orientation practices and cultural evaluations can help explain this association. Current evidence nevertheless does not conclusively establish whether left preceded the inauspicious sense or the two developed alongside one another.

Bodily and geographical right and left are relative directions. Neither inherently denotes virtue or sin. Being left-handed does not itself make someone ominous or ill-fated.

Lexicographers also explain al-Shaʾm or al-Shaʾam through the leftward direction. In this account, the region lay to the left relative to an observer's point and direction of orientation. This is a transmitted lexical explanation, not conclusive proof of the name's historical origin.

The geographical connection should not become a moral judgment about the region or its people. A spatial name contains no verdict about its inhabitants' goodness or badness. The Quran does not use al-Sham as this geographical name.

4. Quranic uses of SH ʾ M

Al-Waqiʿah 56:9: the people of the left and the people of the right

After describing the resurrection, Al-Waqiʿah states:

Al-Waqiʿah 56:7

وَكُنْتُمْ أَزْوَاجًا ثَلَاثَةً

wa-kuntum azwājan thalāthah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:7

“And you become three groups.”

The three groups are then introduced:

Al-Waqiʿah 56:8

فَأَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ مَا أَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ

fa-aṣḥābu al-maymanati mā aṣḥābu al-maymanah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:8

“The people of the right—what people of the right!”

Al-Waqiʿah 56:9

وَأَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَةِ مَا أَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَةِ

wa-aṣḥābu al-mashʾamati mā aṣḥābu al-mashʾamah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:9

“And the people of the left—what people of the left!”

Al-Waqiʿah 56:10

وَالسَّابِقُونَ السَّابِقُونَ

wa-al-sābiqūna al-sābiqūn

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:10

“And the foremost, the foremost.”

Mashʾama contrasts with maymana here, clearly introducing right and left. Because the passage groups people at the resurrection, their standing and outcome also matter.

Another clue appears within the same surah: Al-Waqiʿah 56:27 mentions أَصْحَابُ الْيَمِين, and Al-Waqiʿah 56:41 mentions أَصْحَابُ الشِّمَال.

Both sections continue the contrast between groups with different outcomes. The people of the right receive blessing and ease; the people of the left are described through punishment and then their earlier conduct.

This connection within the surah identifies ashab al-mashʾama and ashab al-shimal with the same category of people. The words nevertheless belong to different roots, and mashʾama additionally evokes the family of inauspiciousness and an unhappy outcome.

Shimal names the left side; mashʾama has both a spatial sense and a connection with shuʾm. Verse 9 preserves the left-side image, while the continuation clarifies the group's unwelcome outcome.

In مَا أَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَةِ, the interrogative ma highlights the group's condition and significance. The question serves wonder and warning. The same construction also appears for the people of the right, but the later context connects the two expressions of wonder with different outcomes.

The phrase alone does not specify exactly where people stand or how they move at the resurrection. It introduces a category whose characteristics and outcome are then explained.

Al-Balad 90:19: faith, conduct, and responsibility

The second Quranic setting is Al-Balad. The surah depicts the demanding path of responsibility and illustrates crossing the steep pass through freeing a person from bondage, feeding others in hunger, and caring for an orphaned relative or a destitute person.

Al-Balad 90:17 then mentions faith, mutual encouragement to patience, and mutual encouragement to mercy.

Al-Balad 90:18

أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ الْمَيْمَنَةِ

ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-maymanah

Al-Balad 90:18

“Those are the people of the right.”

In contrast, it states:

Al-Balad 90:19

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِنَا هُمْ أَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَةِ

wa-alladhīna kafarū bi-āyātinā hum aṣḥābu al-mashʾamah

Al-Balad 90:19

“Those who rejected Our signs are the people of the left.”

Al-Balad 90:20 then describes their outcome:

عَلَيْهِمْ نَارٌ مُؤْصَدَةٌ

ʿalayhim nārun muʾṣadah

Al-Balad 90:20

“Over them is a fire closed in.”

This setting is decisive for Quranic mashʾama.

The subject is people's relationship to divine signs and their standing in final judgment. Ashab al-mashʾama here does not signify accidental bad luck or innate ominousness.

The contrasting group is described through faith, social responsibility, patience, and mercy. The people of the left are explicitly described as rejecting the signs. This is a judgment concerning people, not a belief that someone is created unlucky independently of choice and responsibility.

Mashʾama here identifies a group with an unhappy outcome while retaining the left-side image.

Nevertheless, failure to perform any single good deed mentioned earlier does not automatically establish someone as a complete instance of this category. Verse 19 specifically identifies the group as الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِنَا; that qualification matters for the statement's scope.

5. Mashʾama, superstition, and neighboring roots

SH ʾ M and SH M L: direction and outcome

Shimal belongs to SH M L and denotes left in uses concerning direction or a bodily limb.

For example, Al-Haqqah 69:25 states:

وَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَابَهُ بِشِمَالِهِ فَيَقُولُ يَا لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أُوتَ كِتَابِيَهْ

wa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-shimālihi fa-yaqūlu yā laytanī lam ūta kitābiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25

“As for the one given his record in his left hand, he says: If only I had not been given my record!”

Shimalihi refers to the person's left hand here, with clear support from the construction.

By contrast, mashʾama in Al-Waqiʿah and Al-Balad identifies a group with a specified outcome. It relates to the left, while its lexical family also includes inauspiciousness.

The expressions can therefore identify the same group in some settings without having identical roots or semantic ranges.

SH ʾ M and Y M N: opposing families

The Arabic contrast between shuʾm and yumn is one of inauspiciousness and good fortune. Maymana likewise contrasts with mashʾama.

Quranically, this becomes a picture of groups with differing outcomes. Their favorable or unfavorable outcome comes from their standing before God and divine judgment, rather than a natural property of the right or left hand.

One should not invert every meaning of Y M N and assign it to SH ʾ M. Independent senses of yamin, such as an oath or power, do not make mashʾama mean oath-breaking or powerlessness.

SH ʾ M, N H S, and T Y R

Quranic nahs concerns an adverse, severe condition, as with the adverse day in Al-Qamar 54:19 and adverse days in Fussilat 41:16.

Although nahs and shuʾm can overlap in ordinary language, their roots differ and each use requires examination.

Some uses of T Y R concern omen-taking and attributing events to external signs. In Ya Sin 36:18, opponents call the messengers ill omens. The response begins in Ya Sin 36:19:

قَالُوا طَائِرُكُمْ مَعَكُمْ

qālū ṭāʾirukum maʿakum

Yā Sīn 36:19

“They said: Your omen is with yourselves.”

In Al-Naml 27:47, Salih's people likewise attribute ill omen to him and his companions, but the answer does not accept that attribution.

These uses matter because the Quran does not uncritically endorse omen beliefs. People cannot evade responsibility by blaming misfortune on a messenger, another individual, or an external sign.

Ashab al-mashʾama should therefore not be translated as people whose mere presence brings others bad luck. These verses concern their own standing and outcome in divine judgment.

6. Structure and translation suggestions for Forqan

In all three Quranic occurrences, al-mashʾama is definite and genitive after ashab. The construction relates people to a side, position, or condition; context determines the precise relationship.

In Al-Waqiʿah 56:9, the contrast with maymana, followed by yamin and shimal, supports left side. The contrast also occurs in Al-Balad 90:19, where explicit rejection and the following account of the outcome clarify the title's evaluative significance.

The following translation distinctions are useful:

شَأْمَة: left side or leftward direction.

شُؤْم: ill omen, inauspiciousness, or an unhappy outcome, depending on whether the subject is belief about omens or an unwelcome consequence.

أَشْأَم: more inauspicious or deeply ill-fated, according to the construction.

مَشْؤُوم: ominous or inauspicious; considered ominous may be more precise where others' belief is at issue.

تَشَاؤُم: regarding something as an ill omen or expecting an unwelcome outcome.

مَشْأَمَة: left side spatially; inauspiciousness or an unhappy outcome evaluatively.

For أَصْحَابُ الْمَشْأَمَة, people of the left or companions of the left is a suitable base rendering because it preserves the verbal contrast with the people of the right. Lexical and contextual explanation should clarify the group's unhappy outcome.

The ill-fated can convey the resulting sense, but as the sole base translation it loses the right-and-left image important in both surahs.

Conversely, bearers of bad luck or people who bring misfortune introduce a superstitious effect upon others that the phrase itself does not express.

The direct rendering should preserve the linguistic image, with the unhappy outcome explained in context. These suggestions do not change the installed verse translations.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic SH ʾ M has identifiable branches for left and for ill omen or an unhappy outcome. Early poetry attests bodily direction as well as descriptions of harm, inauspiciousness, and destructive consequences.

The cultural associations of right with good fortune and left with ill omen help explain the connection, without proving that left is inherently bad or that every meaning arose through one certain historical development.

The Quran uses only mashʾama, always with ashab. Its contrast with maymana and connection with ashab al-shimal establish the left-side image; Al-Waqiʿah and Al-Balad show that it identifies a group with an unhappy outcome.

Quranic ashab al-mashʾama can therefore be understood as “the people of the left, with an implication of their unhappy outcome.” That outcome belongs within divine judgment and human responsibility, rather than bad-luck contagion, omen-taking, or an inherently ominous person, limb, or geographical direction.
