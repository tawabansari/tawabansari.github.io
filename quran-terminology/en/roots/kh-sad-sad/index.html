The Qur’anic Root KH Ṣ Ṣ

Khaṣṣa: singling out; khaṣāṣah: a need that remains unmet

The root KH Ṣ Ṣ appears in the Qur’an in the principal forms yakhtaṣṣu, khāṣṣatan, and khaṣāṣah.

At first sight, its two fields seem far apart.

In yakhtaṣṣu and khāṣṣah, the subject is particularization: a person, group, or effect is distinguished from others, and something is specially associated with it.

In khaṣāṣah, the subject is need, shortage, and straitened circumstances.

Alongside these uses, Arabic also knew khaṣāṣ and khaṣāṣah as words for gaps and openings: a gap between things, an aperture in something, or a space between its parts.

This suggests a possible semantic connection between the two branches. In particularization, something is set apart from a general whole; in khaṣāṣah, there is a gap or shortfall that has not been filled.

The connection is meaningful, but it should not be turned into a certain account of the word’s historical origin. For understanding the Qur’an, it is better to keep two directions distinct:

Ikhtiṣāṣ: being singled out in relation to a particular matter;

And:

Khaṣāṣah: a shortage, gap, or need that has not been met.

An initial definition of the root may be offered as follows:

KH Ṣ Ṣ: singling out an individual or group in a particular matter; and, in the khaṣāṣah branch, a gap, shortfall, or need that leaves a person’s circumstances lacking and in need of support.

Morphology: khāṣṣah and khaṣāṣah are not the same form

KH Ṣ Ṣ is a doubled root: its second and third root letters are identical. This feature is visible in forms such as khaṣṣa, khāṣṣ, and ikhtaṣṣa.

Khāṣṣ is morphologically an active participle of khaṣṣa, and khāṣṣah can be its feminine form, on the pattern fāʿilah. In some uses, however, khāṣṣah has an established nominal or verbal-noun function; its role in every sentence cannot be determined simply from its outwardly feminine form.

In Al-Anfāl 8:25, khāṣṣatan is accusative and means “exclusively.” Syntactic analyses include a circumstantial expression and a modifier of an omitted verbal noun, giving something close to:

إصابةً خاصّةً

Iṣābatan khāṣṣatan.

“A restricted, exclusive affliction”

Or:

“With this affliction applying exclusively to them.”

In both analyses, the central idea is particularization and exclusivity.

Khaṣāṣah has a different construction, on the pattern faʿālah. It is recorded in Arabic for gaps, need, poverty, and adverse circumstances.

The pattern faʿālah does not, by itself, establish that khaṣāṣah must be a lasting state or permanent characteristic. Morphology shows the word’s form; its precise semantic contribution comes from actual usage and context.

The difference between khāṣṣah and khaṣāṣah is therefore not merely a difference of vowels or translations. They are two distinct word structures in one family, and the Qur’an uses them in different directions.

Early Arabic: singling out and the gap that remains

In Arabic:

خَصَّهُ بالشيء

Khaṣṣahu bi-l-shayʾ.

Means that someone assigned something specially to a person, distinguishing that person from others in that matter.

A line attributed to Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī reads:

إِنَّ امْرَأً خَصَّنِي عَمْدًا مَوَدَّتَهُ
عَلَى التَّنَائِي لَعِنْدِي غَيْرُ مَكْفُورِ

Inna mraʾan khaṣṣanī ʿamdan mawaddatahu / ʿalā l-tanāʾī la-ʿindī ghayru makfūri.

The line means: “Someone who has deliberately singled me out for his affection, despite the distance, does not go unacknowledged by me.” In explaining khaṣṣanī … mawaddatahu, lexical sources propose an omitted bāʾ: khaṣṣanī bi-mawaddatihi, “he singled me out for his affection.” They also record the analysis “because of his affection for me.” The line should therefore not be presented without explanation as an example of a verb taking two direct objects.

The same field remains in khāṣṣ, khuṣūṣ, and ikhtiṣāṣ: something is assigned to an individual or group, rather than equally to everyone.

In the other branch, al-khaṣāṣ was used for spaces and gaps, such as an opening between parts, a hole, or an aperture through which something can be seen.

Khaṣāṣah was also used for poverty, need, adverse circumstances, and shortage. It can even describe a person who has eaten but is not yet full, or an animal that has drunk but is still thirsty: a need remains unmet.

This usage matters for Al-Ḥashr 59:9. Khaṣāṣah is not merely a label for a social class called “the poor”; it can describe a situation in which a real need has not yet been met.

Yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashāʾ: singling out in a defined respect

Al-Baqarah states:

وَاللَّهُ يَخْتَصُّ بِرَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَاللَّهُ ذُو الْفَضْلِ الْعَظِيمِ

Wa-Allāhu yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashāʾ; wa-Allāhu dhū l-faḍli l-ʿaẓīm.

“And The God singles out for His mercy whomever He wills; and The God is the possessor of immense bounty.”

Al-Baqarah 2:105

The same expression also occurs in Āl ʿImrān:

يَخْتَصُّ بِرَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ ذُو الْفَضْلِ الْعَظِيمِ

Yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashāʾ; wa-Allāhu dhū l-faḍli l-ʿaẓīm.

“He singles out for His mercy whomever He wills; and The God is the possessor of immense bounty.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:74

Yakhtaṣṣu is the imperfect of form VIII. Arabic uses ikhtaṣṣa both transitively and intransitively, but in these two verses it is transitive: The God is its subject, and man yashāʾ is its object. He singles out for His mercy whomever He wills. Thus the sense here is “singles out,” not “is singled out.”

The relevant relationship is specified within the sentence itself:

بِرَحْمَتِهِ

Bi-raḥmatihi.

There is therefore no reason to equate the verb with broader notions such as “inherent superiority,” “an elect class,” or “permanent rank.”

“Singling out” and “choosing” are not entirely identical. “Choosing” emphasizes selecting someone from among others; “singling out for something” more explicitly preserves the relationship between a person and what is specially assigned to them.

Particularization is also always relative to a specified matter. If someone is singled out in one respect, the word itself does not say that they are superior to others in every respect.

In these two verses, the matter is explicit: the mercy of The God.

Both verses end with:

وَاللَّهُ ذُو الْفَضْلِ الْعَظِيمِ

Wa-Allāhu dhū l-faḍli l-ʿaẓīm.

This ending places particularization within the context of bounty. The expression alone does not define an inherently and permanently “special” human class.

Singling out is not the same as unrestricted exclusivity

In contemporary English, “exclusive” can suggest complete exclusion: something belongs to one person alone, and nobody else has any share in it.

The root KH Ṣ Ṣ can restrict a scope, but the extent of that restriction must be understood from the sentence.

When something is assigned specially to someone, that person is distinguished from others in that respect. The root itself does not determine how extensive the distinction is, how long it lasts, or precisely who falls outside it.

In:

يَخْتَصُّ بِرَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ

Yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashāʾ.

The verb says that The God singles someone out for His mercy. The kind of mercy, its extent, and its relationship to other manifestations of mercy cannot be extracted from yakhtaṣṣu alone.

The word’s core is singling out in a specified relationship.

Khāṣṣatan: exclusively, not an elite class

Al-Anfāl states:

وَاتَّقُوا فِتْنَةً لَا تُصِيبَنَّ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْكُمْ خَاصَّةً وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

Wa-ittaqū fitnatan lā tuṣībanna alladhīna ẓalamū minkum khāṣṣatan wa-iʿlamū anna Allāha shadīdu l-ʿiqāb.

“And guard yourselves against a fitnah whose reach will not be confined to the wrongdoers among you; and know that The God is severe in punishment.”

Al-Anfāl 8:25

Khāṣṣatan here does not mean “society’s elite” or “distinguished individuals.”

In structure, the word is related to khāṣṣ and the pattern fāʿilah, but its function in this sentence is adverbial: it restricts the scope of the affliction.

In the common reading of the verse, the expression conveys the following meaning:

لا تصيبن الذين ظلموا منكم خاصّةً

Lā tuṣībanna alladhīna ẓalamū minkum khāṣṣatan.

That is:

“This fitnah does not strike only the wrongdoers among you.”

In other words, khāṣṣah expresses exclusivity, and on this reading the sentence warns that the fitnah’s effects do not remain confined to wrongdoers. The syntactic analysis of lā tuṣībanna, including the relationship between lā and the emphatic nūn, is debated. This explanation of the meaning should therefore not be presented as the only possible grammatical analysis.

For this reason, the rendering:

“Only”

Is very natural:

“Guard yourselves against a fitnah that will not strike only the wrongdoers among you.”

The verse does not say that fitnah strikes “special people.” On the contrary, it warns that its consequences will not remain confined to a particular group.

This is one of the clearest examples of particularization within the root’s field.

Wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah: they themselves remain in need

Al-Ḥashr states:

وَالَّذِينَ تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ يُحِبُّونَ مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ

Wa-alladhīna tabawwaʾū l-dāra wa-l-īmāna min qablihim yuḥibbūna man hājara ilayhim wa-lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan mimmā ūtū wa-yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah.

“And those who had settled in the dwelling place and become established in faith before them love those who migrated to them. They feel no envy or resentment in their hearts over what the migrants have been given, and they prefer others over themselves, even when they themselves are in need and want.”

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

In this translation, “envy or resentment” is a contextual explanation of ḥājatan mimmā ūtū, not the fixed, independent meaning of ḥājah. The sentence does not declare them materially self-sufficient; its ending explicitly mentions their own need. Two expressions placed together are important:

وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ

Wa-yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim.

They prefer others over themselves;

And:

وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ

Wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah.

Even if they themselves experience khaṣāṣah.

The verse does more than say that they are “poor.” More precisely, they themselves have a need or shortfall, yet prefer another person despite that need remaining unmet.

This distinction also matters for understanding īthār, preferring others. Giving away something one does not need is one situation; putting someone else first while one’s own need remains is another.

In this context, khaṣāṣah can be rendered as:

Need

Want

Or:

Straitened circumstances

These are possible translations.

“Poverty” may also describe an instance of it, but in English that word can suggest an overall economic condition too strongly. Khaṣāṣah can refer to a real need present in the particular situation.

Is khaṣāṣah the same as poverty?

Arabic uses khaṣāṣah for poverty and adverse circumstances, but its field is broader than a rigid economic label.

The image of a gap helps clarify the meaning: something that should be present is lacking, or its place remains unfilled.

Thus a person may have eaten and still experience khaṣāṣah because they are not full, or an animal may have drunk without its need being satisfied.

Al-Ḥashr 59:9 likewise emphasizes their own need at the time they put someone else first. Yet “remaining” in this explanation should not imply that part of the need must previously have been met: khaṣāṣah itself does not require that sequence of events.

Khaṣāṣah follows the pattern faʿālah, but this pattern alone does not establish that the condition was necessarily prolonged, permanent, or stable. The meaning of need and shortage is established by actual usage.

In this respect, khaṣāṣah overlaps with faqr, poverty, but their ranges of usage cannot be treated as identical in every context.

Faqr belongs to the root F Q R and has its own range of usage. Two words being synonymous in one context does not establish that all their uses are identical.

In khaṣāṣah, an existing shortfall or need is more prominent.

Do particularization and khaṣāṣah arise from one image?

The relationship between these two branches is an important question.

On one side:

خَصَّهُ بالشيء

Khaṣṣahu bi-l-shayʾ.

Means to distinguish and single someone out in a particular matter.

On the other:

الخَصَاص / الخَصَاصَة

Al-khaṣāṣ / al-khaṣāṣah.

Is used for gaps, openings, shortage, and need.

The idea of space and separation can suggest a semantic connection between them.

In khaṣāṣah, something is incomplete in the circumstances, and a gap remains.

In particularization, one case is set apart from a wider whole, and a ruling or benefit applies to it specifically.

The opposition between khāṣṣ and ʿāmm, “particular” and “general,” also helps explain this connection: the general encompasses a wider domain, while the particular sets a portion of it apart.

Nevertheless, a connection between these images does not prove a historical path by which one developed from the other. Nor does understanding the Qur’an require translating khaṣāṣah in Al-Ḥashr through “particularization,” or explaining yakhtaṣṣu through poverty.

They are two branches of one lexical family; each branch’s contribution is determined by its own context.

KH Ṣ Ṣ (khāṣṣ), F R D (fard), and ʿ M M (ʿumūm): particular, individual, and general

Khāṣṣ and fard can approach one another in expressing separateness, but they do not express the same thing.

Fard emphasizes being one or being alone. The Qur’an speaks of a person coming before The God:

فَرْدًا

Fardan.

That is, alone (Maryam 19:95).

Khāṣṣ does not necessarily mean alone. A group of several people can also be singled out in a particular matter. The central idea is a restricted scope distinguished from a wider one.

By contrast, ʿāmm encompasses something within a wider scope without that restriction.

As a general tendency:

Fard — one person or thing on its own;

Khāṣṣ — one individual or a restricted set in relation to others;

ʿĀmm — the wider scope without such particularization.

Al-Anfāl 8:25 illustrates the distinction well. The question is not whether wrongdoers are “alone”; it is whether fitnah’s effects are confined to them.

KH Ṣ Ṣ (khaṣāṣah) and F Q R (faqr): need and poverty

Khaṣāṣah and faqr can come close in translation.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:9:

وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ

Wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah.

Can be rendered:

“Even though they themselves are in need”

Or:

“Even though they themselves are in straitened circumstances and want.”

Both are possible translations.

“Poverty” may encompass the same situation, but khaṣāṣah more clearly preserves the image of a shortfall that is still present.

The distinction is not absolute. Arabic itself uses khaṣāṣah for poverty. The aim is therefore not to construct two separate categories, but to avoid replacing khaṣāṣah with a social label without considering its own semantic field.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For:

وَاللَّهُ يَخْتَصُّ بِرَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ

Wa-Allāhu yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashāʾ.

A rendering close to the construction is:

“And The God singles out for His mercy whomever He wills.”

“He specially favors with His mercy” is another possibility. “Chooses” conveys part of the meaning, but emphasizes selection more than particularization itself.

For:

لَا تُصِيبَنَّ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْكُمْ خَاصَّةً

Lā tuṣībanna alladhīna ẓalamū minkum khāṣṣatan.

A natural rendering is:

“Whose reach will not be confined to the wrongdoers among you.”

Here, khāṣṣah is best understood as “only” or “exclusively,” not “the elite.”

For:

وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ

Wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah.

“Even though they themselves are in need”

Or:

“Even though they themselves are in straitened circumstances and want”

Are natural renderings.

“Poverty” may also be used in a fluent translation, but “need and want” brings out the word’s own field more clearly.

Conclusion

The root KH Ṣ Ṣ has two clear directions in the Qur’an. In yakhtaṣṣu and khāṣṣah, the focus is particularization: singling out an individual or group in a defined relationship. Yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashāʾ speaks of singling out for mercy, while lā tuṣībanna alladhīna ẓalamū minkum khāṣṣatan warns that fitnah’s effects will not be confined to wrongdoers.

In khaṣāṣah, the field turns toward need and shortage. Arabic also uses this form for gaps, openings, poverty, and unmet need. Al-Ḥashr 59:9 highlights that situation: they put others before themselves while they themselves are in need.

The two forms also differ morphologically. Khāṣṣah is related to khāṣṣ and the pattern fāʿilah, and in Al-Anfāl 8:25 it has the adverbial sense “exclusively.” Khaṣāṣah follows the pattern faʿālah, but its meaning of need and shortage must be understood from usage, not from the pattern alone.

The idea of a gap or separation can help explain the relationship between the particularization branch and the khaṣāṣah branch, but the precise historical path between them is not certain.

A concise definition of the root may therefore be offered:

KH Ṣ Ṣ: in the particularization branch, singling out an individual or group in a specified matter; and in the khaṣāṣah branch, a gap, shortfall, or need that has not been met. The shared idea of separation can help explain the connection between these fields, but the word’s form and each verse’s context determine the precise meaning.
