The Qur’anic Root AYN Ẓ M (ʿ Ẓ M)

Magnitude, structure, weight, rank, and the heaviness of effect

The central meaning of the root

If the root ع ظ م (AYN Ẓ M / ʿ Ẓ M) in the Qur’an is translated only as “greatness,” it loses its structural and weight-bearing force. In ordinary usage, greatness often points to size, volume, amount, or outward intensity. But greatness in the Qur’an is not only size. Something ʿaẓīm has weight, structure, consequence, rank, and determining heaviness in meaning, responsibility, reward, danger, truth, or honor.

For this reason, the Qur’an calls reward ʿaẓīm, bounty ʿaẓīm, success ʿaẓīm, punishment ʿaẓīm, sin ʿaẓīm, slander ʿaẓīm, speech ʿaẓīm, the Throne ʿaẓīm, and God Himself Al-ʿAẓīm. This range shows that AYN Ẓ M is not always praise. Greatness may be in good or evil, reward or sin, guidance or danger, the majesty of truth or the heaviness of falsehood.

So a difference must be kept between “big” in the ordinary sense and ʿaẓīm in the Qur’anic sense. ʿAẓīm means something is not light, not marginal, not weak in effect, and cannot be passed over carelessly. ʿAẓamah is the real weight of a thing in the order of truth.

The central meaning of this root can be understood this way: AYN Ẓ M means greatness of weight, structure, and effect, not only greatness of size.

ʿAẓm: bone, structure, and hidden support

عَظْم (ʿaẓm) in the Qur’an means bone. At first glance, this branch may seem far from greatness, but it is very important for understanding the root. Bone is the body’s pillar and structure. The body stands, moves, and keeps its form through bone. Bone is usually hidden beneath skin and flesh, but this hidden part carries the primary structure of the body.

When Zechariah says in Maryam 19:4:

رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي

My Lord, the bone within me has weakened.

The statement is not only about one body part. The weakening of bone signals the weakening of the body’s pillar and the decline of human strength. This branch shows that AYN Ẓ M is connected with structure, support, and structural weight, not only outward largeness.

From this angle, great things in the Qur’an also have a kind of hidden structure. Great reward, great punishment, great slander, and the Great Qur’an are not merely “large”; behind them are structure, weight, effect, and a framework that carries consequence.

ʿAẓīm: the real weight of a matter

عَظِيم (ʿaẓīm) is the most frequent form of this root in the Qur’an. Depending on context, it can refer to good or evil, reward or punishment, the greatness of truth or the heaviness of falsehood. This breadth is the key to understanding the root.

When the Qur’an speaks of great reward, the meaning is not only abundant reward. It is a reward with real weight, permanence, and destiny-shaping value. When it speaks of great punishment, the meaning is not only more severe hardship; it is a heavy and determining consequence. When it speaks of great sin, the sin is called great because of its moral weight and destructive effect. When it speaks of great slander, the slander is not merely a false sentence; it is a heavy destruction of truth and human dignity.

Therefore, ʿaẓīm in the Qur’an is not a surface adjective. It reveals the real weight of a matter.

Aʿẓam: difference in weight and degree

أَعْظَم (aʿẓam) means greater, weightier, or higher in rank and degree. This form shows that not everything is on one level. Actions, ranks, rewards, and situations do not have equal weight. Two acts may appear similar outwardly, but before God their weight may differ because intention, time, danger, cost, truthfulness, and effect are not the same.

From here we understand that the Qur’anic measure of greatness is not merely outward action. Sometimes an act that appears small to people has great weight before God, while something noisy and public may in truth be light and rootless.

Honoring God’s signs and sanctities

Taʿẓīm in the Qur’an is not merely attributing greatness to something. It is the ethical response of the human being before something that has sanctity and rank with God. In Al-Ḥajj 22:30, it says:

ذَٰلِكَ وَمَنْ يُعَظِّمْ حُرُمَاتِ اللَّهِ فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَهُ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِ

And whoever honors the sanctities of God, that is better for him with his Lord.

And in Al-Ḥajj 22:32, it says:

ذَٰلِكَ وَمَنْ يُعَظِّمْ شَعَائِرَ اللَّهِ فَإِنَّهَا مِنْ تَقْوَى الْقُلُوبِ

And whoever honors the signs of God, it is from the taqwā of hearts.

These two verses are very important. Taʿẓīm means seeing something in its proper place and not treating it lightly. Honoring the signs of God is from the taqwā of hearts, not from outward display. A person may outwardly magnify religious signs, but if his heart lacks taqwā, that honoring becomes habit and appearance.

So Qur’anic taʿẓīm is not pride, ceremony, monopoly, or sectarian inflation. True taʿẓīm means recognizing the divine weight of a matter and dealing with it responsibly.

Great reward, great bounty, great success

When the Qur’an says God makes reward great for someone, the issue is not merely numerical increase. Greatness of reward means a reward that has weight in the order of truth, remains, and raises the human being’s destiny.

In the Qur’anic view, great reward comes in response to righteous action, taqwā, truthfulness, spending, patience, struggle, and loyalty to truth. This great reward is not the result of a surface transaction; it is the divine response to the serious orientation of the human being.

Great reward, great bounty, and great success are positive uses of AYN Ẓ M. Great reward is not light or temporary. Great bounty is giving beyond ordinary measure and beyond human expectation. Great success is not only temporary success, but a destiny-shaping salvation.

Great punishment, great sin, great slander

The same root that calls reward, bounty, and success great also calls punishment, sin, and slander great. This shows that greatness in the Qur’an is not always positive value. ʿAẓīm means weighty, and weight may be the weight of good or the weight of evil.

Great punishment means a consequence that is not light and cannot be treated as small. Great sin means a sin with moral weight and destructive effect. Great slander means an accusation that is not merely a simple lie, but a wound against truth, justice, honor, and human dignity.

This is very important for the Forqan method. The Qur’an teaches that not all errors have the same weight. Some statements, judgments, accusations, and betrayals are so heavy that their effect crosses the individual level and reaches social corruption and the destruction of truth.

Great speech and the responsibility of words

The Qur’an uses the expression great speech for some statements. This shows that speech also has weight. In the Qur’anic order, a word is not merely a sound. Speech may preserve truth or destroy it; it may guide the human being or mislead him; it may be light or carry heavy responsibility.

When a statement is called great, its consequence has gone beyond an ordinary sentence. Speech about God, attributing something to God, distorting truth, or speaking greatly about the unseen can all become dangerous in weight. In the Qur’an, the tongue is not a light instrument; it carries responsibility.

Al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm

In Sūrat al-Ḥijr, the Qur’an is called the Great Qur’an. In Al-Ḥijr 15:87, it says:

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَاكَ سَبْعًا مِنَ الْمَثَانِي وَالْقُرْآنَ الْعَظِيمَ

And We have certainly given you seven of the oft-repeated and the Great Qur’an.

The greatness of the Qur’an is not limited to volume, outward form, or verbal beauty. The Qur’an is great because it carries the weight of guidance, is furqān, brings the human being out of heedlessness, separates truth from falsehood, and tests the inner state of the human being and society.

The Qur’an is great because through its signs it engages the human qalb, hearing, sight, fuʾād, reason, conscience, and action. Its greatness is that it is not only a text to be read; it is a criterion of measurement, warning, mercy, light, and responsibility.

Al-ʿArsh al-ʿAẓīm

The Great Throne in the Qur’an points to the rank of divine rule, governance, and sovereignty. In Al-Tawbah 9:129, it says:

وَهُوَ رَبُّ الْعَرْشِ الْعَظِيمِ

And He is the Lord of the Great Throne.

This expression should not be understood through bodily or material imagination. In such verses, the Throne is the station of sovereignty and governance, not a throne like human thrones.

When the Qur’an speaks of the Great Throne, it turns the human mind toward the greatness of divine rule and encompassing Lordship. This greatness is not material measurement. The Great Throne points to the vastness of God’s judgment, governance, and authority over the heavens and the earth.

Al-ʿAẓīm: God’s transcendent greatness

When the Qur’an calls God الْعَظِيم (Al-ʿAẓīm), we enter the field of transcendent theology. Transcendent theology means speaking about God while declaring Him free from the limitations, likenesses, measures, needs, and attributes of created things. Therefore, Al-ʿAẓīm for God must not be understood like the greatness of a human being, mountain, throne, reward, or event.

In Al-Baqarah 2:255, it says:

وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ

And He is The Most High, The Magnificent.

Al-ʿAẓīm means that God has absolute greatness in existence, power, rank, encompassing authority, judgment, knowledge, and self-sufficiency. His greatness is not bodily size, social rank, political power, or relative superiority over creatures. He is The Magnificent beyond created measure; His greatness is the origin and criterion of every other greatness, without being measured by anything.

ʿAẓīm and kabīr

Alongside ʿaẓīm, the Qur’an also uses the root K B R for greatness. The two are not always identical. Kabīr can more directly show largeness, superiority, or high rank; while ʿaẓīm is more connected with weight, structure, heaviness of effect, and structural rank.

Something may look kabīr in people’s eyes but not be ʿaẓīm, meaning it lacks true weight and structure in the order of truth. This distinction should be understood carefully, not as a rigid separation. But in translation, it helps prevent ʿaẓīm from always being flattened into simple “big.”

Greatness and taqwā of the heart

One deep point in this root is the connection between taʿẓīm and the taqwā of the heart. The Qur’an makes honoring the signs of God part of the taqwā of hearts. This means the human being honors something rightly when his heart recognizes its weight.

If the heart is sick, it may make small things great and treat great things as small. It may treat ceremony, sectarian identity, power, clothing, title, or religious appearance as great, while treating justice, mercy, truthfulness, trust, and the signs of God lightly.

Qur’anic taʿẓīm means returning the measure to its proper place. The human being must learn what is heavy before God and what is only inflated in people’s eyes.

Methodological note

In translating this root, one should not always be satisfied with “great.” In many verses, “great” is not enough, because ʿaẓīm may point to moral weight, rank, effect, danger, reward, or divine transcendence.

For great punishment, “great punishment” may not be enough; sometimes “heavy punishment” or “weighty consequence” is more precise. For great sin, “great sin” is correct, but the meaning should be understood as a sin with heavy weight and destructive effect. For great reward, “great reward” is acceptable, but “a weighty and valuable reward” may convey the meaning better. For Al-ʿAẓīm as a divine attribute, translation must remain transcendent and avoid bodily imagination or comparison.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

This root should be rendered according to context.

  • ʿaẓm: bone, the body’s support, bodily structure; according to context.
  • ʿaẓīm: great, tremendous, weighty, possessing weight and effect, beyond ordinary measure; according to context.
  • aʿẓam: greater, weightier, higher in rank and degree.
  • yuʿaẓẓim: honors, magnifies, preserves the real weight and sanctity of something.
  • great reward: great reward, weighty and lasting reward, reward with true weight.
  • great bounty: great bounty, expansive grace, giving beyond ordinary measure.
  • great success: great salvation, determining success, salvation with final weight.
  • great sin: great sin, heavy error, sin with destructive effect.
  • great slander: great slander, heavy falsehood, destructive accusation against truth and dignity.
  • great speech: great speech, weighty statement, speech with dangerous consequence.
  • Al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm: the Great Qur’an, the Qur’an carrying great weight of guidance and furqān.
  • Al-ʿArsh al-ʿAẓīm: the Great Throne, the great station of divine rule and governance, without bodily imagination.
  • Al-ʿAẓīm for God: The Magnificent, The Absolutely Great, the One whose greatness is beyond size, body, place, comparison, and created limit.

Summary

ع ظ م (AYN Ẓ M / ʿ Ẓ M) in the Qur’an is not only the root of size. It shows weight, structure, rank, effect, and going beyond ordinary measure. Something is ʿaẓīm when it is not light in the order of truth: whether reward, sin, punishment, success, Qur’an, Throne, or a divine attribute.

This root teaches the human being not to measure everything by outward appearance. Many things people consider great are light before God, and many things people treat as small are great before God. Taqwā of the heart means recognizing these weights.

In the Forqan method, AYN Ẓ M should be understood this way: greatness is the real, structured weight of a thing in the order of truth. And when this root reaches God, Al-ʿAẓīm is a transcendent attribute: God is The Magnificent beyond size, body, place, comparison, and limit, and every other greatness before His greatness is only a limited and dependent sign.
