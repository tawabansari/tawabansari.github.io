Qur’anic Root Q DH F

Casting, throwing, and directing something toward a destination

In early Arabic, Q DH F denotes casting or throwing something. This often involves releasing it from its initial position and sending it toward a destination. Throwing stones or arrows, casting something into water, and directing words at another person are established uses of this word family.

In a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī, a she-camel is described as follows:

مَقْذُوفَةٍ بِدَخِيسِ النَّحْضِ بَازِلُهَا / لَهُ صَرِيفٌ صَرِيفَ الْقَعْوِ بِالْمَسَدِ

A she-camel laden with dense flesh, whose canine tooth grinds with a sound like the wooden pulley support as the rope moves.

Here مَقْذُوفَة (maqdhūfa) describes a camel as though abundant flesh had been cast upon her body. This poetic usage illustrates the figurative and descriptive potential of qadhf alongside the throwing of objects.

Arabic lexicons record قَذْف (qadhf) for throwing, قَذِيفَة (qadhīfa) for a projectile, and قَذَّافَة (qadhdhāfa) for a throwing device.

Forms examined: قَذَفَ (qadhafa), يَقْذِفُ (yaqdhifu), قَذْف (qadhf), قَاذِف (qādhif), مَقْذُوف (maqdhūf), قَذِيفَة (qadhīfa), قَذَّافَة (qadhdhāfa), قَذُوف (qadhūf), اِنْقَذَفَ (inqadhafa), and تَقَاذَفَ (taqādhafa).

Qur’anic forms: All nine occurrences, in eight verses, belong to the basic verb قَذَفَ (qadhafa), appearing in past, present, imperative, active, and passive forms. The root occurs twice in 20:39.

1. The semantic core and physical image

When someone releases a stone and throws it toward a place, this is an act of qadhf.

Something leaves its initial position and is sent toward a destination or direction. Many Arabic uses involve forceful throwing, but intensity, violence, and injury are not necessary in every context.

Qadhf itself does not guarantee that a projectile reaches or affects its target. The outcome is established by context.

This semantic field extends beyond physical objects. Just as a stone can be cast toward someone, words can be directed at them; just as something can be placed in a container, fear or another feeling can be cast into a human heart.

The Qur’an uses both the physical and nonphysical potential of this root.

2. Physical uses in the Qur’an

Casting Moses into the water to save him

أَنِ اقْذِفِيهِ فِي التَّابُوتِ فَاقْذِفِيهِ فِي الْيَمِّ فَلْيُلْقِهِ الْيَمُّ بِالسَّاحِلِ يَأْخُذْهُ عَدُوٌّ لِي وَعَدُوٌّ لَهُ ۚ وَأَلْقَيْتُ عَلَيْكَ مَحَبَّةً مِنِّي وَلِتُصْنَعَ عَلَىٰ عَيْنِي

Ani qdhifīhi fī l-tābūti fa-qdhifīhi fī l-yammi fa-l-yulqihi l-yammu bi-l-sāḥili yaʾkhudhhu ʿaduwwun lī wa-ʿaduwwun lahu. Wa-alqaytu ʿalayka maḥabbatan minnī wa-li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī.

“Place him in the chest, then cast him into the water, and let the water cast him onto the shore; an enemy of Mine and an enemy of his will take him.” And I cast upon you love from Me, and so that you would be brought up under My eye.

Ta-Ha 20:39

The verb occurs twice here as an imperative addressed to Moses’ mother.

The first اقْذِفِيهِ (iqdhifīhi) concerns placing Moses in the chest; the second concerns casting him, with the chest, into the water.

There are two grammatical interpretations of the second verb’s pronoun. It can still refer to Moses, now inside the chest, or to the chest itself. In either interpretation, Moses is entrusted to the water in the chest; these are interpretations of the pronoun, not distinct recitations.

The verse shows that qadhf need not mean a violent throw. Moses’ mother is commanded to act for her child’s rescue.

Q DH F and L Q Y occur alongside each other here. His mother casts him into the water, and the water brings and casts him onto the shore.

The following clause also uses ilqāʾ for love. Both word families can describe casting physical and nonphysical things.

Casting the people’s ornaments in the account of the Sāmirī

قَالُوا مَا أَخْلَفْنَا مَوْعِدَكَ بِمَلْكِنَا وَلَٰكِنَّا حُمِّلْنَا أَوْزَارًا مِنْ زِينَةِ الْقَوْمِ فَقَذَفْنَاهَا فَكَذَٰلِكَ أَلْقَى السَّامِرِيُّ

Qālū mā akhlafnā mawʿidaka bi-malkinā wa-lākinnā ḥummilnā awzāran min zīnati l-qawmi fa-qadhafnāhā fa-kadhālika alqā l-sāmirī.

They said, “We did not break our promise to you by our own choice, but we were made to carry burdens of the people’s ornaments, so we cast them away; and thus did the Sāmirī cast.”

Ta-Ha 20:87

فَقَذَفْنَاهَا (fa-qadhafnāhā) is a first-person plural past verb. Its attached object pronoun refers to the burdens of the people’s ornaments.

Qadhf and ilqāʾ again appear together. Moses’ people speak of casting the ornaments, while the Sāmirī’s action is expressed by أَلْقَى (alqā).

The verse itself does not specify where the ornaments were cast. The verb expresses releasing and casting them.

Being pelted from every side

لَا يَسَّمَّعُونَ إِلَى الْمَلَإِ الْأَعْلَىٰ وَيُقْذَفُونَ مِنْ كُلِّ جَانِبٍ

Lā yassammaʿūna ilā l-malaʾi l-aʿlā wa-yuqdhafūna min kulli jānib.

They cannot listen to the highest assembly, and they are pelted from every side.

As-Saffat 37:8

يُقْذَفُونَ (yuqdhafūn) is a passive present verb. This construction directs attention to those being pelted.

The preceding verses describe protection against every rebellious devil, and the following verse describes their expulsion. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 also mentions a piercing flame pursuing one who snatches something away.

Here qadhf accompanies repulsion and expulsion. Yet the root does not mean only repelling: repulsion is the purpose and outcome in this context.

3. Casting truth against falsehood and speaking conjecturally

Truth is cast against falsehood

بَلْ نَقْذِفُ بِالْحَقِّ عَلَى الْبَاطِلِ فَيَدْمَغُهُ فَإِذَا هُوَ زَاهِقٌ ۚ وَلَكُمُ الْوَيْلُ مِمَّا تَصِفُونَ

Bal naqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqqi ʿalā l-bāṭili fa-yadmaghuhu fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun. Wa-lakumu l-waylu mimmā taṣifūn.

Rather, We cast the truth against falsehood, and it crushes it; then behold, it vanishes. And woe to you for what you describe!

Al-Anbiya 21:18

The Qur’an uses the image of throwing and impact to describe the confrontation between truth and falsehood.

نَقْذِفُ (naqdhifu) expresses the casting; بِالْحَقِّ (bi-l-ḥaqq) identifies what is cast; عَلَى الْبَاطِلِ (ʿalā l-bāṭil) identifies its direction and target.

The following فَيَدْمَغُهُ (fa-yadmaghuhu), from D M GH, describes crushing falsehood.

The God is the agent of casting, but the grammatical agent of crushing is truth itself. The God casts truth against falsehood, and truth crushes falsehood.

Qadhf, damgh, and zuhūq express three distinct notions here: casting, crushing, and vanishing.

Falsehood cannot withstand the proof of truth. An erroneous belief may be widespread and defended for centuries, but numerous followers and antiquity cannot turn it into truth.

The God casts the truth

قُلْ إِنَّ رَبِّي يَقْذِفُ بِالْحَقِّ عَلَّامُ الْغُيُوبِ

Qul inna rabbī yaqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqqi ʿallāmu l-ghuyūb.

Say, “Indeed, my Lord casts the truth, the Knower of all unseen things.”

Saba 34:48

The construction qadhf bi-l-ḥaqq appears again, but the destination of the casting is not explicitly stated.

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18, truth is cast against falsehood; here the Qur’an speaks of presenting and casting truth by the Lord who knows unseen things.

The following verse describes the outcome of the encounter between truth and falsehood:

قُلْ جَاءَ الْحَقُّ وَمَا يُبْدِئُ الْبَاطِلُ وَمَا يُعِيدُ

Qul jāʾa l-ḥaqqu wa-mā yubdiʾu l-bāṭilu wa-mā yuʿīd.

Say, “Truth has come, and falsehood neither originates nor restores.”

Saba 34:49

Casting truth here does not mean throwing a physical body. The image conveys the emergence and presentation of truth and its confrontation with falsehood.

Casting at the unseen: speaking without knowledge

وَقَدْ كَفَرُوا بِهِ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَيَقْذِفُونَ بِالْغَيْبِ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

Wa-qad kafarū bihi min qablu wa-yaqdhifūna bi-l-ghaybi min makānin baʿīd.

They had already disbelieved in it before, and they cast conjectures at the unseen from a distant place.

Saba 34:53

In the same surah, after the casting of truth by The God, the text describes casting at the unseen by people who have denied the truth.

The two constructions contrast:

  • In Sabaʾ 34:48, the Lord casts truth.
  • In Sabaʾ 34:53, people cast conjectural statements without access to the truth.

The reference to a distant place evokes someone throwing toward a target from far away without adequate knowledge of it.

In 34:48, The God is described as the Knower of all unseen things; those speaking in 34:53 express opinions about matters of which they have no knowledge.

Al-Kahf 18:22 likewise describes conjecture about the number of the Companions of the Cave through the image of casting at the unseen.

When people lack sufficient knowledge, they can ask questions, examine possibilities, or seek the truth. They should not present conjecture as knowledge.

Here qadhf evokes speech cast toward an unknown reality without a foundation in knowledge.

4. Casting fear into human hearts

وَأَنْزَلَ الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ صَيَاصِيهِمْ وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ فَرِيقًا تَقْتُلُونَ وَتَأْسِرُونَ فَرِيقًا

Wa-anzala lladhīna ẓāharūhum min ahli l-kitābi min ṣayāṣīhim wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu l-ruʿba farīqan taqtulūna wa-taʾsirūna farīqā.

And He brought down from their fortresses those among the People of the Book who had supported them, and cast terror into their hearts: some you were killing, and some you were taking captive.

Al-Ahzab 33:26

Qadhf here concerns fear arising in human hearts.

الرُّعْبَ (al-ruʿb) is the object of the verb, while the reference to their hearts specifies where it arises.

The same construction occurs in al-Ḥashr:

هُوَ الَّذِي أَخْرَجَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ لِأَوَّلِ الْحَشْرِ ۚ مَا ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنْ يَخْرُجُوا ۖ وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ فَأَتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَمْ يَحْتَسِبُوا ۖ وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ ۚ يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَيْدِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ فَاعْتَبِرُوا يَا أُولِي الْأَبْصَارِ

Huwa lladhī akhraja lladhīna kafarū min ahli l-kitābi min diyārihim li-awwali l-ḥashri. Mā ẓanantum an yakhrujū wa-ẓannū annahum māniʿatuhum ḥuṣūnuhum mina llāhi fa-atāhumu llāhu min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu l-ruʿba. Yukhribūna buyūtahum bi-aydīhim wa-aydī l-muʾminīna fa-ʿtabirū yā ulī l-abṣār.

He is the One who expelled those who disbelieved among the People of the Book from their homes at the first gathering. You did not think they would leave, and they thought their fortresses would protect them against The God. But The God came upon them from where they had not reckoned and cast terror into their hearts. They were destroying their homes with their own hands and the hands of the believers. So take heed, you who possess insight.

Al-Hashr 59:2

Those who trusted the strength of their fortresses encountered circumstances they had not anticipated.

Strong walls and abundant material resources do not always ensure psychological security or the capacity to persevere. A fortress can remain standing while the confidence of its occupants collapses.

In both verses, qadhf describes casting fear into hearts. The kind of feeling is specified by رُعْب (ruʿb), and its location by the reference to their hearts.

This is a nonphysical use of the root and should not be interpreted as The God throwing a physical object.

5. Qadhf as a juristic term and its distinction from Qur’anic wording

In Arabic, qadhf can mean directing insulting or accusatory speech at another person.

The expression قَذَفَ الْمُحْصَنَةَ (qadhafa l-muḥṣana) is used for accusing a chaste woman of illicit intercourse, and qadhf acquired a specialized meaning in jurisprudence.

However, the Qur’anic verses concerning this accusation employ R M Y, rather than Q DH F.

وَالَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَأْتُوا بِأَرْبَعَةِ شُهَدَاءَ فَاجْلِدُوهُمْ ثَمَانِينَ جَلْدَةً وَلَا تَقْبَلُوا لَهُمْ شَهَادَةً أَبَدًا ۚ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْفَاسِقُونَ

Wa-lladhīna yarmūna l-muḥṣanāti thumma lam yaʾtū bi-arbaʿati shuhadāʾa fa-jlidūhum thamānīna jaldatan wa-lā taqbalū lahum shahādatan abadan wa-ulāʾika humu l-fāsiqūn.

Those who accuse chaste women and then do not produce four witnesses: flog them with eighty lashes, and never accept testimony from them; and those are the defiantly disobedient.

An-Nur 24:4

Al-Nūr 24:6 and al-Nūr 24:23 also use يَرْمُونَ (yarmūna) for making accusations.

Thus, what juristic terminology calls the prescribed penalty for qadhf is expressed in the Qur’anic text through derivatives of R M Y.

This technical designation relates to the Arabic semantic field of qadhf, but the meaning of an accusation of illicit intercourse must not be imposed on its other Qur’anic uses.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

Q DH F (qadhf) and R M Y (ramy)

Both roots denote throwing and casting, and both can describe directing speech or an accusation at another person in Arabic.

Ramy has a broad semantic field covering shooting, throwing objects, and imputing wrongdoing or accusations.

Many uses of qadhf emphasize casting something, sometimes with force or intensity.

Nevertheless, its use for placing Moses in the chest shows that force and violence cannot serve as an absolute distinction between the roots.

Q DH F (qadhf) and L Q Y (ilqāʾ)

Ilqāʾ concerns casting, placing, or bringing something into a field of reception or encounter.

Qadhf emphasizes the act of casting, while ilqāʾ may also highlight placement or arrival in a field of reception. This is a contextual tendency, not a rigid rule governing every use.

In Ṭā Hā 20:39 and Ṭā Hā 20:87 the two roots appear together. Their proximity shows their overlapping meanings and the possibility of expressing different aspects of one event.

Q DH F (qadhf) and R J M (rajm)

In physical usage, rajm often concerns throwing stones; in nonphysical usage, it concerns conjecture and speech directed at others.

In Sabaʾ 34:53, qadhf at the unseen, and in al-Kahf 18:22, rajm at the unseen, describe speaking without sufficient knowledge.

Both images portray casting something toward a target that is not adequately known.

Q DH F (qadhf) and N B DH (nabdh)

Nabdh denotes throwing and putting something aside. Nonphysical uses can describe abandoning a covenant or putting something behind oneself.

Qadhf highlights casting and sending; nabdh more often foregrounds discarding or setting aside.

Q DH F (qadhf) and D M GH (damgh)

Physically, damgh concerns a blow reaching the brain. Figuratively, it can express decisively crushing something.

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18, qadhf describes casting truth against falsehood, and damgh describes truth’s decisive effect upon it.

Throwing and crushing are therefore distinct stages; their meanings should not be merged.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

According to its object and context, قَذَفَ (qadhafa) may be rendered “cast,” “threw,” or “placed in”; nonphysical uses may call for “cast into the heart” or “spoke conjecturally.”

  • Ṭā Hā 20:39: place him in the chest; then cast him into the water.
  • Ṭā Hā 20:87: we cast them away.
  • Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18: We cast truth against falsehood.
  • Al-Aḥzāb 33:26 and al-Ḥashr 59:2: He cast terror into their hearts.
  • Sabaʾ 34:48: He casts the truth.
  • Sabaʾ 34:53: they cast conjectures at the unseen.
  • Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8: they are pelted from every side.

Brief root meaning: Casting or throwing something toward a destination or into a place; figuratively, directing speech, a claim, or an accusation at another, or casting a feeling or meaning into a human heart.

Conclusion

Q DH F concerns casting and throwing. Early Arabic uses it for physical objects as well as speech and other nonphysical matters.

The Qur’an employs it for placing Moses in the chest and casting him into water, casting ornaments, being pelted, casting terror into hearts, presenting truth, and making unsupported assertions.

One striking contrast appears in Sabaʾ: The God casts truth as the Knower of unseen things, whereas people removed from the truth cast their conjectures toward it.

The core image remains casting; the contrast lies in what is cast and the knowledge supporting it. The object and context determine the particular meaning.
