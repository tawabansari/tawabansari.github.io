A study of nafal and nāfilah in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from spoils and gifts to an additional gift to Abraham and the Prophet’s night worship

The three-letter root N F L concerns giving, granting, and adding something to what already exists or has been prescribed. Nafal may denote spoils or a gift; nāfilah a gift or addition beyond an original amount or recognized share; and anfāl is the plural of nafal, referring in warfare and the distribution of property to spoils and gifts from them.

The family also extends to worship in Arabic. An act performed in addition to a duty or principal act may be called nāfilah. Expressions such as ṣalāt al-nāfilah and al-tanafful are familiar in religious and jurisprudential literature.

N F L should not, however, be defined by starting with the later technical expression “supererogatory prayer.” The Qur’an uses the root in three settings: war spoils in Al-Anfāl, the Prophet’s night vigil in Al-Isrāʾ, and the granting of Isaac and Jacob to Abraham in Al-Anbiyāʾ.

These uses show that the family is confined neither to worship nor to war property. Giving and addition form an important semantic connection, but the kind of gift, its status, and what it supplements must be determined from each verse’s construction and context.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • نَفَلَ: To give, grant, or add something for another person.
  • نَفَل: Spoils, a gift, or a grant.
  • نَفْل: An addition or gift; in religious usage, an act beyond a prescribed duty.
  • أَنْفَال and نِفَال: Plurals of nafal, especially spoils and acquired property.
  • نَافِلَة: A gift, additional grant, or something added to an original amount or previous share.
  • نَوَافِل: The plural of nāfilah: gifts and additions; technically, acts of worship beyond obligatory duties.
  • نَفَّلَ: To grant something to another person or increase their share.
  • أَنْفَلَ: To give someone an additional grant or share.
  • تَنْفِيل: Giving, granting, or adding to a share.
  • تَنَفَّلَ: To perform an act voluntarily; in religious usage, to undertake worship beyond a prescribed act.
  • نَوْفَل: Descriptively, a very generous man; also, in some lexical uses, a name for the sea.

Arabic dictionaries record other forms under this entry. For example, intafala can mean denying something or dissociating oneself from an accusation. Some lexical accounts relate it to intafā and explain it through sound substitution.

Nafal also names a plant, while nufal in some accounts denotes the fourth through sixth nights of a month. These limited uses should not be forced into derivations from giving or spoils.

Only two forms from this family occur in the Qur’an: anfāl and nāfilah.

Al-anfāl occurs twice in Al-Anfāl 8:1, and nāfilah once each in Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72.

The root therefore occurs four times in three Qur’anic verses. No verb from it appears in the Qur’an.

The semantic core: giving and addition

One lexical analysis identifies giving and granting as the root’s semantic core.

Other lexical explanations also emphasize “an addition to the original” in defining nafl and nāfilah.

The two explanations are related but not identical.

Giving concerns a grant passing from giver to recipient. Being additional describes its relationship to something already present or regarded as the original amount.

Someone may, for example, have a share in property and receive more. The new grant is then additional to the previous share.

Someone may also have a child and then be granted a grandchild. The grandchild adds a further generation, without any financial share or legal entitlement being involved.

Nāfilah should therefore not always be understood as something necessarily given after a religious obligation or legal entitlement. The original and the addition may have different relationships depending on the subject.

Nor is every gift in Arabic necessarily called nafl. The root has its own attested uses, and not every grant should be assigned to it without evidence.

2. N F L in Early Arabic: Spoils, Gifts, and Additions

Nafal in warfare and acquiring spoils

Arabic uses nafal for spoils and property acquired through armed confrontation.

Its plural is anfāl and, in some uses, nifāl.

An elegy attributed to Janūb, the sister of ʿAmr Dhī al-Kalb, reads:

وَقَدْ عَلِمَتْ فَهْمٌ عِنْدَ اللِّقَاءِ
بِأَنَّهُمْ لَكَ كَانُوا نِفَالَا

wa-qad ʿalimat fahmun ʿinda al-liqāʾi / bi-annahum laka kānū nifālā

Translation: “Fahm knew at the encounter that they were spoils for you.”

The poet describes her brother’s prowess in battle, likening his enemies to spoils helpless before him.

Nifāl here appears in the setting of battle and victory.

The example matters because it shows the use of nafal for spoils in early Arabic poetry, rather than only in later legal terminology.

The verse is transmitted in collections of Hudhalī poetry and early literary works and attributed to a pre-Islamic poet. The attribution is established in the literary tradition, although a poet’s period must be distinguished from the certain date of every individual verse.

Nafal as a benefit or gift

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, says:

إِنَّ تَقْوَى رَبِّنَا خَيْرُ نَفَلْ
وَبِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ رَيْثِي وَعَجَلْ

inna taqwā rabbinā khayru nafal / wa-bi-idhni Allāhi raythī wa-ʿajal

Translation: “Mindfulness of our Lord is the finest bounty; and by The God’s permission are my delay and haste.”

Here nafal is not restricted to war spoils but denotes a valuable benefit or gift.

The poet calls mindfulness the best nafal: something he regards as more valuable than other gains.

The example shows that nafal can occur in an ethical and religious context without meaning supererogatory prayer.

Labīd experienced both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The date of this piece cannot be inferred from the poet’s name alone. It is useful evidence for Arabic close to the Qur’an’s period, but should not be called securely pre-Qur’anic without further evidence.

A gift beyond a share

Arabic also uses naffala for giving something beyond an existing share.

For example:

نَفَّلَهُ شَيْئًا

naffalahu shayʾan

Translation: “He gave him something.”

And in the distribution of spoils:

نَفَّلَ الْقَائِدُ الْجُنْدَ

naffala al-qāʾidu al-junda

Translation: “The commander granted the troops spoils or an additional share.”

In such uses, tanfīl may concern assigning property to a person or group who took part in an undertaking.

The verb alone does not establish that the grant was fair or that the recipient necessarily had a greater entitlement. Those questions depend on the circumstances and criteria of distribution.

Spoils must also be distinguished from an additional share of spoils. In some early uses, nafal names spoils generally; in others, a special grant from them.

Restricting all uses to an extra share following distribution would therefore be inaccurate.

Nawfal: very generous

Nawfal is a familiar Arabic description of a generous man.

For example, a poem attributed to Aʿshā Bāhilah reads:

أَخُو رَغَائِبَ يُعْطِيهَا وَيُسْأَلُهَا
يَأْبَى الظُّلَامَةَ مِنْهُ النَّوْفَلُ الزُّفَرُ

akhū raghāʾiba yuʿṭīhā wa-yusʾaluhā / yaʾbā al-ẓulāmata minhu al-nawfalu al-zufaru

Translation: “He deals in precious gifts: he gives them and is asked for them; that generous, resourceful man refuses to submit to injustice.”

Nawfal here describes someone known for generosity.

Dictionaries also attest the word for the sea. Abundant seawater and a person’s generosity may help explain the two uses, but the historical direction of the semantic transfer is uncertain.

Nawfal has also been a personal name. Bearing that name does not establish that someone was actually very generous or that this quality necessarily motivated the naming.

Other uses in the family

Arabic dictionaries also record nafal as the name of a small plant.

Some accounts describe a low-growing wild plant. Some dictionaries also record nafl for cold weather or other conditions.

Such limited uses show that identical forms in Arabic dictionaries cannot all be derived from giving and addition without evidence.

Similarly, intafala in the sense of denying something or clearing oneself has been associated with the family of negation.

Being grouped under a lexical entry must here be distinguished from proof of a clear historical relationship.

For understanding the Qur’an, giving, spoils, and addition are central, since all four Qur’anic occurrences belong to that semantic field.

3. Al-Anfāl in the Qur’an: Spoils and Their Distribution

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْأَنفَالِ قُلِ الْأَنفَالُ لِلَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَأَصْلِحُوا ذَاتَ بَيْنِكُمْ وَأَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-anfāli quli al-anfālu li-Allāhi wa-al-rasūli fa-ittaqū Allāha wa-aṣliḥū dhāta baynikum wa-aṭīʿū Allāha wa-rasūlahu in kuntum muʾminīna

Translation: “They ask you about the spoils. Say: The spoils belong to The God and the Messenger. So be mindful of The God, set right the relations among yourselves, and obey The God and His Messenger, if you are believers.”

Al-Anfāl 8:1

This is the only verse containing al-anfāl. The word occurs twice: first in the question and then in the response.

In this context, anfāl concerns property and gains acquired after armed confrontation, raising questions about authority over them or their distribution.

“Spoils” is an appropriate lexical rendering here, although the root’s history also includes gifts and additional shares.

Why were spoils called nafal?

Lexical accounts propose several explanations for this designation.

One treats spoils as a gain additional to the primary purpose of battle: something obtained alongside its principal outcome.

Another concerns extra shares of spoils given to particular individuals.

Some exegetical accounts also describe spoils as an additional grant to Muslims, comparing the rules governing spoils in different religious communities.

These explanations are not historically or exegetically identical.

Arabic usage establishes the word’s connection with spoils and giving, but no one explanation should be treated as its certain historical origin without independent evidence.

Nor does the association with giving mean that every item acquired in war belongs unconditionally, ethically or legally, to whoever obtained it.

The verse itself addresses this by referring authority over the spoils to The God and the Messenger.

قُلِ الْأَنْفَالُ لِلَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ

The question about spoils leads to this response:

قُلِ الْأَنْفَالُ لِلَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ

quli al-anfālu li-Allāhi wa-al-rasūli

“Say: The spoils belong to The God and the Messenger.”

The expression refers authority over the spoils and their disposition to The God and the Messenger.

It does not allow individuals or groups to determine their final entitlement independently of divine command merely on the basis of their power or role in acquiring the spoils.

Nor should li-Allāhi wa-al-rasūl immediately be turned into unlimited personal ownership of all the property by the Messenger.

The manner of distribution requires examining other passages about spoils and the Messenger’s role in carrying out divine command.

Lexically, anfāl itself says nothing about ownership relations or distribution methods. These follow from the sentence’s construction and other Qur’anic rulings.

Spoils, mindfulness, and mending relations

Immediately after the answer about spoils, the Qur’an states:

فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَأَصْلِحُوا ذَاتَ بَيْنِكُمْ

fa-ittaqū Allāha wa-aṣliḥū dhāta baynikum

Translation: “So be mindful of The God and set right the relations among yourselves.”

The connection matters.

After identifying authority over the spoils, the Qur’an directs the audience’s attention to mindfulness and mending their mutual relations.

The issue concerns more than property: conduct when claiming and dividing benefits also matters.

People who shared a difficult experience may disagree over their shares. Insisting solely on individual claims can turn disagreement into hostility and destroy cooperation.

The verse places mending relationships alongside the disposition of spoils.

Mindfulness, reconciliation, and justice should not, however, be presented as lexical meanings of nafal. These are principles the verse expresses for its audience’s conduct concerning anfāl.

Anfāl and acquired spoils in Al-Anfāl 8:41

The same surah states:

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا غَنِمْتُم مِّن شَيْءٍ فَأَنَّ لِلَّهِ خُمُسَهُ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ

wa-iʿlamū annamā ghanimtum min shayʾin fa-anna li-Allāhi khumusahu wa-li-al-rasūli wa-li-dhī al-qurbā wa-al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīni wa-ibni al-sabīli

Translation: “Know that, whatever spoils you acquire, one fifth belongs to The God, the Messenger, near relatives, orphans, the needy, and the stranded traveller.”

Al-Anfāl 8:41

The verb ghanimtum is from GH N M, rather than N F L.

The two verses nevertheless concern related subjects.

Al-Anfāl 8:1 raises the question of spoils and authority to decide about them. Al-Anfāl 8:41 describes the fifth and the groups considered in its distribution.

Exegetical history records disagreement over the verses’ relationship. Some understood verse 41 as establishing the ruling after the opening verse; others regarded the opening verse as still operative, expressing basic authority and responsibility for distributing anfāl.

That disagreement should not be transferred into the root’s lexical definition.

Clearly, the Qur’an treats property acquired in war as more than individual appropriation: it also addresses distribution, others’ rights, and responsibility before divine command.

The legal details of spoils, individual shares, and the distinction from fayʾ require separate study of the relevant passages, including Al-Anfāl 8:41, Al-Ḥashr 59:6, and Al-Ḥashr 59:7.

A root study of N F L should not replace an independent investigation of the laws of spoils and public property.

4. Nāfilah in the Formation of Abraham’s Family

وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ إِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ نَافِلَةً وَكُلًّا جَعَلْنَا صَالِحِينَ

wa-wahabnā lahu isḥāqa wa-yaʿqūba nāfilatan wa-kullan jaʿalnā ṣāliḥīna

Translation: “We granted him Isaac and Jacob as an additional gift, and made each righteous.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72

The verse is important for understanding nāfilah because the word has no direct connection here with prayer or a human act of worship.

The Qur’an speaks about Abraham and the granting of Isaac and Jacob to him.

Wahabnā, from W H B, means giving or granting. Nāfilah appears in the same setting, emphasizing the grant’s additional or bountiful character.

Does nāfilah refer only to Jacob?

Two familiar readings of the expression exist.

In the first, nāfilah refers to Jacob. Abraham received Isaac as a son, then Jacob, Isaac’s son, was also granted to him.

Jacob is thus an addition to the gift of Isaac.

Arabic also attests nāfilah for a child’s child, a grandchild. This is explained through the same relationship: a child is one’s direct offspring, and that child’s child adds a further generation.

In the second reading, nāfilah concerns the combined gift of Isaac and Jacob. Both are presented as an additional grant and divine bounty to Abraham.

The readings do not fundamentally disagree about the root’s basic meaning. Both connect nāfilah with giving and addition.

They differ over whether nāfilah in the sentence applies to Jacob alone or encompasses both gifts.

The expression itself does not require imposing either interpretation as certain.

Connections with other passages about Abraham

Maryam 19:49 states:

فَلَمَّا اعْتَزَلَهُمْ وَمَا يَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ وَهَبْنَا لَهُ إِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَكُلًّا جَعَلْنَا نَبِيًّا

fa-lammā iʿtazalahum wa-mā yaʿbudūna min dūni Allāhi wahabnā lahu isḥāqa wa-yaʿqūba wa-kullan jaʿalnā nabiyyan

Translation: “When he withdrew from them and what they worshipped apart from The God, We granted him Isaac and Jacob, and made each a prophet.”

This verse also describes the granting of Isaac and Jacob to Abraham, but does not use nāfilah.

The comparison shows that wahabnā expresses giving by itself, while nāfilah in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72 emphasizes the gift’s additional or bountiful character.

Ibrāhīm 14:39 also reports Abraham saying:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي وَهَبَ لِي عَلَى الْكِبَرِ إِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِسْحَاقَ

al-ḥamdu li-Allāhi alladhī wahaba lī ʿalā al-kibari ismāʿīla wa-isḥāqa

Translation: “Praise belongs to The God, who granted me Ishmael and Isaac in old age.”

Abraham thus regards his children as a divine gift.

These passages do not establish that “grandchild” or “child” is the invariable meaning of nāfilah, explaining its other uses too.

“Grandchild” is one attested application: in this setting, a child’s child is understood as an addition to the previous generation.

Giving and addition therefore take precedence over the particular sense “grandchild” in the root’s general definition.

5. Nāfilah in Night Worship: Al-Isrāʾ 17:79

وَمِنَ اللَّيْلِ فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ نَافِلَةً لَّكَ عَسَىٰ أَن يَبْعَثَكَ رَبُّكَ مَقَامًا مَّحْمُودًا

wa-mina al-layli fa-tahajjad bihi nāfilatan laka ʿasā an yabʿathaka rabbuka maqāman maḥmūdan

Translation: “Keep vigil with it during part of the night, as an addition for you; perhaps your Lord will raise you to a praised station.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:79

This is one of the Qur’an’s two uses of nāfilah.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 commands the establishment of ṣalāh and mentions the dawn recitation. Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 then commands the Prophet to keep vigil during part of the night.

Nāfilah here concerns worship, rather than property or children.

فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ and the role of nāfilah

Tahajjad is from H J D. In familiar usage it concerns wakefulness and keeping vigil, especially rising after sleep.

In light of the preceding verse, the pronoun in bihi is understood as referring to the Qur’an.

The command therefore concerns keeping vigil with the Qur’an.

Nāfilatan laka describes the additional character of this act for its addressee.

The meaning of nāfilah must nevertheless be distinguished from the act of tahajjud itself.

Tahajjud is the commanded act. Nāfilah describes its position relative to other duties or acts.

N F L should therefore not be defined as waking at night or performing night prayer.

Does nāfilah necessarily mean a non-obligatory act?

In jurisprudential literature, nāfilah commonly denotes worship performed beyond obligatory duties, whose omission does not have the status of omitting an obligation.

This technical use became established in the history of jurisprudence.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:79 requires closer attention, however, because the verse uses an imperative:

فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ

fa-tahajjad bihi

“So keep vigil with it.”

It then calls the act nāfilatan laka.

The word nāfilah alone therefore does not prove that this command entailed no obligation or special responsibility for the Prophet.

Exegetical history also records disagreement about the command’s status.

Some interpreters regarded night vigil as a special duty imposed on the Prophet in addition to the community’s general duties.

Others understood it as an additional act bringing him greater bounty and reward.

Both readings relate to addition, but they do not yield the same practical ruling.

The disagreement shows that a definite ruling on whether tahajjud was obligatory cannot be derived from the lexical meaning of nāfilah alone.

نَافِلَةً لَّكَ: an addition for the addressee

Laka refers to the verse’s addressee, the Prophet.

It identifies the addition in relation to him.

Nāfilah itself does not restrict an act to a particular person. That feature follows from laka and the construction of the address.

The verse’s closing phrase also mentions maqāman maḥmūdan, a praised station.

This expression matters in examining the Prophet’s position and the act’s outcome, but the praised station is not the meaning of nāfilah itself.

The basic meaning here is therefore addition. Its particular devotional status and outcome require examining the whole verse and related passages.

Does the Qur’an call every additional act of worship nāfilah?

No. The Qur’an discusses worship, remembrance, supplication, spending, glorification, and good deeds in various settings without calling all of them nāfilah.

Within the Qur’an, nāfilah concerns night worship only in this verse.

The technical category nawāfil, covering a range of additional prayers and acts, should therefore not be imported wholesale into the verse as a predetermined classification.

This does not make worship beyond prescribed duties worthless or forbidden. The issue is the distinction between Qur’anic wording and later technical classifications.

A full account of tahajjud, night ṣalāh, and additional acts requires an independent study of worship in the Qur’an.

The N F L article should clarify addition and its relationship to the verse’s construction.

6. N F L and Related Roots

N F L and W H B

Wahb concerns giving and granting in Arabic.

Nafl also belongs to giving, but many of its uses emphasize an addition or a special share.

The two families appear together in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72:

وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ إِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ نَافِلَةً

wa-wahabnā lahu isḥāqa wa-yaʿqūba nāfilatan

Wahb expresses the granting, while nāfilah emphasizes the gift’s additional character.

This contextual distinction does not establish that every wahb lacks bounty or that every nafl necessarily comes without a prior request.

A gift may both be an instance of wahb and be called nāfilah.

N F L and F Ḍ L

Faḍl concerns excess, superiority, and something beyond another amount or condition.

This semantic field approaches nāfilah.

Faḍl nevertheless has a broad range, from superiority and distinction to giving and enjoyment of benefits.

Nafl in its principal branch is more closely associated with a gift, spoils, or an addition given to or obtained for someone.

Nāfilah can therefore often be explained as an additional gift, but not every faḍl is necessarily called nafl.

Nor should moral virtue become part of every use of nafl. Spoils or an additional share may become the object of dispute or unjust demands.

Study of F Ḍ L

N F L and GH N M

In financial usage, ghunm concerns an acquired gain or spoils.

Nafal can also mean spoils, but some military uses denote a grant or special share from them.

Anfāl in Al-Anfāl 8:1 clearly concerns war spoils, while Al-Anfāl 8:41 uses ghanimtum.

This overlap does not make the roots fully synonymous.

Ghunm may emphasize acquiring the gain or spoils; nafl may also emphasize their being granted or additional.

Yet familiar Arabic usage also employs anfāl for the whole body of spoils. It should not always be restricted to a share separate from the main spoils.

N F L and F R Ḍ

Farḍ has familiar uses involving prescribing, determining, or making obligatory.

Nāfilah, by contrast, technically denotes an act of worship beyond a prescribed obligation.

This opposition is familiar in jurisprudence, but the Qur’anic definition of N F L does not begin with it.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72, nāfilah is a divine gift; no human act is being classified as obligatory or recommended.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:79, nāfilah accompanies a command. Its ruling therefore cannot be determined merely from the technical opposition between nāfilah and farḍ.

“Addition” is thus more fundamental to understanding both Qur’anic uses; “non-obligatory worship” is one later specialized use.

Study of F R Ḍ

N F L and N F ʿ

The roots share their first letters but differ in meaning.

Nafʿ concerns benefit and usefulness.

Nafl concerns giving, spoils, and addition.

A nafl may benefit its recipient, but benefit alone is not the meaning of nafl.

Nor is everything beneficial an additional gift or spoils.

Similarity of sound and overlapping applications therefore do not establish semantic or historical identity.

Study of N F ʿ

7. Difficulties in Understanding and Translation

A common limitation is defining the root by starting with the legal term nāfilah.

Understanding nāfilah solely as supererogatory prayer makes Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72 obscure or calls for an artificial explanation, since it concerns the gift of Isaac and Jacob.

Examining the family through giving and addition makes both uses intelligible.

The response to a restricted definition should not go to the opposite extreme. The general meaning “additional gift” does not invalidate the devotional use in Al-Isrāʾ 17:79.

Addition there concerns night worship, and that setting must remain part of the explanation.

Another difficulty is restricting anfāl to a commander’s extra allocation to particular warriors. That use existed in language and the history of distributing spoils, but does not encompass all Arabic uses of anfāl.

Al-Anfāl 8:1 concerns war spoils and authority over their distribution. “Spoils” is therefore a natural and defensible equivalent.

The association with gifts and addition also does not make spoils a guaranteed moral reward for every participant in war. Entitlement and distribution are separate ethical and legal matters.

A further restriction should be avoided in Abraham’s case. One reading takes nāfilah as Jacob, Abraham’s grandson, but “grandchild” should not become its general meaning everywhere.

In each example, the definition must encompass legitimate uses without becoming so vague that gifts, spoils, and acts of worship lose their distinctions.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For nafal generally, “gift,” “grant,” or “additional benefit” is suitable.

In warfare, “spoils” may be more precise. When nafal denotes an amount beyond an allotted share, “additional share” or “special grant” is preferable.

For anfāl in Al-Anfāl 8:1, “spoils” is the best concise equivalent.

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْأَنفَالِ

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-anfāli

“They ask you about the spoils.”

قُلِ الْأَنفَالُ لِلَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ

quli al-anfālu li-Allāhi wa-al-rasūli

“Say: The spoils belong to The God and the Messenger.”

The explanation should note that anfāl belongs to giving and addition and in warfare denotes spoils or sometimes special allocations from them.

“Additional gift” is suitable for nāfilah in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72.

وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ إِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ نَافِلَةً

wa-wahabnā lahu isḥāqa wa-yaʿqūba nāfilatan

“We granted him Isaac and Jacob as an additional gift.”

This retains the general reading of an additional grant. The alternative referring especially to Jacob can be explained in a note.

“An addition for you” is suitable for nāfilah in Al-Isrāʾ 17:79.

وَمِنَ اللَّيْلِ فَتَهَجَّدْ بِهِ نَافِلَةً لَّكَ

wa-mina al-layli fa-tahajjad bihi nāfilatan laka

“Keep vigil with it during part of the night, as an addition for you.”

The addition is explained through night worship, without restricting the word’s direct equivalent to “supererogatory prayer.”

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: Giving and granting; a gift or benefit additional to an original amount or recognized share. In warfare, spoils and a special allocation.

Al-anfāl in Al-Anfāl 8:1: Spoils and property acquired through armed confrontation, concerning authority over and distribution of which a question was raised.

Nāfilah in Al-Isrāʾ 17:79: An addition for the Prophet; in context, keeping vigil with the Qur’an as commanded to him.

Nāfilah in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72: An additional gift or grant concerning Isaac and Jacob, possibly referring especially to Jacob as Abraham’s grandson.

9. Conclusion

N F L concerns giving, gifts, and addition in Arabic.

In general usage, nafal may be a gift or benefit received by someone. In warfare, nafal and its plural anfāl denote spoils and, in some cases, additional shares from them.

Arabic also uses nāfilah for an additional grant, a child’s child, and, in religious terminology, an act beyond a prescribed duty.

The Qur’an uses the root in three different settings.

Al-Anfāl 8:1 concerns war spoils and authority over their disposition. The same verse also recalls mindfulness and mending relations among believers.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72, nāfilah is an additional gift expressed in granting Isaac and Jacob to Abraham. This shows that the word is not restricted to worship.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:79, nāfilah concerns the Prophet’s night worship. This fits addition, but its legal status does not follow from nāfilah alone.

The three settings clarify how the meanings relate without letting any one replace all the others.

Nafal is not always war spoils, nāfilah is not always supererogatory prayer, and addition does not invariably mean something beyond a legal entitlement or religious obligation.

Each use requires identifying what is given or added, to whom it belongs, and how it relates to an original amount or previous condition.

Final definition: In its principal branch, N F L denotes giving and granting, and a benefit or gift received as an addition or special favour. In warfare, nafal and anfāl denote spoils and sometimes special allocations from them. Nāfilah may be an additional gift or act: a grant such as Jacob continuing Abraham’s line, or additional worship in the setting of the Prophet’s night vigil. Each derivative’s precise meaning and legal or devotional status follow from construction and context, rather than a single fixed technical term imposed on the whole family.
