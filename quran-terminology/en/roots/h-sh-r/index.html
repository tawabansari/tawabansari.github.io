1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root «ح ش ر» (Ḥ SH R) concerns gathering scattered people or creatures, directing them, and bringing them toward a specified place. This gathering may follow someone’s command, occur within a social or military event, or be brought about by The God’s power.

The Qur’an most often uses this root in connection with gathering people for resurrection, reckoning, and disclosure of the reality of their deeds. Its use nevertheless extends beyond the Resurrection. The same family describes assembling Sulaymān’s troops, collecting magicians at Pharaoh’s command, gathering people on an appointed day, and a group among the People of the Book departing from their homes.

The Qur’an also describes the gathering of animals and birds. These uses show that ḥashr is not inherently restricted to humans.

Ḥ SH R occurs forty-three times, in forty-three verses across twenty-eight surahs. Its Qur’anic forms fall into four groups: thirty-seven Form I verbs, two occurrences of the verbal noun ḥashr, three of the active participle ḥāshirīn, and one of the passive participle maḥshūrah.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: forty-three occurrences and four morphological groups

Important forms in this family include:

  • حَشَرَ / يَحْشُرُ (ḥashara / yaḥshuru): to gather, assemble, and, where appropriate, direct people or creatures onward. Qur’anic use: yes, in various Form I inflections.
  • حَشْر (ḥashr): gathering or assembly; in some contexts, gathering for reckoning or a specified event. Qur’anic use: yes, twice, in Qāf 50:44 and Al-Ḥashr 59:2.
  • حَاشِر (ḥāshir): a gatherer, someone collecting people or groups. Qur’anic use: yes, as the plural ḥāshirīn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:111, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:53.
  • مَحْشُور (maḥshūr): gathered or assembled. Qur’anic use: yes, as the feminine maḥshūratan in Ṣād 38:19.
  • حَشَرَ / يَحْشِرُ (ḥashara / yaḥshiru): another Form I pattern recorded in some lexicons. Qur’anic use: no, in the muṣḥaf vocalization used as this article’s basis.
  • مَحْشَر (maḥshar): a place of assembly, or a place toward which people are gathered and directed. Qur’anic use: no, as an independent noun.
  • مَحْشِر (maḥshir): a gathering place; another vocalization of the place noun recorded in lexicons. Qur’anic use: no.
  • تَحْشِير (taḥshīr): extensive gathering or assembling people or things. Qur’anic use: no.
  • حَشَّرَ (ḥashshara): to gather or pack together; in some uses, compressing and making something slender. Qur’anic use: no.
  • اِنْحَشَرَ (inḥashara): to assemble or be brought into a gathering. Qur’anic use: no.
  • تَحَشَّرَ (taḥashshara): to gather together. Qur’anic use: no.
  • حَشَرَة (ḥasharah): in early Arabic, a name for small land creatures; in modern usage, chiefly an insect. Qur’anic use: no.
  • حَشَرَات (ḥasharāt): plural of ḥasharah. Qur’anic use: no.
  • حَشْر / حَشْرَة (ḥashr / ḥashrah): in some descriptive uses, slender, compact, or pointed, as in descriptions of ears and arrow components. Qur’anic use: no.
  • حَشْوَر (ḥashwar): in some early uses, a person or animal of large, compact build. Qur’anic use: no.

Forqan’s Qur’anic root index should record this family under Ḥ SH R. Its nominal and adjectival uses should also be distinguished from finite verbs.

Importantly, ḥashr in the Qur’an is not restricted to bringing the dead to life. In resurrection contexts, it relates to raising and returning people, but the root itself emphasizes gathering.

2. The linguistic core: gathering with directed movement

Ḥashr: gathering from dispersion

Arabic ḥasharahum describes someone assembling a group.

The group may consist of a town’s people, troops, animals, or persons required to appear in a specified place.

Early lexical accounts connect ḥashr with two concepts: al-jamʿ and al-sawq.

Jamʿ concerns collecting or gathering, whereas sawq emphasizes driving or directing movement toward a particular destination.

In Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, al-Fayyūmī records the following definition after explaining the verb:

«الْحَشْرُ الْجَمْعُ مَعَ سَوْقٍ» (al-ḥashru al-jamʿu maʿa sawq).

That is, gathering accompanied by directing or driving onward.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Maqāyīs al-Lughah: gathering with directed movement

This helps explain many Qur’anic uses.

For example, assembling troops may involve summoning people from different locations and directing them toward a common place.

At the resurrection gathering too, people are brought together from dispersion and placed where they face the reality of their deeds.

The definition does not, however, establish that every ḥashr necessarily involves physical violence or military force.

Sawq can describe directing and moving onward. The kind of compulsion, intensity of movement, and treatment of those gathered must be established independently from context.

Gathering and coincidental presence

Imagine people happening to be present in a market.

Their presence is not necessarily ḥashr in the sense of collecting and directing them.

If someone is commissioned to bring people from different homes and neighborhoods to a designated square, the action has a clearer connection with ḥashr.

Ḥashr therefore usually highlights a process of assembling or becoming part of one gathering.

Arabic can also use the root for the resulting assembly itself, rather than every preceding stage.

A particular official, written order, or movement from several locations should therefore not be made a fixed requirement in every use.

The physical image: gathering and directing a herd

Gathering scattered animals provides one clear image for understanding ḥashr.

Imagine a shepherd whose animals graze in different directions. Bringing them back requires directing their movement toward a specified place.

Gathering and directing are connected in this image.

Ḥashr nevertheless extends beyond it.

In the Qur’an, troops may be assembled, people summoned to a public occasion, or humans gathered for reckoning.

Those gathered may also come willingly or resist.

The herd image clarifies the lexical meaning, but every feature of animals, shepherds, and herding should not be transferred to the gathering of humans.

Does ḥashr always involve compulsion?

In many uses, gathering accompanies a command, power, or obligation.

The root alone, however, does not determine the intensity or kind of obligation.

Troops may assemble at their commander’s order; elsewhere, people gather to watch a public event.

Both processes may be called ḥashr, although the relationship between gatherer and gathered differs.

Resurrection verses attribute gathering to The God’s power, but the condition of every group is not identical.

In Maryam 19:85, the mindful are gathered as a delegation, while other verses describe offenders or wrongdoers differently.

Driving onward should therefore not imply that everyone is gathered in the same humiliating manner.

3. Early Arabic evidence and branches of meaning

Ḥashr in Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry

A rajaz verse by Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj reads:

وَمَا نَجَا مِنْ حَشْرِهَا الْمَحْشُوشِ
وَحْشٌ وَلَا طَمْشٌ مِنَ الطُّمُوشِ

wa-mā najā min ḥashrihā al-maḥshūshi / waḥshun wa-lā ṭamshun mina al-ṭumūshi

English translation

Neither wild beast nor any human escaped that harsh year’s gathering pressure.

Ibn Barrī explains the witness’s ḥashr as the effect of a harsh year of drought.

Drought can drive people and animals from scattered places toward centers with water and resources. It may also destroy their property and livestock.

Some Arabic uses thus describe a hard year as having gathered a group’s possessions: it destroyed them or pressed their owners out of their previous circumstances.

This semantic extension connects gathering and directing with the pressure of harsh conditions.

It does not establish that Qur’anic ḥashr means drought or destruction of possessions.

Ruʾbah was an Islamic-era poet; this verse is therefore early Arabic evidence, rather than securely pre-Islamic evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ṭ M SH: Ruʾbah’s witness and Ibn Barrī’s explanation

A slender, compact ear in early poetry

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab attribute this verse to al-Namir ibn Tawlab. Tāj al-ʿArūs also records al-Ṣaghānī’s view that it belongs to Rabīʿah ibn Jusham al-Namarī. The attribution is therefore not treated as uniform or certain:

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Tāj al-ʿArūs: differing attributions of the slender-ear witness

لَهَا أُذُنٌ حَشْرَةٌ مَشْرَةٌ
كَإِعْلِيطِ مَرْخٍ إِذَا مَا صَفِرْ

lahā udhunun ḥashratun mashratun / ka-iʿlīṭi markhin idhā mā ṣafir

English translation

It has a slender, leaflike ear, like the seed casing of the markh plant when emptied.

Ḥashrah here describes the ear. In Al-Amālī, Abū ʿAlī al-Qālī explains mashrah as leaflike and ṣafira as becoming empty; the final expression therefore does not mean becoming yellow.

Abū ʿAlī al-Qālī, Al-Amālī: iʿlīṭ, mashrah, and ṣafira

Lexical explanations relate the word to an ear being small, slender, compact, or pointed.

Not every form of the Arabic Ḥ SH R family therefore concerns gathering people or animals.

Some uses also involve compactness of structure or something becoming slender and trimmed.

This descriptive branch does not occur in the Qur’an and should not displace gathering in defining all its Qur’anic uses.

Ḥasharāt in early Arabic

In present-day Arabic, ḥasharāt normally describes small animals such as ants and flies.

Early Arabic ḥasharah and ḥasharāt al-arḍ had a different range.

Some lexical accounts include jerboas, hedgehogs, and certain small reptiles.

The early use therefore differs from modern zoological classification.

Ḥasharah itself also does not occur in the Qur’an.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: ḥasharah and ḥashrah

Wa-idhā al-wuḥūshu ḥushirat in Al-Takwīr 81:5 therefore cannot be translated as “when the insects are gathered.”

Al-wuḥūsh belongs to a different root and refers to wild animals.

Similarity between ḥashr and ḥasharah should not alter the verse’s meaning.

Ḥashr and sharpening a spearhead

Some early uses record ḥashara al-sinān for making a spearhead slender and sharp.

Certain arrow components are likewise described with words from this family when made slender and compact.

This use concerns compactness and reduction in size.

The available evidence nevertheless does not conclusively derive every branch of the family from one physical image.

Gathering and compacting may have been connected in part of the language’s history, but that connection should not become a certain origin for every derivative.

Finding from the evidence: early Arabic prominently uses the root for gathering with directed movement. Alongside this are uses concerning pressure, compactness, slenderness, and small creatures. In the Qur’an, gathering is the common focus of its uses.

4. Gathering troops and people in worldly events

Al-Naml 27:17: assembling Sulaymān’s troops

Verse text

وَحُشِرَ لِسُلَيْمَانَ جُنُودُهُ مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ وَالطَّيْرِ فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

wa-ḥushira li-Sulaymāna junūduhu mina al-jinni wa-al-insi wa-al-ṭayri fa-hum yūzaʿūna

English translation

Sulaymān’s troops of jinn, humans, and birds were assembled for him, and they were held in ordered ranks.

Examining the use

Ḥushira is a Form I passive perfect verb.

Here, Sulaymān’s troops are gathered.

Three groups are named: jinn, humans, and birds.

Ḥashr here therefore concerns assembling the forces and creatures belonging to Sulaymān’s army.

The event occurs in Sulaymān’s earthly life, without any need to interpret it as resurrection.

The following expression also matters:

«فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ» (fa-hum yūzaʿūn).

Yūzaʿūna belongs to W Z ʿ and concerns restraining, controlling, or ordering movement.

The troops’ assembly is thus followed by their control and order.

Ḥashr and wazʿ are therefore related but distinct.

Ḥashr concerns gathering; wazʿ concerns restraining or regulating the group’s movement.

The verse does not yield precise details of the army’s formation, numbers, or the order of jinn, humans, and birds.

Those details are not stated in the verse itself.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:111: gatherers at Pharaoh’s command

Verse text

قَالُوا أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ وَأَرْسِلْ فِي الْمَدَائِنِ حَاشِرِينَ

qālū arjih wa-akhāhu wa-arsil fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīna

English translation

They said, “Give him and his brother respite, and send gatherers through the cities.”

Examining the use

Ḥāshirīn is a plural active participle.

A group proposes that Pharaoh send people through the cities to collect others.

The following verses concern bringing skilled magicians.

Ḥāshirīn here therefore means agents of gathering.

The word itself does not mean magician.

The magicians are those to be collected; the ḥāshirīn are those sent to collect them.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

The same form, ḥāshirīn, appears in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:53.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:53, Pharaoh himself sends agents through the cities.

These three uses thus concern collecting and mobilizing people within social and political events.

Ṭā Hā 20:59: gathering people on the Day of Adornment

Verse text

قَالَ مَوْعِدُكُمْ يَوْمُ الزِّينَةِ وَأَنْ يُحْشَرَ النَّاسُ ضُحًى

qāla mawʿidukum yawmu al-zīnati wa-an yuḥshara al-nāsu ḍuḥan

English translation

He said, “Your appointment is on the Day of Adornment, with the people assembled in the morning.”

Examining the use

Yuḥshara is a passive imperfect verb.

Here, gathering the people is proposed for a specified time.

Ḍuḥan specifies the gathering’s time.

Ḥashr here therefore concerns people assembling for a public event.

This use likewise has no necessary, direct connection with reviving the dead.

The verb itself does not establish that every person in an entire country attended without exception.

The event’s context determines the scope of al-nās.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:23: Pharaoh gathered the people

Verse text

فَحَشَرَ فَنَادَىٰ

fa-ḥashara fa-nādā

English translation

Then he gathered and called out.

Examining the use

Fa-ḥashara is an active perfect verb.

The surrounding verses identify its subject as Pharaoh.

He assembles people, then calls out.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:24 reports Pharaoh’s claim concerning his position.

Ḥashr here thus occurs within political power, assembling an audience, and making a public declaration.

Ḥashr itself does not mean claiming lordship or sovereignty. Those concepts come from the following verses.

Lexical finding: these uses show that Qur’anic ḥashr can describe actual worldly assembly of people, agents, troops, and social groups. Defining every occurrence as gathering the dead at resurrection is therefore incorrect.

5. Li-awwali al-ḥashr: gathering in the context of departure from homes

Al-Ḥashr 59:2

Verse text

هُوَ الَّذِي أَخْرَجَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ لِأَوَّلِ الْحَشْرِ مَا ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنْ يَخْرُجُوا وَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُمْ مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ فَأَتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَمْ يَحْتَسِبُوا وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ بِأَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَيْدِي الْمُؤْمِنِينَ فَاعْتَبِرُوا يَا أُولِي الْأَبْصَارِ

huwa alladhī akhraja alladhīna kafarū min ahli al-kitābi min diyārihim li-awwali al-ḥashri mā ẓanantum an yakhrujū wa-ẓannū annahum māniʿatuhum ḥuṣūnuhum mina Allāhi fa-atāhumu Allāhu min ḥaythu lam yaḥtasibū wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba yukhribūna buyūtahum bi-aydīhim wa-aydī al-muʾminīna fa-iʿtabirū yā ulī al-abṣāri

English translation

He is the One who expelled those among the People of the Book who disbelieved from their homes at the first gathering. You did not think they would leave, and they thought their fortresses would protect them against The God. Then The God came upon them from where they did not expect and cast terror into their hearts. They destroyed their homes with their own hands and the believers’ hands. So take heed, people of insight.

Examining the use

Al-ḥashr is a definite verbal noun here.

It accompanies awwal: the first gathering, or the gathering’s initial stage.

The sentence itself establishes the subject: expelling a group among the People of the Book from their homes.

The verb akhraja also appears at the beginning.

Ḥashr here thus relates to gathering, directing movement, and a group leaving its dwelling place.

This is a worldly event.

Neither the surah’s name nor the presence of al-ḥashr therefore restricts this verse to the Day of Resurrection.

Does ḥashr here mean the same as expulsion?

They concern one event, but differ lexically.

Ikhrāj describes bringing or driving out.

Ḥashr highlights gathering and directing movement.

People may be expelled from their homes and then proceed collectively toward another destination.

In such a case, expulsion and gathering can describe two aspects of one event.

Al-ḥashr itself, however, does not yield every detail of their route, destination, or manner of transfer.

Must awwal al-ḥashr mean the first historical exile?

Early historical explanations connect the verse with Banū al-Naḍīr’s departure from Madinah.

Those accounts may be examined to understand the historical setting.

The verse itself, however, names neither Banū al-Naḍīr, their destination, nor a list of subsequent groups.

Awwal al-ḥashr expresses a beginning or first stage, but the set of events within which it is first requires contextual and independent evidence.

It therefore cannot be called the first gathering of all humans before resurrection without evidence.

Nor should awwal al-ḥashr confidently be presented as the name of a fixed system of historical exiles with specified destinations.

The verse emphasizes departure, the group’s confidence in its fortresses, the collapse of that confidence, and taking heed.

Gathering and siege

This verse contains ḥuṣūnuhum.

Ḥuṣūn belongs to Ḥ Ṣ N and concerns fortresses and strength.

Ḥashr belongs to another root.

Siege or military pressure may form part of the event, but ḥashr itself does not establish that its central meaning is besieging.

Siege limits movement; ḥashr gathers people or directs them onward.

The two actions may accompany each other within one event, but are not synonyms.

Lexical finding: al-ḥashr in Al-Ḥashr 59:2 concerns gathering and moving a group in the course of its departure from home. The word is neither restricted to resurrection nor sufficient to specify every detail of historical exile.

6. Gathering animals: a scope beyond humans

Al-Anʿām 6:38

Verse text

وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ إِلَّا أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِي الْكِتَابِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يُحْشَرُونَ

wa-mā min dābbatin fī al-arḍi wa-lā ṭāʾirin yaṭīru bi-janāḥayhi illā umamun amthālukum mā farraṭnā fī al-kitābi min shayʾin thumma ilā rabbihim yuḥsharūna

English translation

There is no creature moving on the earth, nor a bird flying with its two wings, except that they are communities like you. We have neglected nothing in the Book. Then they are gathered to their Lord.

Examining the use

Yuḥsharūna is a passive imperfect verb.

The verse has just mentioned earthbound creatures and birds.

Its plural pronoun therefore relates to these beings.

Importantly, the Qur’an applies community and gathering beyond humans.

Animals too form communities, and their gathering toward their Lord is stated.

This should not immediately establish that every detail of their moral responsibility, judgment, reward, and punishment is identical to humans’.

The verse says they are communities like you and will be gathered to their Lord.

Umamun amthālukum, however, does not mean absolute identity in every feature.

Humans and birds may share communal life, order, and belonging to divine creation while differing in capacities and responsibility.

Must gathering animals concern resurrection?

Given the broad Qur’anic uses of ḥashr and the phrase ilā rabbihim, connecting this verse with the creatures’ final gathering is defensible.

The sentence itself, however, does not explicitly name the Day of Resurrection.

The exact manner and timing of animals’ gathering should therefore be considered alongside other verses.

Al-Takwīr 81:5 also describes wild animals being gathered, amid great events transforming the world.

This connection strengthens an eschatological reading of animals’ gathering.

Nevertheless, Ḥ SH R itself does not specify what happens to them afterward.

Al-Takwīr 81:5

Verse text

وَإِذَا الْوُحُوشُ حُشِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-wuḥūshu ḥushirat

English translation

And when the wild animals are gathered.

Examining the use

Ḥushirat is a passive perfect verb.

Ḥushirat is passive; the expressed verb’s grammatical passive subject is the implicit pronoun hiya, referring to al-wuḥūsh. Semantically, the animals are those gathered, rather than the agents gathering others.

Muʿjam Iʿrāb Mufradāt Alfāẓ al-Qurʾān: the passive subject of ḥushirat

Wild animals are gathered here.

Wuḥūsh belongs to W Ḥ SH and should not be equated semantically with ḥasharāt or ḥashr.

Al-Takwīr places this among major events concerning the sun, stars, mountains, animals, and other parts of the world.

The wild animals’ gathering is therefore part of that larger scene.

Ḥushirat itself nevertheless specifies neither how, where, nor for how long they assemble.

Ṣād 38:19: birds assembled in Dāwūd’s story

Verse text

وَالطَّيْرَ مَحْشُورَةً كُلٌّ لَهُ أَوَّابٌ

wa-al-ṭayra maḥshūratan kullun lahu awwābun

English translation

And the birds, assembled; all were turning back to Him.

Examining the use

Maḥshūratan is a feminine Form I passive participle.

Here, the birds appear in connection with Dāwūd.

The context is Dāwūd’s life and rule, rather than a direct description of resurrection.

Maḥshūrah thus means gathered and should not automatically become brought back to life after death.

This clearly shows that ḥashr can concern birds in a worldly event.

Their presence in this account can also be compared with Sulaymān’s assembled troops in Al-Naml 27:17, although the events differ.

Findings from uses concerning animals

In Al-Anʿām 6:38, creatures and birds are gathered toward their Lord.

In Al-Takwīr 81:5, wild animals are gathered.

In Ṣād 38:19, birds are described as assembled in Dāwūd’s context.

Qur’anic ḥashr therefore applies to animals, but each instance requires its own setting.

Gathering animals alone establishes neither their complete identity with humans nor that every animal gathering necessarily occurs at resurrection.

7. Gathering humans at resurrection: assembly after dispersion

Al-Kahf 18:47

Verse text

وَيَوْمَ نُسَيِّرُ الْجِبَالَ وَتَرَى الْأَرْضَ بَارِزَةً وَحَشَرْنَاهُمْ فَلَمْ نُغَادِرْ مِنْهُمْ أَحَدًا

wa-yawma nusayyiru al-jibāla wa-tarā al-arḍa bārizatan wa-ḥasharnāhum fa-lam nughādir minhum aḥadan

English translation

On the day We set the mountains in motion and you see the earth laid bare, We gather them and leave none of them behind.

Examining the use

Ḥasharnāhum is an active perfect verb with an object pronoun.

In the Qur’anic statement, The God is the agent, while the object is those gathered.

The verse continues:

«فَلَمْ نُغَادِرْ مِنْهُمْ أَحَدًا» (fa-lam nughādir minhum aḥadan).

This establishes that no member of the group in question is excluded from the gathering.

That conclusion comes from the second clause, rather than every use of ḥashr inherently including every member of a group.

Al-Naml 27:83, for example, describes gathering a group from every community, rather than explicitly every member of each community.

Thus, the completeness of gathering should be preserved where stated, but not extended from the root itself to every occurrence.

Qāf 50:44: gathering as people emerge from the earth

Verse text

يَوْمَ تَشَقَّقُ الْأَرْضُ عَنْهُمْ سِرَاعًا ذَٰلِكَ حَشْرٌ عَلَيْنَا يَسِيرٌ

yawma tashaqqaqu al-arḍu ʿanhum sirāʿan dhālika ḥashrun ʿalaynā yasīrun

English translation

On the day the earth splits open from around them as they emerge swiftly: that gathering is easy for Us.

Examining the use

Ḥashr appears here as a verbal noun.

The context describes the earth splitting open and people emerging swiftly.

Ḥashr here therefore relates to resurrection and gathering after emergence from the earth.

Ḥashr itself, however, does not mean the earth splitting open.

Splitting is expressed by tashaqqaqu, and the speed of emergence by sirāʿan.

Ḥashr names the gathering process.

ʿAlaynā yasīr expresses the ease of this gathering for The God.

That ease is likewise not part of ḥashr’s lexical meaning, but a description added in this verse.

Raising and gathering: related stages

In some Qur’anic contexts, baʿth concerns being raised and rising after death.

Ḥashr emphasizes collecting and directing toward a group or specified place.

These are closely related in resurrection scenes, but not complete synonyms.

Being raised from death and subsequently gathered are conceptually distinct.

Their lexical distinction should nevertheless not generate a precise, certain timetable of every resurrection stage unless the verses state that order.

The Qur’an may describe a large scene with several verbs without intending to specify the time between them.

Gathering toward The God

Several verses state that people are gathered toward The God, including Al-Baqarah 2:203, Āl ʿImrān 3:158, Al-Anʿām 6:72, and Al-Mulk 67:24.

This highlights the gathering’s final direction and return to divine reckoning and judgment.

Ilā should nevertheless not imply that The God occupies a point in physical space and people must traverse a bodily distance to reach Him.

The Qur’an also describes The God’s knowledge and encompassing power beyond human limitations.

Ilayhi tuḥsharūna should therefore be understood alongside Qur’anic statements concerning return, reckoning, judgment, and responsibility.

Conversely, this consideration should not reduce actual gathering and accountability to an inward feeling or metaphor of self-knowledge.

The resurrection verses describe a real event.

8. Different groups gathered: differing destinations and conditions

Maryam 19:85

Verse text

يَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ الْمُتَّقِينَ إِلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ وَفْدًا

yawma naḥshuru al-muttaqīna ilā al-Raḥmāni wafdan

English translation

On the day We gather those mindful of The God to the Entirely Merciful as a delegation.

Examining the use

Naḥshuru here concerns gathering those mindful of The God.

They are described with wafdan.

Arabic wafd describes a group arriving to appear before someone.

This verse therefore describes a gathering differently from the gathering of offenders.

Wafdan conveys arrival as a group or delegation, but specifies neither the means of transport nor details of their reception.

Ḥashr thus need not mean humiliatingly driving people onward.

Ṭā Hā 20:102: gathering offenders

Verse text

يَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ وَنَحْشُرُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ يَوْمَئِذٍ زُرْقًا

yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri wa-naḥshuru al-mujrimīna yawmaʾidhin zurqan

English translation

On the day the Trumpet is blown, We gather the offenders that day in the condition described as zurqan.

Examining the use

Naḥshuru is the same verb of gathering.

Its object, however, is al-mujrimīn: the offenders.

Zurqan is also present; its sense concerning color, faces, appearance, or condition requires separate examination.

Ḥashr itself does not establish that those gathered necessarily have that color or condition.

The accompanying words identify both the group and its description.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97: gathering upon their faces

Verse text

وَمَنْ يَهْدِ اللَّهُ فَهُوَ الْمُهْتَدِ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلْ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُمْ أَوْلِيَاءَ مِنْ دُونِهِ وَنَحْشُرُهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ عُمْيًا وَبُكْمًا وَصُمًّا مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ كُلَّمَا خَبَتْ زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا

wa-man yahdi Allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadi wa-man yuḍlil fa-lan tajida lahum awliyāʾa min dūnihi wa-naḥshuruhum yawma al-qiyāmati ʿalā wujūhihim ʿumyan wa-bukman wa-ṣumman maʾwāhum jahannamu kullamā khabat zidnāhum saʿīran

English translation

Whomever The God guides is guided; whomever He leaves astray, you will find no protectors for them besides Him. We gather them on the Day of Resurrection upon their faces, blind, mute, and deaf. Their refuge is Hell; whenever it subsides, We increase its blaze for them.

Examining the use

Here, gathering accompanies ʿalā wujūhihim and three further descriptions.

These express the condition of a group being gathered.

Naḥshuruhum itself, however, does not mean making blind, mute, or deaf.

Ṭā Hā 20:124 likewise describes someone gathered blind; in Ṭā Hā 20:125, he asks why he is in that condition.

The manner of gathering and accompanying descriptions therefore require separate investigation.

Without evidence, these descriptions should neither be reduced to inward metaphors nor have every physical detail conclusively derived from the root itself.

Lexically, ḥashr remains gathering, while accompanying expressions describe its manner.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:22

Verse text

احْشُرُوا الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا وَأَزْوَاجَهُمْ وَمَا كَانُوا يَعْبُدُونَ

uḥshurū alladhīna ẓalamū wa-azwājahum wa-mā kānū yaʿbudūna

English translation

Gather those who did wrong, their counterparts and associates, and what they used to worship.

Examining the use

Uḥshurū is a second-person plural imperative.

This verse concerns gathering wrongdoers and persons or things associated with them.

Azwājahum belongs to Z W J and can refer to counterparts, associates, or their corresponding pairs.

It should not be restricted to the wrongdoers’ spouses without examination.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:23 then describes directing them toward the path of the blazing Fire.

Ḥashr thus expresses gathering, while the following sentence specifies the destination more clearly.

Gathering toward Hell

In Āl ʿImrān 3:12, Al-Anfāl 8:36, Al-Furqān 25:34, and Fuṣṣilat 41:19, ḥashr is connected with Hell or the Fire.

Gathering some people can therefore accompany their outcome and punishment.

Ḥashr itself, however, does not mean punishment or fire.

Maryam 19:85 uses the same root for those mindful of The God.

Context therefore determines the gathered people’s differing destinations and conditions.

Finding: gathering different groups shares a lexical core, but not an identical value or manner. The mindful, offenders, and wrongdoers appear in different verses with distinct descriptions and destinations.

9. General and selective gathering: everyone or a group?

Al-Naml 27:83

Verse text

وَيَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ فَوْجًا مِمَّنْ يُكَذِّبُ بِآيَاتِنَا فَهُمْ يُوزَعُونَ

wa-yawma naḥshuru min kulli ummatin fawjan mimman yukadhdhibu bi-āyātinā fa-hum yūzaʿūna

English translation

On the day We gather from every community a group of those who deny Our signs, they are held in order.

Examining the use

Naḥshuru is the verb of gathering.

Its object, however, is introduced by min kulli ummatin fawjan.

The verse itself thus describes gathering a group from each community.

This differs from Al-Kahf 18:47, where none of the group in question is left behind.

Min kulli ummatin fawjan in Al-Naml 27:83 emphasizes selecting or gathering a group from each community.

This distinction should be preserved.

Does the verse describe a gathering separate from resurrection?

Some readings infer from the difference between selective gathering in Al-Naml and general gathering elsewhere that two entirely different gatherings occur in separate stages.

That possibility can be examined, but the expression alone does not establish it.

One verse may focus on a particular group of deniers while another describes the gathering’s general scope.

Different events or stages may also be involved.

A firm judgment requires examination of surrounding verses, the order of events, and further evidence.

What is linguistically clear is the differing scope of the object: a group from each community in Al-Naml; a gathering leaving none of the relevant group behind in Al-Kahf.

Fa-hum yūzaʿūn: gathering and restraint

The same expression also appears in Al-Naml 27:17.

There, Sulaymān’s troops are assembled and ordered.

In Al-Naml 27:83, groups of deniers are gathered, followed by yūzaʿūn.

Ḥashr and wazʿ can therefore accompany each other in quite different contexts.

Gathering a group and restraining or regulating its members’ movement are related matters.

The repeated yūzaʿūn, however, does not establish identical moral conditions or destinations for Sulaymān’s troops and the deniers at resurrection.

10. Gathering humans and what they worshipped: revealing the truth

Al-Anʿām 6:22

Verse text

وَيَوْمَ نَحْشُرُهُمْ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ نَقُولُ لِلَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا أَيْنَ شُرَكَاؤُكُمُ الَّذِينَ كُنْتُمْ تَزْعُمُونَ

wa-yawma naḥshuruhum jamīʿan thumma naqūlu li-alladhīna ashrakū ayna shurakāʾukumu alladhīna kuntum tazʿumūna

English translation

On the day We gather them all, We then say to those who associated partners, “Where are the partners you used to claim?”

Examining the use

Naḥshuruhum expresses gathering people.

Jamīʿan emphasizes the entirety of the relevant group.

A dialogue then concerns the partners they had claimed.

The sequence matters.

Gathering provides the setting for confronting a claim made during worldly life.

Ḥashr itself, however, does not mean revealing association of partners or the partners’ falsehood.

That judgment comes from the dialogue and the surrounding verses.

Yūnus 10:28

Verse text

وَيَوْمَ نَحْشُرُهُمْ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ نَقُولُ لِلَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا مَكَانَكُمْ أَنْتُمْ وَشُرَكَاؤُكُمْ فَزَيَّلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ وَقَالَ شُرَكَاؤُهُمْ مَا كُنْتُمْ إِيَّانَا تَعْبُدُونَ

wa-yawma naḥshuruhum jamīʿan thumma naqūlu li-alladhīna ashrakū makānakum antum wa-shurakāʾukum fa-zayyalnā baynahum wa-qāla shurakāʾuhum mā kuntum iyyānā taʿbudūna

English translation

On the day We gather them all, We then say to those who associated partners, “Remain in your place, you and your partners.” We separate them, and their partners say, “You did not worship us.”

Examining the use

Naḥshuruhum jamīʿan appears here too.

Gathering is followed by separation and dialogue.

Gathering and separating are therefore two different actions.

People may first be assembled together and then have their groups or relationships separated.

Ḥashr itself consequently does not mean permanent unity or an identical fate for everyone gathered.

Gathering jinn and humans

Al-Anʿām 6:128 also describes gathering them all, followed by an address to the jinn.

In Al-Furqān 25:17 and Sabaʾ 34:40, gathering accompanies questions about worship and claims concerning others besides The God.

Qur’anic ḥashr therefore extends beyond people silently assembling: in some contexts, it prepares for accountability and revealing the truth of relationships and claims.

Accountability, judgment, and testimony nevertheless belong to independent concepts and words, rather than the meaning of Ḥ SH R itself.

11. Gathering, knowledge, reckoning, and responsibility

Al-Anʿām 6:111

Verse text

وَلَوْ أَنَّنَا نَزَّلْنَا إِلَيْهِمُ الْمَلَائِكَةَ وَكَلَّمَهُمُ الْمَوْتَىٰ وَحَشَرْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ قُبُلًا مَا كَانُوا لِيُؤْمِنُوا إِلَّا أَنْ يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ يَجْهَلُونَ

wa-law annanā nazzalnā ilayhimu al-malāʾikata wa-kallamahumu al-mawtā wa-ḥasharnā ʿalayhim kulla shayʾin qubulan mā kānū li-yuʾminū illā an yashāʾa Allāhu wa-lākinna aktharahum yajhalūna

English translation

Even if We sent the angels down to them, the dead spoke to them, and We gathered everything before them, they would not believe unless The God willed; but most of them do not know.

Examining the use

Ḥasharnā ʿalayhim kulla shayʾin occurs within a conditional construction.

The verse supposes angels descending, the dead speaking, and everything being assembled before the addressees.

It therefore need not report such a gathering as an actual occurrence.

The hypothetical statement clarifies the addressees’ condition of belief and denial.

The perfect verb ḥasharnā alone therefore does not prove that all these events happened historically.

Qubulan also involves questions of vocalization, grammar, and interpretation.

Both things being face to face and their coming group by group appear in readings and interpretations of this word; the differing meanings should not be restricted solely to differing vocalizations.

That variation concerns qubulan, rather than gathering’s central meaning.

Gathering and The God’s knowledge

Al-Baqarah 2:203 reminds people that they will be gathered toward The God.

Al-Anfāl 8:24 and Al-Mujādilah 58:9 state the same truth alongside ethical commands and social responsibility.

Remembrance of gathering can thus connect present conduct with future accountability.

Ḥashr itself, however, does not mean reckoning.

Reckoning belongs to Ḥ S B, and witnessing to SH H D.

Gathering may precede reckoning, but the concepts differ lexically.

Gathering and human choice

People ultimately being gathered does not establish that their worldly deeds lack choice or responsibility.

Verses of gathering repeatedly place people’s deeds, denial, belief, and outcome alongside their assembly.

Ḥashr therefore does not mean the disappearance of human responsibility; in many contexts it concerns the disclosure of conduct’s consequences.

Conversely, every difficult event or forced worldly gathering should not be called the final gathering or certain divine punishment without evidence.

Ḥashr can occur in worldly life or in resurrection scenes.

Context identifies the kind of event.

12. Morphology and distinctions from related roots

Ḥ SH R compared with J M ʿ (jamaʿa)

Study of J M ʿ

Jamʿ concerns gathering and placing people or things together.

Ḥashr is related, but its recognized uses highlight directing movement and assembling from dispersion toward a group or specified place.

The distinction is nevertheless not absolute.

Both words may suit one event in some contexts.

In Al-Anʿām 6:22, naḥshuruhum accompanies jamīʿan.

Their presence together shows that gathering and emphasis on the group’s entirety perform different roles.

Ḥ SH R compared with B ʿ TH (baʿatha)

Study of B ʿ TH

Arabic baʿth concerns raising, sending, and setting in motion.

In some resurrection verses, it concerns rising after death.

Ḥashr places greater emphasis on gathering and directing onward.

Wa-ibʿath fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīn in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36 brings both families together.

Baʿth there concerns sending agents, while the ḥāshirīn are those who assemble others.

This clearly illustrates the two roots’ distinction.

Ḥ SH R compared with N SH R (nashara)

Study of N SH R

N SH R concerns spreading, dispersing, and, in some uses, being raised after death.

Ḥ SH R focuses on gathering.

In imagery, dispersing and collecting can be different movements.

This does not establish that nashr is precisely a stage before ḥashr in every verse.

In resurrection contexts, both families may concern related events, but their exact order requires each verse’s text.

Ḥ SH R compared with S W Q (sāqa)

Study of S W Q

Sawq concerns driving or directing toward a destination.

Ḥashr expresses gathering with the possibility of directed movement.

Maryam 19:85 uses naḥshuru for the mindful, while Maryam 19:86 describes offenders being driven toward Hell with nasūqu.

The distinction matters.

Sawq and ḥashr are linguistically close, but the Qur’an uses distinct verbs for the two groups and conditions.

Translation should preserve that distinction.

Ḥ SH R compared with Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara)

Study of Ḥ Ṣ R

Ḥ Ṣ R concerns limiting, surrounding, and restricting movement.

Ḥ SH R concerns gathering and directing onward.

In a military event, people might first be surrounded, then assembled or removed.

The two actions nevertheless differ.

Nor does ḥuṣūn in Al-Ḥashr 59:2 make Ḥ Ṣ N, Ḥ Ṣ R, and Ḥ SH R one root.

Ḥ SH R compared with Ḥ SH D (ḥashada)

Arabic ḥashd concerns gathering a group, mobilizing forces or a crowd, and social support.

Ḥashr can likewise describe assembling people or troops.

Many uses of ḥashr, however, give greater prominence to driving and directing the group’s movement.

This is a tendency, rather than a rule separating every Arabic use without exception.

Ḥ SH R compared with W Z ʿ (wazaʿa)

Study of W Z ʿ

Wazʿ accompanies ḥashr in Al-Naml 27:17 and Al-Naml 27:83.

Gathering and ordering or restraining the group are related parts of the event in these contexts.

Fa-hum yūzaʿūn therefore should not be discarded as a meaningless repetition of ḥashr.

Assembling a group differs from regulating its members’ movement.

Ḥ SH R compared with Ṭ R D (ṭarada)

Study of Ṭ R D

Ṭ R D concerns driving away and expelling a person.

Ḥ SH R concerns gathering and directing toward a place.

Someone may be driven from a location and transferred to a group; expulsion and gathering can then describe two aspects of one event.

Not every expulsion, however, is gathering, and not every gathering is expulsion from home or homeland.

13. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic equivalent in most uses is “gather.”

Ilayhi tuḥsharūna in Al-Baqarah 2:203 can be rendered “you are gathered toward Him.”

Naḥshuruhum jamīʿan in Al-Anʿām 6:22 and Yūnus 10:28 means “We gather them all.”

Wa-ḥasharnāhum in Al-Kahf 18:47 is close to “and We gathered them.”

Ḥashrun in Qāf 50:44 is a verbal noun, suitably rendered “gathering.”

Ḥushira li-Sulaymāna junūduhu in Al-Naml 27:17 should become “Sulaymān’s troops were assembled for him.”

Ḥāshirīn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:111, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:53 means “gatherers” or “agents of gathering.”

An yuḥshara al-nāsu in Ṭā Hā 20:59 becomes “that the people be assembled.”

Fa-ḥashara fa-nādā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:23 can be rendered “then he gathered and called out.”

Li-awwali al-ḥashr in Al-Ḥashr 59:2 is best translated as “at the first gathering” or “for the first assembly.” Its connection with the group leaving their homes should be explained from context.

Yuḥsharūna in Al-Anʿām 6:38 is close to “they are gathered,” rather than automatically “they die” or “they are revived.”

Ḥushirat in Al-Takwīr 81:5 becomes “were gathered,” and maḥshūratan in Ṣād 38:19 “assembled.”

Naḥshuru al-muttaqīna in Maryam 19:85 is close to “We gather those mindful of The God.” Wafdan should also be preserved separately.

Uḥshurū in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:22 is an imperative, directly rendered “gather.”

Min kulli ummatin fawjan in Al-Naml 27:83 must retain “a group from every community,” rather than becoming every member of each community without evidence.

Verses concerning gathering toward The God should preserve the destination and final direction without adding a spatial interpretation that confines Him to a bodily location.

The central principle is not to translate every ḥashr as resurrection, raising, reckoning, exile, or siege. These may accompany gathering in some contexts, but are not all fixed components of the root’s meaning.

14. Final synthesis

Arabic Ḥ SH R concerns gathering scattered people or creatures and, in many uses, directing them toward a specified place.

The Qur’an applies this meaning to both worldly events and resurrection.

In Sulaymān’s story, his troops of jinn, humans, and birds are assembled.

In Pharaoh’s story, agents are sent to collect people and magicians.

Ṭā Hā 20:59 describes gathering people on a specified day and time.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:2, gathering accompanies a group among the People of the Book leaving their homes.

Ḥashr therefore is not inherently a term exclusive to resurrection.

Many other verses describe people being gathered toward The God to face the reality of their deeds and outcome.

Al-Kahf 18:47 connects gathering with leaving no member of the relevant group behind.

Al-Naml 27:83 describes gathering a group from each community.

The scope of gathering should therefore be determined from the verse, rather than a fixed lexical definition.

Animals and birds are also objects of gathering in the Qur’an.

In Al-Anʿām 6:38, creatures and birds are gathered toward their Lord.

In Al-Takwīr 81:5, wild animals are gathered.

In Ṣād 38:19, birds are described as assembled in Dāwūd’s story.

Ḥashr is therefore not restricted to humans, but the root itself determines neither the kind, timing, nor consequences of every creature’s gathering.

Nor does every group gathered at resurrection receive the same description.

The mindful, offenders, wrongdoers, and deniers each appear in particular contexts.

Thus, ḥashr itself is neither punishment nor reward: it is gathering that can occur in different settings.

Ḥashr also relates to raising, collecting, directing, spreading, and reckoning, but none is its complete synonym.

Baʿth may describe raising; jamʿ emphasizes placing together; sawq highlights directing toward a destination; and ḥashr describes gathering with the possibility of directing the assembled group onward.

Final definition: Ḥ SH R concerns gathering people, animals, or beings from dispersion and, in many uses, directing them toward a shared place or situation. This may occur in worldly life or at resurrection; each verse’s context determines its manner, scope, destination, and consequences.

Understanding each use requires identifying who or what is gathered, who or what power performs the gathering, and its destination or purpose.

These distinctions prevent Sulaymān’s troops’ assembly from being equated with the resurrection gathering, or animals’ gathering from being turned into the human legal system without evidence.

The root’s semantic core is gathering and directing onward; whether this serves attendance, movement, accountability, punishment, or another event is established by the Qur’an and each use’s context.
