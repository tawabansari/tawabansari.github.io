The Qur’anic Root KH D ʿ

From concealing reality to creating a deceptive appearance

The root خ د ع occurs only five times in the Qur’an: twice as the simple verb يخدع, twice as the Form III verb يخادع, and once as the active participle خادع. All five occurrences are in three verses: Al-Baqarah 2:9, An-Nisa 4:142 and Al-Anfal 8:62.

In its Qur’anic uses, khadʿ is more than telling a lie. A person presents an appearance to someone else while concealing a different intention or reality. If the recipient takes that appearance for reality, the conditions for being deceived are in place.

Deception can therefore use a lie, but it is not confined to false speech. Conduct, a profession of faith, a proposal of peace or another outward display can serve as its means.

KH D ʿ in its Qur’anic uses: concealing a reality or intention and presenting a different appearance, with the aim of leading another person to a mistaken understanding or decision on the basis of that appearance.

Qur’anic forms

يَخْدَعُونَ / يَخْدَعُوكَ
Form I imperfect; 2 occurrences

يُخَادِعُونَ
Form III imperfect; 2 occurrences

خَادِعُهُمْ
Active participle; 1 occurrence

Pre-Qur’anic evidence and the limits of reconstructing a basic meaning

The evidence available here does not establish an original physical image for this root or a historical progression from “hiding” to “deceiving.” A traceable poetic example follows. However, this examination did not establish reliable, independent evidence dating expressions about a lizard, an inner room or diminishing saliva specifically to the pre-Qur’anic period. The definition of Qur’anic usage is therefore drawn from the verses themselves and kept distinct from a claim about the historical origin of every branch.

Pre-Islamic poetry: khadʿ beyond religious terminology

The pre-Islamic poet al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī says in poem 58 of al-Aṣmaʿiyyāt:

أَرِقْتُ فَلَمْ تَخْدَعْ بِعَيْنَيَّ وَسْنَةٌ
وَمَنْ يَلْقَ مَا لَاقَيْتُ لَا بُدَّ يَأْرَقِ

“I lay awake, and no drowsiness found its way into my eyes; whoever encounters what I have encountered must lie sleepless.”

Al-Aṣmaʿiyyāt attributes this verse to al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī. In the context of the poet’s sleeplessness, لم تخدع بعيني وسنة can be understood as “no drowsiness found its way into my eyes.” This does not directly describe deceiving another person. The evidence is the verse transmitted from the pre-Islamic poet; by itself, it cannot establish the chronological development of the family’s meanings.

The branch used in the Qur’an must therefore be distinguished from the root’s other historical uses.

Al-Baqarah 2:9: “We believe” on the surface, a different reality within

The preceding verse supplies the setting:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَقُولُ آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَبِالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَمَا هُمْ بِمُؤْمِنِينَ

“Among the people are those who say, ‘We believe in The God and the Last Day,’ although they are not believers.”

Al-Baqarah 2:8

Then:

يُخَادِعُونَ اللَّهَ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَمَا يَخْدَعُونَ إِلَّا أَنْفُسَهُمْ وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ

“They seek to deceive The God and those who believe, but they deceive none except themselves, and they do not perceive it.”

Al-Baqarah 2:9

These two verses present khadʿ in a clear situation.

The outward appearance:

آمنا — “We believe.”

The concealed reality:

وما هم بمؤمنين — “They are not believers.”

Deception here is more than an ill intention: it is presenting an appearance different from the concealed reality.

The end of the verse, however, reverses the direction of the deception:

وما يخدعون إلا أنفسهم — “They deceive none except themselves.”

What the sentence clearly states is that their deception ultimately concerns themselves, and they do not perceive this reality.

This also invites reflection on “self-deception,” but that should not be treated as the word’s definitive meaning. The verse can accommodate both their becoming trapped in a perception they have created and the consequences of their deception returning upon them.

Yukhādiʿūna: Form III does not require mutual deception

يخادعون is a Form III verb. Its pattern alone does not establish that both parties successfully deceive each other; the continuation of the verse rules out that reading here.

This matters particularly in Al-Baqarah, because the Qur’an first says:

يخادعون الله — “They seek to deceive The God.”

Then:

وما يخدعون إلا أنفسهم — “They deceive none except themselves.”

Form III should therefore not be taken to mean that The God and the hypocrites are equal parties in a game of deception.

An-Nisa 4:142: an appearance for other people

An-Nisa states:

إِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ يُخَادِعُونَ اللَّهَ وَهُوَ خَادِعُهُمْ وَإِذَا قَامُوا إِلَى الصَّلَاةِ قَامُوا كُسَالَىٰ يُرَاءُونَ النَّاسَ وَلَا يَذْكُرُونَ اللَّهَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

“The hypocrites seek to deceive The God, and He is their deceiver. When they rise for Salat, they rise lazily, making a display before people, and they remember The God only a little.”

An-Nisa 4:142

يراؤون الناس, “making a display before people,” gives a practical example of the same division between appearance and inward reality. Religious conduct is put on view, while the motivation and inward condition do not match it.

Khadʿ thus concerns outward display as well as the telling of a false report.

Nevertheless, khadʿ and nifāq are not synonyms. Hypocrisy is one setting in which deception occurs. Al-Anfal shows that this root is not confined to hypocrites.

Wa-huwa khādiʿuhum: the same root, but different positions

In وَهُوَ خَادِعُهُمْ, the active participle of this same root refers to The God. Both the verbal connection and the parties’ different roles in the context need to be retained.

On the one hand, the Qur’an retains the same word family for the hypocrites’ conduct and the response of The God. Translating khādiʿuhum simply as “He punishes them” would lose that verbal connection.

On the other hand, Al-Baqarah 2:9 has already made clear that their deception does not place The God in ignorance:

وما يخدعون إلا أنفسهم — “They deceive none except themselves.”

The two sides of An-Nisa 4:142 are therefore not symmetrical. The hypocrites conceal something, but what they conceal is not hidden from Him.

The Qur’an uses the same root for the divine response to their deception, but khādiʿuhum alone does not specify how that response works. Assigning it a particular mechanism requires evidence beyond this word itself.

A translation close to the wording can say:

“And He is their deceiver.”

The explanation can then clarify that, unlike their deception, this does not arise from ignorance or from having been deceived.

“And He causes them to be caught in the consequences of their deception” explains how this clause may be read alongside Al-Baqarah 2:9; it is not a purely lexical equivalent of the active participle. Structurally, khādiʿuhum means “the one who deceives them” and does not itself state the mechanism of the response.

Al-Anfal 8:61–62: the possibility of deception behind peaceful conduct

Al-Anfal states:

وَإِنْ جَنَحُوا لِلسَّلْمِ فَاجْنَحْ لَهَا وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

“If they incline to peace, incline to it as well, and place your trust in The God. He is the Hearing, the Knowing.”

Al-Anfal 8:61

Then:

وَإِنْ يُرِيدُوا أَنْ يَخْدَعُوكَ فَإِنَّ حَسْبَكَ اللَّهُ ۚ هُوَ الَّذِي أَيَّدَكَ بِنَصْرِهِ وَبِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“If they intend to deceive you, The God is sufficient for you. He is the One who supported you with His help and with the believers.”

Al-Anfal 8:62

These verses show that deception can operate through conduct and proposals, not only through a false sentence.

The verse, however, speaks conditionally:

وَإِنْ يُرِيدُوا

“If they intend…”

Their inclination to peace in the preceding verse should therefore not be assumed to have been deceptive. The Qur’an first commands the acceptance of peace, then separately raises the possibility of deception.

If deception is involved, outward conduct may differ from concealed intention. The verse raises the possibility of that difference, not its certain occurrence.

KH D ʿ and K DH B: deception is not always a spoken lie

Kadhib primarily concerns the falsity of a report or claim.

Khadʿ extends further. A person can create an appearance from which someone draws a false conclusion without uttering a wholly false sentence.

In Al-Baqarah, saying “We believe” is part of the deception. In An-Nisa, يراؤون الناس highlights display through conduct. In Al-Anfal, conduct or a proposal may become a means of deception if a concealed intention is present.

A lie can therefore be an instrument of deception, but deception is not always simply a lie.

KH D ʿ and M K R: overlap without identity

Khadʿ and makr overlap. Both can involve a concealed intention and leading another party towards an outcome they do not anticipate.

In Al-Baqarah 2:8–9, khadʿ appears in the gap between professed belief and actual unbelief. In Al-Anfal 8:30, makr concerns a plan to detain, kill or expel the person addressed.

In these examples, khadʿ highlights a deceptive appearance, while makr highlights a plan against another person. This contextual difference does not establish an absolute boundary for every use of the two roots: a plan can also employ a deceptive appearance.

KH D ʿ and GH R R: proximity in the field of deception

Fatir 35:5 states: فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا — “So do not let the worldly life deceive you.” The family غ ر ر can likewise concern being drawn into a deceptive perception.

The human examples of khadʿ in Al-Baqarah and An-Nisa highlight deceptive conduct and the gap between appearance and inward reality. In this phrase from Fatir, worldly life is what must not deceive a person.

These examples show the proximity of the two families, but do not justify treating one as always concerned with the deceiver and the other as always concerned with the person deceived.

Translation challenges

In Al-Baqarah 2:9 and An-Nisa 4:142, يخادعون can be translated “they seek to deceive.” “Seek to” is useful because it does not impose successful deception of The God upon the sentence.

For وما يخدعون إلا أنفسهم, “but they deceive none except themselves” is both close and natural.

For وهو خادعهم, “and He is their deceiver” or “and He is the one who deceives them” retains the active participle’s structure and the repeated root. “And He causes them to be caught in the consequences of their deception” is a contextual explanation and should remain distinct from a translation close to the wording. Directly replacing the phrase with “He punishes them” also removes part of the verse’s verbal connection.

In Al-Anfal 8:62, أن يخدعوك is simply “to deceive you”; it needs no additional explanation within the translation.

Summary

The Qur’anic uses of خ د ع highlight the distance between appearance and reality. Concealing an intention and creating a different appearance describes what these contexts show; the available evidence does not establish it as the historical origin of all the root’s meanings.

The pre-Islamic poetry of al-Mumazzaq al-ʿAbdī also shows uses beyond interpersonal deception: sleep can fail to “find its way” into the eyes. The root’s historical branches should not all be reduced to a single ethical meaning.

The Qur’an uses the root only five times, in three verses. All its Qur’anic occurrences concern human deception and the response to it.

Al-Baqarah 2:8–9 gives a clear example of the division between appearance and inward reality: “We believe” on the surface, but “they are not believers” in fact. This conduct is then described as yukhādiʿūna.

The verse concludes with وما يخدعون إلا أنفسهم. This certainly directs the deception back towards themselves. Specifying it as either “psychological self-deception” or “the return of deception’s consequences,” however, goes beyond the root’s basic meaning.

Form III in yukhādiʿūna does not require successful, mutual deception. In Al-Baqarah 2:9, the continuation وما يخدعون إلا أنفسهم prevents that reading.

An-Nisa 4:142 makes the same division tangible through يراؤون الناس. In وهو خادعهم, the Qur’an uses the same root for the divine response, while Al-Baqarah directs the deception back towards the deceivers themselves. The active participle alone does not determine how that response works.

Al-Anfal 8:61–62 shows that deception can employ conduct. It does not, however, say that the peace proposal was necessarily a deception; it raises the possibility through “if they intend to deceive you.”

Compared with kadhib, khadʿ is not confined to a false report and can employ conduct and display. The Qur’anic examples of makr and ghurūr also overlap with this field without establishing complete synonymy.

KH D ʿ in its Qur’anic uses: concealing reality, intention or an inward condition and presenting a different appearance, with the aim of leading another person to a mistaken understanding or decision on that basis. Deception can operate through speech or conduct and is not necessarily confined to a spoken lie. The root itself does not determine whether deception succeeds, whether it is mutual, or how its consequences return; context specifies those matters.
