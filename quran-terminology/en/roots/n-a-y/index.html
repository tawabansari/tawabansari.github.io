Keeping away, withdrawing, and distancing oneself from someone or something

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

The Arabic root N ʾ Y concerns moving away, keeping a distance, and separation from someone or something. نَأَىٰ means moved away or distanced oneself; نَأْي means distance, remoteness, or separation.

The distance may be spatial, as when someone leaves a house, city, or person. It may also appear in conduct and relationships, as when someone withdraws from communication, accepting a statement, or accompanying another person. Such distance does not necessarily involve bodily movement.

The root itself does not mean arrogance, denial, or hostility. These may follow from someone’s conduct and the context, but should not be built into the basic definition.

The Qur’an uses it three times, in only two verbal forms:

  • يَنْأَوْنَ: they keep away; Al-Anʿām 6:26.
  • نَأَىٰ: moved away or withdrew; Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 and Fuṣṣilat 41:51.

All three are Form I verbs. Arabic also has نَأْي, نَاءٍ, مُنْتَأَىٰ, اِنْتَأَىٰ, تَنَاءَىٰ, and أَنْأَىٰ, but these nominal and derivative forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic background

Distance and separation in Arabic

Early Arabic uses naʾā for both spatial distance and withdrawing from a person or relationship.

One clear witness is a familiar verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī:

فَإِنَّكَ كَاللَّيْلِ الَّذِي هُوَ مُدْرِكِي
وَإِنْ خِلْتُ أَنَّ الْمُنْتَأَى عَنْكَ وَاسِعُ

fa-innaka ka-al-layli alladhī huwa mudrikī / wa-in khiltu anna al-muntaʾā ʿanka wāsiʿu

Translation:

“You are like the night that will overtake me, even though I imagine that the place for withdrawing from you is vast.”

Al-muntaʾā names a distant place, or somewhere one can retreat and keep away. In the poetic context, the distance also has a human and political dimension: withdrawing from a powerful person whose reach the poet does not believe he can escape.

On the familiar attribution to Al-Nābighah, the verse illustrates distance and withdrawal in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, beyond religious or moral conduct. The attribution should remain distinct from independently establishing the exact date of composition.

Nuʾy: keeping water away from a tent

نُؤْي belongs to the same lexical family and names a channel or earthwork around a tent that keeps rainwater away from the dwelling.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā contains this verse:

أَثَافِيَّ سُفْعًا فِي مُعَرَّسِ مِرْجَلٍ
وَنُؤْيًا كَجِذْمِ الْحَوْضِ لَمْ يَتَثَلَّمِ

athāfiyya sufʿan fī muʿarrasi mirjalin / wa-nuʾyan ka-jidhmi al-ḥawḍi lam yatathallami

Translation:

“[I recognized] blackened hearthstones where a cooking pot had stood, and the earthwork around the tent, like a remaining portion of a basin’s wall that had not crumbled.”

The poet describes traces remaining at a former campsite. The nuʾy is a real structure that kept water away from the tent.

Its semantic connection with keeping something away is intelligible, but this does not establish that naʾā originated in constructing a channel or earthwork. The two uses can be distinguished without claiming a certain historical derivation of one from the other.

The nuʾy image illuminates part of the root’s semantic range, but is not the proven origin of every use.

The range of non-Qur’anic derivatives

Arabic derivatives allow several relationships to be expressed.

نَأَىٰ can describe a person moving away; أَنْأَىٰ, distancing someone or something; اِنْتَأَىٰ, keeping away; تَنَاءَىٰ, moving apart from one another; and مُنْتَأَىٰ, a distant place or place of withdrawal.

The family therefore concerns not only an existing distance, but also moving away or creating distance.

These non-Qur’anic forms should not replace the forms actually used in the verses. Understanding the Qur’anic meaning requires examining its three occurrences in their constructions and contexts.

3. Qur’anic uses of N ʾ Y

A. Al-Anʿām 6:26: deterring others and keeping away themselves

وَهُمْ يَنْهَوْنَ عَنْهُ وَيَنْأَوْنَ عَنْهُ ۖ وَإِنْ يُهْلِكُونَ إِلَّا أَنْفُسَهُمْ وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ

wa-hum yanhawna ʿanhu wa-yanʾawna ʿanhu wa-in yuhlikūna illā anfusahum wa-mā yashʿurūna

Translation:

“They deter others from it and keep away from it themselves; they destroy only themselves, though they do not perceive it.”

Al-Anʿām 6:26

Two similar-sounding verbs occur together, but belong to different roots and meanings:

يَنْهَوْنَ belongs to N H Y and means prohibiting or deterring.

يَنْأَوْنَ belongs to N ʾ Y and means keeping away or distancing oneself.

The construction describes two behaviors together: they deter others and keep their own distance.

Yanʾawna is a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb. ʿAnhu specifies what or whom they keep away from.

The preceding verse helps identify the pronoun’s referent:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَسْتَمِعُ إِلَيْكَ وَجَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً أَنْ يَفْقَهُوهُ وَفِي آذَانِهِمْ وَقْرًا وَإِنْ يَرَوْا كُلَّ آيَةٍ لَا يُؤْمِنُوا بِهَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءُوكَ يُجَادِلُونَكَ يَقُولُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

wa-minhum man yastamiʿu ilayka wa-jaʿalnā ʿalā qulūbihim akinnatan an yafqahūhu wa-fī ādhānihim waqran wa-in yaraw kulla āyatin lā yuʾminū bihā ḥattā idhā jāʾūka yujādilūnaka yaqūlu alladhīna kafarū in hādhā illā asāṭīru al-awwalīna

Translation:

“Some among them listen to you, and We have placed coverings over their hearts so that they do not understand it, and heaviness in their ears. Even if they see every sign, they do not believe in it, until, when they come to you, they argue with you. Those who disbelieve say: This is nothing but tales of the ancients.”

Al-Anʿām 6:25

The context concerns people encountering the Messenger and what he presents, yet responding with opposition and argument.

The pronoun in verse 26 can therefore refer to the Messenger or his Qur’anic message. Neither reading fundamentally changes the point: the group keeps away from acceptance and engagement while deterring others as well.

The proposed basic Persian translation retains a pronoun, leaving its precise referent for contextual explanation.

The important difference between the verbs

Yanhawna concerns their conduct toward others; yanʾawna describes their own distancing.

Naʾā alone does not mean deterring others, campaigning against something, or preventing access. These behaviors follow from the separate verb yanhawna here.

The ending states the consequence of this combined behavior:

وَإِنْ يُهْلِكُونَ إِلَّا أَنْفُسَهُمْ

wa-in yuhlikūna illā anfusahum

“They destroy only themselves.”

This destruction or ruin is not naʾā’s lexical meaning, but the consequence stated for their conduct.

B. Al-Isrāʾ 17:83: withdrawing when favored

وَإِذَا أَنْعَمْنَا عَلَى الْإِنْسَانِ أَعْرَضَ وَنَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ كَانَ يَئُوسًا

wa-idhā anʿamnā ʿalā al-insāni aʿraḍa wa-naʾā bi-jānibihi wa-idhā massahu al-sharru kāna yaʾūsan

Translation:

“When We favor the human being, he turns away and draws himself aside; when harm touches him, he becomes deeply despairing.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83

Naʾā occurs in نَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ, a construction extending beyond simple spatial withdrawal.

Jānib names a side or flank. Drawing the body aside becomes an image of withdrawal and disregard.

Arabic also uses this expression for someone drawing aside out of indifference or arrogance.

The verse places two behaviors together:

Aʿraḍa means “turned away.”

Naʾā bi-jānibihi means “drew himself aside and kept his distance.”

They are close in meaning without having the same emphasis. Iʿrāḍ emphasizes turning away; naʾā emphasizes distancing. Together they provide a fuller image of withdrawal.

They need not describe two separate, consecutive acts; they may express two aspects of one behavior.

Does naʾā mean arrogance here?

Naʾā bi-jānibihi can figuratively express arrogant conduct. The root’s basic meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the meaning of the complete expression and context.

Here a person turns away and draws aside after receiving favor. This suggests disregard and ingratitude in context, but arrogance should not become naʾā’s sole lexical meaning.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 presents the Qur’an as healing and mercy for believers while mentioning the wrongdoers’ loss. Verse 83 then describes the human response to favor and adversity.

The contrast is central: someone who keeps away when favored becomes despairing when afflicted.

Naʾā therefore describes conduct during well-being, rather than naming despair or defining everyone who distances themselves for any reason.

C. Fuṣṣilat 41:51: withdrawal in favor and extensive prayer in hardship

وَإِذَا أَنْعَمْنَا عَلَى الْإِنْسَانِ أَعْرَضَ وَنَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ فَذُو دُعَاءٍ عَرِيضٍ

wa-idhā anʿamnā ʿalā al-insāni aʿraḍa wa-naʾā bi-jānibihi wa-idhā massahu al-sharru fa-dhū duʿāʾin ʿarīḍin

Translation:

“When We favor the human being, he turns away and draws himself aside; when harm touches him, he offers extensive prayer.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:51

The first part is identical to Al-Isrāʾ 17:83:

وَإِذَا أَنْعَمْنَا عَلَى الْإِنْسَانِ أَعْرَضَ وَنَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ

wa-idhā anʿamnā ʿalā al-insāni aʿraḍa wa-naʾā bi-jānibihi

“When We favor the human being, he turns away and draws himself aside.”

The second part, however, differs.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 describes the person afflicted by harm as yaʾūsan: deeply despairing.

Fuṣṣilat 41:51 describes him as dhū duʿāʾin ʿarīḍin: offering extensive prayer.

These describe two aspects of responding to adversity. Despair and extensive prayer do not necessarily exclude one another: someone may feel deeply hopeless about their situation while repeatedly praying for relief.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 and Fuṣṣilat 41:50 also portray this instability. Harm brings despair, yet mercy after hardship may lead someone to claim it for themselves and entertain false expectations about resurrection and judgment.

The criticism does not target prayer in hardship itself. It concerns inconsistent conduct: feeling self-sufficient and keeping away during well-being, then revealing one’s need in adversity.

Naʾā again means distancing. Ingratitude, pride, and instability emerge from the wider context.

Together with Al-Isrāʾ 17:83, this shows the same basic meaning of naʾā bi-jānibihi. The difference concerns the response to hardship, rather than a changed root meaning.

4. Linguistic constructions and interpretive challenges

Naʾā ʿanhu and naʾā bi-jānibihi

Two constructions occur in the Qur’anic uses.

نَأَىٰ عَنْهُ includes the preposition ʿan and means distancing oneself from a person, thing, or matter. Al-Anʿām 6:26 uses it as يَنْأَوْنَ عَنْهُ.

نَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ mentions a person’s side, picturing withdrawal. In Al-Isrāʾ and Fuṣṣilat, it accompanies turning away and has a negative behavioral context.

This is not an absolute rule that ʿan always concerns bodily distance and bāʾ always concerns belief. Al-Anʿām can also concern distancing oneself from accepting a message without bodily movement.

Bi-jānibihi specifies neither the right nor the left side, nor any exact bodily posture.

The readings naʾā and nāʾa

The familiar Ḥafṣ reading has نَأَىٰ in both Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 and Fuṣṣilat 41:51.

The readings transmitted from Ibn Dhakwān and Abū Jaʿfar also have نَاءَ, placing the hamzah after the alif.

Two linguistic analyses are proposed: nāʾa may be a metathesized form of naʾā with the same meaning of withdrawing; alternatively, it may relate to نَاءَ يَنُوءُ, used among other senses for rising, especially with heaviness.

If the latter is assigned to N W ʾ, it cannot simply be treated as an ordinary inflection of N ʾ Y without explanation.

Despite the morphological alternatives, withdrawal and turning away remain intelligible in both contexts. The reading difference should not create two wholly unrelated accounts of the conduct described.

Is keeping away always voluntary?

The Qur’anic occurrences describe human responses to a message or favor. Distancing in these contexts has a behavioral and chosen aspect.

The root alone does not make every instance conscious, deliberate, or oppositional. Arabic also uses distance for spatial remoteness or separation.

Intention, moral responsibility, and the kind of conduct must therefore be established contextually rather than from the verb alone.

5. Comparison with related roots

N ʾ Y (نَأَىٰ) and B ʿ D (بَعُدَ): moving away and being distant

B ʿ D also concerns distance, whether spatial, temporal, or figurative.

A difference of emphasis is that baʿuda may simply describe something as distant, while naʾā often emphasizes moving away or distancing oneself.

This is not absolute: naʾā can express a state of distance, while other constructions of B ʿ D describe distancing or moving away.

They should therefore not be restricted to static distance versus voluntary movement.

Study of B ʿ D

N ʾ Y (نَأَىٰ) and ʿ R Ḍ (أَعْرَضَ): withdrawal and turning away

Aʿraḍa, from ʿ R Ḍ, frequently expresses turning away and disregard in the Qur’an.

Its pairing with naʾā in two verses places them in a shared semantic field without making them identical.

Iʿrāḍ emphasizes turning away from something; naʾā emphasizes distancing. Someone can turn away from a statement without changing location, or leave a place without being hostile toward it.

Al-Isrāʾ and Fuṣṣilat use both for ungrateful conduct, strengthening the picture of withdrawal.

Study of ʿ R Ḍ

N ʾ Y (نَأَىٰ) and N H Y (نَهَىٰ): withdrawing and deterring

This comparison is particularly important for Al-Anʿām 6:26.

Nahā concerns prohibiting and deterring; naʾā concerns keeping a distance.

The similar sounds of yanhawna and yanʾawna do not make them one root. The verse uses the resemblance while distinguishing two behaviors.

One concerns other people; the other describes the subjects’ own conduct.

The translation must preserve this distinction. Rendering both as keeping away or both as deterring would lose precision.

N ʾ Y (نَأَىٰ) and N W Y (نَوَىٰ): similar forms, different roots

Arabic N W Y has other meanings, including intention and purpose. Nawā also occurs in the Qur’an meaning date stones or kernels.

Some written or spoken forms resemble one another, but a shared historical root cannot be asserted without evidence.

Naʾā should not be defined through intention, inward purpose, or a mental decision. Any intention inferred in a Qur’anic context comes from examining the conduct and sentence, rather than from N ʾ Y directly.

6. Translation challenges and proposals for Forqan

A key difficulty is that every Qur’anic occurrence places withdrawal in a critical context. This may tempt a translator to insert arrogance, rebellion, or denial into the verb itself.

Lexical meaning should nevertheless remain distinct from the contextual meaning.

The following renderings are proposed for Forqan:

Form or expressionProposed rendering
نَأَىٰMoved away; distanced oneself
يَنْأَوْنَThey keep away; they distance themselves
يَنْأَوْنَ عَنْهُThey keep away from it
نَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِDrew himself aside; withdrew
نَأْيDistance; remoteness; separation
مُنْتَأَىٰA distant place; a place of withdrawal

“Drew himself aside” suits Al-Isrāʾ and Fuṣṣilat, preserving distance and the image of bi-jānibihi.

“Withdrew arrogantly” is also defensible contextually, but arrogance is better explained in discussion rather than made a fixed component of the root’s translation.

“Fled from truth,” “denied The God’s command,” and “rejected religion” should not directly replace naʾā. Some may follow from a wider context, but the root does not itself express those details.

“They keep away from it” is precise and sufficient for Al-Anʿām 6:26. Whether the pronoun refers to the Messenger or his Qur’anic message can be discussed contextually.

7. Conclusion

N ʾ Y concerns distance, moving away, and separation. The distance may be spatial or appear in human conduct and relationships.

The verses attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī and Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā illustrate this range in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period: al-muntaʾā concerns a place of withdrawal, and nuʾy a structure keeping water away. These witnesses neither fix every composition date nor prove the historical origin of every branch.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an. In Al-Anʿām 6:26, yanʾawna ʿanhu describes a group keeping away from the message or Messenger while also deterring others.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 and Fuṣṣilat 41:51 combine naʾā bi-jānibihi with aʿraḍa to describe someone turning away and drawing aside when favored.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

N ʾ Y means moving away, distancing oneself, or separating from a person, thing, or situation. The distance can be bodily or behavioral. Naʾā bi-jānibihi pictures drawing oneself aside. Arrogance, ingratitude, and opposition follow from the construction and context, rather than the root’s independent meaning.

Keeping away is therefore a defensible semantic core, with withdrawal the most suitable expression for its uses in Al-Isrāʾ and Fuṣṣilat.
