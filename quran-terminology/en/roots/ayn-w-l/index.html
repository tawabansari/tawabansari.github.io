Departure from balance, injustice, burdens of responsibility, and providing for others

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ʿ–w–l has several semantic branches. The main ones concern leaning and losing balance, departing from right and justice, the weight and difficulty of a matter, providing for dependents, and relying on someone else.

In the first branch, ʿāla yaʿūlu means to incline, depart from balance, or deviate from what is right in judgment.

When the pans of a balance are unequal and one rises or falls relative to the other, Arabic can describe this as ʿāla al-mīzān. This family has also been used for an arrow that deviates from its proper course and misses the target.

In judgment and human conduct, this becomes departure from right and injustice. ʿĀla al-ḥākim means that the judge departed from justice and acted unjustly.

These uses are recorded in authoritative Arabic dictionaries. Their recording in later lexicons, however, does not by itself establish when each meaning first arose.

Another early Arabic branch concerns providing a livelihood and meeting others' needs. ʿĀla al-rajulu ʿiyālahu means that a man provided the living expenses of his dependents.

Here ʿāla is transitive, and the people provided for are its object.

Arabic also uses ʿālahu al-amru for a matter weighing heavily upon someone or placing them under pressure.

These branches have connections, but they should not be assumed in advance to derive from a single original physical image.

Arabic witnesses from before the Quran and near the period of revelation

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays contains:

وَإِنَّ شِفَائِي عَبْرَةٌ مُهَرَاقَةٌ
فَهَلْ عِنْدَ رَسْمٍ دَارِسٍ مِنْ مُعَوَّلِ

wa-inna shifāʾī ʿabratun muharāqatun / fa-hal ʿinda rasmin dārisin min muʿawwali

Translation: My remedy is a tear poured out; but is there anything to rely upon, or any place for weeping, beside the dwelling's worn-away traces?

Muʿawwal in this verse has two recognized interpretations.

One derives it from taʿwīl, reliance and trust: can one find hope in the traces of a ruined dwelling and place one's confidence in them?

The other connects it with ʿawīl and weeping: is there a place for weeping and lamentation beside these worn-away traces?

Both interpretations occur in early commentaries on the poem. The verse is significant for the antiquity of this linguistic form, but cannot conclusively establish one of the two disputed senses.

Nor does this witness directly establish the meaning of injustice or a balance tipping.

For the branch concerning departure from justice, a poem attributed to Abū Ṭālib contains:

بِمِيزَانِ صِدْقٍ لَا يُغِلُّ شَعِيرَةً
لَهُ شَاهِدٌ مِنْ نَفْسِهِ غَيْرُ عَائِلِ

bi-mīzāni ṣidqin lā yughillu shaʿīratan / lahu shāhidun min nafsihi ghayru ʿāʾili

Translation: With a truthful balance that cheats not even by a barley grain; it has a witness from itself that does not depart from justice.

Ghayru ʿāʾil here means one who does not deviate from what is right.

The verse is transmitted in poetry attributed to Abū Ṭālib, but its date of composition is not independently clear. It therefore cannot be assigned unconditionally to a time before Quranic revelation.

Another clear witness occurs in poetry attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Ḥārith al-Sahmī concerning the Muslims' migration to Abyssinia:

إِنَّا تَبِعْنَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ وَاطَّرَحُوا
قَوْلَ الرَّسُولِ وَعَالُوا فِي الْمَوَازِينِ

innā tabiʿnā rasūla allāhi wa-iṭṭaraḥū / qawla al-rasūli wa-ʿālū fī al-mawāzīni

Translation: We followed God's Messenger, while they cast aside the Messenger's words and departed from justice in the balances.

ʿĀlū fī al-mawāzīn here signifies departure from the proper standard and from justice.

The poem is associated with the beginning of the Islamic period. It is therefore evidence close to the period of revelation, rather than a pre-Quranic witness.

Together these witnesses show that deviation and loss of balance were recognized meanings in Arabic near the period of revelation. Yet the available independent pre-Quranic evidence does not justify confidently making one branch the historical origin of all the others.

Principal semantic branches

Several main branches can be distinguished in the family ʿ–w–l:

Deviation and imbalance: departing from a balanced state or from right and justice.

Weight and difficulty: a matter becoming more severe or weighing heavily upon someone.

Provision and support: meeting the needs of people dependent on another person's support and expenditure.

Reliance and seeking help: trusting or seeking assistance from someone or something.

Wailing and lamentation: raising the voice in weeping and sorrow.

These branches are recorded in Arabic. In the Quranic use of ʿ–w–l, the interpretation “departure from justice” has stronger contextual support, although a reading concerning the number of dependents has also been transmitted.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal Arabic forms in this family include:

ʿĀla yaʿūlu: in one use, to depart from balance or what is right; in another, to provide for one's dependents.

ʿAwl: deviation, imbalance, or heaviness; also a technical term in inheritance calculations.

ʿĀʾil: in the deviation branch, someone or something departing from balance; in the provision branch, someone responsible for others' living expenses.

ʿĀla ʿiyālahu: he provided for his dependents.

Aʿāla: in some uses, having an increased number of dependents or providing for others. The precise meaning depends on the construction.

ʿIyāl: people whose livelihood and needs are another person's responsibility.

ʿIyālah: providing a livelihood and meeting dependents' needs.

ʿAwwala ʿalā: he relied on or trusted someone or something.

Taʿwīl: reliance, trust, or dependence.

ʿAwīl and iʿwāl: loud weeping and lamentation.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran.

The form assigned to ʿ–w–l occurs only once in the Quran: taʿūlū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3.

It is a Form I verb addressed to a plural audience. In allā taʿūlū, the imperfect is subjunctive after an, and its final nūn is deleted.

Morphologically, taʿūlū and taʿīlū are not identical. Differences of vowel and verbal formation can distinguish semantic branches.

This matters when examining the relationship between ʿ–w–l and ʿ–y–l.

3. Quranic use: “allā taʿūlū”

وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تُقْسِطُوا فِي الْيَتَامَىٰ فَانْكِحُوا مَا طَابَ لَكُمْ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ مَثْنَىٰ وَثُلَاثَ وَرُبَاعَ ۖ فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا فَوَاحِدَةً أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَلَّا تَعُولُوا

wa-in khiftum allā tuqsiṭū fī al-yatāmā fa-inkiḥū mā ṭāba lakum mina al-nisāʾi mathnā wa-thulātha wa-rubāʿa fa-in khiftum allā taʿdilū fa-wāḥidatan aw mā malakat aymānukum dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū

Al-Nisāʾ 4:3

Translation: And if you fear that you will not deal equitably with orphans, marry such women as are pleasing to you: two, three, or four. If you fear that you will not act justly, then one, or what your right hands possess. That is nearer to your not acting unjustly.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:3

Here ʿ–w–l closes a passage that concerns equity and justice from its beginning.

Orphans are addressed first, with a warning about treating them equitably.

The discussion then raises the fear of failing to act justly in marriage and sets out a restriction when that concern exists.

The verse ends with dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū.

To understand it, three verbs should be considered together: tuqsiṭū, taʿdilū, and taʿūlū.

They are not synonymous, but they have a clear relationship within this verse.

Tuqsiṭū concerns observing equity and what is right.

Taʿdilū concerns acting justly and respecting balance and people's rights.

Taʿūlū, in this context, concerns departure from right and justice.

Simply put, someone responsible for several people's rights may distribute attention, resources, expenses, or other entitlements so that one receives more and another less than their due.

The issue is not merely outward inequality, but deprivation of a person's rights and the responsible person's departure from justice.

The root ʿ–w–l in this verse concerns that deviation.

Why prefer “departure from justice”?

One of the strongest indications is the verse's repeated concern with justice.

If the final phrase referred only to increasing family numbers, its connection with fa-in khiftum allā taʿdilū would require an additional explanatory step.

Departure from right, by contrast, relates directly to that condition.

The verse does not say the sole problem is rising living expenses; it speaks of the fear of failing to act justly.

This relationship becomes clearer alongside Al-Nisāʾ 4:129:

وَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعُوا أَنْ تَعْدِلُوا بَيْنَ النِّسَاءِ وَلَوْ حَرَصْتُمْ ۖ فَلَا تَمِيلُوا كُلَّ الْمَيْلِ فَتَذَرُوهَا كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ ۚ وَإِنْ تُصْلِحُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

wa-lan tastaṭīʿū an taʿdilū bayna al-nisāʾi wa-law ḥaraṣtum fa-lā tamīlū kulla al-mayli fa-tadharūhā ka-al-muʿallaqati wa-in tuṣliḥū wa-tattaqū fa-inna allāha kāna ghafūran raḥīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

Translation: You will never be able to act justly between women, however eagerly you desire it. So do not incline entirely to one side and leave her as though suspended. If you put things right and remain mindful, then The God is Forgiving, Merciful.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129

Here fa-lā tamīlū kulla al-mayl concerns inclining to one side and abandoning the other.

The verb belongs to m–y–l, not ʿ–w–l, but both verses concern justice between people.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 warns about the fear of failing to uphold justice.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129 describes human inability to achieve complete justice between women and warns against a one-sided inclination that leaves the other in uncertainty.

This connection supports understanding taʿūlū as departure from justice.

It does not, however, make ʿ–w–l and m–y–l synonyms in every Arabic use.

The role of “dhālika adnā”

Adnā here expresses being nearer.

The verse thus presents a course closer to avoiding deviation and injustice.

This does not mean that merely limiting the number of spouses automatically guarantees justice without respecting their rights.

A person with only one spouse can still wrong that spouse. The verse concerns rights from the beginning; the number one does not itself prevent every kind of injustice.

Thus allā taʿūlū concerns avoiding deviation and injustice, while dhālika adnā identifies the stated course's relationship to that aim.

4. The lexical disagreement over “taʿūlū”

Early sources offer two main interpretations of this expression.

First interpretation: do not depart from justice

On this reading, ʿāla yaʿūlu means to turn aside from right and act unjustly.

This relates to uses such as a balance tipping and a judge departing from fairness.

Suppose someone is responsible for distributing property among several people. If they reduce others' entitlements to favor one person instead of respecting each share, they have departed from justice.

That illustrates ʿawl in the field of judgment.

Justice between people is central to Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, so this reading has clear contextual support.

Second interpretation: your dependents should not increase

Some lexical and exegetical reports understand allā taʿūlū as avoiding an increase in dependents.

This interpretation has also been attributed to al-Shāfiʿī.

Arabic uses ʿāla yaʿūlu for providing for a household, and uses concerning an increase in dependents have also been reported.

Al-Kisāʾī and other early language scholars transmitted ʿāla yaʿūlu in the sense of having an increased number of dependents.

This reading therefore cannot be dismissed simply by asserting that no such Arabic usage existed.

Yet an attested meaning is not necessarily the preferred meaning in every verse.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, the final phrase follows two warnings concerning equity and justice. Departure from justice thus has a more direct connection to the verse's reasoning.

“Not increasing dependents” requires inferring a reduction in their number from a restriction on marriage. That may be conceivable under some circumstances, but the preceding sentence explicitly concerns acting justly, rather than the number of dependents.

The presence of aw mā malakat aymānukum in that same sentence also cautions against reducing the argument to a simple calculation of children or household expenses.

The first reading is therefore preferred, while the historical transmission and reported lexical basis of the second should not be denied.

Distinguishing “ʿāla yaʿūlu” from “ʿāla yaʿīlu”

In Arabic, the past form ʿāla can belong to verbs whose imperfect and verbal noun differ.

In its deviation branch, ʿāla yaʿūlu concerns inclining and acting unjustly.

Another recognized use, ʿāla yaʿīlu, concerns becoming needy or poor.

The morphological distinction matters because Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 has taʿūlū.

Some transmitted readings also report taʿīlū, but that differs from the commonly read Quranic text and the verbal form under discussion.

Poverty cannot therefore be imposed on taʿūlū merely because the verbs resemble one another.

Conversely, morphological differences alone do not disprove every historical or lexical connection between ʿ–w–l and ʿ–y–l.

5. Other branches and later technical terminology

ʿAwl as heaviness and intensification

Arabic uses ʿāla al-amru for a matter becoming difficult, burdensome, or more severe.

ʿĀlanī al-shayʾu can likewise mean that something weighed heavily upon or overpowered me.

This use concerns bearing pressure and difficulty.

Someone may, for example, be responsible for several tasks whose number and difficulty increase beyond their capacity.

That example does not establish that taʿūlū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 necessarily means “to come under the pressure of responsibility.”

Burdensome responsibility is one use within the family, but the verse's intended sense must be determined from its context of justice.

ʿAwl as providing a livelihood

ʿĀla ʿiyālahu means attending to the household's livelihood and providing its living expenses.

Unlike injustice, this use is not inherently negative.

Someone supporting children, a spouse, or people in need may be described by this verb.

The subject here is responsibility for meeting others' needs, not departure from justice.

Not every use of ʿ–w–l therefore carries a negative moral evaluation.

Taʿwīl as reliance and trust

ʿAwwala ʿalā fulānin means that someone relied on or trusted another person.

A person may, for example, count on another's knowledge and ability to accomplish a difficult task.

The preposition ʿalā belongs to this established construction.

Trust and reliance do not mean the same as departure from justice. Though both are recorded under ʿ–w–l, they should not all be derived from one original image without historical evidence.

ʿAwīl and weeping

ʿAwīl denotes loud weeping and lamentation.

This meaning is recognized in early Arabic and also appears in poetry.

Wailing relates to the burden of grief and the cry it produces. That semantic relationship alone does not prove that weeping developed from the idea of a tipped balance or injustice in judgment.

This branch should be understood through its own evidence.

ʿAwl in inheritance calculations

In later inheritance terminology, ʿawl acquired a specific technical meaning.

When the total calculated shares exceed the estate, the well-known method of ʿawl changes the basis of division and reduces the shares proportionally.

If the calculated shares add up to more than the whole estate, for example, a method of distributing it must be determined.

This technical use relates to an increased total of shares and the resulting imbalance in the calculation.

It should not, however, be imposed on Al-Nisāʾ 4:3.

That verse concerns justice in relation to orphans and marriage, rather than a technical method for calculating inheritance shares.

The inheritance term ʿawl should therefore remain distinct from Quranic taʿūlū.

6. Comparison with related roots

ʿ–W–L compared with ʿ–Y–L (ʿayl)

These families are close in form and overlap in part of their semantic range.

In Quranic morphological indexing, ʿaylah in Al-Tawbah 9:28 and ʿāʾilan in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 are placed under ʿ–y–l.

Taʿūlū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, by contrast, is placed under ʿ–w–l.

ʿAylah denotes need and poverty.

ʿĀʾil in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 likewise occurs in a context of need and being made self-sufficient.

This should not be equated, merely by outward resemblance, with ʿāʾil in the branches of deviation from balance or responsibility for others' livelihood.

Some lexicographers propose a historical relationship or interchange of weak consonants between these wāw and yāʾ forms. Their separate classification in Quranic morphology therefore does not establish complete historical independence.

The translation point is that the occurrence of ʿaylah in Al-Tawbah 9:28 does not justify rendering taʿūlū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 as “become poor” without further evidence.

ʿ–W–L compared with M–Y–L (mayl)

Mayl denotes inclining or leaning toward one side.

The inclination may be physical, emotional, or behavioral.

The deviation branch of ʿ–w–l also concerns inclining, but in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 departure from justice is prominent.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:129, fa-lā tamīlū kulla al-mayl concerns a one-sided inclination that leaves another person in uncertainty.

M–y–l can thus express inclination itself, whereas ʿ–w–l in the occurrence under discussion signifies departure from the standard of right and justice.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary across every use of both roots.

ʿ–W–L compared with ʿ–D–L (ʿadl)

One important Quranic branch of ʿ–d–l concerns justice and respecting rights and balance; that is the branch under comparison here.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, taʿdilū and taʿūlū appear in two related parts of the verse.

Their co-occurrence shows that avoiding ʿawl, on the preferred reading, relates to maintaining justice.

Yet ʿ–w–l is not simply the lexical opposite of ʿ–d–l. Its other uses, including provision, wailing, and reliance, have their own meanings.

The opposition concerns the relevant semantic branch and context, rather than the entirety of both families.

ʿ–W–L compared with J–W–R (jawr)

One Arabic branch of j–w–r concerns injustice and departure from right.

In some Arabic uses, ʿāla and jāra describe unjust judgment.

This proximity explains the use of tajūrū to gloss taʿūlū.

J–w–r nevertheless has its own semantic family, and not all derivatives of ʿ–w–l should be defined through it.

For Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, “do not act unjustly” is a natural rendering, since the verse concerns justice and preventing violations of others' rights.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating ʿ–w–l first requires distinguishing verbal form, semantic branch, and context.

In the justice branch, ʿāla yaʿūlu may be rendered “departed from right,” “acted unjustly,” or “lost balance.”

ʿĀla al-mīzān means “the balance became unbalanced.”

ʿĀla al-ḥākim may be rendered “the judge departed from justice” or “the judge acted unjustly.”

ʿĀla ʿiyālahu means “he provided for his household.”

In the reliance sense, ʿawwala ʿalā means “relied upon someone or something.”

A natural equivalent for ʿawīl is “loud weeping and lamentation.”

Two suitable equivalents for Quranic taʿūlū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 are:

“Depart from justice,” which makes its connection with the lexical sense of deviation clearer.

“Act unjustly,” which is shorter and natural, and suits the verse's concern with rights and justice.

For the Forqan base translation, “not act unjustly” is a suitable, defensible equivalent. The more precise sense of departure from justice can be explained in the lexical discussion.

By contrast, “not become poor” or “your dependents should not increase,” though discussed in the history of interpretation and language, are not the first contextual preference.

8. Conclusion

Arabic ʿ–w–l is a family with several branches. Its meanings cannot all be reduced to one historical image without evidence.

In one branch, ʿawl concerns departure from balance and justice, as with a tipped balance or unjust judgment.

Other attested branches concern a matter's heaviness and intensification, providing for dependents, reliance on another, and loud weeping and lamentation.

Taʿūlū is the sole clear Quranic occurrence of ʿ–w–l, appearing at the end of Al-Nisāʾ 4:3.

Its construction and relationship with tuqsiṭū and taʿdilū favor the sense of departure from right and justice.

The reading concerning an increase in dependents also appears in early sources and has a reported lexical basis, but its connection with the verse's condition of justice is no more direct than that of the first reading.

ʿAylah and ʿāʾil elsewhere in the Quran are classified under ʿ–y–l in morphological indexing. The families' proximity and possible historical relationship must not erase the distinctions between their derivatives in individual verses.

The technical inheritance term ʿawl is likewise a later, distinct use that should not replace the verse's meaning.

Final definition of ʿ–w–l:

In the branch concerning justice, ʿ–w–l signifies inclining away from a balanced state and departing from the standard of right, a deviation that may amount to injustice in judgment and human conduct. Other branches include heaviness and difficulty, providing a livelihood, reliance, and wailing. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, the context of equity and justice favors understanding taʿūlū as departing from justice and acting unjustly.
