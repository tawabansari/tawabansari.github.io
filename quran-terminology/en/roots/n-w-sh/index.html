Reaching to take, attaining something, engaging across a distance, and seeking an opportunity already lost

In Arabic, N W SH concerns reaching and taking something, extending a hand or instrument to attain it. A person may pick something up, an animal reach food with its mouth, or a fighter reach an opponent with a spear. Each involves reaching something and taking hold of or affecting it.

Some derivatives extend to pulling someone out of danger, conveying benefit or harm, and conflict between groups.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as التَّنَاوُشُ (al-tanāwush) in Sabaʾ 34:52. No physical object is being taken. People seek something they did not turn toward when the opportunity existed, and which has now become remote.

The root’s meaning must be distinguished from the complete verse. Nawsh basically concerns attaining and taking; distance, lateness, and impossible access emerge from the construction and context, rather than the root alone.

1. The semantic core in early Arabic

An early lexical definition says:

النُّونُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالشِّينُ أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَنَاوُلِ الشَّيْءِ

al-nūnu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-shīnu aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā tanāwuli al-shayʾi

“Nūn, wāw, and shīn form a sound root denoting taking or attaining something.”

The same explanation continues:

نُشْتُهُ نَوْشًا، وَتَنَاوَشْتُ: تَنَاوَلْتُ

nushtuhu nawshan wa-tanāwashtu: tanāwaltu

That is, “I took it or attained it.”

The definition fits varied Arabic witnesses and provides a starting point. However, tanāwul here broadly means reaching and taking. It should not initially be confined to taking from afar, fighting, rescuing, or arriving after an opportunity has ended.

Arabic usage also records:

نَاشَهُ بِيَدِهِ يَنُوشُهُ نَوْشًا: تَنَاوَلَهُ

nāshahu bi-yadihi yanūshuhu nawshan: tanāwalahu

“He reached it with his hand and took it.”

The example makes the concrete sense clear: something is within reach, and a person takes it.

Reaching with the mouth or a spear, or conveying something to another person, can also be understood within this field.

Is proximity part of the basic meaning?

One lexical analysis distinguishes two forms:

التَّنَاوُشُ (al-tanāwush), without hamzah, is explained as taking from nearby; التَّنَاؤُشُ (al-tanāʾush), with hamzah, as taking from afar.

This distinction matters within the lexical tradition, but should not become an exceptionless definition for every N W SH use.

In combat usage, for example, two groups reach one another with spears without yet drawing completely close. Reaching and taking can therefore involve a distance covered by a hand or an instrument.

“Attaining and taking” is the preferable basic definition, with distance or proximity established through evidence and construction where relevant.

This caution matters in Sabaʾ because the verse explicitly mentions مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ (makānin baʿīd), a distant place.

2. Arabic derivatives and uses

Nāsha yanūshu: reaching and taking

نَاشَ (nāsha) is a simple triliteral verb. Transitively, it can mean reaching, picking up, or taking something.

Arabic records:

نَاشَهُ بِيَدِهِ (nāshahu bi-yadihi): he took it with his hand.

نَاشَهُ بِالرُّمْحِ (nāshahu bi-al-rumḥi): he reached or struck at him with a spear.

The instrument differs, while reaching a target remains.

Nawsh: taking and conveying something

نَوْش (nawsh) is the verb’s verbal noun.

Early Arabic also records نُشْتُهُ خَيْرًا (nushtuhu khayran): “I brought him some good.”

The same construction is also recorded for conveying harm.

The root is therefore not inherently good or bad. Reaching another person may bring benefit or injury. The act and context determine its ethical value.

Tanāwasha and tanāwush: mutual reaching or an effort to take

تَنَاوَشَ (tanāwasha) is Form VI; تَنَاوُش (tanāwush) is its verbal noun.

In combat, تَنَاوَشَ الْقَوْمُ (tanāwasha al-qawmu) describes groups reaching one another with spears before fully close, hand-to-hand engagement.

The use adds confrontation and conflict to the field, but tanāwush does not invariably mean fighting.

Form VI can express participation between two sides in some uses. In Sabaʾ, however, tanāwush need not mean passing something between two people. Within the verse it means attaining or taking.

Munāwashah: engagement and limited conflict

مُنَاوَشَة (munāwashah) is a Form III verbal noun. In combat, it denotes engagement between two sides without necessarily involving an entirely close or general battle.

Extended uses can also concern a dispute or limited confrontation.

This is intelligible through the possibility of reaching the other side by hand or another instrument.

Intāsha and intiyāsh: extracting and rescuing

اِنْتَاشَ (intāsha) is recorded for taking, extracting, and, in some settings, rescuing.

For example:

اِنْتَاشَنِي فُلَانٌ مِنَ الْهَلَكَةِ

intāshanī fulānun mina al-halakati

“Someone rescued me from destruction.”

One person reaches another and draws them out of danger.

However, rescue is not the meaning of every use of this form. It emerges from taking and bringing someone out of هَلَكَة (halakah), destruction.

Nawūsh: powerful in seizing

نَوُوش (nawūsh) is also recorded for a strong person with power to seize or grasp.

The description fits reaching and taking, but is not the meaning of Qur’anic tanāwush.

Other forms, including تَنْوِيش (tanwīsh), have less frequent recorded uses. These include inviting, promising, preparing hospitality, and descriptions involving animal movement or bodily structure.

They help establish the historical range, although their precise links with the root’s first core are not equally clear.

Only al-tanāwush from this family occurs in the Qur’an.

3. Early Arabic and poetic witnesses

Spears reaching a fighter

A clear witness occurs in poetry of Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah:

فَجِئْتُ إِلَيْهِ وَالرِّمَاحُ تَنُوشُهُ
كَوَقْعِ الصَّيَاصِي فِي النَّسِيجِ الْمُمَدَّدِ

fa-jiʾtu ilayhi wa-al-rimāḥu tanūshuhu / ka-waqʿi al-ṣayāṣī fī al-nasīji al-mumaddadi

“I came to him as spears reached him, striking like weaving tools entering a stretched fabric.”

The poet describes a battle in which spears repeatedly reach a person.

تَنُوشُهُ (tanūshuhu) here concerns reaching and striking. The spear is the means of reaching the target.

The verb does not itself mean killing. A spear may reach and injure someone, but the final outcome must be understood from the rest of the account.

Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah is known from the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The verse occurs in poetry concerning battle and his brother ʿAbd Allāh’s death, and is transmitted in both lexical and early poetic accounts.

Some transmissions begin نَظَرْتُ إِلَيْهِ (naẓartu ilayhi), “I looked at him,” instead of فَجِئْتُ إِلَيْهِ (fa-jiʾtu ilayhi). The variant should be retained without fundamentally changing tanūshuhu’s meaning.

A gazelle reaching food

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says:

فَمَا أُمُّ خِشْفٍ بِالْعَلَايَةِ شَادِنٍ
تَنُوشُ الْبَرِيرَ حَيْثُ نَالَ اهْتِصَارُهَا

fa-mā ummu khishfin bi-al-ʿalāyati shādinin / tanūshu al-barīra ḥaythu nāla ihtiṣāruhā

A rendering close to the sense is: “The mother of a growing fawn at al-ʿAlāyah, reaching and eating arāk fruit wherever she can draw a branch toward her.”

Tanūshu describes the animal reaching its food.

Unlike Durayd’s witness, where spears reach a person, an animal here reaches food with its mouth.

The verb itself is therefore not specifically injury or eating. Reaching and taking something is shared.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived through both periods. The verse is recorded in the Hudhalī collected poetry, but cannot be securely dated before the Qur’an without establishing when it was composed.

Camels taking water from a trough

A rajaz passage attributed to Ghaylān ibn Ḥurayth reads:

فَهِيَ تَنُوشُ الْحَوْضَ نَوْشًا مِنْ عَلَا
نَوْشًا بِهِ تَقْطَعُ أَجْوَازَ الْفَلَا

fa-hiya tanūshu al-ḥawḍa nawshan min ʿalā / nawshan bihi taqṭaʿu ajwāza al-falā

“They reach the trough’s water from above, drinking enough to cross the expanses of the wilderness.”

Tall, long-necked camels are described reaching the trough with their mouths and drinking. The feminine singular هِيَ (hiya) refers to إبل (ibil), the collective name for camels.

This likewise supports reaching and taking.

Across the three witnesses, one idea appears in distinct situations: spears reaching a fighter, a gazelle’s mouth reaching fruit, and a camel reaching water.

Nawsh in early Arabic is therefore not the name of one particular act in war or feeding. It covers a broader field of access and taking.

The rajaz attributed to Ghaylān is recorded lexically, but no independent evidence establishing a pre-Islamic date for this particular passage was found in the present study.

4. Qur’anic al-tanāwush

Sabaʾ 34:52

وَقَالُوا آمَنَّا بِهِ وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمُ التَّنَاوُشُ مِن مَّكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-qālū āmannā bihi wa-annā lahumu al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīdin

A rendering close to the wording:

“They said: We believe in it. But how can they attain it from a distant place?”

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence. Both the word’s structure and the surrounding verses must therefore be examined.

Morphology and syntax

Al-tanāwush is the Form VI verbal noun of tanāwasha.

It is definite and nominative. A common syntactic analysis treats it as a delayed subject, with أَنَّىٰ (annā) serving as the preceding predicate.

لَهُمُ (lahum) refers to those saying آمَنَّا بِهِ (āmannā bihi). مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ (min makānin baʿīd) expresses the distance involved in attaining.

Annā questions how, or whether, attaining is possible. Context gives the question a force of denial and impossibility.

The noun “faith” does not appear again after tanāwush. Attaining faith, or its benefit, is understood through the preceding words and context.

Adding faith in an explanation is defensible, while a strictly direct rendering should distinguish that inference from an explicit noun following tanāwush.

5. The readings al-tanāwush and al-tanāʾush

This is a particularly significant point in the root study.

In Ḥafṣ’s familiar transmission from ʿĀṣim, the word is al-tanāwush, without hamzah.

Other recognized readings, including Abū ʿAmr, Ḥamzah, al-Kisāʾī, Khalaf the Tenth, and Shuʿbah’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, have al-tanāʾush with hamzah.

Two principal lexical analyses explain the difference.

First analysis: hamzah as a variant of wāw

Both readings belong to N W SH under this analysis.

The vowelled wāw becomes hamzah, a change also found in certain other Arabic constructions.

Early lexical and interpretive accounts propose this possibility.

Al-tanāwush and al-tanāʾush would then share a root and basic sense, differing mainly in pronunciation and sound development.

Second analysis: the hamzah form belongs to N ʾ SH

Some lexical analyses propose a different semantic origin for the hamzah form.

N ʾ SH is associated here with lateness, distance, and slow movement.

One lexical explanation says:

النَّئِيشُ: الْحَرَكَةُ فِي إِبْطَاءٍ

al-naʾīshu: al-ḥarakatu fī ibṭāʾin

“Naʾīsh is movement with slowness and delay.”

One account also explains tanāʾush with hamzah as taking from afar.

A verse attributed to Nahshal ibn Ḥarrī illustrates the sense:

تَمَنَّى نَئِيشًا أَنْ يَكُونَ أَطَاعَنِي
وَقَدْ حَدَثَتْ بَعْدَ الْأُمُورِ أُمُورُ

tamannā naʾīshan an yakūna aṭāʿanī / wa-qad ḥadathat baʿda al-umūri umūru

“Belatedly he wished he had obeyed me, after further events had followed those events.”

Naʾīshan marks the wish as late, when the earlier decision can no longer readily be remedied.

Poetic transmissions and some word forms vary. The witness helps establish naʾīsh’s meaning without proving the historical origin of the reading with hamzah.

Assessing the two analyses

Both are recorded in early Arabic and should not be dismissed without examination.

The first reads al-tanāʾush as attaining, with hamzah a sound variant of wāw.

In the second, the hamzah form can emphasize lateness and distance: trying to attain something after its opportunity has passed.

Their contextual interpretation nevertheless approaches the same point. Those who did not turn toward truth when able are now remote from reaching it or benefiting from it.

A boundary must remain in the N W SH study. N ʾ SH has a different consonantal sequence. If the hamzah reading is assigned to that root, its entire range should not be transferred to N W SH without explanation.

6. Context: distance from an opportunity once near

Sabaʾ 34:51, Sabaʾ 34:52, Sabaʾ 34:53, and Sabaʾ 34:54 matter for understanding al-tanāwush.

Sabaʾ 34:51 says:

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذْ فَزِعُوا فَلَا فَوْتَ وَأُخِذُوا مِن مَّكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ

wa-law tarā idh faziʿū fa-lā fawta wa-ukhidhū min makānin qarībin

“If you could see when they became terrified: there was no escape, and they were seized from a nearby place.”

Sabaʾ 34:52 then says:

وَقَالُوا آمَنَّا بِهِ وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمُ التَّنَاوُشُ مِن مَّكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-qālū āmannā bihi wa-annā lahumu al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīdin

“They said: We believe in it. But how can they attain it from a distant place?”

Sabaʾ 34:53 says:

وَقَدْ كَفَرُوا بِهِ مِن قَبْلُ وَيَقْذِفُونَ بِالْغَيْبِ مِن مَّكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-qad kafarū bihi min qablu wa-yaqdhifūna bi-al-ghaybi min makānin baʿīdin

“They had denied it before and cast statements about the unseen from a distant place.”

The repeated contrasts of near and far are striking.

First, they are seized from nearby and have no escape. Next, they seek to attain something from afar. The third verse describes their earlier conduct as casting toward the unseen from a distant place.

“Place” therefore does more than supply a geographical address. Distance describes their situation in relation to access, escape, and knowledge of truth.

Attaining while possible and after the opportunity ends

One early interpretation explains tanāwush as seeking faith or repentance after its effective opportunity has passed.

Those able to accept truth in worldly life say āmannā bihi when confronting the outcome.

The verse asks how they now intend to attain it from afar.

This fits Sabaʾ 34:51, Sabaʾ 34:52, Sabaʾ 34:53, and Sabaʾ 34:54. In particular, Sabaʾ 34:54 says:

وَحِيلَ بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ مَا يَشْتَهُونَ

wa-ḥīla baynahum wa-bayna mā yashtahūna

“A barrier was placed between them and what they desired.”

Distance here is more than physical separation. It also concerns lost opportunity and a barrier to what they want.

Why does their faith no longer benefit them?

Ghāfir 40:84 and Ghāfir 40:85 describe people believing in The God upon seeing punishment, without that faith benefiting them in that situation.

Ghāfir 40:85 says:

فَلَمْ يَكُ يَنْفَعُهُمْ إِيمَانُهُمْ لَمَّا رَأَوْا بَأْسَنَا

fa-lam yaku yanfaʿuhum īmānuhum lammā raʾaw baʾsanā

“Their faith did not benefit them when they saw Our punishment.”

Al-Anʿām 6:158 also says:

لَا يَنْفَعُ نَفْسًا إِيمَانُهَا لَمْ تَكُنْ آمَنَتْ مِن قَبْلُ أَوْ كَسَبَتْ فِي إِيمَانِهَا خَيْرًا

lā yanfaʿu nafsan īmānuhā lam takun āmanat min qablu aw kasabat fī īmānihā khayran

“Faith will not benefit someone who did not believe before or earn good through their faith.”

These witnesses help clarify the setting of Sabaʾ 34:52.

They distinguish accepting truth while free choice remains from professing faith upon confronting certain consequences.

This should not become the lexical definition of nawsh. The root denotes attaining; the verse and related Qur’anic passages explain why attaining is no longer possible or beneficial in that setting.

An example of wording and context

Suppose someone disregards a clear truth while there is still time to revise a decision. After its consequences appear and the opportunity to return is lost, they want to change the outcome.

The example illustrates belated access. It does not give every mistaken human decision the same final judgment described in the verse.

Sabaʾ 34:52 therefore does not establish that every lost opportunity is irreparable in every circumstance for everyone. It addresses a particular confrontation with truth and the outcome of previous conduct.

7. Distinctions from related roots

N W SH and N W L: tanāwush and tanāwul

Tanāwul belongs to N W L, a family involving taking, attaining, and giving.

Lexical explanations repeatedly use tanāwul to explain nawsh. The semantic closeness does not make the roots identical.

Nawsh witnesses clearly show reaching with a hand, mouth, or another instrument. Some derivatives also develop confrontation and conflict within that field.

These should not become absolute distinctions between nawsh and tanāwul; each has its own range of uses.

N W SH and N ʾ SH: taking and delay

This distinction appeared in discussing the readings of Sabaʾ 34:52.

The lexical definition examined connects N W SH with attaining something.

Some early accounts associate N ʾ SH with lateness, distance, and belated movement.

Al-tanāʾush has prompted discussion of the families’ relationship. A shared contextual meaning does not by itself erase their morphological difference.

N W SH and N W Ṣ: attaining and escape

مَنَاص (manāṣ), from N W Ṣ, occurs in Ṣād 38:3:

وَلَاتَ حِينَ مَنَاصٍ

wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣin

“It was no longer a time of escape.”

Arabic N W Ṣ concerns movement to and fro, moving aside, and escape.

Manāṣ and al-tanāwush can both describe a lost opportunity, while one concerns escape and the other attaining.

A shared theme should not merge the roots.

N W SH and N H SH: taking and biting

نَهْش (nahsh), from N H SH, concerns taking with the mouth and teeth, and biting.

Nawsh can describe an animal reaching food with its mouth, but is not restricted to biting.

The trough, hand, and spear witnesses establish a broader field.

Similar sounds and overlap in some physical scenes do not make the roots identical.

8. Proposed translation for Forqan

Two equivalents suit al-tanāwush in Sabaʾ 34:52:

“Attaining” is natural and close to the root’s sense.

“Gaining access” also fits, particularly given the emphasis on distance and lost access.

For the expression:

وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمُ التَّنَاوُشُ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-annā lahumu al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīdin

The proposed rendering is:

“But how can they attain it from a distant place?”

The rendering avoids replacing the Arabic word with a specific interpretive referent.

An explanation can clarify that faith or its benefit is sought after an earlier opportunity has been lost.

A direct rendering should preferably not replace tanāwush with repentance, returning to the world, believing after death, or futile remorse. Those explain the context rather than the word itself.

Nor should tanāwush become fighting. Combat is one Arabic use, without a supporting indication here.

9. Conclusion

Early Arabic N W SH concerns reaching, taking, and attaining something. Documented witnesses show a hand, mouth, or instrument such as a spear providing the means.

Limited combat, conveying benefit or harm, extracting something, and rescuing someone from danger develop within this field.

One lexical analysis identifies tanāwul al-shayʾ, attaining something, as the basic meaning. Durayd’s and Abū Dhuʾayb’s poetry support it in different settings.

Only al-tanāwush occurs in the Qur’an, in Sabaʾ 34:52. This Form VI verbal noun concerns attaining something now remote from access.

The reading al-tanāʾush also matters. Some accounts treat it as the same root with hamzah; others explain it through N ʾ SH and lateness or distance. The analyses must remain distinct.

Sabaʾ 34:51, Sabaʾ 34:52, Sabaʾ 34:53, and Sabaʾ 34:54 place the distance in a situation where someone confronting the outcome seeks a truth they could previously have accepted.

Impossible access is not established by nawsh alone. It comes from the expression wa-annā lahumu al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīd and the surrounding verses.

A concise practical definition for Forqan is:

N W SH: reaching and attaining or taking something with a hand, mouth, or another instrument. Some derivatives extend to conflict, engagement, or extraction from danger. Al-tanāwush in Sabaʾ 34:52 means attaining; min makānin baʿīd expresses its distance and difficulty.
