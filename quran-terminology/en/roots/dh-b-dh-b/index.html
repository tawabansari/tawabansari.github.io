1. Root meaning and word structure

«ذَبْذَبَ» (dhabdhaba) is an Arabic quadriliteral verb concerning unstable movement, going back and forth, wavering, and failing to settle in one direction or condition. Movement may be bodily, as with a hanging object swaying from side to side, or concern human conduct and allegiance, as with someone repeatedly shifting between two decisions or groups without remaining firm with either.

In early Arabic, «الذَّبْذَبَة» (al-dhabdhabah) describes the swaying of something suspended in the air. The dictionaries’ expression «نَوْسُ الشَّيْءِ الْمُعَلَّقِ فِي الْهَوَاءِ» (nawsu al-shayʾi al-muʿallaqi fī al-hawāʾi) conveys that meaning.

The movement does not reach a stable position: something inclines one way, returns, and moves the other way. The word therefore means more than movement alone; it involves changing direction and lack of stability.

Dhabdhaba repeats the consonant pair DH B and follows quadriliteral faʿlala. Arabic uses this structure for certain verbs of movement, sound, or repeated action, such as zalzala and waswasa.

Repeated consonants alone nevertheless do not prove that every action with this structure happens several times or at regular intervals. Actual usage determines the precise meaning.

For dhabdhaba, lexical witnesses themselves confirm back-and-forth movement and instability. This meaning is therefore not inferred merely from structural resemblance to repetitive verbs.

2. Linguistic usages and principal derivatives

Dhabdhaba and tadhabdhaba

Dhabdhaba can describe a person or thing moving restlessly, or an agent causing something else to move or oscillate.

In «ذَبْذَبَ الشَّيْءَ» (dhabdhaba al-shayʾa), for example, the subject moves something and unsettles it.

«تَذَبْذَبَ» (tadhabdhaba) more directly emphasizes the person’s or object’s own restless movement. In tadhabdhaba al-shayʾu, the thing oscillates without a settled position.

Applied to people, dhabdhaba al-rajulu and tadhabdhaba al-rajulu describe someone wavering between two matters or people without settling with either.

This can concern decisions, allegiance, relationships, or orientation. Someone may incline one way today and return the other way tomorrow without a clear position.

Mudhabdhib, mudhabdhab, and mutadhabdhib

Distinguishing these three forms matters for the Qur’anic occurrence.

«مُذَبْذِب» (mudhabdhib) is the active participle of dhabdhaba. In transitive use, it describes someone or something moving another into oscillation; given the verb’s intransitive use, it can also describe the oscillating subject. Its definition should therefore not invariably be restricted to moving someone else.

«مُذَبْذَب» (mudhabdhab) is the passive participle of the same verb: someone or something put into oscillation or caught in instability and vacillation.

«مُتَذَبْذِب» (mutadhabdhib) is the active participle of tadhabdhaba, generally denoting someone or something itself oscillating or restless.

This morphological distinction should not be overlooked. Mudhabdhab and mutadhabdhib can describe a similar condition in ordinary usage, but their structures differ.

In the common text used by Forqan, «مُذَبْذَبِينَ» (mudhabdhabīna) is the masculine plural of mudhabdhab, rather than mutadhabdhib. The text describes the people through a structure also expressing being affected or placed in an unstable condition.

A passive participle does not establish that a person lacks agency or responsibility, or that an external force necessarily moves him against his will. The form expresses an affected condition; identifying its cause requires context.

Dhabādhib and bodily applications

Arabic uses «ذَباذِب» (dhabādhib) for ornaments suspended from a litter or a camel’s head. They sway from side to side as the animal moves.

Dhabdhabah is also recorded for hanging fringes at a garment’s edge, which move as the wearer moves.

Some sources use «الذَّبْذَب» (al-dhabdhab) for the tongue and for the male sexual organ, explaining these names through their movement.

These usages illuminate the word’s tangible semantic field. Unsettled movement, with something shifting rather than staying fixed, provides a recognizable common feature.

They do not, however, establish that every name necessarily arose from one historical image. The evidence shows Arabic usages concerning movement and oscillation.

3. Early Arabic witnesses and the connection with DH B B

Al-ʿAyn, among the earliest comprehensive Arabic dictionaries, explains al-dhabdhabah as the swaying of something suspended in the air. The same entry applies mudhabdhab and mutadhabdhib to someone wavering between two matters or people without maintaining steady companionship with either.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris likewise identifies movement and restlessness as a principal branch of DH B B. He brings together a suspended object’s swaying, human vacillation, and an animal’s movement without remaining in one place.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, DH B B: movement and restlessness

This shows that instability was recognized in tangible and general Arabic usage, rather than arising solely from interpretation of the Qur’anic verse.

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits another poetic witness:

وَحَوْقَلٍ ذَبْذَبَهُ الْوَجِيفُ
ظَلَّ لِأَعْلَى رَأْسِهِ رَجِيفُ

wa-ḥawqalin dhabdhabahu al-wajīfu / ẓalla li-aʿlā raʾsihi rajīfu

The image may be rendered: “And an old man whom rapid movement has shaken; the top of his head keeps trembling.” Rapid movement and its jolts have made the old man shake restlessly.

The witness matters for transitive dhabdhabahu: something or someone has made him move or tremble. It helps explain the passive participle mudhabdhab.

Thaʿlab, an early lexicographer, transmits the verse. Without securely identifying its poet and date, however, it cannot conclusively be assigned to the pre-Islamic period.

Al-Muḥkam: Thaʿlab’s transmission and transitive dhabdhaba

Is DH B DH B derived from DH B B?

Several dictionaries discuss dhabdhaba under DH B B. Some linguists likewise explain it through expansion or structural alteration of that base.

«ذَبَّ» (dhabba) has several Arabic uses, two of which matter here.

One uses dhabba ʿanhu for repelling, driving away, or protecting against something. Someone removing a threat from another person has defended him.

Another records dhabba for moving, going back and forth, and not staying in one place.

The second branch is semantically closer to dhabdhaba. Unsettled movement can extend to moving between positions without a settled direction.

Some lexical accounts also connect the word with repelling or driving away. Under that reading, mudhabdhab can describe someone driven from one side to another or not fully accepted by either party.

These explanations are not identical.

The first centers on oscillation and instability; the second emphasizes being driven away and failing to settle with either group.

Repeated DH B consonants or neighboring dictionary entries alone do not establish that dhabdhaba necessarily developed from rejection or expulsion. A possible historical connection should remain distinct from attested meaning and Qur’anic usage.

Nor can every triliteral DH B B meaning automatically be transferred to quadriliteral DH B DH B. Movement, instability, and vacillation provide more direct support for studying this Qur’anic root.

4. The Qur’anic occurrence in Al-Nisāʾ 4:143

In the Qur’anic text used by Forqan, DH B DH B occurs only once, as mudhabdhabīna.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one passive participle from the quadriliteral root

Al-Nisāʾ 4:143

مُذَبْذَبِينَ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ لَا إِلَىٰ هَٰؤُلَاءِ وَلَا إِلَىٰ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ۚ وَمَن يُضْلِلِ اللَّهُ فَلَن تَجِدَ لَهُ سَبِيلًا

mudhabdhabīna bayna dhālika lā ilā hāʾulāʾi wa-lā ilā hāʾulāʾi wa-man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-lan tajida lahu sabīlan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Wavering and unsettled between that; neither toward these nor toward those. Whoever The God leads astray, you will never find a way for him.”

The grammatical role of mudhabdhabīna

Mudhabdhabīna is a passive participle, a sound masculine plural, and accusative here. A common grammatical analysis treats it as a circumstantial expression describing the hypocrites whose conduct appears in the preceding verse.

It is therefore not simply a general adjective for every hesitant person, but describes a particular group within the context of their conduct and allegiances.

Bayna dhālika identifies the sphere of their wavering; lā ilā hāʾulāʾi wa-lā ilā hāʾulāʾi makes their lack of a settled orientation toward either side explicit.

Ilā also matters. The verse does not simply place these people physically between two groups; it concerns allegiance and direction: neither toward these nor toward those.

The context and the nature of this instability

Al-Nisāʾ 4:141 describes people waiting for the outcome of confrontations. If the believers gain victory, they present themselves as their companions; if the opposing side gains a share, they also speak to it of their role and support.

This behavior shifts position according to advantage and outcome. In practice, such people adjust their relationships and loyalty to the sides’ victories and defeats.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:142 describes their hypocritical conduct toward The God. They rise for ṣalāh reluctantly, act to be seen by people, and remember The God only a little.

Mudhabdhabīna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:143 therefore follows two notable features: shifting position for advantage and a divide between outward conduct and actual orientation.

This context prevents reducing their wavering to intellectual doubt or difficulty choosing a belief. The central issue is lack of sincerity and steadiness in practical allegiance.

They seek to appear aligned with whichever side benefits them in each situation, without clear and lasting loyalty.

Mudhabdhabīna here therefore describes more than someone who has not yet answered a question. The word expresses instability; the context associates it with hypocrisy and pursuit of advantage.

Al-Ṭabarī: wavering, allegiance, and the hypocrites’ context

The passive participle and being affected

Mudhabdhabīna in Forqan’s base text is a passive participle, rather than mutadhabdhibīna. Al-Kashshāf reports the reading mudhabdhibīna, with kasrah on the second dhāl, and gives mutadhabdhibīna in its report of ʿAbd Allāh’s codex. These reports should remain distinct from the installed text; morphological analysis should address the particular form being examined.

Al-Kashshāf: reported readings distinguished from Forqan’s base text

The passive structure can evoke someone subject to competing pulls and pressures, drawn from one condition to another without an independent, firm position.

It does not, however, make the hypocrites merely victims of outside forces lacking responsibility. The preceding verses describe their deliberate, self-interested conduct.

Nor does it conclusively require rejection by both groups. That reading exists in lexical sources, but the context clearly supports the broader instability of allegiance and direction.

Morphology can thus foreground being affected and unsettled, while context also makes practical responsibility clear.

Wa-man yuḍlili Allāhu and the outcome of this condition

The verse ends: “Whoever The God leads astray, you will never find a way for him.”

This relates misguidance to The God, but does not establish that the preceding hypocritical conduct arose against the people’s will and without their responsibility.

The following verses also matter. Al-Nisāʾ 4:146 explicitly describes repentance, reform, holding fast to The God, and making religion sincere for those returning from hypocrisy.

The warning in Al-Nisāʾ 4:143 should therefore not become a conclusion that reform is forever closed to every person caught in hypocrisy. Al-Nisāʾ 4:146 gives conditions for leaving that state.

For DH B DH B, the main point is that the described instability relates to human conduct and its outcome; the root itself cannot yield a complete theory of agency and guidance.

5. Dhabdhabah, hesitation, doubt, bewilderment, and hypocrisy

Dhabdhabah relates to hesitation, rayb, and bewilderment, but is not completely identical with any of them.

Hesitation: in ordinary usage, inability to prefer one possibility or decision over others. Someone may hesitate and withhold a hasty position until the evidence is clearer. This need not be blameworthy vacillation.

رَيْب (rayb): Qur’anic usage concerns doubt, suspicion, and lack of assurance. It more directly raises uncertainty about something’s truth or soundness, whereas dhabdhabah foregrounds instability and changing direction.

Study of R Y B

Bewilderment: being at a loss about the right path or decision. A bewildered person may stand still; dhabdhabah’s image also involves movement between directions without settling.

Hypocrisy: an ethical and behavioral condition involving divergence between outward appearance and inward position, or professed faith and actual allegiance. Mudhabdhab is not itself a lexical synonym for hypocrite. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:143, vacillation is one feature of the hypocrites described in context.

Study of N F Q

This distinction matters: not every change of opinion or temporary difficulty deciding indicates hypocrisy.

Someone may abandon an incorrect view after hearing better evidence. Such change can reflect sincerity and pursuit of truth rather than blameworthy instability.

The difference from the hypocritical conduct in Al-Nisāʾ concerns the change’s cause and direction. One may follow evidence and correction of error; the other accompanies self-interest, display, and changing loyalty according to circumstances.

Mudhabdhab should therefore not be used to condemn every inquiring person, critic, or person examining a question. Its Qur’anic use belongs within a particular network of conduct.

6. Modern usages and conclusion

Modern Arabic and Persian also use tadhabdhub in the natural sciences: oscillating waves, electric current, pendulum movement, and periodic changes in physical quantities.

Such scientific movement or change can be described by frequency, amplitude, and period.

This technical usage should not be projected back onto Qur’anic Arabic to claim that mudhabdhabīna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:143 necessarily means regular, periodic, or measurable oscillation.

Movement or change between states remains shared between early and modern usage. The scientific definition is a technical extension of this semantic field, rather than an independent determination of the word’s historical meaning.

Arabic DH B DH B concerns oscillation, restless movement, and lack of a settled position. Applied to people, it extends to wavering between two matters or groups without steady allegiance.

Forqan’s Qur’anic base text uses only mudhabdhabīna, the plural passive participle of dhabdhaba. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:143, it describes hypocrites whose divided and self-interested behavior is presented in the preceding verses.

The lexical tradition also offers “driven away from both sides,” a narrower reading that should not replace the clear oscillation and instability without evidence.

Brief root meaning: Movement and oscillation between directions or states, without a settled or stable position.

Meaning of Qur’anic mudhabdhabīna: People in a state of wavering and unstable allegiance; in context, hypocrites with no clear, lasting attachment to either group.

Translation proposal: “Wavering and unsettled,” or “unsteady and unstable.”

In the full translation of Al-Nisāʾ 4:143, “wavering and unsettled between that” conveys the word. The following phrase, “neither toward these nor toward those,” supplies the Qur’an’s own explanation of the wavering’s direction and sphere. Fear, self-interest, and hypocrisy need not be added to the direct meaning of this one word.
