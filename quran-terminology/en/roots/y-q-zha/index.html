Wakefulness, alertness, and care: apparent wakefulness and actual sleep in the Qur’an

In its familiar Arabic sense, Y Q Ẓ denotes being awake rather than asleep. Someone who has awakened or is awake is called yaqiẓ or yaqẓān; istayqaẓa and tayaqqaẓa can express waking from sleep.

Early Arabic gives the family wider uses. Yaqiẓ can describe an alert, attentive, perceptive person who notices what happens around them. Figurative uses also speak of awakening dust or the sound of jewelry.

Bodily wakefulness and intellectual alertness nevertheless differ. Someone may be physically awake yet unaware of an event, or mistake a truth despite being awake.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as ayqāẓan in Al-Kahf 18:18. It is the plural of yaqiẓ and stands against ruqūd:

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun

“You would think them awake, while they are asleep.”

This contrast establishes wakefulness rather than sleep as the contextual meaning, without necessarily implying spiritual awakening, religious awareness, or intelligence.

1. Basic meaning and principal forms

Yaqaẓah: wakefulness rather than sleep

Early dictionaries give this basic definition:

الْيَقَظَةُ نَقِيضُ النَّوْمِ

al-yaqaẓatu naqīḍu al-nawmi

Meaning: “Wakefulness is the opposite of sleep.”

Lexical definitions also record attentive and alert for yaqiẓ and yaquẓ alongside wakefulness.

These uses are related but should be distinguished.

In the first, wakefulness is a state opposed to sleep: the person is not asleep and can engage with the surroundings in the ordinary waking way.

In the second, wakefulness extends to attention and care. An awake person may miss something through inattention; yaqiẓ in this use describes someone attentive and quick to notice.

Lexical explanations also associate yaqiẓ and yaquẓ with maʿrifah and fiṭnah: recognition and perceptiveness.

The attested core is thus wakefulness, with alertness and care as related extended uses.

Forms and derivatives

Yaqiẓa yayqaẓu: he awoke or was awake. Dictionaries also record other forms, including yaquẓa.

Yaqaẓah and yaqāẓah: nouns or verbal nouns for wakefulness and alertness.

Yaqiẓ and yaquẓ: awake, attentive, or perceptive. Lexicographers record the vowel variation in qāf.

Yaqẓān: someone awake; in some contexts, alert and attentive.

Yaqẓā: the feminine of yaqẓān.

Ayqāẓ: plural of yaqiẓ, awake people. This form occurs in the Qur’an.

Yiqāẓ: a recorded plural of yaqẓān.

Yaqāẓā: a feminine plural of yaqẓā.

Ayqaẓa: he awakened someone from sleep or made them aware.

Īqāẓ: verbal noun of ayqaẓa, awakening someone.

Yaqqaẓa and tayqīẓ: awakening or rousing another.

Tayaqqaẓa: he awoke, noticed something, or became alert.

Istayqaẓa: he awoke from sleep; nonphysical uses can mean becoming aware or attentive.

Istayqaẓahu: some sources record it for awakening someone, although ayqaẓahu is the more familiar causative.

Qur’anic ayqāẓ is a broken plural of an adjective, not the verbal noun īqāẓ or a Form IV verb. Similar spelling does not give these forms the same function.

Differing analyses of the plural

One transmitted morphological analysis distinguishes the singular underlying ayqāẓ.

It treats ayqāẓ as the plural of yaqiẓ, rather than directly of yaquẓ, because the faʿil adjective pattern is more common than faʿul and more productive in broken plurals.

Another analysis likewise distinguishes ayqāẓ as the plural of yaqiẓ from yiqāẓ as the plural of yaqẓān.

Some interpreters instead give both yaqiẓ and yaqẓān as singular explanations of ayqāẓ.

The morphological question does not change the meaning in Al-Kahf 18:18: both analyses concern people who are awake.

2. From bodily wakefulness to attention and care

Alertness and perceptiveness

Rajulun yaqiẓun can describe a person who carefully follows events and notices dangers or opportunities.

Tayaqqaẓa li-al-amri means becoming attentive to or aware of a matter.

This does not require having been physically asleep. The person may have been awake throughout, while lacking knowledge of the matter or failing to grasp its significance.

Someone who notices signs of danger and becomes more watchful can thus be described figuratively as mutayaqqiẓ.

Such alertness is not a necessary meaning of every derivative.

In the Qur’anic ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūd, the explicit contrast with ruqūd gives wakefulness rather than sleep priority over extended uses.

Sound awakening

Poetic Arabic uses istayqaẓa al-khalkhālu or istayqaẓa al-ḥalyu: the anklet or jewelry made a sound.

Jewelry does not literally sleep or awaken.

When movement diminishes or its sound ceases, figurative language can call it asleep. Renewed movement and sound can then be called awakening.

The extension from stillness and silence to movement and sound is understandable, while human sleep must remain distinct from poetic descriptions of objects.

Awakening dust: an early lexical dispute

One lexical explanation says:

أَيْقَظْتُ الْغُبَارَ: أَثَرْتُهُ

ayqaẓtu al-ghubāra athartuhu

Meaning: “I awakened the dust: I stirred it up.”

Other lexical transmissions also record this use.

An early lexical critique objects: the writer does not recall yaqqaẓa or ayqaẓa meaning scattering earth and suspects a copying error. He proposes baqqaṭa al-turāba as the correct wording.

The disagreement matters.

On one side, awakening dust is recorded in dictionaries and subsequently used in rhetorical works.

On the other, an early critique explicitly questions its validity.

This disputed witness cannot establish that the root’s original, certain meaning was stirring dispersed particles or setting anything still in motion.

Wakefulness rather than sleep remains the firmer definition; stirring earth and dust should be presented as recorded but disputed.

3. Arabic poetic witnesses

Ayqāẓ in poetry attributed to Ruʾbah or al-ʿAjjāj

An interpretation of Al-Kahf 18:18 cites this rajaz:

وَوَجَدُوا إِخْوَتَهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا
وَسَيْفَ غَيَّاظٍ لَهُمْ غَيَّاظًا

wa-wajadū ikhwatahum ayqāẓan / wa-sayfa ghayyāẓin lahum ghayyāẓan

Meaning: “They found their brothers awake, and Ghayyāẓ’s sword was fierce and hurtful to them.”

Ayqāẓan describes a group of people and their wakefulness.

The textual attribution matters: the interpretive citation does not definitively identify a poet. Some lexical transmissions assign it to Ruʾbah, while it also appears among additions to the collected poetry of his father, al-ʿAjjāj.

Examination of these transmissions notes that the father’s and son’s poetry has sometimes become mixed.

It is therefore more precise to call the verse attributed to Ruʾbah or al-ʿAjjāj.

Both belong to the Islamic period, so it should not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

Sleep and wakefulness in Ṭurayḥ al-Thaqafī’s poetry

Ṭurayḥ ibn Ismāʿīl al-Thaqafī says:

نَامَتْ خَلَاخِلُهَا وَجَالَ وِشَاحُهَا
وَجَرَى الْوِشَاحُ عَلَى كَثِيبٍ أَهْيَلِ

nāmat khalākhiluhā wa-jāla wishāḥuhā / wa-jarā al-wishāḥu ʿalā kathībin ahyali

“Her anklets fell silent and her sash moved about; the sash flowed over a mound of loose, soft sand [an image for her body].”

The following line says:

فَاسْتَيْقَظَتْ مِنْهُ قَلَائِدُهَا الَّتِي
عُقِدَتْ عَلَى جِيدِ الْغَزَالِ الْأَكْحَلِ

fa-istayqaẓat minhu qalāʾiduhā allatī / ʿuqidat ʿalā jīdi al-ghazāli al-akḥali

“Then her necklaces, fastened around the neck of that dark-eyed gazelle, awoke into movement and sound.”

The image calls the anklets’ silence sleep and the necklaces’ sound awakening.

It illustrates semantic extension: states of stillness and activity are described through sleeping and waking.

It does not mean every Qur’anic ayqāẓ denotes the movement or activity of objects or people.

Ṭurayḥ was an Umayyad poet, so his verse attests usage after the Qur’an’s revelation.

Alertness and heedlessness in al-Farazdaq

A verse of al-Farazdaq is cited in explaining extended uses:

يَسْتَيْقِظُونَ إِلَى نُهَاقِ حَمِيرِهِمْ
وَتَنَامُ أَعْيُنُهُمْ عَنِ الْأَوْتَارِ

yastayqiẓūna ilā nuhāqi ḥamīrihim / wa-tanāmu aʿyunuhum ʿani al-awtāri

Its sense: “They wake to their donkeys’ braying, while their eyes sleep concerning blood claims and the obligations they entail.”

Bodily wakefulness is placed against heedlessness about another matter.

The point is not that they awaken at an animal’s sound and then biologically fall asleep again. The poet criticizes attention to some matters and indifference to others.

Sleeping and waking thus extend from physical states to attention and inattention.

Al-Farazdaq was an Umayyad poet; this witness likewise is not pre-Qur’anic.

Waking and sleeping dust in heroic poetry

A heroic poetic witness says:

إِذَا نَحْنُ سِرْنَا بَيْنَ شَرْقٍ وَمَغْرِبٍ
تَحَرَّكَ يَقْظَانُ التُّرَابِ وَنَائِمُهُ

idhā naḥnu sirnā bayna sharqin wa-maghribin / taḥarraka yaqẓānu al-turābi wa-nāʾimuhu

Meaning: “When we travel between east and west, the waking earth and its sleeping earth both stir.”

Waking and sleeping earth are poetic descriptions evoked by the extensive movement of travelers or warriors.

Waking earth can be explained as earth already stirred by traffic; sleeping earth as what has not yet been moved.

Literary collections differ over attribution and some wording. It illustrates metaphor but should not bear excessive weight in establishing the root’s original meaning or a certain date of composition.

What the witnesses establish

The poems show three uses: actual human wakefulness, attention rather than heedlessness, and figurative descriptions of objects and movement.

This study has not established a securely attributed and worded pre-Islamic poetic witness decisive for the root’s meaning.

That limitation should remain explicit. Umayyad witnesses attest early Arabic usage, but do not alone date a meaning to before the Qur’an.

4. Qur’anic ayqāẓan in Al-Kahf 18:18

Al-Kahf 18:18

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ ۚ وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ ۖ وَكَلْبُهُم بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ بِالْوَصِيدِ ۚ لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli wa-kalbuhum bāsiṭun dhirāʿayhi bi-al-waṣīdi lawi iṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim la-wallayta minhum firāran wa-la-muliʾta minhum ruʿban

Forqan’s installed Persian translation is equivalent to:

“You would think them awake, while they are asleep. We turn them to the right and left, and their dog stretches its forelegs at the threshold. Had you looked upon them, you would have fled and been filled with fear of them.”

Its rendering of ayqāẓan is correct and requires no change.

Morphology and syntax

Ayqāẓan is the broken plural of yaqiẓ, following afʿāl.

It is indefinite and accusative here.

Taḥsabu is a verb of supposition and can take two objects.

Hum in taḥsabuhum is the first object, and ayqāẓan the second.

The construction thus means:

“You would think them awake.”

It continues:

وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ

wa-hum ruqūdun

A nominal circumstantial clause states their actual condition:

“While they are asleep.”

Ruqūd belongs to R Q D, rather than Y Q Ẓ, and is a plural of rāqid.

The clauses form a clear contrast:

  • Taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan: what an observer supposes.
  • Wa-hum ruqūd: what is actually stated about them.

The verse thus distinguishes an observer’s impression from the people’s actual condition.

5. Why did they appear awake?

Early interpretations ask this question and give different answers.

First possibility: open eyes

Some interpreters say the companions’ eyes remained open during sleep, leading an observer to think them awake.

Interpretive reports record this explanation.

In ordinary observation, open eyes can be a sign that misleads someone about wakefulness.

The verse itself, however, does not state that their eyes were open.

This is an interpretive possibility for the observer’s impression, rather than an explicit account of the companions’ bodily condition.

Second possibility: movement and turning

Another explanation connects their being turned right and left with appearing awake.

It draws on the next phrase:

وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāli

“We turn them to the right and left.”

Caution is again needed.

The verse explicitly says they were turned, without identifying that movement as the only or certain cause of appearing awake.

It specifies neither the amount, number, nor intervals of turning.

Some reports mention once or twice a year, but those numbers are not supplied by the Qur’an itself.

Assessing the possibilities

Both occur in interpretive tradition and neither plainly conflicts with the wording. Neither is established by ayqāẓan itself.

The verse gives two facts: a hypothetical observer would think them awake, while they are actually asleep.

Its explicit wording does not identify the precise cause of that impression.

Neither open eyes nor details of bodily movement should therefore enter the translation of ayqāẓan.

6. Connections with the companions’ story

Prolonged sleep and awakening

Al-Kahf 18:11 says:

فَضَرَبْنَا عَلَىٰ آذَانِهِمْ فِي الْكَهْفِ سِنِينَ عَدَدًا

fa-ḍarabnā ʿalā ādhānihim fī al-kahfi sinīna ʿadadan

“We drew a covering over their ears in the cave for a number of years.”

Al-Kahf 18:12 says:

ثُمَّ بَعَثْنَاهُمْ

thumma baʿathnāhum

“Then We roused them.” (Al-Kahf 18:12)

Al-Kahf 18:19 says:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ بَعَثْنَاهُمْ لِيَتَسَاءَلُوا بَيْنَهُمْ

wa-ka-dhālika baʿathnāhum li-yatasāʾalū baynahum

“Thus We roused them so that they might question one another.”

These phrases, together with wa-hum ruqūd in Al-Kahf 18:18, clarify their state in the story.

They slept for a long time, then reached a state in which they spoke together and asked about the length of their stay.

The verb is baʿatha, not istayqaẓa; it requires independent lexical examination.

The story nevertheless connects rousing them here with the end of their sleep.

Baʿathnāhum alone does not necessarily report death and resurrection in the sense of bodily life ending, particularly when Al-Kahf 18:18 calls them ruqūd, asleep.

Appearance and reality

Wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūd clearly illustrates the difference between impression and reality.

An outside observer may suppose a condition different from the actual one.

This does not make ayqāẓ itself mean deception, false appearance, or mistaken impression.

The distinction comes from taḥsabuhum contrasted with wa-hum ruqūd.

Without that contrast in another sentence, ayqāẓ can simply denote people actually awake.

Supposed wakefulness belongs to this sentence, rather than the root’s inherent meaning.

Guidance and steadfastness

Al-Kahf 18:13 introduces the companions as youths who believed in their Lord.

Al-Kahf 18:14 describes their hearts being strengthened as they stood and declared their belief.

A separate reflection can connect their stand against their people’s beliefs with their preservation in the cave.

Those qualities of faith should not enter the lexical meaning of ayqāẓ.

In Al-Kahf 18:18 they are physically asleep, although earlier verses commend their faith and firmness.

Their belief and guidance do not make ayqāẓ necessarily mean spiritually awake here. The sentence does not support that as the direct meaning.

7. Related words and roots

Y Q Ẓ and N W M: wakefulness and sleep

Nawm is the familiar name for sleep.

Basic dictionary definitions set yaqaẓah against it.

Al-Baqarah 2:255 says:

لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ

lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawmun

“Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him.”

Yaqaẓah does not occur there, but the verse is useful for sleep and its distinction from drowsiness.

Nawm and yaqaẓah form a lexical contrast, while actual experience also includes dozing, drowsiness, and gradual awakening.

The lexical opposition should not be made into a scientific theory that sleeping and waking are always wholly separate states.

Study of N W M

Y Q Ẓ and R Q D: wakefulness and repose

Ruqūd stands against ayqāẓ in Al-Kahf 18:18.

It denotes sleep in that sentence.

Arabic R Q D also concerns repose and stillness and can sometimes describe prolonged sleep.

Ruqūd itself supplies no numerical duration.

Other verses in the story discuss the length of the companions’ stay; it is not encoded in this word alone.

Y Q Ẓ and N B H: waking and drawing attention

Intabaha and nabbaha concern noticing and making someone attentive.

Dictionaries use nabbahahu to explain ayqaẓahu.

The families are nevertheless distinct.

Someone can be alerted to news without having been asleep. This is the wider sense of drawing attention.

Ayqaẓahu min nawmihi clearly means awakening someone from sleep.

The object and context determine the meaning.

Y Q Ẓ and F Ṭ N: alertness and perceptiveness

Fiṭnah concerns quick understanding and perception.

A lexical explanation places yaqiẓ beside faṭin.

Perceptiveness and bodily wakefulness are not synonyms.

A highly perceptive person may show no conscious response to a surrounding event while asleep. An awake person may also grasp a matter slowly.

Alertness is an established extension of yaqiẓ, rather than a meaning to substitute for wakefulness everywhere.

Y Q Ẓ and GH F L: alertness and heedlessness

Ghaflah concerns inattention or unawareness.

Arabic and Persian sometimes use waking for awareness and sleeping for heedlessness.

This imagery should not change the direct meaning in Al-Kahf.

Al-Kahf 18:18 describes the companions as asleep, but their sleep does not mean moral or religious heedlessness.

Conversely, someone awake may be heedless of truth.

This also matters for reflection: bodily sleep proves no spiritual heedlessness, just as bodily wakefulness guarantees neither insight nor ethical attentiveness.

Study of GH F L

Y Q Ẓ and Y Q N: wakefulness and certainty

Y Q N is a separate root concerning certainty and assurance of knowledge.

Sharing the first two radicals does not make the meanings identical.

Someone may be awake and attentive while remaining uncertain about a matter.

Ayqāẓan in Al-Kahf 18:18 cannot therefore be translated as people of certainty or those who have attained certainty.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Awake people” is lexically accurate for the sole Qur’anic form.

In the sentence, the natural rendering is:

“You would think them awake, while they are asleep.”

This corresponds to Forqan’s installed Persian rendering and preserves the meaning without adding interpretation.

A word box should keep the following elements distinct:

A fuller explanation can describe the contrast between apparent wakefulness and actual sleep, but that belongs to the verse’s construction rather than ayqāẓ alone.

Spiritual awakening, awareness of truth, political vigilance, people of certainty, or safeguarding religion should not enter a literal translation without supporting context.

9. Conclusion

Early Arabic Y Q Ẓ concerns wakefulness rather than sleep. Yaqaẓah names that state, yaqiẓ and yaqẓān describe someone awake, and ayqaẓa means awakening another.

Extended uses concern alertness, attention, care, and perceptiveness. Poetry clearly attests some figurative descriptions, such as the sound of jewelry awakening.

Stirring earth and dust with this root, although recorded in some dictionaries, has been questioned in an early lexical critique. It should not be presented as the certain origin of every meaning.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as ayqāẓan in Al-Kahf 18:18.

It is the plural of yaqiẓ, meaning awake people. Ruqūd and taḥsabuhum establish a contrast between the companions’ apparent wakefulness and actual sleep.

Interpretations explain the appearance through open eyes or bodily movement, but the Qur’an itself does not identify the precise cause.

Lexical study should preserve wakefulness and avoid unsupported detail about the companions’ sleep or the story’s messages of faith.

A proposed final definition for Forqan:

Y Q Ẓ primarily denotes wakefulness rather than sleep, with extended uses for alertness, attention, and care. Ayqāẓan in Al-Kahf 18:18 means awake people. The companions appeared awake although they were actually asleep; the verse does not identify the precise cause of that appearance.
