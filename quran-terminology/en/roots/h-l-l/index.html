1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic Ḥ L L has several important uses: untying a knot, opening something bound, removing a restriction, an act becoming permissible, alighting and settling somewhere, and something reaching its appointed place or time.

Physically, ḥalla al-ʿuqdata means untying a knot: a tied rope is released from its binding. In prescriptions and conduct, ḥalāl concerns what is not prohibited or may permissibly be done or used. Spatially, ḥalla bi-al-makāni means alighting and residing in a place.

These recognized uses cannot all take one translation. Untying a knot, lawful food, punishment descending, and a ritual offering reaching its destination are distinct events despite their shared root.

The Qur’an uses this root 51 times in 43 verses, distributed across eight morphological groups: fourteen Form I verbs and twenty Form IV verbs, together with ḥill, ḥalāl, ḥalāʾil, maḥill, taḥillah, and muḥillī.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: counts and forms

Four broad Qur’anic fields can be distinguished: opening and untying; lawfulness and prohibition; alighting and reaching a destination; and removal of a restriction or obligation.

Lawfulness concerns more than food. The Qur’an also uses this family for marriage, trade, pilgrimage, oaths, financial rights, religious prescriptions, and human conduct.

Conversely, a derivative of Ḥ L L need not mean religious lawfulness. Yaḥillu ʿalayhi ʿadhābun concerns punishment descending upon someone, rather than becoming permissible.

2. The semantic core and important forms

Opening something that was bound

Ibn Fāris proposes one semantic core for this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْحَاءُ وَاللَّامُ لَهُ فُرُوعٌ كَثِيرَةٌ وَمَسَائِلُ، وَأَصْلُهَا كُلُّهَا عِنْدِي فَتْحُ الشَّيْءِ، لَا يَشُذُّ عَنْهُ شَيْءٌ

al-ḥāʾu wa-al-lāmu lahu furūʿun kathīratun wa-masāʾilu wa-aṣluhā kulluhā ʿindī fatḥu al-shayʾi lā yashudhdhu ʿanhu shayʾun

“Ḥāʾ and lām have many branches and applications; in my view their common foundation is opening something, with no exception.”

He illustrates this with untying a knot, connecting lawfulness with removal of restriction and opening.

He explains alighting through a traveler binding their load and undoing it on arrival, thus relating opening to settling.

This analysis helps relate the senses, provided its limits remain clear.

When ḥalla concerns punishment descending, the verse says nothing about unpacking a traveler’s load. Nor does lawful food require imagining a physical knot undone in that food.

Opening can supply a shared image; each established usage must still be understood independently.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ḥ L L

A physical image: untying a knot

Imagine a load tied with rope. While the knot is fastened, the rope holds the load together and restricts its parts’ free movement.

Untying removes that restriction. Rope and load may remain, while their binding relationship changes.

Arabic calls this ḥallu al-ʿuqdati, untying the knot.

Untying need not destroy anything: the knot can be undone without cutting or destroying the rope.

It also differs from breaking an object. Breaking may separate by damaging its parts; untying can remove the binding while preserving them.

Uses concerning removal of a restriction, release from an obligation, and the end of a limitation become intelligible through this field.

Important Arabic forms

Transitive ḥalla yaḥullu means opening or untying, as with a knot. Intransitive and other constructions concern alighting, settling, arriving, or becoming permissible.

Ḥall is a verbal noun or noun concerning opening. In some uses it also concerns removal of a constraint and freedom from restriction.

Ḥill, opposed to ḥarām, describes what is permissible or free of the relevant prohibition. This form occurs in the Qur’an.

Ḥalāl concerns what is permissible and not prohibited. It may describe a thing or express its status in contrast with ḥarām.

Form IV aḥalla yuḥillu means declaring or making something permissible in prescriptive usage. Elsewhere it means causing to alight or placing somewhere.

Ḥalīl names a spouse in the field of shared life. The plural of feminine ḥalīlah, ḥalāʾil, occurs in the Qur’an.

Maḥill concerns an appointed place or time something reaches, or where a condition ends. Its Qur’anic uses concern pilgrimage rites.

Taḥillah is a verbal noun concerning opening or removing a constraint. In the Qur’an it relates to release from an oath’s obligation.

Muḥill is a Form IV active participle. In prescriptive contexts it concerns someone treating something as permissible or removing a prohibited status.

Ḥāll is an active participle of ḥalla in the sense of alighting or residing. Its morphology must not be equated with ḥill meaning permissible.

Spatial maḥallah names a place where people alight and reside.

Ḥullah names a suit of garments, commonly consisting of two pieces.

In obligations and pilgrimage, taḥallala concerns release from a constraint or departure from a restricted state.

Form II ḥallala concerns declaring permissible, opening, or removing a constraint, and later acquired wider technical uses.

Taḥlīl is the Form II verbal noun. In religious and legal contexts it concerns declaring permissible or lifting a prohibition; modern Arabic and Persian also use it for examining and analyzing a subject.

This modern meaning must not automatically enter Qur’anic translation. Aḥalla Allāhu al-bayʿa does not mean The God scientifically analyzed trade; it states its lawfulness.

3. Early Arabic usage

Untying a knot and considering an obligation’s consequences

Arabic records the following proverb:

يَا عَاقِدُ اذْكُرْ حَلًّا

yā ʿāqidu udhkur ḥallan

“You who tie a knot, remember its untying.”

Another transmission reads:

يَا حَابِلُ اذْكُرْ حَلًّا

yā ḥābilu udhkur ḥallan

“You who bind with rope, remember its untying.”

The proverb concerns considering the outcome and consequences of binding something.

For example, binding a camel’s load too tightly can make undoing it difficult or harm the load or animal.

Ḥall means opening or untying here, while the physical image illustrates a practical principle: when creating a bond, consider whether and how it can be undone.

Lexical accounts differ over the precise wording: some transmit yā ʿāqidu, others yā ḥābilu. Neither should be presented without qualification as the sole established pre-Islamic form.

Lisān al-ʿArab: variants of the proverb

Alighting and residing

Ḥalla al-qawmu bi-al-makāni means a group alighted in a place.

Nomadic life illustrates this: people travel with bound loads, undoing them when they arrive and settle.

Maḥallah, a place of settlement, belongs to this field.

A verse of the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā reads:

لَقَدْ كَانَ فِي شَيْبَانَ لَوْ كُنْتَ عَالِمًا
قِبَابٌ وَحَيٌّ حِلَّةٌ وَقَبَائِلُ

la-qad kāna fī shaybāna law kunta ʿāliman / qibābun wa-ḥayyun ḥillatun wa-qabāʾilu

“Had you known, among Shaybān there were tents, a settled community, and many clans.”

Ḥillah here names a group that has alighted at a dwelling place.

It means neither clothing nor lawfulness: the verse concerns people and their settlement.

Early lexical sources record this use, while discussing variant transmissions and explanations. Ḥillah with initial i concerns a settled group; ḥullah with initial u names clothing. Ibn Barrī corrected the ending to wa-qabāʾilu because the poem rhymes in lām, distinguishing it from another poetic witness ending wa-darāhimu.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥillah and Ibn Barrī’s correction

Lawfulness and inviolability in social relations

The opposition between ḥill and ḥarām was known in Arabic before the Qur’an.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s Muʿallaqah reads:

جَعَلْنَ الْقَنَانَ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَحَزْنَهُ
وَكَمْ بِالْقَنَانِ مِنْ مُحِلٍّ وَمُحْرِمِ

jaʿalna al-qanāna ʿan yamīnin wa-ḥaznahu / wa-kam bi-al-qanāni min muḥillin wa-muḥrimi

“They kept al-Qanān and its rugged ground on their right; how many there at al-Qanān were in a state of ḥill or inviolability!”

Early explanations of muḥill and muḥrim here differ.

One reading concerns people considered open to attack and those protected by inviolability.

Another concerns lawful and sacred months, or entering and leaving a protected state.

Their differences show the opposition’s social and legal presence while leaving this verse’s precise referent disputed.

A single explanation cannot establish that muḥill necessarily means a person without a covenant, or necessarily someone outside ritual consecration.

Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: Zuhayr’s witness

This witness matters for Qur’anic understanding: lawfulness and prohibition were not confined to food. Inviolability could concern persons, times, places, and conduct toward others.

Ḥalīl and ḥalīlah: spouses and shared residence

Arabic uses ḥalīl for a husband and ḥalīlah for a wife.

Two explanations relate this naming to spouses residing together, or to a relationship permitted to them within marriage but prohibited to others.

Ibn Fāris links the first to settling beside one another and also transmits the second explanation.

Abū ʿUbayd adds that someone who alights beside a person and becomes their neighbor can also be called ḥalīl.

Its lexical basis should therefore not be reduced without examination to sexual relations: residence, companionship, and marriage are related fields.

Qur’anic plural ḥalāʾil concerns sons’ wives, with the context identifying the marital relationship.

4. Qur’anic opening, alighting, and the end of restrictions

A. Wa-uḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī: untying a knot

Moses’ prayer provides a clear use:

وَاحْلُلْ عُقْدَةً مِنْ لِسَانِي

wa-uḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī

“And untie a knot from my tongue.”

Ṭā Hā 20:27

Uḥlul is a Form I imperative with ʿuqdatan as its object.

The verse illustrates the root’s fundamental meaning through the opposed concepts of a knot and its untying.

ʿUqdah belongs to ʿ Q D, binding and knotting; uḥlul to Ḥ L L, undoing that binding.

Study of ʿ Q D

Ṭā Hā 20:28 states the prayer’s purpose: that the audience understand Moses’ speech.

The tongue’s knot therefore concerns difficulty communicating intelligibly.

Uḥlul itself does not mean eloquence, or making people understand. Untying is requested; understanding is the result sought in the next verse.

Ḥall can thus concern removing an impediment without religious permission or prohibition being at issue.

B. Wa-idhā ḥalaltum fa-iṣṭādū: leaving consecration

After mentioning sacred rites and certain ritual limits, Al-Māʾidah 5:2 permits hunting once the state of consecration has ended.

Ḥalaltum is a Form I perfect verb.

Here it means leaving consecration and lifting the restrictions belonging to that state.

Entering consecration, its restrictions, and the end of that state must be distinguished.

Ḥalaltum concerns the third.

The knot image helps: a condition previously imposing restrictions has ended.

That image must not be imposed on every use of Ḥ L L. Here the technical sense of leaving consecration comes from pilgrimage and hunting’s context.

The subsequent instruction to hunt does not make hunting obligatory: removal of prohibition differs from a binding command to perform the act.

Ending one restriction does not automatically remove others, including sacred times or places and other people’s rights.

C. Maḥill: an appointed ritual destination or time

Maḥill occurs three times: Al-Baqarah 2:196, Al-Ḥajj 22:33, and Al-Fatḥ 48:25.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 concerns an offering reaching its destination; Al-Fatḥ 48:25 concerns an offering prevented from reaching it.

Al-Ḥajj reads:

لَكُمْ فِيهَا مَنَافِعُ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ثُمَّ مَحِلُّهَا إِلَى الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ

lakum fīhā manāfiʿu ilā ajalin musamman thumma maḥilluhā ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīqi

“In them you have benefits until an appointed time; then their destination is toward the Ancient House.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:33

Maḥilluhā concerns the appointed end or destination of the matter under discussion.

Lexically, maḥill may concern the place or time something is realized or a restriction ends.

In offerings and pilgrimage rites, it concerns something reaching its ritual destination.

The word alone cannot establish every ritual detail, method, or precise geographical limit.

Ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīqi independently supplies direction or destination and must not be absorbed into maḥill’s lexical definition.

Some early explanations relate maḥill here to the place of the offering’s rite; others to the completion of pilgrimage stages. The precise referent depends on the subject and the pronoun’s antecedent.

Al-Ṭabarī: the pronoun’s referent and maḥill

“Appointed destination” or “place of arrival” may preserve the basic meaning; ritual explanation belongs in reflection.

D. Ḥalla ʿalayhi: descending and arriving

Several verses combine ḥalla with punishment or wrath.

Hūd 11:39 and Al-Zumar 39:40 describe enduring punishment descending upon people.

Ṭā Hā 20:81 warns that overstepping in wholesome provisions may bring divine wrath.

Ṭā Hā 20:86 similarly concerns wrath from the Lord.

Ḥalla ʿalā here concerns something reaching, descending upon, or resting upon someone.

Its subject is punishment or wrath, rather than something becoming permissible.

Hūd 11:39 describes the punishment as muqīm, enduring, consistent with alighting and establishment.

Ṭā Hā 20:81 concerns overstepping’s consequence: wrath descends upon someone, rather than an act being declared lawful.

Verb form alone therefore does not settle the meaning: preposition, subject, and context matter.

E. Taḥullu qarīban min dārihim: alighting nearby

Al-Raʿd 13:31 describes a calamity striking people because of their deeds, or alighting near their homes. Another explanation takes the verb’s subject as the addressee, the Prophet: you alight near their homes. This concerns the subject’s identification, rather than the basic meaning of alighting.

Taḥullu is accompanied by qarīban min dārihim, “near their homes.”

It concerns alighting or being situated near a dwelling place.

On the reading with calamity as subject, this illustrates nonhuman alighting; on the reading addressing the Prophet, human alighting is meant.

Neither reading makes taḥullu mean the calamity becoming lawful. The concern is arrival or alighting rather than permission.

Al-Ṭabarī: the reading addressing the Prophet in taḥullu

F. Aḥallū qawmahum dāra al-bawāri: causing others to alight

Ibrāhīm reads:

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ بَدَّلُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ كُفْرًا وَأَحَلُّوا قَوْمَهُمْ دَارَ الْبَوَارِ

a-lam tara ilā alladhīna baddalū niʿmata Allāhi kufran wa-aḥallū qawmahum dāra al-bawāri

“Have you not seen those who exchanged The God’s favor for disbelief and brought their people into the abode of ruin?”

Ibrāhīm 14:28

Aḥallū is Form IV.

Here it means causing to alight or placing somewhere, rather than declaring lawful.

Qawmahum is its object; dāra al-bawāri is the destination into which they led their people.

Ibrāhīm 14:29 identifies this abode with Hell.

“Brought their people into the abode of ruin” fits the construction and context.

Morphologically, aḥalla therefore need not mean making lawful: spatially it can mean causing to alight or settling.

Fāṭir 35:35 uses the same form when the Garden’s people say The God settled them in the abode of lasting residence.

The destinations and outcomes differ: ruin in one verse, residence and ease in the other.

Aḥalla carries no fixed positive or negative value by itself; destination and context identify the outcome.

5. Lawfulness, prohibition, and authority

A. Ḥalālan ṭayyiban: lawfulness and wholesomeness

Al-Baqarah reads:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ كُلُوا مِمَّا فِي الْأَرْضِ حَلَالًا طَيِّبًا وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا خُطُوَاتِ الشَّيْطَانِ إِنَّهُ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ مُبِينٌ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu kulū mimmā fī al-arḍi ḥalālan ṭayyiban wa-lā tattabiʿū khuṭuwāti al-shayṭāni innahu lakum ʿaduwwun mubīnun

“O people, eat what is lawful and wholesome on earth, and do not follow Satan’s footsteps; he is a clear enemy to you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:168

Ḥalālan and ṭayyiban occur together.

Ḥalāl belongs to Ḥ L L, concerning permissibility and absence of prohibition.

Ṭayyib belongs to Ṭ Y B, concerning purity, goodness, and agreeableness.

Their evaluative fields meet without lexical identity.

Translating both merely as “good” obscures their distinction. Making ḥalāl directly mean medically healthy instead substitutes a possible concern of wholesomeness for religious permission.

The pairing recurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:88 and Al-Naḥl 16:114; Al-Anfāl 8:69 similarly concerns eating lawful and wholesome gains.

The Qur’an thus mentions the food’s goodness and wholesomeness alongside absence of prohibition.

Their pairing alone does not establish that personal dislike creates a divine prohibition for everyone.

Food accepted in one culture may be disliked in another; an individual may also avoid it because of sensitivity or health.

These differ from proclaiming a general prohibition in The God’s name.

Lawfulness neither requires consuming nor liking a food. Someone may avoid permissible food without claiming The God prohibited it.

B. Aḥalla Allāhu al-bayʿa: permission in trade

Al-Baqarah reads:

الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ الرِّبَا لَا يَقُومُونَ إِلَّا كَمَا يَقُومُ الَّذِي يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا الْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ الرِّبَا وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا فَمَنْ جَاءَهُ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ فَانْتَهَىٰ فَلَهُ مَا سَلَفَ وَأَمْرُهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

alladhīna yaʾkulūna al-ribā lā yaqūmūna illā ka-mā yaqūmu alladhī yatakhabbaṭuhu al-shayṭānu mina al-massi dhālika bi-annahum qālū innamā al-bayʿu mithlu al-ribā wa-aḥalla Allāhu al-bayʿa wa-ḥarrama al-ribā fa-man jāʾahu mawʿiẓatun min rabbihi fa-intahā fa-lahu mā salafa wa-amruhu ilā Allāhi wa-man ʿāda fa-ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūna

“Those who consume ribā rise only as one whom Satan has confounded through touch. That is because they say trade is like ribā, whereas The God has permitted trade and prohibited ribā. Whoever receives an admonition from their Lord and desists may retain what is past, and their affair rests with The God. Whoever returns: those are the Fire’s people, remaining there.”

Al-Baqarah 2:275

Aḥalla and ḥarrama stand opposed.

Their respective objects are trade and ribā.

Lawfulness and prohibition extend beyond food to economic relationships.

The paired prescriptions also distinguish trade from ribā, which the statement earlier in the verse equates.

Aḥalla alone does not define valid transactions or which increase constitutes ribā. Those require the relevant verses and separate studies of B Y ʿ and R B W.

Study of B Y ʿ; Study of R B W

Here aḥalla expresses trade’s permissibility and ḥarrama ribā’s prohibition.

C. Is everything lawful obligatory?

An act’s being lawful does not alone make it obligatory.

Al-Baqarah 2:275 permits trade without requiring everyone to transact in every circumstance.

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 permits sea game and its food without thereby obliging people to hunt.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 permits sexual relations during fasting nights, lifting a restriction without alone commanding every person to engage in them each night.

Permission must therefore be distinguished from obligation.

An imperative may accompany a declaration of lawfulness; its force and limits require separate examination.

Ḥalāl concerns an act’s permissible status; obligation, responsibility, conditions, and timing come from other elements.

D. Do not prohibit the wholesome things The God permitted

The Qur’an warns against prohibiting what The God permitted as well as against prohibited acts:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُحَرِّمُوا طَيِّبَاتِ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla Allāhu lakum wa-lā taʿtadū inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīna

“O you who believe, do not prohibit the wholesome things The God has permitted you, and do not transgress; The God does not love transgressors.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:87

Aḥalla Allāhu concerns what The God has permitted.

Lā tuḥarrimū addresses believers.

Personal abstention differs from declaring a prohibition in The God’s name.

One person may avoid a food’s flavor, another avoid it through illness or sensitivity.

Neither circumstance alone makes the food divinely prohibited for everyone.

Turning personal preference or caution into a general rule attributed to The God moves from choice to a claim of legislation.

The verse also includes wa-lā taʿtadū and ends with inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīna. This combination emphasizes respecting divine limits concerning lawfulness and prohibition.

Iʿtidāʾ nevertheless has its independent meaning and must not be inserted into aḥalla’s lexical definition.

E. Authority to determine lawfulness and prohibition

Yūnus reads:

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ مِنْ رِزْقٍ فَجَعَلْتُمْ مِنْهُ حَرَامًا وَحَلَالًا قُلْ آللَّهُ أَذِنَ لَكُمْ أَمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ تَفْتَرُونَ

qul a-raʾaytum mā anzala Allāhu lakum min rizqin fa-jaʿaltum minhu ḥarāman wa-ḥalālan qul āllāhu adhina lakum am ʿalā Allāhi taftarūna

“Say: have you considered the provision The God sent down for you, of which you made some prohibited and some lawful? Say: has The God authorized you, or do you fabricate against The God?”

Yūnus 10:59

This clearly addresses authority to determine lawfulness and prohibition.

The issue goes beyond what people eat: by whose authorization have they classified something as permitted or prohibited?

Āllāhu adhina lakum explicitly raises divine authorization.

Al-Naḥl also reads:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ لَا يُفْلِحُونَ

wa-lā taqūlū li-mā taṣifu alsinatukumu al-kadhiba hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun li-taftarū ʿalā Allāhi al-kadhiba inna alladhīna yaftarūna ʿalā Allāhi al-kadhiba lā yufliḥūna

“Do not say, through the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and this prohibited,’ fabricating lies against The God. Those who fabricate lies against The God do not prosper.”

Al-Naḥl 16:116

Attributing permission or prohibition to The God cannot rest on conjecture, custom, or personal desire.

In these settings the terms go beyond description: declaring them in The God’s name claims a divine prescription.

Al-Tawbah 9:37 also describes people permitting something one year and prohibiting it another to match their calculation of sacred months.

Making permissible or prohibited can thus concern time and social regulations as well as food.

Aḥalla here concerns making permissible; authority to legislate and a permission claim’s validity depend on its source and context.

These verses must not be used to attribute deliberate, knowing falsehood to everyone mistaken about a religious rule. Ignorance, flawed reasoning, and knowingly fabricating against The God differ.

F. Food and marriage

Āl ʿImrān 3:93 states that all food was lawful for the Children of Israel except what Israel prohibited for himself before the Torah’s revelation.

Ḥillan stands opposed to prohibiting.

The opposition distinguishes lawful food from personal abstention; the verse also distinguishes the period before the Torah from later prescriptions.

Al-Māʾidah 5:5 permits the food of the People of the Book to believers and believers’ food to them.

It also addresses marriage with muḥṣanāt women, extending the field beyond food.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 speaks of permission beyond specified marriage prohibitions while stating conditions for the relationship.

Lawfulness does not mean every associated behavior is unconditional. A relationship may be permissible while its proper realization requires limits and conditions.

Al-Baqarah 2:228, Al-Baqarah 2:229, and Al-Baqarah 2:230 use Form I yaḥillu or taḥillu for divorce, financial rights, and marital relationships.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:19 prohibits inheriting women against their will.

Yaḥillu can thus concern social conduct, human rights, and family relationships.

Translation must distinguish permission itself from each prescription’s particular conditions.

G. Ḥalāʾilu abnāʾikumu: sons’ wives

Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 combines ḥalāʾil with sons.

Ḥalāʾil is plural of ḥalīlah, meaning wives here.

The phrase concerns biological sons’ wives, mentioned among prohibited marital relationships.

Though from Ḥ L L, ḥalīlah does not merely mean a “lawful woman”: Arabic recognizes it as a word for a spouse.

It cannot be extended here to every woman whom someone could permissibly marry.

It means the person’s wife, with the relationship to sons supplied by the construction and context.

6. Rituals, oaths, and release from restrictions

A. Lā tuḥillū shaʿāʾira Allāhi: violating sacred rites

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 places tuḥillū under prohibition.

This differs from uḥilla lakum elsewhere in the surah.

Uḥilla lakum declares something permissible for the audience.

Lā tuḥillū shaʿāʾira Allāhi prohibits human conduct toward divine rites and inviolabilities.

It concerns treating violation of the sacred as permissible, or removing its inviolability.

A bare “do not make The God’s rites lawful” may be unclear; “do not violate the sanctity of The God’s rites” clarifies the contextual meaning.

Tuḥillū itself must nevertheless not be lexically equated with “break”: violating sanctity results from its use here.

The passage also addresses sacred times, offerings, and ritual signs, each requiring its own textual limits.

B. Ghayra muḥillī al-ṣaydi: treating hunting as permissible during consecration

Al-Māʾidah 5:1 uses muḥillī.

It is a plural Form IV active participle in an annexation with al-ṣaydi.

The verse permits livestock with specified exceptions, then addresses hunting while consecrated.

Muḥillī al-ṣaydi concerns treating hunting as permissible or acting as though its prohibition were removed.

Ghayra and wa-antum ḥurumun qualify it; hunting’s permissibility during consecration is thus excluded.

The active participle can describe human conduct toward a prohibition’s limits.

Muḥillī alone cannot establish all details of hunting, species, or conditions.

C. Taḥillata aymānikum: release from an oath’s constraint

Al-Taḥrīm reads:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ تَبْتَغِي مَرْضَاتَ أَزْوَاجِكَ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu li-ma tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla Allāhu laka tabtaghī marḍāta azwājika wa-Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīmun

“O Prophet, why do you prohibit what The God has permitted you, seeking your wives’ pleasure? The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

Al-Taḥrīm 66:1

The next verse reads:

قَدْ فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَاللَّهُ مَوْلَاكُمْ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

qad faraḍa Allāhu lakum taḥillata aymānikum wa-Allāhu mawlākum wa-huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīmu

“The God has prescribed for you release from your oaths. The God is your protector, and He is the Knowing, the Wise.”

Al-Taḥrīm 66:2

Taḥillah belongs to Ḥ L L, concerning loosening or removing an oath’s constraint.

The knot image is clear: an oath may bind someone to an act or abstention; taḥillah concerns release from that obligation.

The word does not establish that every oath is ineffective in all circumstances or that obligations can be discarded without responsibility.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 discusses unintentional oaths, binding oaths, and their expiation.

It helps explain dealing with oaths, but taḥillah and kaffārah remain independent words.

Taḥillah concerns release from an oath’s binding force; kaffārah concerns prescribed reparation.

They may coincide in a particular situation without being complete lexical synonyms.

Al-Taḥrīm also shows that even the Prophet cannot declare what The God permitted prohibited as a divine rule on personal grounds.

Personal abstention, an oath, and public legislation must remain distinct even when related in one event.

D. Wa-anta ḥillun bi-hādhā al-baladi: several readings

Al-Balad begins:

لَا أُقْسِمُ بِهَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ

lā uqsimu bi-hādhā al-baladi

“I swear by this city.”

Al-Balad 90:1

وَأَنْتَ حِلٌّ بِهَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ

wa-anta ḥillun bi-hādhā al-baladi

“And you are in a state of ḥill in this city.”

Al-Balad 90:2

Ḥill is lexically recognized, but its contextual meaning here is disputed.

Three major readings have been proposed.

The first relates it to alighting and residence: the addressee is present and resides in the city.

The second concerns permission or lifting a restriction: the addressee is exempt or released from a particular prohibition in the city.

The third understands the city’s people as disregarding the addressee’s inviolability and treating attacks upon him as permissible.

Each requires examination through the sentence and surah’s context.

Morphologically, ḥill must not simply be equated with active participle ḥāll, “resident.” A residence reading must explain how the form ḥill conveys that sense.

Conversely, a reading about lifted inviolability must not be confined without evidence to a specific historical event unnamed in the verse.

The existence of several lexical explanations does not make every possibility the verse’s established intention.

The basic text requires independent reflection here: clarity about ḥill’s semantic field does not settle its precise referent.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ L L (ḥall) and ʿ Q D (ʿaqd)

ʿAqd concerns binding, knotting, and creating a bond or obligation.

A principal use of ḥall concerns undoing that binding.

Ṭā Hā 20:27 clearly illustrates the contrast in Moses’ prayer to untie his tongue’s knot.

Socially and legally, ʿaqd may create an obligation and ḥall remove its constraint.

This does not invalidate every contract: each obligation has its conditions and rights.

Study of ʿ Q D

Ḥ L L (ḥalāl) and Ḥ R M (ḥarām)

Ḥalāl and ḥarām oppose one another in prescriptive usage.

The former concerns permissibility or absence of prohibition; the latter, prohibition or inviolability.

Ḥ R M extends beyond prohibition to sanctity, protected bounds, and inviolability.

Ḥ L L likewise has nonprescriptive uses for alighting, opening, and residence.

Lawfulness versus prohibition is thus one relationship between the families, not either root’s entire meaning.

Study of Ḥ R M

Ḥ L L (aḥalla) and ʾ DH N (adhina)

Idhn concerns permitting and making known.

Iḥlāl concerns making permissible in prescriptive usage.

Yūnus 10:59 asks whether The God authorized people’s declarations of lawfulness and prohibition.

Religious permission must thus relate to its valid authority.

Every authorization is not necessarily a general rule of religious lawfulness: permission to enter someone’s home differs from public religious legislation.

Study of ʾ DH N

Ḥ L L (ḥalāl) and Ṭ Y B (ṭayyib)

Ḥalāl concerns permissible status.

Ṭayyib concerns purity, goodness, and agreeableness.

They occur together in several verses.

Lawfulness must not be equated with personal enjoyment, medical health, or cultural acceptance.

Someone may avoid what is not prohibited because of health, caution, or preference; that differs from declaring a divine prohibition.

Study of Ṭ Y B

Ḥ L L (ḥall) and N Z L (nuzūl)

Nuzūl concerns coming down or descending.

Spatial ḥall also concerns alighting and residing.

The fields can meet, with ḥall also emphasizing settlement and reaching a destination.

Punishment alighting upon a people concerns its arrival and establishment.

Not every use of nuzūl should conversely become residence or the end of travel.

Study of N Z L

Ḥ L L (taḥillah) and K F R (kaffārah)

Taḥillah concerning an oath refers to release from its constraint.

Kaffārah belongs to K F R and in legal and devotional usage concerns reparation or covering a violation’s consequences.

They may relate in an oath without having identical roles.

Release and prescribed reparation must be understood through the prescription’s construction, without substituting one word’s meaning for the other.

Study of K F R

Ḥ L L (maḥill) and B L GH (bulūgh)

Bulūgh concerns reaching.

Ritual maḥill may concern an appointed destination or time of arrival.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 expresses the offering’s reaching its destination with yablugha.

Reaching and its destination are independent concepts in the same construction.

Maḥill alone does not mean reaching; balagha does not necessarily mean leaving consecration or completing rites.

Study of B L GH

8. Challenges in understanding and translation

Is lawfulness merely absence of prohibition?

Prescriptively, ḥalāl concerns permission and absence of prohibition.

This does not make every behavior unconditional.

Trade may be lawful in principle, while a particular transaction involves deception, injustice, or breach of covenant.

Marriage may likewise be permissible in principle while its conditions and rights are disregarded.

An act’s general permissibility must therefore be distinguished from the validity of every particular implementation.

Is everything unpleasant prohibited?

No. Individual or cultural dislike does not alone establish a divine prohibition.

Food may be unpalatable or unsuitable for someone’s health without being generally prohibited for others.

Conversely, absence of religious prohibition does not require consuming something or ignoring known dangers.

Personal caution, health considerations, and a prohibition are distinct.

Can someone personally abstain?

Yes. Abstaining from a permissible thing differs from declaring it prohibited.

Someone may abstain through preference, need, a legitimate undertaking, or particular circumstances.

Turning abstention into a claim of divine law for everyone enters the field of legislation.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:1 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:2 highlight divine permission, personal restriction, and release from an obligation.

Does aḥalla always mean making lawful?

No.

Many verses oppose aḥalla to ḥarrama, expressing permission.

In Ibrāhīm 14:28, however, it means bringing a people into the abode of ruin.

Fāṭir 35:35 also concerns settling or causing to alight.

Form IV cannot be confined to one legal meaning.

Are ḥalāl and ḥill identical in every context?

They approach one another in permission while differing in Qur’anic roles and uses.

Ḥalālan describes provision or a permissible state in several verses.

Ḥillan and ḥillun concern food, marriage, and Al-Balad’s particular setting.

Description, predication, the word’s referent, and its semantic field must be distinguished.

A shared root does not justify one fixed rendering regardless of the sentence.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ḥalla meaning opening can be “opened” or “untied.” Wa-uḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī in Ṭā Hā 20:27 naturally becomes “And untie a knot from my tongue.”

For alighting, context may call for “alighted,” “arrived,” “befell,” or “settled.”

Prescriptive aḥalla principally means “permitted,” “declared lawful,” or “made permissible.”

Ḥalāl means “lawful” or “permissible”; “not prohibited” may clarify its lexical sense.

Ḥill can similarly mean “lawful” or “permissible”; Al-Balad 90:2 requires adjudicating its several readings before choosing a final translation.

Ḥalāʾil in Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 naturally means “wives,” rather than “lawful women.”

Ritual maḥill may be “appointed destination,” “place of arrival,” or, where context warrants, “appointed end time.”

Taḥillah may be “release from a constraint” or “means of release from an obligation.” In Al-Taḥrīm 66:2, “release from your oaths” clarifies its use.

Muḥillī in Al-Māʾidah 5:1 suits “those treating hunting as permissible”; the full expression must preserve negation and the condition of consecration.

Alighting or settling must not be replaced with legal lawfulness.

Conversely, prescriptive ḥalāl should not directly become healthy, pleasant, useful, or obligatory. Any such concerns have independent contextual roles.

10. Summary

Arabic Ḥ L L forms a broad family, with opening and undoing a binding among its clearest cores.

Removing restrictions, permissibility, leaving consecration, and release from obligations belong to this field; alighting, residing, and reaching a destination are also established uses.

Qur’anic uses go beyond lawful food to marriage, trade, rituals, financial rights, oaths, punishment descending, and people settling in the hereafter.

Lawfulness concerns permission and absence of prohibition without alone making an act obligatory.

It also differs from wholesomeness, personal preference, and medical benefit. Personal choice and caution must not become general divine prohibitions without authorization.

Outside prescriptive use, ḥall can mean alighting, settling, or reaching an appointed place. Verb, referent, preposition, and context determine the appropriate sense.

Al-Balad 90:2 likewise requires distinguishing ḥill’s readings without imposing one as certain without adequate evidence.

Briefly, the root’s fundamental branch concerns opening and undoing a binding; its prescriptive branch, permissibility and removal of prohibition; its spatial use, alighting and settling.

Recognizing these distinctions prevents reducing every Qur’anic form to making lawful, or substituting a knot image for a verse’s actual sense.
