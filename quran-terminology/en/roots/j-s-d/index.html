1. Introducing the root

The triliteral Arabic root J S D concerns a physical, perceptible body with volume. Its principal word, jasad, denotes the human body and, in some uses, other beings’ bodily forms or things shaped like bodies.

Early Arabic also records blood, particularly dried or adhering blood, and dyes that settle strongly into cloth. These branches help establish the family’s linguistic range alongside the bodily sense.

A central question is whether jasad inherently means a lifeless body or can also describe a living person’s body.

Arabic and Qur’anic evidence does not make lifelessness a fixed component. Jasad names the body; life, lifelessness, eating, movement, speech, and awareness require context. Brief root meaning: A body or bodily form; the word alone does not determine life, death, or awareness.

Jasad should also be distinguished from jism and badan. Their meanings overlap, but their ranges and ways of referring to bodies are not invariably identical.

J S D occurs four times in the Qur’an, always as jasadan:

  • Al-Aʿrāf 7:148: ʿijlan jasadan, a calf-shaped body that lowed.
  • Ṭā Hā 20:88: ʿijlan jasadan, the same calf-shaped body in Al-Sāmirī’s story.
  • Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8: jasadan lā yaʾkulūna al-ṭaʿāma, bodies that do not eat, within a statement denying this description of the messengers.
  • Ṣād 38:34: jasadan, a body cast upon Solomon’s throne during his trial.

No verb or other adjectival derivative from the root occurs in the Qur’an. The plural ajsād, verbs such as tajassada, and the blood and dye senses are recorded elsewhere in Arabic.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Gathering and becoming firm

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَالسِّينُ وَالدَّالُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَجَمُّعِ الشَّيْءِ أَيْضًا وَاشْتِدَادِهِ.

al-jīmu wa-al-sīnu wa-al-dālu yadullu ʿalā tajammuʿi al-shayʾi ayḍan wa-ishtidādih

Translation: “Jīm, sīn, and dāl also indicate something gathering together and becoming firm.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: gathering and firmness

He then discusses the human jasad and dried blood within the family.

Gathering and acquiring firmness provide the connection he proposes between the body and certain other usages.

A body has a discernible form and consistency, distinguishable from its surroundings. Blood that dries or adheres to a surface likewise loses its former fluidity and acquires greater consistency.

This connection does not prove that jasad originally meant dried blood and subsequently came to mean a human body, or the reverse.

Ibn Fāris offers a lexical analysis; a historical sequence requires independently dated evidence for each use.

Jasad as a body

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَسَدُ جِسْمُ الْإِنْسَانِ.

al-jasadu jismu al-insān

Translation: “Jasad is the human body.”

Lisān and Ṣiḥāḥ: the human body and badan

It also explains jasad through badan.

A principal use therefore names the human being’s physical, perceptible body.

Language can describe someone’s identity or responsibility, or instead their body, size, strength, color, and outward shape.

Bodily jasad concerns the physical form, rather than every emotional, ethical, or social quality of its possessor.

Knowledge, choice, faith, and personality do not follow from the word itself, even if they belong to the person whose body is described.

A living person also has a jasad. Defining it solely as a dead body unjustifiably restricts its range.

Is jasad used only of human beings?

The opening definition in Lisān emphasizes the human body, but other reports in the same compilation extend it to nonhuman beings.

Lexicographers therefore did not entirely agree on its range.

The Qur’an’s two instances of ʿijlan jasadan matter here: ʿijl is a calf, rather than a human.

These verses describe a calf-shaped body. Even if ordinary usage favored humans, the word cannot be declared categorically inapplicable to nonhuman forms.

The Qur’anic expression allows another being’s bodily form to be described as jasad.

This does not make every physical thing, such as stone, mountain, or wood, a jasad. Being material and actually receiving that name are different matters.

Jasad, life, and awareness

Some lexical explanations apply jasad to a form lacking discernment. Al-Zajjāj, for example, explains the Israelites’ calf as a bodily form without reason or discrimination.

Al-Zajjāj in Lisān: the calf and discernment

This explanation should not immediately define every use of the family.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8 uses the same word in discussing living, aware, food-eating human messengers.

The Qur’anic evidence therefore does not establish that every jasad must lack life or awareness.

The word conveys bodily form; its awareness, life, and abilities require separate evidence.

3. Blood, dryness, and dye

Jāsid: adhering or dried blood

Jasida al-damu describes blood adhering to something or drying.

Jāsid can qualify such blood.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالْجَاسِدُ مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ: مَا اشْتَدَّ وَيَبِسَ.

wa-al-jāsidu min kulli shayʾin mā ishtadda wa-yabis

Translation: “The jāsid of anything is what has become firm and dry.”

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān: jāsid, firm and dry

It also records jasad, jasid, jāsid, and jasīd for blood.

The subject is a substance’s adhering, thickened, or dried condition, rather than a human body.

This approaches Ibn Fāris’s gathering-and-firmness explanation.

Jasad nevertheless never means dried blood in the Qur’an. Its existence in Arabic does not justify importing it into the calf or Solomon passages without evidence.

Jasad meaning blood

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also records jasad for blood.

This is not confined to dried blood: literary witnesses apply it to blood that has been poured out.

The family therefore includes distinct references to bodies, blood, and blood’s dried or adhering condition.

These attested senses should not all become “lifeless body” or “motionless matter” merely to produce an apparently unified definition.

Jisād: dye and saffron

Early Arabic jisād concerns saffron and certain strong yellow or red dyes.

Ibn al-Aʿrābī records it as a saffron name; Al-Layth also applies it to saffron and intensely red or yellow coloring.

The referent is coloring material, rather than a body or blood.

Saffron and safflower settle into cloth; their strong dye may alter its appearance and consistency.

Lexicographers have connected blood’s red color with plant dyes, but similarity does not establish every form’s historical derivation.

Mujassad: dyed cloth

Mujassad can describe cloth thoroughly dyed with saffron, safflower, or another strong coloring.

Some explanations describe dye so substantial that the cloth’s appearance or stiffness changes.

This connects with jisād, dye.

Mijsad also names a garment worn directly against the body.

Lisān records disagreement over vocalization: one usage concerns bodily contact, another intense dye.

Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Miṣbāḥ: garment vocalization

Mijsad, mujsad, and mujassad should therefore not be assigned one fixed sense without examining form and context.

Tajassada and later usage

Tajassada is recorded for acquiring bodily form or becoming corporeal.

Modern tajsīd can mean representing a concept perceptibly or giving form to an abstract idea.

Later philosophical, artistic, and theological terminology should not replace the Qur’anic meaning.

Saying today that an idea is embodied in art does not make Qur’anic jasadan mean an idea’s manifestation or an invisible truth taking form.

Such a reading would require independent evidence.

Ṣiḥāḥ and Lane: tajassada and later usage

4. Pre-Islamic Arabic and early poetry

Jasad as blood in Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

A clear pre-Qur’anic witness comes from Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī:

فَلَا لَعَمْرُ الَّذِي مَسَحْتُ كَعْبَتَهُ
وَمَا هُرِيقَ عَلَى الْأَنْصَابِ مِنْ جَسَدِ

fa-lā la-ʿamru alladhī masaḥtu kaʿbatahu / wa-mā hurīqa ʿalā al-anṣābi min jasadi

Translation: “No, by the life of Him whose Kaʿbah I have touched, and by the blood poured upon the ritual stones.”

Jasad here means blood.

Al-Nābighah is a recognized pre-Islamic poet; the line appears in his transmitted Dīwān and early lexical sources.

Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān: Al-Nābighah’s blood witness

The oath invokes familiar pre-Islamic practices. Anṣāb here concerns stones used in ritual and sacrifice.

The witness establishes that pre-Qur’anic jasad was not confined to the human body.

It describes poured blood without necessarily saying that it has dried.

Jāsid’s dried-blood sense therefore should not restrict every blood-related jasad.

Nor does this witness transfer “blood” into all Qur’anic occurrences, which require their own contexts.

Jāsid in Al-Ṭirimmāḥ

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm, an Umayyad poet, describes arrows and their points:

فِرَاغٌ عَوَارِي اللِّيطِ تُكْسَى ظُبَاتُهَا
سَبَائِبَ مِنْهَا جَاسِدٌ وَنَجِيعُ

firāghun ʿawārī al-līṭi tuksā ẓubātuhā / sabāʾiba minhā jāsidun wa-najīʿu

Translation: “Broad-pointed arrows stripped of their surface covering, whose edges are clothed in strands of blood, some dried and some shed blood.” ʿAwārī al-līṭ concerns the shafts’ stripped bark or surface covering; the descriptions should not all be confined to arrowheads.

Lisān al-ʿArab: arrows and broad points

Jāsid means dried blood here. Najīʿ is variously recorded as blood, blood from within the body, dark blood, and sometimes fresh blood. “Fresh and flowing” is not its sole necessary sense.

Ṣiḥāḥ, Maqāyīs, and Tāj: the range of najīʿ

The pairing illustrates the blood branch, with jāsid emphasizing adhering or dried blood. It should not establish a fixed, universal dry-versus-fresh opposition.

The witness fits jāsid’s lexical definition and documents the blood-and-dryness branch in literary Arabic.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ lived after Islam, so this is not direct pre-Islamic evidence.

It clarifies a meaning independently recorded by lexicographers.

Color in Mulayḥ al-Hudhalī

Mulayḥ al-Hudhalī says:

كَأَنَّ مَا فَوْقَهَا مِمَّا عَلِينَ بِهِ
دِمَاءُ أَجْوَافِ بُدْنٍ لَوْنُهَا جَسِدُ

ka-anna mā fawqahā mimmā ʿalīna bihi / dimāʾu ajwāfi budnin lawnuhā jasidu

Translation: “What lies upon them, with which they have been covered, seems like blood from the insides of large camels, its color richly red.”

Jasid describes color here.

Ibn Sīdah connects the witness with jisād, coloring material.

Ibn Sīdah, Al-Muḥkam: Mulayḥ’s witness and jisād

It establishes the family’s color and intense-dye branch.

It does not make Qur’anic bodily jasad inherently red: that feature belongs to the poem’s coloring context.

Dating the witnesses

Al-Nābighah’s line provides clearer support for the root in pre-Islamic poetry.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ is post-Islamic. Assigning this particular Mulayḥ witness to the pre-Qur’anic period also requires independent dating; transmission in early sources alone is insufficient. Neither should be presented as certain pre-Islamic evidence without that examination.

The witnesses establish physical and material usages; each form’s exact meaning follows from its morphology and context.

5. Qur’anic usage: the Israelites’ calf

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148: a body that lowed

وَاتَّخَذَ قَوْمُ مُوسَىٰ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ مِنْ حُلِيِّهِمْ عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ ۚ أَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّهُ لَا يُكَلِّمُهُمْ وَلَا يَهْدِيهِمْ سَبِيلًا ۘ اتَّخَذُوهُ وَكَانُوا ظَالِمِينَ

wa-ttakhadha qawmu Mūsā min baʿdihi min ḥuliyyihim ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun a-lam yaraw annahu lā yukallimuhum wa-lā yahdīhim sabīlan ittakhadhūhu wa-kānū ẓālimīn

Proposed translation based on this study: “After Moses, his people took from their ornaments a calf, a body that lowed. Did they not see that it neither spoke to them nor guided them to a way? They took it, and they were wrongdoers.”

The verse concerns Israelites turning to the calf during Moses’s absence.

Min ḥuliyyihim names their ornaments. In ʿijlan jasadan, ʿijl identifies the calf form and jasad its bodily character.

Syntactically, jasadan can explain or qualify ʿijlan. Either way, the calf and its body concern the same referent.

The construction does not describe two independent beings.

Lahu khuwār then attributes lowing to it: a calf-shaped body from which a sound was heard.

Does jasadan mean a lifeless body?

Some interpretations describe ʿijlan jasadan as lifeless, without a spirit, or merely metallic.

Min ḥuliyyihim supports a material connection with people’s ornaments and a fashioned bodily form.

Its material, having a body, and being alive must nevertheless be distinguished.

Ḥuliyy names ornaments, jasad the body, and khuwār a sound like bovine lowing.

Jasad alone proves neither life nor lifelessness.

If the story’s overall context establishes a manufactured form, that conclusion must rest on the story’s evidence rather than a contrived definition that every jasad lacks life.

Al-Ṭabarī: the calf body and differing reports

Lowing and speaking

After mentioning the sound, the verse asks:

“Did they not see that it neither spoke to them nor guided them to a way?”

This distinguishes making a sound from speaking or guiding.

The calf lowed but neither spoke to the people nor showed them a way.

The criticism concerns more than an inert or lifeless object: making sounds does not establish a claim to divinity either.

The Qur’an calls attention to its inability to speak to or guide them.

A perceptible body and striking sound do not in themselves establish awareness, agency, knowledge, or worthiness of worship.

That is a contextual inference, rather than jasad’s lexical meaning.

Ṭā Hā 20:88: bringing forth the calf-shaped body

فَأَخْرَجَ لَهُمْ عِجْلًا جَسَدًا لَهُ خُوَارٌ فَقَالُوا هَٰذَا إِلَٰهُكُمْ وَإِلَٰهُ مُوسَىٰ فَنَسِيَ

fa-akhraja lahum ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun fa-qālū hādhā ilāhukum wa-ilāhu Mūsā fa-nasiy

Proposed translation based on this study: “He then brought forth for them a calf, a body that lowed. They said: This is your deity and Moses’s deity, but he forgot.”

The verse presents the same event from another angle.

The preceding verses discuss ornaments and Al-Sāmirī’s action, before fa-akhraja introduces bringing forth the calf.

ʿIjlan jasadan lahu khuwār appears again.

Its repetition emphasizes three features: calf shape, bodily form, and lowing.

None alone establishes divinity.

Ṭā Hā 20:89 also describes its inability to reply or control benefit and harm.

The context thus criticizes attributing divinity to something lacking the capacities required by that claim.

How was the calf’s sound produced?

Commentarial reports propose wind passing through openings, a special construction, or a miraculous event.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88, however, mention only khuwār.

Neither verse explains the sound-producing mechanism.

A technical or narrative explanation should not be inserted into the direct wording and then treated as decisive evidence of the body’s nature.

For the root study, jasad names the body and khuwār the sound attributed to it.

Al-Ṭabarī: the calf’s lowing and Al-Sāmirī

6. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8: bodies and the need for food

وَمَا جَعَلْنَاهُمْ جَسَدًا لَا يَأْكُلُونَ الطَّعَامَ وَمَا كَانُوا خَالِدِينَ

wa-mā jaʿalnāhum jasadan lā yaʾkulūna al-ṭaʿāma wa-mā kānū khālidīn

Proposed translation based on this study: “We did not make them bodies that do not eat food, nor were they immortal.”

The verse concerns earlier messengers, continuing the discussion of their humanity.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:7 identifies them as men receiving revelation. Verse 8 denies that they were non-eating bodies or immortal.

This construction particularly matters for jasad.

Denying bodies independent of food

In wa-mā jaʿalnāhum jasadan lā yaʾkulūna al-ṭaʿāma, negation concerns the complete construction: The God did not make the messengers physical beings that do not eat.

Lā yaʾkulūna al-ṭaʿāma is therefore a description denied within the sentence.

It cannot be detached to make jasad itself mean a being that does not eat.

That would ignore the following clause’s role.

The verse concerns human messengers and their living needs.

They ate and had bodily lives. Having bodies and needing food do not conflict with revelation and messengership.

Why is jasadan singular?

Hum is plural, while jasadan is singular.

Arabic can use a singular generic noun for a group, referring to a shared kind or nature.

Jasadan can be read that way; it does not mean all prophets shared one body.

Al-Ṭabarī: messengers’ bodies and eating

Yaʾkulūna and khālidīn are plural and refer back to the messengers.

The construction concerns their bodily and human life, rather than a single shared body.

Jasad and immortality

The verse ends:

“Wa-mā kānū khālidīn.”

That is: “Nor were they immortal.”

This limits the messengers’ worldly lives.

Jasad itself does not lexically mean perishable or mortal. Non-immortality follows from the separate statement wa-mā kānū khālidīn.

Nor does the verse alone supply a complete account of body, self, and life after death.

It answers a specific point: earlier messengers were humans with bodily needs and finite worldly lives.

Implications for the root’s meaning

This usage challenges restricting jasad to a soulless or dead body.

The word occurs within a discussion of the messengers’ bodily lives.

“Physical body” is therefore more precise. Life, eating, and awareness follow from the sentence and context.

7. Ṣād 38:34: a body upon Solomon’s throne

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ

wa-laqad fatannā Sulaymāna wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan thumma anāb

Proposed translation based on this study: “We tested Solomon and cast a body upon his throne; then he turned back.”

This is one of the shortest yet most difficult Qur’anic uses of jasad.

The verse describes testing Solomon, casting a body upon his throne, and his subsequent return.

It does not explicitly identify the body.

What the verse itself establishes

First, Solomon undergoes a trial.

Second, something named jasadan is cast upon his throne.

Third, anāba describes Solomon turning back or toward his Lord afterward.

These three elements appear in the text.

The verse does not identify whose body it was, whether it was alive or dead, its relationship to Solomon, or why it was placed there.

Jasadan is the object of alqaynā: the sentence describes casting a body.

This syntax alone does not identify the body.

Different accounts of the body

Commentaries record different narratives and hypotheses.

Some describe a being assuming Solomon’s appearance and occupying his throne; others connect the body with a child of Solomon, or with Solomon himself in severe illness and weakness.

Their contents and details differ.

The Qur’anic wording names neither a substitute ruler, a royal ring, days of absence, nor a child whose body lay on the throne.

None of these accounts should therefore become jasad’s lexical definition or the verse’s certain wording.

The conflicting reports themselves reinforce the need to distinguish clear Qur’anic information from external explanations.

Al-Ṭabarī: external accounts of the body upon the throne

Does jasad mean a dead body?

In contemporary Persian, jasad commonly evokes a dead body.

That Persian usage should not be imposed on Qur’anic Arabic.

Ṣād 38:34 includes no mayyit, mawt, or direct statement of death alongside jasadan.

“A body” is therefore more precise than an unqualified “corpse” or “dead body.”

Independent evidence might establish its identity or condition; jasadan alone does not.

The body, the trial, and Solomon’s return

The surrounding verses concern Solomon, his rule, and his relationship with his Lord.

In Ṣād 38:35, he asks forgiveness and a grant of sovereignty.

The body episode is thus connected with his trial and return.

That connection does not reconstruct the trial’s details without evidence.

A body on a throne may evoke power and rule, but the exact event is not explained by the verse alone.

The defensible lexical conclusion is a physical body whose identity and condition are unspecified.

The nature of Solomon’s trial requires a separate reflection on the verse.

8. Comparisons with related roots

J S D (jasad) and J S M (jism)

Study of J S M

Jism concerns bodies, volume, and corporeal form.

Jasad likewise names a body, with human bodies and beings or body-like forms prominent in attested usage.

Lexical sources sometimes explain one through the other; their meanings overlap.

The Qur’an nevertheless uses them in different settings.

Al-Baqarah 2:247 describes Saul’s increased knowledge and bodily stature. Jism concerns his physical qualities and capacity.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 describes people whose bodies attract attention, emphasizing outward appearance.

Jasad, by contrast, describes the calf’s bodily character and the messengers’ physical lives in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8.

These differences do not establish that jism is always alive and jasad always lifeless.

Such a division conflicts with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8.

Nor can the final consonants dāl and mīm alone establish a theory of life, movement, or immobility.

Each root requires attested language and actual usage.

J S D (jasad) and B D N (badan)

Badan concerns the body, sometimes with bodily size or bulk.

Bi-badanika in Yūnus 10:92 concerns Pharaoh’s body after drowning.

The Qur’anic family is not confined to human bodies.

Al-budn in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 names large sacrificial animals.

B D N thus has its own range.

Jasad’s four Qur’anic occurrences concern bodily form, without a derivative naming sacrificial animals.

The distinction does not make either family invariably alive or dead.

Badan can describe the drowned Pharaoh, while another form concerns living animals designated for ritual.

Life and death therefore follow from context, rather than a fixed difference between the nouns.

Forqan’s separate B D N study examines badan, jasad, jism, and flesh in more detail.

Study of B D N

J S D (jasad) and B SH R (bashar)

Qur’anic bashar denotes a human being within a family connected with skin and perceptible appearance.

It names the human being, rather than merely the body.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:7 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8 place the messengers’ humanity alongside their bodily needs.

They receive revelation, eat, and live in this world.

Jasad refers to their bodies; bashar can identify the person through human nature and perceptible presence.

This does not establish two independent entities called bashar and jasad within a human.

Two words can describe one reality from different perspectives.

Forqan’s B SH R study separately examines perceptible human appearance and Qur’anic uses.

Study of B SH R

J S D (jasad) and L Ḥ M (laḥm)

Study of L Ḥ M

Laḥm means flesh, one bodily tissue of humans or animals.

Jasad names the body without being confined to flesh.

A body can comprise flesh, bone, skin, and other components; jasad does not name just one tissue.

In the calf story, jasad alone does not prove actual flesh and blood.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8, however, uses it in discussing living messengers’ bodies.

Jasad should consequently not always be translated “flesh and blood.”

That expression can explain human bodily life in prose, but is not the root’s direct definition.

J S D (jasad) and N F S (nafs)

Study of N F S

Nafs has a broad Qur’anic range, including the person, life or self, identity, and the subject of responsibility and judgment.

Qur’anic jasad concerns the physical body.

Their different subjects do not by themselves explain the human being’s entire constitution.

Their definitions alone establish neither an entity independent of all bodily conditions nor that humans are wholly reducible to matter.

Those questions require verses concerning nafs, rūḥ, death, taking in death, and resurrection.

The J S D study concerns the body; the root alone should not generate a complete theory of life or awareness.

J S D (jasad) and J TH TH (juththah)

Study of J TH TH

Arabic juththah denotes a body or bodily trunk and is also commonly used for a corpse in modern Arabic. Persian jussah more often concerns physique, build, or bodily size; the ordinary meaning “corpse” should not be attributed to that Persian word.

Ṣiḥāḥ and Asās: juththah as body and person

Persian jasad can suggest a corpse, but Qur’anic Arabic has a broader range.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8 particularly demonstrates its place in discussing living humans’ physical lives.

Persian linguistic habits should therefore not make every Qur’anic jasad a corpse or dead body.

Dehkhodā, tan: Persian physique and bodily build

9. Questions about the root

Does jasad always lack a spirit?

Neither lexical nor Qur’anic evidence establishes that rule.

The calf has bovine lowing but cannot speak or guide.

Solomon’s story places an unidentified body on his throne.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8, however, discusses human messengers.

These settings show that the noun alone does not settle life or lifelessness.

A lifeless body’s condition must follow from the particular verse or context.

Is a jasad always motionless?

Immobility is not a fixed root meaning.

A living human body may move; a manufactured form may remain still.

The calf’s lowing is stated, but the two ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwār expressions do not directly explain bodily movement.

The word alone therefore does not establish immobility.

Does having a jasad imply reason?

Corporeality and awareness are distinct matters.

A body may belong to an aware being, or a bodily form may lack cognition and judgment.

The Qur’an mentions the calf’s inability to speak or guide.

This follows from the story, rather than jasad inherently meaning irrational.

The messengers in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8, however, are aware humans receiving revelation, and the same word occurs in discussing their bodies.

Is every jism a jasad?

Every jasad has bodily form, but Arabic usage does not call every thing with volume a jasad without evidence.

Stones, mountains, houses, trees, and tools have physical form; applying jasad to them requires actual linguistic usage.

Qur’anic jasad concerns beings’ bodies or forms resembling them.

Jism can therefore be broader, while jasad in these usages focuses on bodily form.

This is a usage tendency, rather than an absolute rule for every Arabic sentence.

Does jasad mean an idol in the Qur’an?

Al-Aʿrāf and Ṭā Hā apply it to the calf people adopted as a deity.

It does not follow that jasad itself names an idol or fabricated deity.

Al-Anbiyāʾ discusses messengers’ bodies, and Ṣād a body on Solomon’s throne.

The calf’s idol status follows from people’s actions and the story.

Jasad expresses its bodily character.

Can jasad mean mere appearance without reality?

Later literary and philosophical usage may describe an outward form lacking inner reality or awareness.

The calf story also permits a contrast between striking appearance and inability to guide.

That contrast should not define every jasad.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8 connects the word with the messengers’ actual bodily lives.

“An appearance empty of reality” is therefore indefensible as a definition throughout the Qur’an.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal natural Persian equivalent of jasad is paykar, “body” or “bodily form.”

It preserves corporeality without unjustifiably restricting the word to a corpse, soulless object, or idol.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88, ʿijlan jasadan can be “a calf, a body,” or more fluently “a calf, a body that …”.

“A lifeless calf” introduces something not established by jasad alone.

“A golden calf” may follow from the ornaments and a historical reading, but does not directly translate jasadan.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8, the messengers’ plural pronoun allows Persian “bodies.”

Wa-mā jaʿalnāhum jasadan lā yaʾkulūna al-ṭaʿāma can be rendered:

“We did not make them bodies that do not eat food.”

This preserves the negation without redefining jasad as a being independent of food.

In Ṣād 38:34, “a body” is preferable.

“A corpse,” “a dead body,” “a devil,” or “Solomon’s sick child” without separate explanation would add an identity or condition absent from the word.

Non-Qur’anic forms likewise require equivalents appropriate to the derivative.

Ajsād means bodies.

Blood-related jāsid can be “dried” or “adhering and dried.”

Color-related jisād means saffron or red or yellow coloring material.

Mujassad can describe cloth saturated with dye in early usage.

Later tajassada can mean becoming corporeal or taking perceptible form.

These should not replace Qur’anic jasad without evidence.

11. Conclusion

J S D concerns bodily form and, in other branches, blood, dried material, and substantial dyes.

Ibn Fāris treats gathering and firmness as central. This may connect some uses without proving their historical order.

Early poetry establishes blood-related jasad before the Qur’an. Al-Nābighah uses it for poured blood; later witnesses record jāsid for dried blood and jisād for strong coloring.

The Qur’an uses the root four times, always as jasadan.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88, it describes a calf-shaped body that lowed. Body and sound do not establish divinity; the story emphasizes inability to speak, guide, or control benefit and harm.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:8 discusses messengers’ bodies. They ate and were not immortal in worldly life, so jasad cannot always be confined to a lifeless or dead body.

Ṣād 38:34 describes a body cast upon Solomon’s throne during his trial, without identifying its nature or condition. External accounts should not be imposed on the lexical meaning.

Jasad, jism, badan, and bashar should be distinguished accordingly. Jasad focuses on bodily form; jism, physical form and qualities; badan, the body and sometimes its bulk; bashar, the human through humanity and perceptible presence. Their overlapping meanings do not establish exceptionless rules of life, death, or awareness.

Paykar, “body” or “bodily form,” is therefore the principal suitable Persian equivalent of Qur’anic jasad.

The word identifies corporeal form without alone establishing life, death, awareness, movement, speech, or guidance.

This is the study’s central boundary: the body is physical, while life, awareness, and capacities require separate evidence. Preserving that distinction allows all four occurrences to be understood in context without inserting unstated meanings.
