Sinking, depth, and withdrawal from reach: from water and caves to raids and swift movement

Early Arabic records two familiar semantic branches under غ و ر: sinking, descending, and being in a depth or depression; and swift movement or raiding, especially a sudden attack on another group.

In the first branch, غَوْر denotes depth or a recessed part. غَارَ الْمَاءُ means that water sank into the earth and disappeared from the surface or ordinary reach. غَار denotes a cavity or recess in a mountain or the ground that may shelter humans or animals.

Other uses describe eyes sinking into their sockets, the sun disappearing at the horizon, and travelling to low-lying land.

In the second branch, إِغَارَة concerns rapid movement and attacking another group. أَغَارَ عَلَى الْقَوْمِ means attacking those people. غَارَة denotes a raid, and مِغْوَار someone particularly active or skilled in raiding.

These branches should not be forced into one image. Sinking water and a swift attack may share an aspect of movement, but that alone does not establish that raiding developed from descending into a valley.

Excluding الْمُغِيرَاتِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3, whose classification differs between sources, four occurrences in three forms are counted here: الْغَارِ in Al-Tawbah 9:40, مَغَارَاتٍ in Al-Tawbah 9:57, and غَوْرًا in Al-Kahf 18:41 and Al-Mulk 67:30.

Early lexicons also examine الْمُغِيرَاتِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3 under غ و ر, whereas some modern morphological indexes assign it to غ ي ر. This difference in classification requires separate explanation. The sense of raiding is clear in context; differing labels alone do not establish the word's historical derivation.

1. The Root in Early Arabic and Historical Witnesses

غَوْرُ الشَّيْءِ denotes something's depth or lower part.

For example, غَارَ can describe water entering the earth and reaching layers where it is no longer easily seen or drawn.

غَائِر describes something sunken or situated at depth.

غَارَتْ عَيْنُهُ describes an eye sunken into its socket relative to its usual position. The eye has not disappeared; its visible position has changed relative to its surroundings.

Ghawr also denotes low-lying land. Tihāmah and its lowlands are familiar examples, contrasted with najd, elevated country.

Descending and being at depth are therefore not confined to water.

A Poetic Witness from Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says:

هَلِ الدَّهْرُ إِلَّا لَيْلَةٌ وَنَهَارُهَا
وَإِلَّا طُلُوعُ الشَّمْسِ ثُمَّ غِيَارُهَا

hali al-dahru illā laylatun wa-nahāruhā / wa-illā ṭulūʿu al-shamsi thumma ghiyāruhā

Translation: “What is time but a night and its day, and the rising of the sun, then its disappearance?”

غِيَارُهَا here concerns the sun.

The poet contrasts sunrise with its ghiyār: its disappearance from view.

Early commentary on Hudhalī poetry explains غيارها as the sun's disappearance.

This usage shows that the family غ و ر also concerns disappearance from view alongside visible descent.

At sunset, the sun goes below the observer's horizon and is no longer visible. Ordinary language can describe this as sinking or disappearing.

The usage does not itself constitute a theory of cosmic structure, celestial motion, or the earth's shape. The poet describes an observable phenomenon in ordinary language.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The verse is recorded in the Hudhalī poetry collection, but its composition has not been independently dated before the Quran.

It is valuable evidence of early Arabic usage, but should not be presented without evidence as a definitively pre-Quranic witness to this particular form.

A Witness Concerning Raiding

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

وَإِنْ أُمْسِ مَكْرُوبًا فَيَا رُبَّ غَارَةٍ
شَهِدْتُ عَلَى أَقَبَّ رَخْوِ اللِّبَانِ

wa-in umsi makrūban fa-yā rubba ghāratin / shahidtu ʿalā aqabba rakhwi al-libāni

Translation: “Though I spend the evening distressed, many a raid have I witnessed on a lean-bellied, supple-chested horse.”

غَارَة here denotes a raid or attack.

The poet recalls mounted military action. The subject is movement towards an attack, rather than sinking water or a deep location.

Ghārah was thus a recognized term for military attack in Arabic literary usage.

Poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays also describes swift horses and running wolves with language related to ghārah, showing the prominence of speed in one branch of the family.

Running fast and attacking are distinct. Not every rapid movement is an attack, and not every attack ends in the seizure of property.

The verse is preserved in literary works attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays. Although he belongs to the pre-Islamic period, no contemporary document establishes this verse's original wording and precise date. Its historical attribution should be understood within that limitation.

Two Branches in Lexical Sources

Early Arabic sources identify two principal fields under غ و ر.

The first concerns descent, recession, and depth.

The second concerns advancing to attack and taking property by force or warfare.

This distinction fits attested uses: water sinks, the sun disappears below the horizon, and a cave is a mountain recess. In the other branch, a group attacks its enemies and horses or armies move swiftly forward.

These two fields do not establish that every derivative belongs to one of two wholly unrelated historical roots.

The attested usages differ; establishing which meaning came first or whether one developed from the other requires further historical evidence.

2. Semantic Core and Forms

غ و ر has a weak middle radical.

In غَارَ, the middle wāw changes to alif in the verb's formation. It remains visible in forms such as غَوْر.

Principal recorded forms include:

غَوْر: depth, a depression, or a low place; for water, sinking and disappearing into the earth.

غَارَ: he or it sank, descended, or entered something.

يَغُورُ: he or it sinks or descends into a depth.

غَائِر: sunken, deep, or no longer visible at the usual surface.

غُؤُور: sinking, especially in uses such as sunken eyes.

غَار: a cavity or recess in a mountain or ground, usable for shelter or residence.

غِيرَان: a plural of ghār, meaning caves.

مَغَار and مَغَارَة: a recessed place or cave-like cavity.

مَغَارَات: plural of maghārah; caves and cavities offering shelter.

غَوَّرَ: in some uses, he travelled or descended into low country.

تَغْوِير: going to low country; in another usage, stopping or resting during the midday heat.

تَغَوَّرَ: he or it sank, descended, or disappeared from view.

The movement and raiding branch includes:

أَغَارَ: he raided a group; in some uses, moved rapidly.

يُغِيرُ: he raids or moves swiftly.

إِغَارَة: raiding or swift movement towards an attack.

غَارَة: a raid or attack, especially a sudden attack on another group.

مُغِير: a raider or someone advancing swiftly to attack.

مِغْوَار: a person or animal distinguished in raiding or rapid movement.

مُغَاوَرَة: two groups raiding one another.

تَغَاوُر: reciprocal raiding.

These forms do not have identical meanings in every use.

For example, ghawr applied to water concerns sinking, whereas ghārah in military usage denotes an attack.

Ghār as a mountain cavity also differs from ghārah as a raid, even when both are examined within one lexical family.

More Restricted Uses

Arabic also records ghār as the name of an aromatic tree whose leaves have culinary, medicinal, or fragrant uses.

This plant name must not be equated with a mountain cave.

Identical spelling does not establish identical meanings or histories of naming.

Other uses of ghār describe the inside of the mouth or bodily recesses.

These relate to an inner, recessed space, but none is an independent sense in the four Quranic occurrences examined here.

3. Ghawran: Water Sinking beyond Reach

غَوْرًا occurs twice in the Quran.

Both occurrences concern water sinking into the earth so that ordinary access becomes difficult or impossible.

Garden Water in Al-Kahf

Al-Kahf 18:41

أَوْ يُصْبِحَ مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا فَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعَ لَهُ طَلَبًا

aw yuṣbiḥa māʾuhā ghawran fa-lan tastaṭīʿa lahu ṭalabā

Al-Kahf 18:41

Translation: “Or its water may sink away, and you will be unable to seek it out.”

غَوْرًا here concerns the garden's water.

The preceding conversation between two men provides its context.

One owns two gardens and abundant resources, and boasts of his wealth and the strength of his following.

He thinks his garden will not perish, and even speaks of the Hour and returning to his Lord with confidence in his own superiority.

His companion reminds him that his creation, strength, and resources are not independent of God.

Al-Kahf 18:40 and Al-Kahf 18:41 then present two possible ways the garden might be lost.

Harm from the sky might leave its ground bare and slippery.

Or its water might sink away beyond his reach.

The Meaning of مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا

مَاؤُهَا means the water of that garden.

غَوْرًا describes its condition: it has sunk so that it is no longer accessible at its previous usable level or location.

Grammatically, غَوْرًا is the predicate of يُصْبِحَ.

Arabic can use this verbal noun in place of a derived adjective. مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا is therefore close in meaning to مَاؤُهَا غَائِرًا: its water being sunken.

The construction alone specifies neither the intensity nor the certainty of a particular natural mechanism.

غَوْرًا does not mean evaporation, pollution, salinization, or plants drying out.

It directly expresses water sinking and passing beyond ordinary reach.

Water may remain in lower earth layers while the garden owner can no longer obtain it through the former route.

Complete evaporation, by contrast, should not simply be equated with the particular meaning of ghawr.

The Role of فَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعَ لَهُ طَلَبًا

The sinking is followed by a consequence:

“فَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعَ لَهُ طَلَبًا”

You will no longer be able to seek it out or reach it.

This clause clarifies the water's situation in relation to human access.

Something may exist while people are unable to find or obtain it.

For example, water may lie at a depth that requires appropriate equipment and resources to extract.

The verse specifies no exact depth, soil type, aquifer structure, or drilling method.

Ghawr should therefore not be turned into a particular geological or hydrological theory.

Who Speaks These Words?

The garden owner's companion speaks of the water sinking away.

He reminds the owner of the possibility of losing his blessings.

Without attending to the speaker, the statement must not be treated as a definite report of something already happening at that moment.

Al-Kahf 18:42 reports the destruction of the garden's produce, but does not specify that every possibility previously mentioned occurred exactly as proposed or by the same mechanism.

Keep the warning, proposed possibility, and reported event distinct.

Water's Value alongside Abundant Property

The owner had wealth and resources, but continued use of the garden also depended on water.

Land, trees, and wealth alone do not guarantee continuing production.

If water on which plants depend becomes inaccessible, other resources may lose their practical value.

That conclusion comes from the contextual relationship between water and garden, rather than the root's independent meaning.

Ghawr itself describes the water's sinking.

The story uses that image to recall the limits of human power to preserve blessings.

4. Māʾukum Ghawran: Water and General Human Need

Al-Mulk 67:30

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَصْبَحَ مَاؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا فَمَنْ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِمَاءٍ مَعِينٍ

qul araʾaytum in aṣbaḥa māʾukum ghawran fa-man yaʾtīkum bi-māʾin maʿīn

Al-Mulk 67:30

Translation: “Say, ‘Tell me: if your water were to sink away, who would bring you flowing water?’”

غَوْرًا again concerns sinking water.

The context differs from Al-Kahf 18:41.

Al-Kahf concerns a garden belonging to someone proud of his wealth.

Al-Mulk addresses people more broadly through مَاؤُكُمْ: your water.

It may be a source on which people depend for drinking and continued life.

The Sentence's Construction

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ commands a question that directs the audience's attention.

إِنْ أَصْبَحَ مَاؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا expresses a condition: if your water sinks beyond ordinary reach.

فَمَنْ يَأْتِيكُمْ بِمَاءٍ مَعِينٍ asks who can supply replacement water.

غَوْرًا has the same adjectival use of a verbal noun found in Al-Kahf 18:41.

مَاءٍ مَعِينٍ is a different expression, referring to flowing water or water visible and accessible.

Two conditions are contrasted:

Water sunk beyond easy reach, and water available for use.

Does the Verse Say Water Is Annihilated?

No. Ghawr does not directly mean water's absolute destruction.

Water may sink into the earth and pass beyond human reach without necessarily ceasing to exist materially.

This distinction matters.

People may benefit from something but lose access when natural conditions change.

The issue is therefore not simply water's existence, but access to water people can use.

Water may exist deep underground while depth, insufficient equipment, or expense makes extraction difficult.

A spring may also cease flowing as before because the water's route changes or groundwater levels decline.

These illustrate possible ways water can pass beyond reach; they do not claim that the verse specifically describes one such mechanism.

Water and Limited Human Ability

Earlier verses in Al-Mulk direct attention to creation, the world's ordering, and dependence on natural conditions of life.

Al-Mulk 67:15 discusses the earth and human use of it.

Al-Mulk 67:29 addresses belief and reliance on the Lord.

The surah then ends with a question about water.

This sequence returns attention to a fundamental need of life.

People can cultivate land, dig wells, store water, and build means of transporting it. These are real and valuable abilities.

All these efforts nevertheless depend on water sources and conditions people did not independently create.

The question recalls human dependence on provision and the created order.

It does not imply that people should refrain from studying groundwater, building water infrastructure, or preventing shortages.

The verse recalls limited human ability without dismissing knowledge and resources.

Nor does water sinking in a region establish, without independent evidence, that its people are undergoing divine punishment.

Declining water resources may have various natural and human causes. Identifying them requires evidence rather than automatically attributing every event to punishment.

5. Al-Ghār and Maghārāt: Cavities and Shelters in the Earth

Two other Quranic uses of غ و ر concern recessed spaces and shelter.

They are الْغَار in Al-Tawbah 9:40 and مَغَارَات in Al-Tawbah 9:57.

The Cave in the Account of Muḥammad's Expulsion

Al-Tawbah 9:40

إِلَّا تَنْصُرُوهُ فَقَدْ نَصَرَهُ اللَّهُ إِذْ أَخْرَجَهُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا ثَانِيَ اثْنَيْنِ إِذْ هُمَا فِي الْغَارِ إِذْ يَقُولُ لِصَاحِبِهِ لَا تَحْزَنْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَنَا ۖ فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُ عَلَيْهِ وَأَيَّدَهُ بِجُنُودٍ لَمْ تَرَوْهَا وَجَعَلَ كَلِمَةَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا السُّفْلَىٰ ۗ وَكَلِمَةُ اللَّهِ هِيَ الْعُلْيَا ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

illā tanṣurūhu fa-qad naṣarahu allāhu idh akhrajahu alladhīna kafarū thāniya ithnayni idh humā fī al-ghāri idh yaqūlu li-ṣāḥibihi lā taḥzan inna allāha maʿanā fa-anzala allāhu sakīnatahu ʿalayhi wa-ayyadahu bi-junūdin lam tarawhā wa-jaʿala kalimata alladhīna kafarū al-suflā wa-kalimatu allāhi hiya al-ʿulyā wa-allāhu ʿazīzun ḥakīm

Al-Tawbah 9:40

Translation: “If you do not help him, The God has already helped him when those who disbelieved drove him out, one of two, when they were in the cave and he said to his companion, ‘Do not grieve; The God is with us.’ The God sent His tranquillity upon him and strengthened him with forces you did not see, and made the disbelievers' word the lower; The God's word is the higher. The God is Mighty, Wise.”

الْغَارِ is the noun naming the particular place in which the two were situated, rather than necessarily a morphologically derived place noun.

Ghār denotes a cavity or recess in a mountain or the ground that may serve as shelter or temporary accommodation.

The preposition فِي indicates that both were inside it.

إِذْ هُمَا فِي الْغَارِ clearly means that the two were in a space of the cave type.

Lexical Meaning and Historical Identification

Ghār is not itself a specific geographical proper name.

Arabic can apply it to many caves.

Historical accounts identify the cave with a particular place near Mecca, but this verse does not name the mountain or give geographical coordinates.

Nor does the verse name Muḥammad's companion: it identifies him as صَاحِبِهِ.

Lexical study need not incorporate the companion's historical identity or the place name into the root's meaning.

The Quran directly describes two people in the cave and their conversation in difficulty.

The Cave as Shelter

The context places Muḥammad in circumstances where opponents had driven him out.

He and his companion were in the cave, and Muḥammad told him:

“لَا تَحْزَنْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَنَا”

The cave is where they have taken shelter.

Ghār itself, however, does not mean complete security.

Someone hiding in a cave may still risk discovery or injury.

Tranquillity, divine help, and Muḥammad's reinforcement are expressed in separate clauses.

The lexical meaning of cave must not become “a place of guaranteed safety.”

A cave is a natural space that can offer shelter; actual safety depends on other factors too.

Ghār and Kahf

كَهْف likewise denotes a cave-like mountain space or shelter.

In Al-Kahf 18:10, young men take refuge in a kahf.

Al-Kahf 18:16 also speaks of entering it and hoping for divine mercy and ease.

Ghār and kahf are close in many Arabic uses.

Some lexicographers distinguish a larger cavity, kahf, from a smaller recess, ghār.

This should not be made an absolute rule: usage does not always specify exact dimensions or geometry.

Both Quranic stories present a natural space as a possible human refuge.

The young men's refuge in Al-Kahf and Muḥammad's presence with his companion in Al-Tawbah are nevertheless separate events.

A shared kind of location does not make all circumstances, reasons for shelter, or outcomes identical.

Maghārāt: Caves as Possible Refuges

Al-Tawbah 9:57

لَوْ يَجِدُونَ مَلْجَأً أَوْ مَغَارَاتٍ أَوْ مُدَّخَلًا لَوَلَّوْا إِلَيْهِ وَهُمْ يَجْمَحُونَ

law yajidūna maljaʾan aw maghārātin aw muddakhalan la-wallaw ilayhi wa-hum yajmaḥūn

Al-Tawbah 9:57

Translation: “If they found a refuge, caves, or an entryway, they would turn towards it, rushing headlong.”

مَغَارَاتٍ is the plural of مَغَارَة.

Maghārah denotes a cavity or recess that someone can enter or hide in.

Its spatial sense is close to ghār in the preceding passage.

The contexts differ, however.

Al-Tawbah 9:40 places Muḥammad and his companion in a cave.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 describes people who would rush towards any refuge or escape route they found.

Al-Tawbah 9:56 describes people swearing that they belong to the believers' community, while the Quran says otherwise and depicts them as fearful.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 then portrays their intense desire to escape.

Maghārāt is therefore associated here with hiding and fleeing a feared situation.

Maljaʾ, Maghārāt, and Muddakhal

Three expressions occur together:

“مَلْجَأً”, “مَغَارَاتٍ”, and “مُدَّخَلًا”.

Maljaʾ concerns a place or means of refuge.

Maghārāt are caves and cavities that can offer shelter.

Muddakhal denotes an entrance or route through which someone may go inside.

All relate to shelter and escape, but are not identical.

A refuge may be a cave, but not every refuge is a cave.

An entryway may lead somewhere safe, but the word alone does not establish that safety.

Their combination emphasizes the search for different routes of escape.

It should not become a fixed system of three shelter types, each with specified dimensions, construction, or security.

The wording itself does not supply such details.

One Word in Different Situations

In Al-Tawbah 9:40, the cave belongs to the account of Muḥammad's endurance amid expulsion and threats.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 mentions caves in describing people seeking escape out of fear.

The same kind of place can occur in circumstances with different values and consequences.

A place being a cave does not make its occupant's conduct right or wrong.

Conduct is evaluated through its reason, purpose, and circumstances.

One person may shelter in a cave to preserve life against oppression; another may seek the same shelter to evade responsibility.

The meaning of cave remains unchanged, while the circumstances and behaviour differ.

6. Fa-al-Mughīrāti Ṣubḥan: Raiding and Rapid Movement

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3

فَالْمُغِيرَاتِ صُبْحًا

fa-al-mughīrāti ṣubḥā

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3

Translation: “Then by those raiding at dawn.”

This verse is relevant to غ و ر, but its morphological classification requires explanation.

الْمُغِيرَاتِ is the feminine plural active participle of أَغَارَ.

The verb commonly means to raid or attack swiftly.

Early lexicons record أَغَارَ عَلَى الْقَوْمِ for attacking another group, including under غ و ر.

Some modern morphological indexes instead assign الْمُغِيرَاتِ to غ ي ر.

The form أَغَارَ alone does not resolve whether the middle radical is wāw or yāʾ. Lexical testimony and morphological analysis must be examined together; different classifications do not themselves establish a historical derivation.

This study counts the four earlier occurrences under غ و ر and examines الْمُغِيرَاتِ separately. Assigning it here gives five occurrences in five verses; the site's existing word box assigns it to غ ي ر.

This difference does not change the verse's principal meaning.

Raiding in the Surah's Context

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1 mentions panting runners.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2 mentions striking sparks.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3 presents a dawn raid.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4 describes raising dust.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:5 describes entering the midst of a gathering.

Together these present swift movement and a charge.

A familiar reading understands horses advancing to attack.

Other interpretations have differed over the precise identity of the runners.

For الْمُغِيرَاتِ, the central lexical point remains swift movement and raiding.

Is Every Rapid Movement a Ghārah?

No.

Speed may characterize a raid but does not exhaust its meaning.

A horse may race at great speed without attacking a group.

Mounted riders advancing to attack an enemy may be described as making an ighārah.

The combination of الْمُغِيرَاتِ with صُبْحًا and the images of movement and rising dust support the raiding sense.

The root alone does not establish that this attack necessarily aims at seizing property, killing people, or destroying settlements.

Such acts may occur in raids but are not fixed components of every use of aghāra.

Al-Mughīrāt and Mughayyirāt

مُغِير and مُغَيِّر are different Arabic forms.

Mughīr is Form IV and here denotes someone raiding.

Mughayyir is Form II from غ ي ر and means someone changing something.

Someone who changes a situation may be called mughayyir.

الْمُغِيرَاتِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3 should not become “those who change things” simply because the forms resemble one another.

The verb's established sense and the surah's context support raiding and swift movement.

Root-classification differences must not collapse raiding and changing into the same meaning.

7. Comparison with Related Roots

غ و ر (Ghawr) and غ ر ق (Gharaq)

Gharaq concerns submersion in water, sometimes with loss of the ability to emerge.

Ghawr in the Quranic water passages describes the water itself sinking into the earth.

These are different situations.

A person may drown while the water remains at the surface or in the sea.

Water may sink into the earth without anyone drowning.

The Quran uses غ ر ق in the story of Pharaoh and his people, whereas ghawr in Al-Kahf 18:41 and Al-Mulk 67:30 describes sinking water.

A shared image of sinking does not identify the roots as one.

غ و ر (Ghawr) and غ ي ب (Ghayb)

Ghayb concerns something hidden or beyond sight or knowledge.

Ghawr's first branch concerns sinking and being at depth.

Sinking may hide something from view, as water descends into the earth.

But not everything hidden lies at depth.

Someone may be unaware of an event in a distant land without that event having sunk underground.

Ghayb and ghawr may relate through concealment, but have different meanings.

Al-Mulk 67:30 concerns water sinking beyond reach; ghawr does not itself refer to all unseen matters.

غ و ر (Ghawr) and ه ب ط (Hubūṭ)

Hubūṭ concerns descending from one position to another.

Ghawr's spatial branch concerns depth and recession.

They may overlap in particular movements.

Someone descending from a height into a valley performs a descent, while the valley may constitute low country.

Not every descent means entering a cave or the earth's depths.

Water may also sink into the ground without the verse presenting the same construction or cause associated with hubūṭ.

Descent is a shared field, while the roots' constructions and objects differ.

غ و ر (Ghawr) and ك ه ف (Kahf)

Kahf and ghār both concern cave-like spaces offering shelter.

Kahf appears in the young men's refuge for their belief, whereas ghār appears in Muḥammad's story with his companion in Al-Tawbah.

Different names alone do not establish that one is invariably large and the other small.

Their Quranic uses chiefly differ in subject and narrative context.

Their lexical meanings also do not establish the quality of protection or duration of residence.

غ و ر (Ghawr) and غ ي ر (Ghayr / Taghyīr)

غ ي ر concerns otherness and change in Quranic usage.

Al-Raʿd 13:11 uses يُغَيِّرُ and يُغَيِّرُوا for changing a condition.

This differs from ghawr as sinking water.

Water descending below the surface changes access to it, but that change is a consequence rather than the lexical definition of ghawr.

الْمُغِيرَاتِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3 must likewise be understood through its established raiding sense, not rendered “changers” merely because an index places it under غ ي ر.

Similar morphology, a possible historical relationship, and a word's actual contextual meaning are separate questions.

غ و ر (Ghawr) and غ ز و (Ghazw)

Ghazw concerns movement and action towards war or confrontation with an enemy.

Ghārah likewise means a raid or attack in military usage.

The families are close in warfare but not identical.

Ghazw can describe a broader military undertaking, while ghārah characteristically emphasizes swift movement or a sudden attack.

This tendency must not become an absolute rule that every ghazw is long and every ghārah short.

Duration, scale, and outcome depend on context and historical evidence.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

“Sinking,” “being at depth,” “cave,” and “raiding” suit the family's Quranic uses, each in its particular branch and derivative.

Al-Kahf 18:41

غَوْرًا — “sunken”

A natural translation of مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا is:

“Its water may sink away.”

This is concise, clear, and faithful to the word.

“Its water may dry up” expresses a possible result but narrows ghawr to drying.

“Its water may evaporate” is also inaccurate: evaporation does not follow from the root itself.

Al-Mulk 67:30

مَاؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا — “your water sinks away”

This preserves the same first-branch meaning.

Explanation may add that water has descended into the earth and ordinary access has become difficult.

The basic translation should not insert a specified depth, aquifer type, or scientific mechanism.

Al-Tawbah 9:40

الْغَارِ — “the cave”

A suitable rendering of إِذْ هُمَا فِي الْغَارِ is:

“When the two were in the cave.”

The word names a kind of place and need not become “the Cave of Thawr” or another geographical name.

Historical identification, if needed, belongs outside the direct translation.

Al-Tawbah 9:57

مَغَارَاتٍ — “caves”

“Caves” is natural in this verse.

Contextual explanation may identify them as places to shelter or hide.

“Guaranteed safe refuges” would insert security that the word itself does not convey.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3

الْمُغِيرَاتِ — “those raiding”

This preserves aghāra's established meaning in the surah.

“Those changing things” does not fit the context.

“Plunderers of property” also adds detail absent from the phrase, which does not mention taking wealth.

The root study should explain this form separately because its root classification differs between sources.

Brief Root Meanings for Word Boxes

For غَوْرًا in Al-Kahf 18:41 and Al-Mulk 67:30:

Brief root meaning: “Sinking and being at depth or in a low place.”

Contextual meaning: “Water sinking into the earth beyond ordinary reach.”

For الْغَارِ in Al-Tawbah 9:40:

Brief root meaning: “Depth and a recessed space in the ground or a mountain.”

Contextual meaning: “The cave in which Muḥammad and his companion were situated.”

For مَغَارَاتٍ in Al-Tawbah 9:57:

Brief root meaning: “Cavities and recessed spaces such as caves.”

Contextual meaning: “Caves people might enter to hide or escape.”

For الْمُغِيرَاتِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3:

Brief root meaning: “Raiding and advancing swiftly to attack.”

Contextual meaning: “Raiders advancing swiftly at dawn.”

9. Conclusion

غ و ر has two principal semantic branches in early Arabic.

The first concerns depth, recession, descent, and disappearance from the surface or sight.

Ghawr denotes depth or a recessed part, ghār a cavity in a mountain or earth, and maghārah a cavity or place offering shelter.

Linguistic witnesses describe sinking water, sunken eyes, the sun's disappearance, and travel to low country.

The second branch concerns swift movement and raiding. Ighārah and ghārah denote attacks, and mughīr a raider.

With الْمُغِيرَاتِ examined separately, this study counts four Quranic occurrences of غ و ر in three forms.

Al-Kahf 18:41 presents the possibility of garden water sinking beyond its owner's ability to obtain it again.

Al-Mulk 67:30 asks who can supply flowing, accessible water if people's water sinks away.

In Al-Tawbah 9:40, the cave holds Muḥammad and his companion amid threat and expulsion.

Al-Tawbah 9:57 mentions caves in describing people seeking escape and refuge out of fear.

Early lexicons relate الْمُغِيرَاتِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3 to raiding under غ و ر, while some morphological indexes place it under غ ي ر. Preserve that classification difference without equating raiding with changing.

These uses show a clear connection between sinking, disappearing from sight, and sheltering in a recessed space.

Yet that image does not cover the whole family. Raiding is an established, distinct usage, whatever its possible historical relationship with the first branch.

Final definition:

غ و ر:

1. Depth and recession: descending or being in a recessed, deep place, possibly passing beyond the surface, sight, or ordinary reach; also names for recessed spaces such as caves.

2. Swift movement and raiding: advancing rapidly to attack another group, as recorded in ighārah, ghārah, and mughīr. Its exact historical relationship with the first branch requires caution.

The Quranic water passages distinguish something's existence from people's access to it.

Water may remain underground yet be unobtainable. Owning land and resources does not itself ensure lasting control over a water supply.

A natural cave can shelter people, but the moral value of taking refuge depends on motives and circumstances, not the place's name.

Likewise, rapid movement and attack are distinct from their purpose, legitimacy, and outcome.

Therefore, ghawr in its principal branch concerns sinking and being at depth, rather than necessarily destroying what has sunk.

This distinction is decisive for the two water passages: losing access to a blessing differs from its absolute destruction, yet the practical consequences for those unable to obtain it may be severe.
