The Qur’anic Root KH M Ṣ: Hollowness, Emptiness, and Hunger

From the hollow belly and arch of the foot to makhmaṣah, severe hunger, and necessity in the Qur’an

Arabic خ م ص concerns hollowness, concavity, and reduced prominence. Its clearest physical applications concern the belly and sole: a belly drawn in through hunger or natural build, and the hollow part of the sole that does not touch the ground when standing.

The same family includes makhmaṣah for hunger or famine. The physical connection is understandable: insufficient food may leave a person’s belly empty and drawn in, while lack of nourishment weakens the body.

A hollow belly, however, does not always indicate hunger. Someone may naturally have a slender, less prominent abdomen. Akhmaṣ, the arch of the foot, likewise has no necessary connection to hunger.

The Qur’an contains only makhmaṣah from this root. Al-Māʾidah 5:3 connects it with necessity and food rules; Al-Tawbah 9:120 places it alongside thirst and fatigue among hardships endured in the way of The God.

Qur’anic makhmaṣah thus means hunger or deprivation of food, but functions differently in each verse: necessity in one, hardship endured while fulfilling responsibility in the other.

1. Forms and the Semantic Core

خ م ص is a sound triliteral root with several verbal and nominal forms recorded in early Arabic.

خَمَصَ ـ يَخْمُصُ (khamaṣa–yakhmuṣu) describes the belly becoming empty and drawn in. Khamiṣa and khamuṣa are also recorded with different vowels.

Khamṣ, khumṣ, khumūṣ, and khamāṣah are verbal nouns or related nouns in this semantic field.

Khamīṣ describes a slender, less prominent, or hollow belly or a person with one. Hunger or natural build may produce that condition. The feminine is khamīṣah; one plural is khimāṣ.

Khamṣān and khumṣān also describe someone hungry or hollow-bellied, with the feminine forms khamṣānah and khumṣānah. Tāj al-ʿArūs also records khamaṣān and khamaṣānah with a vowel on mīm; the sources do not all transmit one vocalization.

Makhmaṣah denotes hunger or famine, a condition associated with an empty belly.

As a noun, akhmaṣ names the hollow part of the sole normally clear of the ground when standing. The adjectival rajulun akhmaṣu al-qadami describes a man with an arched foot; that adjective’s feminine is khamṣāʾ. The body-part noun should not be conflated with the adjective describing a person.

Takhāmaṣa also means drawing away, moving aside, or diminishing. Early usage records it for night’s darkness thinning toward dawn.

Inkhamaṣa describes a wound’s swelling subsiding.

Khamīṣah also names a patterned or marked garment of wool or khazz, often dark. This must be distinguished from the adjective khamīṣah describing a slender belly.

Only makhmaṣah occurs in the Qur’an: makhmaṣatin in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 and makhmaṣatun in Al-Tawbah 9:120.

Their case endings differ, but they are one word with one principal meaning.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences of the noun makhmaṣah

Ibn Fāris’s explanation of the core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الضُّمْرِ وَالتَّطَامُنِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-ḍumri wa-al-taṭāmuni

Meaning: “One underlying principle indicating leanness and hollowness.”

Ḍumr here concerns reduced prominence, leanness, or contraction of volume; taṭāmun concerns lowering and concavity.

Both appear clearly in the arch of the foot and a less prominent belly.

Ibn Fāris places makhmaṣah in the same field, because hunger can leave a belly empty and drawn in.

The core need not therefore begin with hunger itself. Hollowness and reduced prominence provide a broader physical field, of which hunger is one application.

This does not establish that Arabic necessarily names every hollow object through this family. Actual uses require linguistic evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: leanness, hollowness, and the garment name

2. The Physical Image and Early Arabic Uses

A belly drawn in through hunger

Khamaṣa al-baṭnu describes a belly becoming empty and less prominent.

Khamaṣahu al-jūʿu likewise means hunger made a person weak and hollow-bellied.

This rests on a familiar relationship between food and bodily condition.

After eating much, the belly may be full and prominent; after a period without food, it may be emptier and less prominent.

This is a linguistic description of ordinary experience, not a medical measure of hunger’s severity.

Someone may be hungry without a visibly hollow abdomen, or naturally slender despite sufficient food.

Arabic poetry also shows this distinction, which matters for understanding makhmaṣah.

Al-Aʿshā: hunger and unequal access to food

A verse by Al-Aʿshā, the prominent pre-Islamic poet, reads:

تَبِيتُونَ فِي الْمَشْتَى مِلَاءً بُطُونُكُمْ
وَجَارَاتُكُمْ غَرْثَى يَبِتْنَ خَمَائِصَا

tabītūna fī al-mashtā milāʾan buṭūnukum / wa-jārātukum gharthā yabitna khamāʾiṣā

Translation:

“You spend winter nights with your bellies full, while your women neighbors spend the night hungry and empty-bellied.”

The poet contrasts two conditions.

One group spends the night well fed, while neighboring women remain hungry.

Khamāʾiṣā, a plural of khamīṣah, describes women whose bellies remain empty through hunger.

Gharthā, “hungry,” accompanying khamāʾiṣā reinforces that meaning.

The poet is not describing natural slenderness or beauty, but contrasts one group’s plenty with its neighbors’ deprivation.

The witness establishes the connection between khamīṣ and hunger in early Arabic independently of Qur’anic exegesis.

It also shows the family describing social realities, including food shortage and disregard for neighbors’ needs.

The social criticism comes from the poet’s whole statement; خ م ص itself does not mean oppression or injustice.

A slender belly without hunger: Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

A verse attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

وَالْبَطْنُ ذُو عُكَنٍ خَمِيصٌ لَيِّنٌ
وَالنَّحْرُ تَنْفُجُهُ بِثَدْيٍ مُقْعَدِ

wa-al-baṭnu dhū ʿukanin khamīṣun layyinun / wa-al-naḥru tanfujuhu bi-thadyin muqʿadi

Translation:

“Her belly, with soft folds, is slender and supple; her upper chest is raised by prominent breasts.”

Khamīṣ describes the belly, but the subject is not hunger or food deprivation.

The poet describes a woman’s physical features, including a slender or less prominent abdomen.

Discussing makhmaṣah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3, Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains that a khamīṣ belly may reflect natural build rather than hunger-induced wasting. This quotation follows Al-Ṭabarī; some versions of the dīwān and Lisān al-ʿArab have laṭīfun ṭayyuhu in place of khamīṣun layyinun. The word khamīṣ therefore does not occur uniformly in every transmission.

Comparing Al-Aʿshā and Al-Nābighah reveals a clear distinction.

In Al-Aʿshā, khamāʾiṣ concerns hunger because it accompanies gharthā and contrasts with full bellies.

In Al-Nābighah, khamīṣ describes bodily shape.

The physical meaning of hollowness is consequently broader than hunger specifically.

Al-Ṭabarī: Al-Aʿshā and Al-Nābighah, and the variant khamīṣun layyinun

The arch of the foot

Akhmaṣu al-qadami is an important use.

Early sources explain akhmaṣ as the hollow underside of the foot that does not meet the ground when standing.

This concerns the arch in the middle of the sole standing clear of the ground, rather than claiming that this part lies closer to the ground.

Parts of the sole bear weight while the hollow region may have less contact with the ground.

Akhmaṣ here concerns the foot’s form and structure, not hunger.

Its connection to the root’s core is concavity and hollowness.

The foot’s arch and an empty belly remain different bodily conditions described through one lexical family.

Al-Miṣbāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: the arch noun and the adjectives akhmaṣ and khamṣāʾ

Takhāmuṣ: drawing away

Takhāmaṣa ʿani al-shayʾi means drawing away or moving aside from something.

A verse attributed to Al-Shammākh reads:

تَخَامَصُ عَنْ بَرْدِ الْوِشَاحِ إِذَا مَشَتْ
تَخَامُصَ حَافِي الْخَيْلِ فِي الْأَمْعَزِ الْوَجِي

takhāmaṣu ʿan bardi al-wishāḥi idhā mashat / takhāmuṣa ḥāfī al-khayli fī al-amʿazi al-wajī

A translation conveying the image:

“As she walks, she draws away from the sash’s coldness, like an unshod horse avoiding painful contact with hard, stony ground.”

The comparison concerns bodily movement and drawing away from direct contact. The dīwān transmits ḥāfī al-khayl, meaning an unshod horse; some lexical quotations have jāfī al-khayl. The opening verb is read here as imperfect takhāmaṣu, with one of the two tāʾ letters omitted, rather than perfect takhāmaṣa.

This relates to the field of hollowness and distance from a contact surface.

Al-Shammākh’s dīwān: ḥāfī al-khayl against the lexical quotation jāfī

The sources explain تَخَامَصْ لَهُ عَنْ حَقِّهِ through أَعْطِهِ: give him his due and move aside from withholding it. The expression should not by itself mean surrendering your own right to someone else.

The image of moving aside here concerns giving another person their due in a social relationship. That meaning comes from the full construction and lexical explanation, not every use of the root.

Asās al-Balāghah and Tāj al-ʿArūs: giving the other person their due

Swelling subsiding and night’s darkness diminishing

Khamaṣa al-jurḥu or inkhamaṣa al-jurḥu describes a wound’s swelling subsiding.

The swollen region loses its prominence and becomes less raised.

Takhāmaṣa al-laylu also describes night’s darkness diminishing toward dawn.

A verse attributed to Al-Farazdaq reads:

فَمَا زِلْتُ حَتَّى صَعَّدَتْنِي حِبَالُهَا
إِلَيْهَا وَلَيْلِي قَدْ تَخَامَصَ آخِرُهْ

fa-mā ziltu ḥattā ṣaʿʿadatnī ḥibāluhā / ilayhā wa-laylī qad takhāmaṣa ākhiruh

Translation:

“I persisted until her ropes raised me toward her, while the end of my night was growing faint.”

Takhāmaṣa here describes night’s darkness retreating.

This witness belongs to the Islamic period and should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

Reduction and subsiding thus extend beyond bodily form.

The evidence does not establish the historical sequence of these developments. The recorded uses are simply compatible with the general field of reduced prominence, recession, and diminution.

Khamīṣah: a garment name

Besides its adjectival application to the belly, khamīṣah names a garment of wool or khazz.

Lexicons describe it as generally black or dark, with distinctive markings.

Ibn Fāris attempts to connect this name to hollowness but treats the explanation as tentative. If that connection is incorrect, he allows the garment sense to stand outside the family’s main pattern.

Its naming should therefore not receive an invented, certain history.

Al-Aʿshā also uses khamīṣah for a dark covering in a comparison with a woman’s black hair.

This differs from khamīṣah describing a slender-bellied woman, although both are listed under the same root.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Asās, and Maqāyīs: garment, Al-Farazdaq, night, and swelling

3. Makhmaṣah: From a Hollow Belly to Hunger

Makhmaṣah follows the pattern mafʿalah.

Arabic sources explain it through jūʿ and majāʿah: hunger and severe shortage of food.

Lisān al-ʿArab defines it as hunger associated with the belly remaining empty of food.

Al-Ṭabarī, discussing Al-Māʾidah 5:3, likewise connects it with hunger and shiddat al-saghab, severe hunger.

The image of a hollow belly becomes the name of a condition capable of causing that hollowness.

Three matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, natural bodily form, which can involve a slender or hollow abdomen.

Second, ordinary hunger after a period without food.

Third, severe hunger and food deprivation capable of threatening strength and health.

Familiar makhmaṣah concerns hunger or famine rather than natural slenderness alone.

In its two Qur’anic applications, context also clarifies severity and consequences.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, its association with necessity concerns hunger placing a person under compulsion.

In Al-Tawbah 9:120, alongside thirst and fatigue, it is a hardship endured while fulfilling responsibility.

The first use does not establish that everyone experiencing makhmaṣah must always be on the verge of death.

Nor does majāʿah in a lexical explanation necessarily mean a famine affecting an entire region. An individual or group can experience severe food shortage.

Al-Ṭabarī: makhmaṣah, severe hunger, and verbal-noun analysis

4. Makhmaṣah and Necessity in Al-Māʾidah 5:3

At the end of the verse on prohibited foods comes:

فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

fa-mani uḍṭurra fī makhmaṣatin ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin fa-inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Whoever is compelled by severe hunger, without inclining toward sin: The God is indeed forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

Makhmaṣatin follows the preposition fī and is genitive.

Fī makhmaṣatin describes the condition in which the person is caught.

The sentence does not stop at hunger: preceding it is uḍṭurra, “was compelled.”

Iḍṭirār comes from ض ر ر and concerns being under necessity and pressure.

Two separate concepts therefore appear together:

Makhmaṣah identifies hunger and lack of food.

Uḍṭurra indicates that the person has been compelled by their situation.

Together they show that ordinary hunger before a meal is not the whole subject. The hunger places someone under necessity.

Does makhmaṣah alone mean necessity?

No. Although it denotes hunger and severe food shortage, it is not identical to necessity.

Defining it alone as compulsion would erase the difference between the expression’s two components.

Hunger provides the setting for necessity; the ruling is expressed through their combination.

Someone may be hungry while sufficient permitted food remains available. Hunger alone does not necessarily constitute the necessity described here.

Conversely, someone facing genuine food shortage may need to preserve health or life while ordinary food choices are unavailable.

Hunger and necessity then coincide.

The expression specifies no number of fasting hours, amount of weight loss, or outward sign such as a visibly hollow abdomen for recognizing this condition.

The root consequently cannot produce a fixed bodily test restricting makhmaṣah to people with visibly drawn-in bellies.

The role of ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin

The passage continues:

غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ

ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin

This expression describes the compelled person’s condition or orientation.

Mutajānif comes from ج ن ف, concerning inclination and deviation.

The person is described as not inclining toward sin.

The verse distinguishes someone forced into difficulty from someone deliberately inclining toward wrongdoing.

This qualification is independent of makhmaṣah’s meaning.

Study of J N F

Nor should it become an impossible condition requiring a severely hungry person to perform calculations beyond their real capacity before preserving life.

The verse recognizes necessity without equating it with deliberate inclination toward sin.

Other verses concerning necessity

Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Anʿām 6:145, and Al-Naḥl 16:115 also discuss necessity after mentioning prohibited foods.

These passages use غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ, “neither seeking transgression nor exceeding bounds.”

Only Al-Māʾidah 5:3 uses makhmaṣah in this food-related setting.

Qur’anic necessity is thus not restricted to one linguistic expression.

Al-Māʾidah names severe hunger as a specific condition capable of leading to necessity.

The other passages discuss necessity and its qualifications without repeating this word.

Its absence does not exclude hunger from the other verses’ applications of necessity.

Its presence in Al-Māʾidah likewise does not alone prove that necessity in every ruling and life situation arises solely from hunger.

A separate study of permitted and prohibited matters must examine the concession’s scope through the relevant verses together.

The lexical conclusion is narrower: makhmaṣah here denotes hunger and food shortage within a context of necessity.

Al-Ṭabarī: necessity and the qualification ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin

5. Hunger alongside Thirst and Fatigue: Al-Tawbah 9:120

مَا كَانَ لِأَهْلِ الْمَدِينَةِ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهُمْ مِنَ الْأَعْرَابِ أَنْ يَتَخَلَّفُوا عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَرْغَبُوا بِأَنْفُسِهِمْ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ لَا يُصِيبُهُمْ ظَمَأٌ وَلَا نَصَبٌ وَلَا مَخْمَصَةٌ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَطَئُونَ مَوْطِئًا يَغِيظُ الْكُفَّارَ وَلَا يَنَالُونَ مِنْ عَدُوٍّ نَيْلًا إِلَّا كُتِبَ لَهُمْ بِهِ عَمَلٌ صَالِحٌ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُضِيعُ أَجْرَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

mā kāna li-ahli al-madīnati wa-man ḥawlahum mina al-aʿrābi an yatakhallafū ʿan rasūli allāhi wa-lā yarghabū bi-anfusihim ʿan nafsihi dhālika bi-annahum lā yuṣībuhum ẓamaʾun wa-lā naṣabun wa-lā makhmaṣatun fī sabīli allāhi wa-lā yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan yaghīẓu al-kuffāra wa-lā yanālūna min ʿaduwwin naylan illā kutiba lahum bihi ʿamalun ṣāliḥun inna allāha lā yuḍīʿu ajra al-muḥsinīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“It was not fitting for the people of Medina and the desert Arabs around them to remain behind the Messenger of The God or prefer their own lives to his. No thirst, fatigue, or hunger befalls them in the way of The God, nor do they tread ground that angers the disbelievers, nor obtain any gain against an enemy, without a righteous deed being recorded for them through it. The God does not let the reward of those who do good go to waste.”

Al-Tawbah 9:120

Makhmaṣatun is indefinite and nominative.

It appears alongside two other nouns:

Ẓamaʾ means thirst.

Naṣab means hardship or fatigue.

Makhmaṣah means hunger and lack of food.

These express three different hardships.

Someone may be thirsty while having food, or fatigued through long activity without severe hunger.

Food shortage can also accompany thirst and fatigue.

Their coordination does not make them synonyms; they identify different aspects of hardship.

Hunger in the way of The God

Fī sabīli allāhi is decisive here.

The Qur’an does not refer indiscriminately to every hunger, but to hunger experienced while accompanying and striving in the way of The God.

The addressees are the people of Medina and surrounding desert Arabs; the subject is not failing to accompany the Messenger of The God.

The verse then lists hardships that can arise along that course.

Being “in the way of The God” is not part of makhmaṣah itself; it comes from fī sabīli allāhi and the whole verse.

This distinction matters.

Hunger may arise from poverty, illness, famine, travel, or other circumstances. Hunger itself does not determine whether someone follows a right or wrong path.

Fulfilling responsibility gives the hardship its particular significance here.

Does the verse make hunger a goal?

It does not instruct people to keep themselves hungry deliberately or make hunger itself a religious goal.

Rather, thirst, fatigue, or makhmaṣah encountered in the responsibility under discussion will not be overlooked.

The verse concludes:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُضِيعُ أَجْرَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

inna allāha lā yuḍīʿu ajra al-muḥsinīna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “The God does not let the reward of those who do good go to waste.”

The value concerns the whole action and its direction; lack of food is not inherently desirable in every circumstance.

This agrees with Al-Māʾidah 5:3, which addresses real human limitations in hunger-driven necessity.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 therefore does not require someone to refuse permitted food in a dangerous emergency.

The verses have different settings: hardship endured while fulfilling responsibility, and compulsion caused by hunger.

Must makhmaṣah here mean near-fatal hunger?

Here it accompanies thirst and fatigue.

This concerns real suffering, but the verse sets no numerical or bodily measure of severity.

Every instance of makhmaṣah in this context therefore cannot confidently be equated with being on the verge of death.

Equally, it should not be reduced to trivial hunger: its familiar usage concerns hunger and food deprivation, and it appears among real hardships.

“Hunger” is accurate in the base translation; its severity and difficult setting can be explained separately.

Al-Ṭabarī: hunger, thirst, and fatigue in the context of accompanying the Messenger

6. Comparison with Related Roots

KH M Ṣ (khamṣ) and J W ʿ (jūʿ)

Arabic jūʿ means hunger.

Qur’anic examples include Al-Baqarah 2:155, Al-Naḥl 16:112, and Quraysh 106:4.

Jūʿ can denote hunger or lack of food generally, without centering the belly’s shape and hollowness.

خ م ص lexically concerns reduced prominence and hollowness; familiar makhmaṣah denotes hunger or famine.

The families overlap, but their lexical imagery differs.

No absolute rule makes every jūʿ mild and every makhmaṣah fatal.

Severity requires the word, its familiar usage, and the verse’s context.

Study of J W ʿ

KH M Ṣ (makhmaṣah) and S GH B (masghabah)

Saghab concerns hunger accompanied by suffering or hardship.

Al-Balad 90:14 reads:

أَوْ إِطْعَامٌ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ

aw iṭʿāmun fī yawmin dhī masghabatin

The verse describes feeding on a day marked by hunger and difficulty.

Masghabah and makhmaṣah are close in reference to food shortage.

Makhmaṣah’s root field concerns the hollow or empty belly, while familiar masghabah concerns hunger’s difficulty and suffering.

Al-Ṭabarī also uses shiddat al-saghab when explaining makhmaṣah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3.

Both can therefore describe severe hunger.

Neither should invariably be placed above or below the other in severity without evidence.

KH M Ṣ (khamṣ) and Ẓ M ʾ (ẓamaʾ)

Ẓamaʾ concerns thirst.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 places ẓamaʾ and makhmaṣah together.

The Qur’an distinguishes lack of water from lack of food.

People may experience both, but their lexical meanings differ.

Makhmaṣah should therefore not mean “thirst,” nor should all hardships caused by lack of water be folded into it.

KH M Ṣ (khamṣ) and Ḍ M R (ḍumūr)

Ḍamara concerns becoming lean, reduced in bulk, or contracted in body.

Ibn Fāris uses ḍumr to define khamṣ.

Ḍamara can express a broader field of leanness or reduced bulk, whereas familiar khamṣ especially concerns the belly’s hollowness and the arch of the foot.

Qur’anic makhmaṣah also means hunger, not natural slenderness alone.

The families are semantically related, but not every derivative is interchangeable.

KH M Ṣ (khamṣ) and KH M S (khams)

خ م س is a separate root concerning five.

خ م ص concerns hollowness and, in Qur’anic usage, hunger.

Similar pronunciation and two shared letters do not give them the same meaning.

Khumus in Al-Anfāl 8:41 means one fifth; makhmaṣah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 describes hunger.

Sound resemblance alone establishes no independent semantic or historical relationship.

Study of KH M S

KH M Ṣ (khamṣ) and Ḥ M Ṣ (ḥumūṣ)

Some Arabic sources record both ḥamaṣa al-jurḥu and khamaṣa al-jurḥu for a wound’s swelling subsiding.

Yaʿqūb and other lexicographers discuss them in connection with consonant substitution.

Ibn Jinnī, however, is cautious about deriving either necessarily from the other: both have established uses and inflected forms.

Similar verbs differing in ḥāʾ and khāʾ do not establish either one’s historical priority.

Sound resemblance requires independent historical and morphological evidence in root research.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Both Qur’anic applications need an equivalent concerning hunger, with severity explained through context.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, fī makhmaṣatin accompanies uḍṭurra. “In severe hunger” or “under the pressure of hunger” are therefore suitable.

In Al-Tawbah 9:120, makhmaṣatun accompanies thirst and fatigue. “Hunger” is natural and clear in the base translation, while explanation can describe the hardship and real lack of food.

Makhmaṣah should not become mere leanness or a hollow belly: hunger is the preferred meaning in both verses.

Conversely, no unsupported number of days without food, fatal outcome, or visible hollow abdomen should enter its direct meaning.

Brief root meaning

Hollowness and reduced prominence, especially of the belly and sole; in food-related uses, hunger and an empty belly.

Meanings in the verses

Makhmaṣatin — Al-Māʾidah 5:3: severe hunger and food deprivation placing someone under necessity.

Makhmaṣatun — Al-Tawbah 9:120: hunger and lack of food experienced during effort and responsibility.

These definitions keep the physical core distinct from the specific Qur’anic meaning.

Conclusion

Arabic خ م ص concerns hollowness, reduced prominence, and diminished bulk, seen in a slender or empty belly, the foot’s arch, swelling subsiding, and other uses.

Early khamīṣ can describe hunger or natural bodily shape. Al-Aʿshā and Al-Nābighah show how context distinguishes them.

Familiar makhmaṣah, however, means hunger or famine, and the Qur’an uses it in that sense.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, hunger appears under necessity. In Al-Tawbah 9:120, it is a hardship alongside thirst and fatigue.

The fundamental root meaning is hollowness and reduced prominence, while Qur’anic makhmaṣah means hunger and deprivation caused by lack of food.

This preserves the word’s physical image without equating natural slenderness, ordinary hunger, widespread famine, and life-threatening necessity. Brief root meaning: Hollowness and reduced prominence, especially of the belly and sole; in food-related uses, hunger and an empty belly.
