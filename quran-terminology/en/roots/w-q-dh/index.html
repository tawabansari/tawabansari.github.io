Severe blows, incapacitation, and fatal injury

The Arabic root w-q-dh concerns striking a severe blow that leaves a person or animal weakened, incapacitated, or near death. Waqdh denotes a hard, injurious blow; waqīdh describes someone brought low by a blow or severe weakness; and mawqūdh means severely struck or injured.

For animals, mawqūdhah describes an animal seriously injured or killed by a heavy blow, such as from wood or stone. In a list of carrion and other dead animals, it denotes one that has died from such a blow.

The root is not restricted to killing. Arabic also uses the family for overwhelming sleep, severe illness, grief, and exhaustion: conditions that can weaken a person as though struck by a heavy blow.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as الْمَوْقُوذَةُ in al-Māʾidah 5:3.

1. Early and classical Arabic usage

In early Arabic, waqadhahu denotes a blow with a severe effect. Mere contact or touching is insufficient: the blow seriously injures or incapacitates the person or animal.

Arabic dictionaries define waqdh in terms of the severity of a blow and record waqadhahu yaqidhuhu waqdhan for striking to the point of weakness, incapacitation, or near death.

The meaning also occurs in descriptions of animals. Shātun mawqūdhatun describes a sheep seriously injured or killed by a blow, particularly from wood or another blunt object.

The effect matters. Gentle contact or a blow causing no serious injury is not a clear example of waqdh.

One poetic witness is attributed to al-Aʿshā, a well-known pre-Islamic Arabic poet:

يَلْوِينَني دَيْني النَّهارَ وَأَقْتَضِي
دَيْني إِذا وَقَذَ النُّعاسُ الرُّقَّدا

yalwīnanī daynī al-nahāra wa-aqtaḍī / daynī idhā waqadha al-nuʿāsu al-ruqqadā

Translation: “By day they defer what they owe me; I collect my due when drowsiness has overcome the sleepers.”

In this line, وَقَذَ النُّعاسُ الرُّقَّدا describes sleep strongly overcoming those asleep.

The poet does not say that sleep has killed them. It has overpowered them, reducing their wakefulness and ability to respond.

This illustrates extension from a bodily blow to a condition that diminishes human strength and interrupts activity.

The verse is transmitted in the poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā and in dictionaries. Some versions differ in the first half’s wording, while the phrase وَقَذَ النُّعاسُ الرُّقَّدا is retained in the witnesses considered here. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently establish the precise date of composition or a particular pre-Islamic year or period.

Other recorded expressions include:

وَقَذَهُ الْمَرَضُ: illness laid him low.

وَقَذَهُ الْغَمُّ: grief severely weakened and exhausted him.

These expressions are recorded in dictionaries, but independent, datable pre-Qur’anic witnesses are not available here for all of them. Their occurrence in classical Arabic can be stated without assigning a certain origin date to each usage.

Overall, the evidence concerns a severe blow or overwhelming pressure that diminishes a person’s or animal’s strength. An animal’s death from a blow is one specific application of this semantic field.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal recorded Arabic forms include:

  • وَقَذَ: struck a severe blow; seriously injured a person or animal.
  • وَقْذ: a severe, incapacitating blow.
  • وَقِيذ: a person or animal brought low or near death by a blow or severe weakness.
  • مَوْقُوذ: struck, seriously injured, or incapacitated.
  • مَوْقُوذَة: feminine of mawqūdh; for an animal, seriously injured or killed by a severe blow.
  • مُوَقَّذَة: feminine Form II passive participle; recorded of a she-camel whose teats have been injured by tight binding.
  • مَوْقِذ: a bodily location where a blow can cause serious injury.
  • مَوَاقِذ: plural of mawqidh, vulnerable bodily locations in relation to blows.
  • وَقِيذَة: in another usage, a stone among stones laid out on the ground; its recorded plural is وَقَائِذ.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Its sole form is الْمَوْقُوذَةُ.

Mawqūdhah is the feminine passive participle of the Form I verb waqadha. The masculine form is mawqūdh.

The root’s initial wāw remains in the passive participle. In the imperfect yaqidhu—written يَقِذُ—it is absent, as in some other Form I verbs beginning with wāw.

The final tāʾ in al-mawqūdhah marks grammatical femininity, corresponding to an understood noun such as shāh or bahīmah. It does not itself mean a female sheep; in context it labels an animal affected by that kind of injury.

3. Qur’anic usage: al-mawqūdhah in al-Māʾidah 5:3

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ وَأَنْ تَسْتَقْسِمُوا بِالْأَزْلَامِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ فِسْقٌ ۗ الْيَوْمَ يَئِسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ دِينِكُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِ ۚ الْيَوْمَ أَكْمَلْتُ لَكُمْ دِينَكُمْ وَأَتْمَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعْمَتِي وَرَضِيتُ لَكُمُ الْإِسْلَامَ دِينًا ۚ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ ۙ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykumu al-maytatu wa-al-damu wa-laḥmu al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri Allāhi bihi wa-al-munkhaniqatu wa-al-mawqūdhatu wa-al-mutaraddiyatu wa-al-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum wa-mā dhubiḥa ʿalā al-nuṣubi wa-an tastaqsimū bi-al-azlāmi dhālikum fisqun al-yawma yaʾisa alladhīna kafarū min dīnikum fa-lā takhshawhum wa-ikhshawn al-yawma akmaltu lakum dīnakum wa-atmamtu ʿalaykum niʿmatī wa-raḍītu lakumu al-islāma dīnan fa-mani uḍṭurra fī makhmaṣatin ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin fa-inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīm

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

Translation: “Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that over which a name other than The God has been invoked; the strangled, that killed by a blow, the fallen, the gored, and what a predator has eaten—except what you slaughter—and what is slaughtered on erected stones, and seeking portions through arrows. That is transgression. Today those who denied have despaired of your religion, so do not fear them, but fear Me. Today I have perfected your religion for you, completed My favour upon you, and approved Islam as your religion. Whoever is compelled by severe hunger, without inclining toward sin—indeed, The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

Al-mawqūdhah appears among several labels for animals injured or killed through particular circumstances or events.

The Qur’an mentions al-munkhaniqah before it, and al-mutaraddiyah and al-naṭīḥah after it.

This arrangement matters because each label distinguishes a particular condition.

Al-munkhaniqah concerns strangulation. The central problem is obstructed breathing and lack of air.

Al-mawqūdhah concerns an animal brought down by a severe blow, such as from wood or stone.

Al-mutaraddiyah concerns an animal that has fallen.

Al-naṭīḥah describes an animal struck by another animal’s horn.

Wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu concerns an animal from which a predator has eaten.

The incident differs in each case, although bodily consequences may overlap. A falling animal may suffer a severe impact on the ground; a gored animal may likewise be brought down by the blow.

The Qur’an nevertheless gives these conditions separate names. They should not all be translated simply as “an animal struck by a blow.”

Al-mawqūdhah identifies injury by a severe blow; al-mutaraddiyah specifically identifies falling; al-naṭīḥah identifies another animal’s horn.

Al-mawqūdhah is also not restricted to a particular animal such as a cow, sheep, or camel. The root does not identify an animal’s sex or species.

4. Is every animal that has been struck mawqūdhah?

A blow, serious injury, and death must be distinguished.

An animal may collide with an obstacle without serious harm. This is not a clear example of waqdh, whose principal usage emphasizes severe injury.

A harder blow may wound or weaken an animal while it remains alive.

In a third situation, the blow is so severe that the animal dies from it.

Linguistically, waqadha can describe serious injury bringing a person or animal near death without death having occurred. Usages concerning illness, incapacitation, and overwhelming sleep confirm that death is not an invariant meaning.

In al-Māʾidah 5:3, however, the word occurs in a dietary prohibition. An animal that has died from a severe blow is therefore a clear instance of the category.

The distinction matters: death is the outcome relevant to the dietary prohibition, while the root’s core concerns a severe blow and the incapacitation it causes.

Al-mawqūdhah should neither be extended to every animal ever struck nor used to reduce the entire root to “carrion.”

5. The exception illā mā dhakkaytum

After naming several conditions involving injured or dead animals, the Qur’an states:

«إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ»

illā mā dhakkaytum

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

This phrase belongs to a different root, dh-k-w, and concerns tadhkiyah, or slaughter.

The exception matters for understanding al-mawqūdhah.

Suppose a sheep is seriously injured by wood or stone but remains alive. Reaching it before death is relevant to the possibility of tadhkiyah; being injured and already being dead are distinct conditions.

If the sheep has already died from the blow, cutting its throat afterwards does not turn it into an animal slaughtered while alive.

There is thus a distinction between an animal that died from a blow and one struck but slaughtered before death.

Illā mā dhakkaytum highlights that distinction in the verse’s context.

Syntactically, the exception follows several injury-related categories. Earlier explanations differ over its precise scope, but the central issue here is reaching the animal before death and performing tadhkiyah. This lexical distinction alone does not settle every case of severe injury.

The phrase does not specify detailed medical signs of life, the slaughtering instrument, or every technical condition. Nor does it imply that all other prohibitions in the verse, including those concerning the animal’s kind or the ritual dedication of slaughter, automatically disappear.

For a detailed examination of dhakkaytum and distinctions within its family, Forqan’s separate study of dh-k-w is available.

Study of dh-k-w

6. Challenges of interpretation and translation

One translation problem is rendering al-mawqūdhah merely as “a beaten animal.”

The Persian expression katak-khordeh is more commonly used of a person and need not convey serious injury or death. It could suggest that the flesh of any animal ever struck is prohibited.

“Killed by a blow” is clearer in al-Māʾidah 5:3 because it conveys both the incident and the relevant outcome.

The root study should nevertheless explain that w-q-dh concerns severe impact and incapacitation, with death being a possible consequence.

Restricting the word to a wooden implement is also inaccurate. Wood and stone are familiar lexical examples, but the core meaning does not depend on the implement’s material. The severe blow and its injury are what matter.

Conversely, the word alone should not yield a general ruling on every use of blunt instruments or all human treatment of animals. The verse directly concerns the specified dietary prohibition. Animal cruelty, responsibility for care, and proper treatment require their own relevant evidence.

7. Comparison with related roots

W-q-dh (waqdh) and ḍ-r-b (ḍarb): striking and an incapacitating blow

Ḍarb has a broad range. In bodily contexts it can mean striking, but not every blow is severe or fatal.

Waqdh emphasizes a blow causing serious injury, incapacitation, or near death.

Waqdh is therefore more specific in this context. An incapacitating blow is an instance of ḍarb, but not every ḍarb is waqdh.

W-q-dh (waqdh) and q-t-l (qatl): serious injury and killing

Qatl concerns killing or taking life; the root itself does not specify the method.

Someone may be killed by a blow, strangulation, or another means.

Waqdh specifies the injury in terms of a severe blow. This can cause death, but in some non-Qur’anic usages the person remains alive and severely weakened.

Mawqūdhah in al-Māʾidah thus concerns an animal whose death or fatal injury relates to a severe blow, rather than every animal killed by any means.

Study of q-t-l

W-q-dh (waqdh) and kh-n-q (khanq): impact and strangulation

Khanq concerns pressure on the throat or respiratory passage and strangulation.

Waqdh emphasizes a severe blow.

An animal may die from obstructed breathing without being struck, or from a heavy blow without being strangled.

The adjacent terms al-munkhaniqah and al-mawqūdhah in al-Māʾidah 5:3 make this distinction clear.

Study of kh-n-q

W-q-dh (waqdh) and r-d-y (rady): impact injury and falling

R-d-y in al-mutaraddiyah concerns falling.

An animal may fall from a height and die on impact. Physically, the event may involve a severe blow.

Al-mutaraddiyah, however, highlights the fall, while al-mawqūdhah identifies a severe blow as the cause of injury.

The categories may overlap in consequence without having identical lexical meanings.

Study of r-d-y

W-q-dh (waqdh) and n-ṭ-ḥ (naṭḥ): severe blows and goring

Naṭḥ concerns striking with a horn.

In al-naṭīḥah, another animal’s horn causes the injury.

Al-mawqūdhah does not restrict the implement to a horn. In its familiar usage, wood, stone, or another blunt implement is prominent.

The words occur separately in al-Māʾidah 5:3 to specify the incident.

Study of n-ṭ-ḥ

W-q-dh (waqdh) and dh-b-ḥ (dhabḥ): felling by a blow and slaughtering

In animal contexts, dhabḥ refers to cutting at the recognized site of slaughter.

Waqdh concerns a severe blow and does not inherently involve cutting or splitting.

An animal killed with wood or stone differs in manner of death from one slaughtered. This distinction also matters for illā mā dhakkaytum.

Study of dh-b-ḥ

W-q-dh (waqdh) and w-q-d (waqd): severe impact and kindling fire

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

W-q-d concerns kindling and burning fire; waqūd and mūqadah belong to it.

W-q-dh concerns severe impact and incapacitation.

Mawqūdhah and some w-q-d forms may look similar, but the roots are independent. Fire, burning, and ignition should not be imported into al-mawqūdhah.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For waqadha in bodily contexts, suitable equivalents are “struck a heavy blow,” “felled with a severe blow,” or, where death is intended, “killed by a blow.”

For waqdh, “severe blow” or “incapacitating blow” is clear.

For waqīdh, use “seriously injured,” “laid low,” or “near death” according to context. Where illness or weakness is intended, “struck” should not be added without evidence.

For mawqūdh, “injured by a blow” or “incapacitated by a blow” is appropriate.

The principal proposal for al-mawqūdhah in al-Māʾidah 5:3 is:

“Killed by a blow.”

This is concise, natural, and suited to the dietary context.

“Dead from a severe blow” is also accurate, but “killed by a blow” reads more smoothly in a continuous translation.

Commentary should explain that the root denotes severe impact and incapacitating injury, while death is the outcome relevant in this verse.

9. Conclusion

Arabic w-q-dh concerns a severe blow and its incapacitating effect. It can leave a person or animal weakened, exhausted, near death, or dead.

Extended usages apply the family to overwhelming sleep, illness, grief, or other conditions that diminish human strength. The verse attributed to al-Aʿshā about sleep overcoming sleepers clearly illustrates this extension.

The Qur’an uses only al-mawqūdhah, in al-Māʾidah 5:3. Beside animals affected by strangulation, falling, goring, and predation, it identifies an animal that has died from a severe blow within the dietary prohibition.

Illā mā dhakkaytum distinguishes an already dead animal from an injured animal still alive and capable of being slaughtered.

The root should not be generalized to every blow or equated with death or killing in every context. A particular implement—wood, stone, or horn—is also not a fixed component of its meaning, although such implements matter in familiar examples or neighbouring words in the verse.

W-q-dh denotes a severe blow with a weakening or incapacitating effect. In Qur’anic usage, al-mawqūdhah refers to an animal that has died from such a blow. The dietary ruling and the exception for tadhkiyah are understood from the verse as a whole, rather than the root alone.
