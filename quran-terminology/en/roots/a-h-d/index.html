The Qur’anic Root ʾ Ḥ D

Aḥad: one, one of, and no one under negation

The Qur’anic root أ ح د, ʾ Ḥ D, appears principally in أَحَد, aḥad, and its feminine form إِحْدَى, iḥdā. Depending on the construction, aḥad can convey “one,” “one of,” “someone,” or, in a negative sentence, “no one.” Iḥdā likewise occurs in expressions such as “one of the two” or “one of…”.

This range shows why aḥad cannot be defined from the outset solely as “the uniquely One” in its theological sense. Most Qur’anic occurrences do not concern The God at all:

يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

Yawaddu aḥaduhum law yuʿammaru alfa sanah.

“One of them wishes to be granted a thousand years of life.”

Al-Baqarah 2:96

And:

أَيُحِبُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ يَأْكُلَ لَحْمَ أَخِيهِ مَيْتًا

A-yuḥibbu aḥadukum an yaʾkula laḥma akhīhi maytā.

“Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother?”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12

In such constructions, أحدهم, aḥaduhum, and أحدكم, aḥadukum, mean one individual from the group indicated by the pronoun.

By contrast, in a negative sentence:

فَإِنْ لَمْ تَجِدُوا فِيهَا أَحَدًا فَلَا تَدْخُلُوهَا

Fa-in lam tajidū fīhā aḥadan fa-lā tadkhulūhā.

“If you find no one in them, do not enter them.”

Al-Nūr 24:28

And:

فَلَا تَدْعُوا مَعَ اللَّهِ أَحَدًا

Fa-lā tadʿū maʿa llāhi aḥadā.

“So do not call upon anyone alongside The God.”

Al-Jinn 72:18

the same word acquires an inclusive scope.

The shared core belongs to the field of “one.” In uses concerning people, the sentence determines whether the reference is to an individual from a group, any possible individual, or no individual under negation. This explanation should not restrict every use to membership in a set.

Linguistic background and the relationship between aḥad and wāḥid

Aḥad was not coined by the Qur’an. Cognate forms meaning “one” have a very ancient history in Semitic languages. Hebrew אֶחָד, ʾeḥād, for example, belongs to this field. Arabic lexical sources also relate aḥad to wāḥid.

This connection has long been recognized in Arabic linguistic discussion, but the proximity of the two forms should not be turned into a simple, certain account of their historical development. For understanding the Qur’an, their actual behavior in Qur’anic Arabic matters more than reconstructing their distant history.

The religious use of the Hebrew cognate in Deuteronomy 6:4 is a pre-Qur’anic example. It belongs to Hebrew, and should not be treated as direct evidence for Arabic aḥad in a pre-Qur’anic text. A precise claim about religious usage in Arabic requires a datable Arabic text or inscription. This comparison establishes the family’s antiquity, not direct dependence of the Qur’anic expression on a particular text.

Conversely, the word’s ancient history should not diminish the distinctive use it receives in Al-Ikhlāṣ. A word’s antiquity and the meaning a particular text gives it in a particular construction are separate matters.

Syntax: from selecting one individual to inclusive negation

One principal use of aḥad selects an individual from a group:

قَالَ أَحَدُهُمَا إِنِّي أَرَانِي أَعْصِرُ خَمْرًا

Qāla aḥaduhumā innī arānī aʿṣiru khamrā.

“One of the two said: I see myself pressing wine.”

Yūsuf 12:36

And:

أَمَّا أَحَدُكُمَا فَيَسْقِي رَبَّهُ خَمْرًا

Ammā aḥadukumā fa-yasqī rabbahu khamrā.

“As for one of you two, he will serve his master wine.”

Yūsuf 12:41

And:

فَخُذْ أَحَدَنَا مَكَانَهُ

Fa-khudh aḥadanā makānah.

“Take one of us in his place.”

Yūsuf 12:78

In these constructions, aḥad singles out a member of a group.

When the word enters a negative construction, however, the issue is no longer selecting one particular individual: every possible individual falls within the scope of the negation:

وَمَا هُمْ بِضَارِّينَ بِهِ مِنْ أَحَدٍ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ

Wa-mā hum bi-ḍārrīna bihi min aḥadin illā bi-idhni llāh.

“They harm no one with it except by The God’s permission.”

Al-Baqarah 2:102

And:

فَمَا مِنْكُمْ مِنْ أَحَدٍ عَنْهُ حَاجِزِينَ

Fa-mā minkum min aḥadin ʿanhu ḥājizīn.

“None of you could protect him from [this punishment].”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:47

The word is singular in form, but the sentence’s scope encompasses all possible individuals.

The same behavior appears in:

لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْهُمْ

Lā nufarriqu bayna aḥadin minhum.

“We make no distinction between any of them.”

Al-Baqarah 2:136

Bayna, “between,” requires a relationship involving more than one party, while aḥad under negation extends to “not any one.” The point is therefore not merely that one particular individual is not distinguished from another: no member of the group falls outside the negation’s scope.

Lexical accounts distinguish aḥad under negation from its numerical uses and its uses in possessive or partitive constructions. The statement that “aḥad is confined to negation” should therefore not be understood as a rule about every occurrence of this form. A condition is also different from explicit negation, but it does not make an affirmative factual assertion about an existing individual. The Qur’an says:

وَإِنْ أَحَدٌ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ اسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ

Wa-in aḥadun mina l-mushrikīna stajāraka fa-ajirh.

“If one of those who associate partners seeks your protection, grant him protection.”

Al-Tawbah 9:6

The individual’s arrival is supposed here, rather than reported as having occurred. By contrast, forms such as aḥaduhum, aḥadukumā, and aḥadunā refer to one member of a group. Negative, conditional, partitive, and numerical uses must therefore be distinguished. The conditional verse alone does not show that aḥad can freely occur in every affirmative declarative sentence.

The feminine form also expresses the relation “one member of a group”:

أَنْ تَضِلَّ إِحْدَاهُمَا فَتُذَكِّرَ إِحْدَاهُمَا الْأُخْرَىٰ

An taḍilla iḥdāhumā fa-tudhakkira iḥdāhumā l-ukhrā.

“So that if one of the two errs [in recalling the testimony], one of them may remind the other.”

Al-Baqarah 2:282

And:

وَإِذْ يَعِدُكُمُ اللَّهُ إِحْدَى الطَّائِفَتَيْنِ أَنَّهَا لَكُمْ

Wa-idh yaʿidukumu llāhu iḥdā ṭ-ṭāʾifatayni annahā lakum.

“When The God promised you that one of the two groups would be yours.”

Al-Anfāl 8:7

And:

إِنَّهَا لَإِحْدَى الْكُبَرِ

Innahā la-iḥdā l-kubar.

“It is indeed one of the great matters.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:35

In these uses, iḥdā is the feminine form of “one” used in selecting from a group. This is grammatical gender and does not necessarily refer to a person’s sex: ṭāʾifah, “group,” and plural forms such as al-kubar, “the great matters,” also govern the feminine choice.

Aḥad also occurs within a compound number:

إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا

Innī raʾaytu aḥada ʿashara kawkabā.

“I saw eleven stars.”

Yūsuf 12:4

Aḥada ʿashara here is a compound number, with no special theological force of its own.

Together these uses show that aḥad’s meaning is not determined by its form alone. Its relationship to a group, negation, a condition, a number, and its syntactic position all play an essential role in translation.

Qul huwa llāhu aḥad: aḥad describing The God

The word’s most distinctive use occurs at the opening of Al-Ikhlāṣ:

قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

Qul huwa llāhu aḥad.

“Say: He, The God, is uniquely One.”

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:1

Several issues must be distinguished in understanding this expression.

First, أَحَدٌ, aḥadun, does not mean “one of…” here. In ordinary “one of” uses, a pronoun, a possessive construction, or an expression such as min, “from,” identifies the group:

أحدهم

Aḥaduhum.

“One of them”

أحد منكم

Aḥadun minkum.

“One of you”

But in:

اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

Allāhu aḥad.

there is no group of which The God is presented as one member.

Second, the absence of al- does not make aḥadun mean “an unspecified individual among several.” A definite subject with an indefinite predicate is normal in an Arabic nominal sentence. The Qur’an can likewise say of The God:

إِنَّ رَبِّي رَحِيمٌ وَدُودٌ

Inna rabbī raḥīmun wadūd.

Hūd 11:90

without raḥīm, “merciful,” and wadūd, “loving,” ceasing to describe The God because they lack al-.

The verse’s precise form therefore matters:

اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

Allāhu aḥad.

rather than:

اللَّهُ الْأَحَدُ

Allāhu l-aḥad.

If al-Aḥad became established as a divine name in later usage, that historical usage should not replace the verse’s actual construction. In the Qur’an itself, aḥad functions here as a predicate and description of The God, without al-.

Third, later philosophical and theological systems should not be placed inside the word itself. Divine simplicity, non-composition, philosophical indivisibility, and other theories about the divine nature are not aḥad’s direct lexical meaning.

The word belongs to the field of “one.” What carries this oneness beyond simple counting in Al-Ikhlāṣ is the surah’s complete structure:

اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ

Allāhu ṣ-ṣamad.

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:2

Then:

لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ

Lam yalid wa-lam yūlad.

“He has neither begotten nor been begotten.”

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:3

And at the end:

وَلَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ

Wa-lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad.

“No one has been equal to Him.”

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112:4

The important point is that the same aḥad occurs at both ends of the surah.

At its opening:

اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

Allāhu aḥad.

And at its close:

لَمْ يَكُنْ لَهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ

Lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad.

At the opening, aḥad describes The God. At the close, the existence of any other aḥad who could be كُفُوًا لَهُ, kufuwan lahu, equal to Him, is denied.

Incomparability in Al-Ikhlāṣ therefore does not come solely from one word’s dictionary definition: the surah itself establishes it. The God is aḥad, neither begotten nor begetting, and no one is His equal.

For this reason, “uniquely One” is a suitable rendering of اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ, Allāhu aḥad. “One” is close to the word’s core, but can be heard merely as a numerical count. “Uniquely One” can better preserve what the whole surah makes clear about this oneness without importing later philosophical concepts into the translation.

ʾ Ḥ D (aḥad) and W Ḥ D (wāḥid)

The Qur’an uses both aḥad and wāḥid of The God, but the words do not appear in exactly the same constructions.

Concerning the object of worship, it says:

وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ الرَّحِيمُ

Wa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥidun lā ilāha illā huwa r-raḥmānu r-raḥīm.

“Your God is one God; there is no god except Him, the Raḥmān, the Raḥīm.”

Al-Baqarah 2:163

Here wāḥid qualifies ilāh, “god,” and stands against the possibility of plurality.

Elsewhere:

أَأَرْبَابٌ مُتَفَرِّقُونَ خَيْرٌ أَمِ اللَّهُ الْوَاحِدُ الْقَهَّارُ

A-arbābun mutafarriqūna khayrun ami llāhu l-wāḥidu l-qahhār.

“Are scattered lords better, or The God, the One, the All-Subduing?”

Yūsuf 12:39

The contrast between arbābun mutafarriqūn, “scattered lords,” and al-wāḥid, “the One,” is explicit.

Al-Ikhlāṣ, by contrast, says:

اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

Allāhu aḥad.

The difference does not establish that “wāḥid is only a number, while aḥad is not numerical at all.” Aḥad also participates in counting through aḥada ʿashara, “eleven,” and wāḥid is not always a bare numeral without further significance.

Their tendencies in use are nevertheless not identical.

Wāḥid readily sets a thing or a god against plurality:

طَعَامٍ وَاحِدٍ

Ṭaʿāmin wāḥid.

“One kind of food”

إِلَٰهًا وَاحِدًا

Ilāhan wāḥidā.

“One god”

Aḥad, by contrast, is prominent in three Qur’anic fields: one individual from a group; any or no individual in constructions with inclusive scope; and the distinctive use Allāhu aḥad.

This behavior gives aḥad a particular capacity in Al-Ikhlāṣ. The same word that can encompass every possible individual under negation is used at the opening to describe The God and at the close to deny every possible equal.

It is nevertheless preferable not to say that “aḥad is inherently stronger than wāḥid.” That would turn a difference in use into an intrinsic ranking of the words. What can be said is that the Qur’an uses them in different constructions, and aḥad has a role within Al-Ikhlāṣ’s internal relationships whose meaning emerges from the surah as a whole.

Likewise, classifying ʾ Ḥ D and W Ḥ D as separate entries for morphological analysis should not be confused with linguistic history. Treating them separately when examining Qur’anic usage is compatible with their very ancient historical relationship.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Aḥad has no single fixed English equivalent. Its translation must follow the sentence’s construction.

When joined to a pronoun or a group:

أَحَدُهُمَا

Aḥaduhumā.

“One of the two”

أَحَدُكُمْ

Aḥadukum.

“One of you”

In a condition:

وَإِنْ أَحَدٌ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ اسْتَجَارَكَ

Wa-in aḥadun mina l-mushrikīna stajārak.

“If one of those who associate partners seeks your protection…”

Under negation:

لَا يَخْشَوْنَ أَحَدًا إِلَّا اللَّهَ

Lā yakhshawna aḥadan illā llāh.

“They fear no one except The God.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:39

And:

فَلَا تَدْعُوا مَعَ اللَّهِ أَحَدًا

Fa-lā tadʿū maʿa llāhi aḥadā.

“So do not call upon anyone alongside The God.”

Al-Jinn 72:18

In:

لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْهُمْ

Lā nufarriqu bayna aḥadin minhum.

“We make no distinction between any of them.”

For iḥdā:

إِحْدَى الطَّائِفَتَيْنِ

Iḥdā ṭ-ṭāʾifatayn.

“One of the two groups”

And in Al-Ikhlāṣ:

قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

Qul huwa llāhu aḥad.

“Say: He, The God, is uniquely One.”

In this last case, “uniquely One” conveys the context more clearly than a bare “one,” which may suggest only numerical counting. Yet expressions such as “a simple essence,” “indivisible,” “non-composite,” or other philosophical terminology should not replace aḥad in the basic translation. The God’s incomparability becomes clear through the surah’s complete structure.

Conclusion

Aḥad’s core belongs to the field of one and oneness. In uses concerning individuals and instances, it refers to one individual or one instance; membership in a set is not, however, required by every use. The construction determines the scope of this “one”: with a possessive construction or pronoun, “one of”; in a condition, any individual to whom the condition applies; and under negation, “no one” or “not any one.” Syntax is therefore an essential part of its meaning in each verse.

Aḥad and wāḥid both belong to the field of “one” and are historically close, but their Qur’anic uses are not completely identical. Wāḥid particularly stands against plurality, while aḥad can express one member of a group and inclusive scope under negation. In Allāhu aḥad, the whole of Al-Ikhlāṣ clarifies the usage as oneness without an equal; all this theological force is not contained in the isolated word alone.

The main caution is not to replace Qur’anic aḥad with later philosophical and theological systems, while also acknowledging the word’s very ancient history. Aḥad belongs to a Semitic word family predating the Qur’an. In Al-Ikhlāṣ, however, the Qur’an places it in a specific network involving ṣamad, denial of begetting and being begotten, and denial of any equal. The verse’s meaning must be understood through the Qur’an’s own language and structure.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

ʾ Ḥ D: belonging to the field of “one” and oneness. Depending on the construction, this “one” can express “one of,” “someone,” “anyone,” or, under negation, “no one.” In Allāhu aḥad, the word describes The God, and the whole surah makes its direction clear as oneness without an equal.
