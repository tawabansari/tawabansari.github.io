Root و ز ر — Heavy burdens, responsibility, refuge, and assistance

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(wizr)

The Arabic root و ز ر has several semantic branches. The most important are وِزْر, a heavy burden and responsibility; وَزَر, a refuge; and وَزِير, a helper and supporter.

The Qur’an uses this family for actual material loads, the burden of sin and consequences of human conduct, responsibility for misleading others, the lifting of Muhammad’s heavy burden, the ending of war, the absence of refuge on Resurrection Day, and Aaron’s assistance to Moses.

These uses show why the root should not always be translated as “sin.” In Ṭā Hā 20:87, أَوْزَار concerns loads of ornaments; in Muḥammad 47:4, the burdens and equipment of war. In Al-Qiyāmah 75:11, وَزَر means refuge rather than a load.

Conversely, the association with burdens and heaviness does not make a وَزِير the bearer of another person’s sin. In Qur’anic usage, a wazīr helps with work and responsibility.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing material loads, moral responsibility, refuge, and assistance, then examining their actual connections.

1. The root’s meanings in early Arabic

Early Arabic evidence distinguishes two principal branches: burdens and heaviness, and refuge and safety.

وِزْر — Heavy loads and equipment

Arabic uses وِزْر for something whose carrying involves weight and effort.

This burden may consist of property, tools, equipment, or a responsibility someone shoulders.

One important witness is a line from a poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, praising Hawdhah ibn ʿAlī al-Ḥanafī:

فَأَعْدَدْتَ لِلْحَرْبِ أَوْزَارَهَا

fa-aʿdadta li-al-ḥarbi awzārahā

رِمَاحًا طِوَالًا وَخَيْلًا ذُكُورَا

rimāḥan ṭiwālan wa-khaylan dhukūrā

“You prepared the equipment of war: long spears and stallions.”

أَوْزَارَهَا here denotes the implements and equipment of war.

The poet names examples himself: spears and warhorses.

The word therefore does not mean sin or moral wrongdoing here. It concerns the resources prepared for warfare.

Some lexical transmissions give the opening as وَأَعْدَدْتُ. Ibn Barrī corrects it to فَأَعْدَدْتَ, with fatḥah on tāʾ, because the poet is addressing the person being praised.

This witness matters because it shows أَوْزَار being used for actual burdens and equipment in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The sense of a heavy burden also provides an intelligible basis for the moral use of وِزْر.

Someone may commit a wrong whose consequences and responsibility remain theirs, as though a load rested on their shoulders.

Al-Aʿshā’s military image alone does not establish that the moral sense historically arose after the material sense. Their chronological order requires independent evidence.

What is clear is the recognized semantic connection between a heavy load and a weighty responsibility.

وَزَر — Refuge and a secure mountain

The other branch is وَزَر, with fatḥah on both wāw and zāy.

Arabic uses this word for a refuge or place to which someone can turn in danger.

Lexicons identify a secure, inaccessible mountain as a principal example.

A line transmitted with varying attributions, including ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm and a Thaqafī poet, reads:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا إِنْ لَهُ صَخْرَةٌ

la-ʿamruka mā in lahu ṣakhratun

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا إِنْ لَهُ مِنْ وَزَرٍ

la-ʿamruka mā in lahu min wazarin

“By your life, he has no rock for shelter; by your life, he has no refuge.”

صَخْرَة and وَزَر occur together in this line.

A rock can offer shelter, while وَزَر expresses the broader sense of refuge.

The witness associates the word with seeking protection from danger in the Arabic literary tradition.

Its attribution and wording are not uniform. Some transmissions name ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm; the Sīrah tradition transmits a related line under a Thaqafī poet’s name, with variation in the first half-line.

It is therefore a witness with disputed attribution, not an unqualified, conclusive document of a particular pre-Islamic poet’s work.

Nevertheless, classical lexicons clearly record the refuge sense of وَزَر, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:11 agrees with it.

Do burden and refuge derive from one original meaning?

Some lexical explanations seek a connection between these branches.

A mountain, for example, is massive and heavy while also offering shelter. That image can suggest a relationship between weight and refuge.

Such an image does not establish that وِزْر meaning burden derives from وَزَر meaning mountain, or the reverse.

Both branches are recorded in Arabic, each with a distinct meaning.

Their historical definitions are therefore best kept separate:

وِزْر: a heavy burden; morally, the burden of sin and responsibility for it.

وَزَر: a refuge or safe place, especially a shelter such as a secure mountain.

This distinction is more precise than imposing one vague shared meaning on all the derivatives.

2. Forms and semantic cores

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

وَزَرَ: carried a load or, morally, incurred the burden of a sin.

يَزِرُ: bears a load or its responsibility.

وِزْر: a heavy burden; morally, sin and the responsibility arising from human conduct.

أَوْزَار: the plural of وِزْر; loads, burdens of sin, or heavy equipment, according to context.

وَازِر: a bearer of a load or someone responsible for a burden.

وَزَر: a refuge or safe place.

وَزِير: a helper and supporter who assists another with work and responsibility.

وِزَارَة: the position and work of a wazīr; in later usage, an administrative or governmental institution.

وَازَرَ: helped another with a task or responsibility. Lexicons also discuss this form in connection with آزَرَ from أ ز ر; its occurrence does not establish that the two families are identical.

مُوَازَرَة: assistance and support in carrying out a task.

اسْتَوْزَرَ: appointed someone as a wazīr to provide assistance and undertake responsibility.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

Forms that actually occur in the Qur’an

The family occurs 27 times in 14 Qur’anic verses, in five principal groups.

Basic verb: seven occurrences, including يَزِرُونَ and تَزِرُ.

The noun وِزْر: twelve occurrences, including وِزْرًا, وِزْرَكَ, and the plural أَوْزَار.

Active participle: وَازِرَةٌ, five occurrences.

The noun وَزَر: refuge, one occurrence.

The noun وَزِير: helper and supporter, two occurrences.

In يَزِرُ and تَزِرُ, the root’s initial wāw is omitted. This is a familiar morphological change in certain Arabic verbs beginning with wāw.

تَزِرُ therefore belongs to و ز ر, rather than an independent root beginning with zāy.

Semantic cores

Three working definitions are useful for this family.

Burden and responsibility: something heavy that a person bears; morally, responsibility for sin and the consequences of conduct.

Refuge: a place to which someone can turn in danger.

Assistance: helping another with a task and its responsibility.

The third branch has an evident semantic connection with relieving another’s burden or being their refuge and support.

Lexical explanations differ, however, over whether وَزِير historically derives from وِزْر, burden, or وَزَر, refuge.

Neither explanation should be declared the word’s definitive origin without sufficient historical evidence.

3. Material and moral burdens in the Qur’an

Ṭā Hā 20:87 — Loads of the people’s ornaments

قَالُوا مَا أَخْلَفْنَا مَوْعِدَكَ بِمَلْكِنَا وَلَٰكِنَّا حُمِّلْنَا أَوْزَارًا مِنْ زِينَةِ الْقَوْمِ فَقَذَفْنَاهَا فَكَذَٰلِكَ أَلْقَى السَّامِرِيُّ

qālū mā akhlafnā mawʿidaka bi-malkinā wa-lākinnā ḥummilnā awzāran min zīnati al-qawmi fa-qadhafnāhā fa-kadhālika alqā al-sāmiriyy

Ṭā Hā 20:87

“They said, ‘We did not break our promise to you of our own choice. Rather, we were made to carry loads of the people’s ornaments, so we cast them away; the Sāmirī likewise cast.’”

أَوْزَارًا here concerns loads of ornaments.

مِنْ زِينَةِ الْقَوْمِ identifies the material or objects being carried.

The primary contextual sense is therefore heavy loads or things being carried.

This verse importantly shows that أَوْزَار does not always mean sins in the Qur’an.

The speakers refer to ornaments they had carried and then cast away.

The root alone does not establish that the ornaments were stolen, unlawfully owned, or sinful to carry.

Any such judgment requires independent evidence about their origin and the people’s conduct.

The meaning of أَوْزَار and the speakers’ conduct

In the account of the Sāmirī’s calf, the people’s conduct and responsibility are moral subjects.

An account set in a context of disobedience does not give every word within it the meaning of sin.

أَوْزَار here concerns loads of ornaments.

Making the calf, following the Sāmirī, and the people’s actions must be examined through the verbs and statements that describe them.

This distinction prevents the moral weight of the entire account from replacing one word’s lexical meaning.

Muḥammad 47:4 — The burdens and equipment of war

Muḥammad 47:4 contains حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا.

In context, it conveys the ending of war and the laying down of its burdens and equipment.

This usage closely parallels al-Aʿshā’s poetic witness, where the burdens of war are spears, horses, and military equipment.

Ordinary language can describe the end of war by saying that war has laid down its burden or that weapons have been put down.

War is not a living being that literally sets its equipment down by hand. The expression attributes an action to war to convey the ending of hostilities.

أَوْزَار should not be restricted to “the sins of war” here, since the established Arabic equipment sense fits the context well.

War’s burdens may also evoke its associated difficulties. That broader implication should not replace the specific image of laying down equipment and ending the fighting.

Rules concerning warfare, prisoners, and the end of hostilities come from the whole verse, rather than from و ز ر alone.

Al-Anʿām 6:31 — Carrying the burden of sin on one’s back

قَدْ خَسِرَ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِلِقَاءِ اللَّهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً قَالُوا يَا حَسْرَتَنَا عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِيهَا وَهُمْ يَحْمِلُونَ أَوْزَارَهُمْ عَلَىٰ ظُهُورِهِمْ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَزِرُونَ

qad khasira alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-liqāʾi allāhi ḥattā idhā jāʾathumu al-sāʿatu baghtatan qālū yā ḥasratanā ʿalā mā farraṭnā fīhā wa-hum yaḥmilūna awzārahum ʿalā ẓuhūrihim a-lā sāʾa mā yazirūn

Al-Anʿām 6:31

“Lost indeed are those who denied meeting The God. When the Hour suddenly comes upon them, they say, ‘Alas for our neglect concerning it!’ They carry their burdens on their backs. How evil is what they bear!”

The root occurs in two forms here:

أَوْزَارَهُمْ

يَزِرُونَ

The first names the burdens they bear.

The second is the verb for carrying those burdens.

The same sentence also uses يَحْمِلُونَ.

The image of carrying a load is thus constructed through two different roots:

ح م ل for carrying.

و ز ر for a heavy burden and bearing it.

Here the burden concerns the sins and consequences of conduct of those who denied meeting God.

The word أَوْزَار does not determine the form or nature of these burdens in the hereafter.

The Qur’an portrays the weight of responsibility and human regret. Without evidence, this image should not be turned into a physical theory of the mass or bodily form of sins.

Ṭā Hā 20:100 — The burden of turning away from the reminder

مَنْ أَعْرَضَ عَنْهُ فَإِنَّهُ يَحْمِلُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وِزْرًا

man aʿraḍa ʿanhu fa-innahu yaḥmilu yawma al-qiyāmati wizrā

Ṭā Hā 20:100

“Whoever turns away from it will bear a burden on Resurrection Day.”

وِزْرًا is indefinite here.

The antecedent of عَنْهُ becomes clear in Ṭā Hā 20:99: the reminder given to Muhammad by God.

The burden therefore relates to turning away from that reminder.

Ṭā Hā 20:101 speaks of remaining in that condition and of how evil the load is on Resurrection Day.

وِزْر here is consequently a moral burden associated with human accountability.

The word itself does not specify the kind or amount of punishment, or how it is borne.

Al-Sharḥ 94:2 and Al-Sharḥ 94:3 — Muhammad’s burden lifted

Al-Sharḥ 94:2 says:

وَوَضَعْنَا عَنْكَ وِزْرَكَ

wa-waḍaʿnā ʿanka wizrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:2

“We lifted your heavy burden from you.”

Al-Sharḥ 94:3 says:

الَّذِي أَنْقَضَ ظَهْرَكَ

alladhī anqaḍa ẓahrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:3

“Which had weighed heavily upon your back.”

وِزْرَكَ contains a singular second-person pronoun referring to Muhammad.

وَضَعْنَا عَنْكَ describes the lifting of the burden.

The next verse depicts its weight as pressure upon the back.

Two readings merit consideration here.

One is the burden of sin and error, whose forgiveness and removal can be associated with God’s mercy.

The other is the heavy responsibility, difficulty, and pressure Muhammad faced.

Lexically, وِزْر can mean a heavy burden, and other Qur’anic uses do not restrict it to sin.

Conversely, its established use for sin cannot be excluded merely because the second possibility exists.

Al-Sharḥ speaks of opening the breast, lifting a burden, raising remembrance, and ease accompanying difficulty.

This sequence agrees well with the lifting of a heavy burden.

The verse itself does not identify exactly which actions or experiences constituted that burden.

For a base translation, “your heavy burden” is therefore more precise than replacing the word, without explanation, with “your sins” or “your responsibility as a Prophet.”

Each detailed reading must be assessed against independent Qur’anic evidence.

4. Individual responsibility and the burden of misleading others

The principle that no bearer bears another’s burden

Al-Najm 53:38 says:

أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ

allā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā

Al-Najm 53:38

“That no bearer bears another’s burden.”

Closely corresponding wording occurs in Al-Anʿām 6:164, Al-Isrāʾ 17:15, Fāṭir 35:18, and Al-Zumar 39:7.

Three forms of one root appear in this construction:

تَزِرُ: bears a burden.

وَازِرَةٌ: someone bearing a burden.

وِزْرَ: the load or responsibility being carried.

أُخْرَىٰ indicates that the burden belongs to another person.

The statement’s basic meaning is that no person bears another’s responsibility in that person’s place.

Its context is moral accountability and the hereafter.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:15, the preceding statement says that whoever follows guidance does so for their own benefit, while whoever goes astray does so to their own detriment.

Al-Najm 53:39 immediately follows this principle by relating human striving to the person concerned.

Al-Zumar 39:7 also discusses responsibility for human choice in connection with kufr and gratitude.

This sequence shows that sin and moral responsibility cannot be transferred to someone merely through family ties, group membership, or following another person.

Fāṭir 35:18 — Even a close relative

After stating that no bearer carries another’s burden, Fāṭir 35:18 says that if someone heavily laden calls another to carry their load, none of it will be carried, even if that person is a close relative.

This explanation clarifies the principle.

Family ties can generate affection, assistance, and social responsibilities. They do not, by themselves, turn one family member’s personal sin into another’s sin.

Children are not responsible for their parents’ wrongdoing, just as parents are not responsible for every independent choice of their children merely because they are related.

Moral responsibility should nevertheless be distinguished from social consequences.

Children may suffer from their parents’ financial corruption, warfare, or misconduct. Their suffering is real, but does not make them guilty.

A society may likewise suffer from its rulers’ wrong decisions, without the people automatically bearing the guilt of those who made them.

The no-bearer principle thus expresses personal responsibility; it does not deny collective consequences of human conduct.

Al-Naḥl 16:25 — The burden of those who mislead others

لِيَحْمِلُوا أَوْزَارَهُمْ كَامِلَةً يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَمِنْ أَوْزَارِ الَّذِينَ يُضِلُّونَهُمْ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَزِرُونَ

li-yaḥmilū awzārahum kāmilatan yawma al-qiyāmati wa-min awzāri alladhīna yuḍillūnahum bi-ghayri ʿilmin a-lā sāʾa mā yazirūn

Al-Naḥl 16:25

“So that they bear their burdens in full on Resurrection Day, and some of the burdens of those whom they lead astray without knowledge. How evil is what they bear!”

Those who mislead others bear their own burdens and also burdens associated with the people whose misguidance they have caused.

At first sight, this may seem inconsistent with the principle that no bearer bears another’s burden.

There is, however, an important distinction.

Someone who knowingly deceives others is responsible not only for personal beliefs or conduct: deceiving and misleading others is itself their action.

For example, someone who fabricates a lie and spreads it through social influence is responsible for that fabrication and deception.

If others are harmed or led into error through it, the instigator’s contribution to that situation is part of their responsibility.

This does not mean that another person’s independent sin is automatically and entirely transferred to them.

The verse places مِنْ before أَوْزَارِ الَّذِينَ. This can convey “from” and support a partitive reading of the burdens here, although grammatical and exegetical explanations differ over its scope.

بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ is also significant. Its attachment to those misleading or those being misled is debated. The base rendering here preserves “without knowledge,” with that syntactic issue left to this explanation; it does not assign the same knowledge and intention to everyone involved.

The misleader and the misled do not have identical responsibility

Several situations should be distinguished.

Someone may know a truth and deliberately teach others its opposite.

Someone may spread an incorrect claim without adequate investigation and thereby mislead others.

Another person may accept an incorrect claim out of trust in a teacher or leader without knowing the facts.

These situations do not involve identical knowledge, intention, or responsibility.

Individual responsibility therefore does not allow the misleader to shift all responsibility onto followers.

Conversely, having been misled does not itself establish deliberate denial of truth. Nor does it automatically establish complete exemption from responsibility; the person’s ability to investigate and any culpable neglect must also be considered.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:13 uses a different root, ث ق ل, to describe carrying one’s own burdens and further burdens alongside them.

It is conceptually related to Al-Naḥl 16:25, but أَثْقَال does not belong to و ز ر.

Taken together, these verses show that responsibility can include the effects of one’s conduct upon others. Each person remains answerable for their own conduct, knowledge, and role.

5. وَزَر as refuge and وَزِير as supporter

Al-Qiyāmah 75:11 — No refuge

كَلَّا لَا وَزَرَ

kallā lā wazar

Al-Qiyāmah 75:11

“No indeed! There is no refuge.”

وَزَرَ here belongs to the refuge branch.

It differs from وِزْر, a heavy burden.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:10, the human being asks where to flee.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:11 answers that there is no refuge.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:12 places the final resting place with the Lord.

The context thus concerns escape and shelter, rather than carrying sin.

The meaning of لَا وَزَرَ

In لَا وَزَرَ, lā negates the entire category: no refuge exists.

Arabic وَزَر may denote a mountain or secure place sought for protection from danger.

In this verse, however, no refuge is available for escaping that day.

“There is no heavy burden” would therefore be a mistranslation, substituting another branch of the root.

Although a mountain is a familiar example of وَزَر, “refuge” is preferable in the base translation because the meaning is broader than a mountain.

The verse does not merely say that mountains will cease to offer shelter; it denies the possibility of finding a refuge for escape.

A wazīr — Someone who lightens the burden of a task

In Ṭā Hā 20:29, Moses asks God:

وَاجْعَلْ لِي وَزِيرًا مِنْ أَهْلِي

wa-ijʿal lī wazīran min ahlī

Ṭā Hā 20:29

“Appoint for me a helper from my family.”

Ṭā Hā 20:30 identifies that person as Aaron, his brother.

In Ṭā Hā 20:31, Moses asks to have his strength reinforced through Aaron.

Ṭā Hā 20:32 also asks for Aaron to share in his task.

This sequence clarifies وَزِير.

Here it denotes someone who helps Moses carry out a demanding mission.

“Helper” or “supporter” therefore best conveys the contextual meaning.

In modern Persian, “vazir” commonly suggests a government minister. A modern administrative office is not the subject of this verse about Moses.

Moses asks for companionship and assistance in the mission God has entrusted to him.

Al-Furqān 25:35 — Aaron as Moses’ helper

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا مُوسَى الْكِتَابَ وَجَعَلْنَا مَعَهُ أَخَاهُ هَارُونَ وَزِيرًا

wa-laqad ātaynā mūsā al-kitāba wa-jaʿalnā maʿahu akhāhu hārūna wazīrā

Al-Furqān 25:35

“We indeed gave Moses the Book and appointed his brother Aaron alongside him as a helper.”

وَزِيرًا has the same meaning of supporter and helper here.

Aaron is placed alongside Moses to assist with the mission.

Maryam 19:53 also identifies Aaron as a Prophet. His being a Prophet is known from that verse and other evidence about his role, rather than from the lexical meaning of وَزِير alone.

وَزِير does not therefore automatically mean Prophet, formal successor, or second ruler.

The word expresses assistance, while the particular mission is determined by context.

Does وَزِير derive from a heavy burden or from refuge?

Arabic lexical explanations propose two connections for the name.

One is that a wazīr undertakes part of another person’s load and responsibility, helping with difficult tasks.

The other is that a wazīr is a refuge and support, someone whose judgment and assistance can be relied upon in difficulty.

Both explanations fit the word’s use in the account of Moses.

Aaron can both share the burden of the mission and support Moses.

Semantic compatibility does not establish both images as the word’s definitive historical origins.

For understanding the Qur’anic passages, it is clear that a wazīr assists another person with work and responsibility.

Helping with responsibility versus carrying another’s sin

Being a wazīr does not conflict with the principle that no bearer bears another’s burden.

Someone can help another perform a mission, administer affairs, and overcome difficulties.

Such assistance does not transfer personal sin or remove the moral responsibility of the person making decisions and acting.

For example, a helper may participate in a good undertaking, with both benefiting from its outcome.

If one independently commits an injustice, the mere title “helper” or “companion” does not automatically transfer all responsibility for it to the other.

Conversely, a helper who knowingly participates in injustice bears responsibility for that participation.

Division of labor, cooperation, and responsibility for sin must therefore be distinguished.

6. Related roots and proposed translations

و ز ر (وِزْر) compared with ح م ل (حَمْل)

ح م ل concerns carrying and bearing loads.

وِزْر names the heavy load or responsibility itself.

Al-Anʿām 6:31 places the roots together: people carry their burdens.

Ṭā Hā 20:87 likewise uses حُمِّلْنَا أَوْزَارًا for loads of ornaments.

حَمْل and وِزْر are therefore not identical concepts.

One expresses carrying, the other the burden or responsibility borne.

In يَزِرُ, however, و ز ر itself also expresses bearing a burden. The distinction must be assessed by word form, rather than turned into an absolute boundary for every derivative.

و ز ر (وِزْر) compared with ث ق ل (ثِقْل)

ث ق ل concerns heaviness and weight and can also express burden and responsibility in moral contexts.

The principal burden branch of وِزْر concerns a heavy load and is frequently used for responsibility for sin in the Qur’an.

The two families converge in Fāṭir 35:18, which speaks of a heavily laden person and a load that cannot be transferred to another.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:13 also uses أَثْقَال in the context of responsibility for misleading others.

This overlap shows that the Qur’an expresses moral burdens through more than one root.

ثِقْل does not itself mean sin, and وِزْر does not mean only sin in every setting.

و ز ر (وِزْر) compared with و ق ر (وِقْر)

Some uses of و ق ر concern burdens and heaviness.

وِقْر, for example, can denote a load that is heavy to carry.

It overlaps with وِزْر in this respect.

The roots are not interchangeable everywhere. و ق ر also includes branches such as heaviness of hearing and dignified composure.

A shared heavy-load sense does not permit transferring every feature of one family to the other.

و ز ر (وَزِير) compared with أ ز ر (أَزْر)

In Ṭā Hā 20:31, Moses says:

اشْدُدْ بِهِ أَزْرِي

ushdud bihi azrī

Ṭā Hā 20:31

“Strengthen me through him.”

أَزْرِي belongs to أ ز ر, rather than و ز ر.

The distinction matters because وَزِير and أَزْر occur in the same supplication and in the related field of assistance and strength, while their Qur’anic forms are morphologically assigned to different roots.

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 likewise uses فَآزَرَهُ from أ ز ر.

Their proximity can clarify the relationship between assistance and strength. It does not make Qur’anic أَزْر a derivative of و ز ر. The lexical discussion of مُوَازَرَة and مُؤَازَرَة remains a separate issue.

و ز ر (وِزْر) compared with ز و ر (زُور)

ز و ر differs from و ز ر in the order of its letters.

زُور is used for false and untrue speech in the Qur’an.

وِزْر concerns burden and responsibility.

Lying may create a sin and moral burden for the speaker, but that moral connection does not make the roots identical.

قَوْل زُور should therefore not be identified as a derivative of و ز ر.

Proposed renderings for Forqan

Equivalents should follow each word’s form and context. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

وِزْر: “heavy burden,” or in moral contexts, “burden of sin” and “responsibility for sin.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:100:

“Whoever turns away from it will bear a burden on Resurrection Day.”

In Al-Sharḥ 94:2:

“We lifted your heavy burden from you.”

أَوْزَار: “loads”; in sinful contexts, “burdens of sin”; in warfare, “the burdens and equipment of war.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:87:

“Loads of the people’s ornaments.”

In Muḥammad 47:4:

“Until war lays down its burdens and equipment.”

يَزِرُ and تَزِرُ: “bears a burden” or “bears the burden of responsibility.”

In Al-Najm 53:38:

“No bearer bears another’s burden.”

وَازِرَة: “a bearer” or “someone bearing a burden.”

“Bearer” suits the accountability construction. An explanation can clarify that responsibility for conduct is intended, rather than the occupation of carrying loads.

وَزَر: “refuge.”

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:11:

“No indeed! There is no refuge.”

وَزِير: “helper” or “supporter.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:29:

“Appoint for me a helper from my family.”

In Al-Furqān 25:35:

“We appointed his brother Aaron alongside him as a helper.”

The Persian loanword “vazir” is possible in these verses, but may suggest a government position to a modern reader. “Helper” conveys the Qur’anic meaning more clearly.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root و ز ر has several semantic branches that should not be confined to an artificial single definition.

The first branch concerns burdens and weight. In poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā, the burdens of war denote military equipment. The Qur’an uses أَوْزَار for loads of ornaments in Ṭā Hā 20:87 and war’s burdens and equipment in Muḥammad 47:4.

In moral usage, the same family expresses sin and responsibility for human conduct.

In Al-Anʿām 6:31, people carry their burdens on their backs. In Al-Naḥl 16:25, those who mislead others bear responsibility for themselves and for their role in misleading others.

Five verses express the principle that no bearer bears another’s burden: no person carries someone else’s independent sin in that person’s place.

This does not conflict with a misleader’s responsibility, because misleading others is itself an action for which that person is accountable.

The second branch is refuge. وَزَر in Al-Qiyāmah 75:11 means refuge; the verse denies the possibility of finding shelter to escape the Day of Reckoning.

The third branch is assistance and support. In the account of Moses and Aaron, a wazīr is someone placed alongside another to help with a mission. The word is not, by itself, the name of a modern administrative office or the title Prophet.

The semantic relationship between a helper, sharing a burden, and offering refuge or support is clear. A definitive historical derivation still requires independent evidence.

The family’s Qur’anic uses can be defined in three parts:

وِزْر: a heavy burden; morally, the burden of sin and responsibility arising from conduct.

وَزَر: a refuge or safe place offering protection from danger.

وَزِير: a helper and supporter who assists another with work and responsibility.

This distinction prevents three errors: treating every أَوْزَار as sins, rendering لَا وَزَرَ as the absence of a load, and equating help with a task with the transfer of another’s sin.

Forqan’s translations should preserve material burdens, moral burdens, refuge, and assistance in their respective settings.

The study’s central implication is that someone can help bear another’s difficulties without thereby acquiring moral responsibility for that person’s independent actions. Conversely, a person’s own contribution to injustice, deception, or misguidance forms part of their burden of responsibility.

The lexical meanings of و ز ر and the Qur’anic principle of responsibility thus become clear together, without conflating burden, sin, refuge, and assistance.
