Nakad in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from reluctant, scant giving and hard living to unproductive land and differing benefit from blessings

The three-consonant root N K D in Arabic concerns difficulty, something being hard to obtain, scant benefit, and unpleasantness. Words from this family apply when someone receives only a little after much insistence, or when land yields a meager, hard-won crop despite conditions for growth.

Other Arabic uses concern difficult living, a disagreeable person, an unwelcome gift, and something causing its owner distress or an unfavorable outcome.

Nakad should not, however, be reduced to a small quantity. Something may be little yet readily and willingly given. Conversely, something may be substantial but difficult to acquire or enjoy without distress.

In many uses, difficulty in obtaining or benefiting from something matters, while a small amount or unpleasantness may accompany it.

N K D occurs only once in the Qur’an, as nakidan in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58. The verse contrasts good land suited to growth with poor land. Vegetation grows from the former by its Lord’s permission; the latter produces only difficult, meager growth.

The Qur’an then places the contrast within its explanation of divine signs. It presents an understandable physical difference between lands and uses it to direct attention to how people benefit from signs and blessings.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing nakad’s own meaning, the land’s quality, the amount of produce, and the verse’s figurative message.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَكِدَ (nakida): to become difficult, involve hardship, or yield less benefit.
  • نَكَدَ (nakada): in some uses, to reduce a gift or withhold something from another.
  • نَكْد and نُكْد (nakd and nukd): difficulty, constraint, and scant benefit; in giving, a small or unwelcome gift.
  • نَكِد and نَكَد (nakid and nakad): difficult, meager, disagreeable, or not easily and pleasantly enjoyed.
  • أَنْكَد (ankad): more difficult or unpleasant; adjectivally, a harsh, disagreeable person.
  • نَكْدَاء (nakdāʾ): a feminine descriptive form; in particular uses, a she-camel with a specified reproductive or milk-producing condition.
  • نِكَاد (nikād): a recorded plural describing unproductive lands.
  • أَنْكَاد and مَنَاكِيد (ankād and manākīd): plurals for harsh, disagreeable people or those offering little good.
  • نَاكِد (nākid): in some uses, someone making gifts difficult or scant; also a specialized livestock description.
  • مَنْكُود (mankūd): something given sparingly or with difficulty; of a person, someone pressed and distressed by many requests.
  • أَنْكَدَ (ankada): a petitioner met a harsh or ungenerous person, or obtained a small, hard-won gift.
  • نَاكَدَ and تَنَاكَدَ (nākada and tanākada): to treat another harshly or disagreeably, or for two parties to make matters difficult for one another.

Other restricted lexical uses include a raven’s harsh call, a well’s water diminishing, and particular conditions of she-camels.

Only nakidan occurs in the Qur’an. The basic verb, verbal nouns, other adjectives, and derivatives involving giving or livestock do not occur in its text.

Semantic core: benefit attained with difficulty

One lexical analysis places the root’s core in something reaching the person seeking it with difficulty.

This fits several clear uses.

Someone requests help, but receives little only after repeated insistence. A well contains water, but its diminished supply is difficult to reach. Land can support growth, yet produces little with difficulty.

These situations involve more than absence: difficult access or limited benefit is also at issue.

This should not become an absolute rule that every nakad involves someone making an unanswered request.

Uses such as difficult living or poor land may involve no particular petitioner.

Nor does nakad always mean a small quantity. In some uses, unpleasantness, harshness, or difficulty of enjoyment is more prominent.

A broader definition of this branch is therefore difficulty and unpleasantness in attaining or enjoying something, often accompanied by scant benefit.

2. N K D in early Arabic

Scant, reluctant giving

Giving is one recognized field of this root’s use.

In the expression:

نَكَدَ الرَّجُلُ الْعَطَاءَ

nakada al-rajulu al-ʿaṭāʾa

Translation: “The man made the gift scant.”

Also:

عَطَاءٌ مَنْكُودٌ

ʿaṭāʾun mankūdun

Translation: “A scant or reluctantly given gift.”

A person may request something, but the giver does not readily fulfill the request.

Sometimes the gift is very small; sometimes it comes only after many requests; sometimes the manner of giving is so unpleasant that the recipient finds no comfort or satisfaction in it.

Nakad here thus concerns more than amount: the giver’s behavior and difficulty of receiving may also matter.

An early poetic witness: a gift that is not nakad

Rabīʿah ibn Maqrūm al-Ḍabbī, who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, praises a man named Masʿūd ibn Sālim:

لَا حِلْمُكَ الْحِلْمُ مَوْجُودًا عَلَيْهِ
وَلَا يُلْفَى عَطَاؤُكَ فِي الْأَقْوَامِ مَنْكُودَا

lā ḥilmuka al-ḥilmu mawjūdan ʿalayhi / wa-lā yulfā ʿaṭāʾuka fī al-aqwāmi mankūdā

Translation: “Your forbearance is not such as to provoke reproach, nor is your gift among the people found scant and unpleasant.”

Mankūd describes the gift here.

By denying that quality, the poet highlights the praised man’s generosity: his gifts involve neither narrowness nor difficulty.

The witness shows early Arabic nakad applied to the quality of giving and the benefit reaching another.

Mankūd here means neither that the recipient is poor nor that the property is forbidden. The subject is the gift’s quality and amount.

The poet lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam, but the line itself does not establish when it was composed. It is useful evidence for early Arabic without being conclusively pre-Qur’anic.

Another poetic witness, of uncertain attribution, says:

وَأَعْطِ مَا أَعْطَيْتَهُ طَيِّبًا
لَا خَيْرَ فِي الْمَنْكُودِ وَالنَّاكِدِ

wa-aʿṭi mā aʿṭaytahu ṭayyiban / lā khayra fī al-mankūdi wa-al-nākidi

Translation: “Give what you give gladly and generously; there is no good in a grudging gift or a harsh giver.”

Mankūd and nākid occur together here.

The first concerns a gift given with difficulty and unpleasantness; the second may describe someone behaving that way.

The witness helps distinguish describing a gift from describing its giver. Since its attribution and date are uncertain, it should not be presented as conclusively pre-Islamic.

Nakad and hard living

Arabic says:

نَكِدَ عَيْشُهُمْ

nakida ʿayshuhum

Translation: “Their living became hard and difficult.”

Nakad here concerns living conditions, rather than financial gifts alone.

Life may become difficult through limited resources, unrest, hard work, or unpleasant circumstances.

The expression does not mean nakad always denotes poverty.

Someone may be financially comfortable yet have a hard, unpleasant life. Another may possess little without a life described as nakad in terms of peace and contentment.

In living conditions, nakad is therefore broader than having little money.

It describes a difficult, unpleasant state, whose particular cause requires separate identification.

Unproductive land

Arabic lexical accounts record:

أَرَضُونَ نِكَادٌ

araḍūna nikādun

Translation: “Lands of scant benefit and poor yield.”

This matters particularly for the Al-Aʿrāf verse.

Land may have unsuitable soil, insufficient water, or conditions hindering growth, producing a small or poor crop.

The general description nakad must nevertheless be distinguished from the natural cause of low yield.

Nakad does not name a particular soil. The root alone cannot establish that land must be saline, rocky, dry, or lacking nutrients.

Each may affect productivity, but none is a fixed part of the root’s meaning.

The Qur’an describes the unsuitable land through alladhī khabutha, while nakidan describes the growth and yield.

Nakad’s land-related sense therefore concerns difficult growth and scant benefit, rather than a specified chemical or structural soil property.

A well with little water

Arabic says:

نَكِدَتِ الْبِئْرُ

nakidati al-biʾru

Translation: “The well’s water diminished.”

Nakad can thus describe a reduced benefit received from a source.

The well remains, but its water has decreased and obtaining enough may become difficult.

The well is the subject, while the relevant feature is diminished water or difficulty in benefiting from it.

This does not make nakad always mean complete drying or the source’s disappearance.

A well with little water may still hold some.

The same distinction matters in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58: it does not say poor land produces absolutely no vegetation.

The nakdāʾ she-camel: a distinct specialized use

Lexicons also record nakdāʾ for a she-camel.

Some accounts describe a camel giving little or no milk.

Others use nukd for she-camels producing abundant milk, especially those whose young have not survived and whose milk remains available because they are not suckling a calf.

The uses appear opposed.

Lexical explanations have noted the difference and recognized distinct meanings in descriptions of camels.

Poor milk production should therefore not be assumed to define every nakdāʾ.

A poem by al-Kumayt, the Umayyad poet, uses al-nukd for she-camels whose young have not survived.

The description is not necessarily restricted to a small quantity of milk: reproduction and the loss of the young also matter.

This branch helps prevent an oversimplified definition.

“Nakad always means little” would not accommodate the abundant-milk use.

The specialized use therefore requires its own treatment and should not be imposed on the Qur’anic meaning.

Harshness and disagreeableness in behavior

Rajulun nakidun can describe a harsh, disagreeable person or one offering little good.

Also:

نَاكَدَهُ

nākadahu

In one use, it means “he treated him harshly and disagreeably.”

Such a person may make something difficult for another or repeatedly create obstacles and unpleasantness in relationships.

The use fits the field of difficulty and hard attainment.

Nakad here is neither the name of a mental illness nor a necessarily unchangeable personality trait. It describes behavior or a situation regarded as unpleasant and difficult in Arabic.

Some accounts also connect nakad with ill omen or a bad outcome: something bringing its owner distress or unpleasant consequences.

This should not establish a hidden force of bad luck in people or objects. “Ill-omened” can describe an unfortunate outcome or people’s perception of a situation without proving an independent unseen cause.

3. Qur’anic usage: Al-Aʿrāf 7:58

وَالْبَلَدُ الطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَالَّذِي خَبُثَ لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا كَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَشْكُرُونَ

wa-al-baladu al-ṭayyibu yakhruju nabātuhu bi-idhni rabbihi wa-alladhī khabutha lā yakhruju illā nakidan kadhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāti li-qawmin yashkurūna

Translation:

“The good, suitable land brings forth its vegetation by its Lord’s permission; the poor land produces only difficult, meager growth. Thus We explain the signs in varied ways for people who give thanks.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58

The verse contains the only Qur’anic use of N K D.

Two types of land with different growth are contrasted.

The first is al-ṭayyib, and its vegetation grows by its Lord’s permission.

The second is alladhī khabutha, and its growth is described as nakidan.

The contrast matters: nakad here describes neither a person nor a punishment, but the way vegetation grows from land.

The meaning of nakidan here

Nakad concerns growth attained with difficulty and yielding scant or undesirable benefit.

Two features stand together: difficult growth and meager yield.

“Little” alone overlooks difficulty.

“With difficulty” alone may leave limited benefit or poor yield insufficiently clear.

“Difficult and meager” is therefore a suitable equivalent here.

This does not mean every Arabic nakad necessarily expresses both features with equal intensity.

Here, the word’s established meaning and the poor land’s condition strengthen both aspects.

Does poor land yield absolutely nothing?

No.

The construction is:

لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا

lā yakhruju illā nakidan

“It comes forth only with difficulty and scant benefit.”

Illā restricts the kind or condition of growth rather than absolutely denying all growth.

Poor land may therefore grow vegetation, but with difficulty and limited benefit.

Simply translating “no vegetation grows” loses an important distinction.

Such a rendering does not even fit nakidan’s presence: the Qur’an describes growth with a particular condition.

Nor does the verse establish that every plant growing there is completely useless or harmful.

Scant benefit and difficulty are more suitable meanings. Complete uselessness is a stronger conclusion not necessarily contained in the word.

The role of alladhī khabutha

Khabutha comes from KH B TH.

It describes the land’s quality as unsuitable for growth.

The meaning of khubth should not, however, be merged into nakad.

Khubth concerns the land’s poor quality in this construction.

Nakad describes the growth and its benefit.

The verse relates the land’s condition to its yield.

For example, unsuitable conditions may cause land to produce little or weak vegetation. The land’s poor quality and the growth’s scant benefit remain distinct.

The Qur’an expresses them through separate words.

The role of yakhruj

The first part says:

يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ

yakhruju nabātuhu

“Its vegetation comes forth.”

The second part, after alladhī khabutha, says:

لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا

lā yakhruju illā nakidan

Growth is still the subject, though nabātuhu is not explicitly repeated.

Grammatically, lā yakhruju can be understood in relation to the poor land and its produce.

Nakidan in the familiar reading is accusative and commonly analyzed as circumstantial, describing the condition of emergence or growth.

It is therefore not the independent name of a particular plant or crop.

The word describes the condition and quality of growth coming forth.

Equivalents such as “meager” or “with difficulty” should consequently be placed within the full construction and applied to that growth.

4. The reading of nakidan and its effect on meaning

The familiar Ḥafṣ reading has:

نَكِدًا

nakidan

With fatḥah on nūn and kasrah on kāf.

Other forms are recorded in early reading and grammatical accounts.

Abū Jaʿfar reads nakadan, with fatḥah on kāf. Nakdan, with sukūn on kāf, is also transmitted from Ṭalḥah. These forms should not be presented as having an identical standing among readings.

Grammatically, familiar nakidan can describe a circumstance: difficult, meager growth.

Nakadan has also been analyzed as a verbal noun, expressing emergence with difficulty and in a state of nakad.

Some grammarians explain nakdan as a lightened form or a linguistic form with a similar meaning.

The differences matter for vocalization and morphology, without transforming the verse’s broad meaning.

In all these readings, poor land’s growth is described through difficulty and limited benefit.

A different kāf vowel alone should not establish an entirely new meaning.

Abū Jaʿfar’s reading belongs to the ten readings and should be distinguished from isolated reports. The chosen Qur’anic text remains the basis for the main translation, while differences of vocalization or analysis are examined in their proper explanatory place.

5. Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 and the surrounding verses

Understanding nakad’s place in the Qur’an requires reading Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 with the preceding verse.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 says:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي يُرْسِلُ الرِّيَاحَ بُشْرًا بَيْنَ يَدَيْ رَحْمَتِهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَقَلَّتْ سَحَابًا ثِقَالًا سُقْنَاهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَّيِّتٍ فَأَنْزَلْنَا بِهِ الْمَاءَ فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ مِن كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ

wa-huwa alladhī yursilu al-riyāḥa bushran bayna yaday raḥmatihi ḥattā idhā aqallat saḥāban thiqālan suqnāhu li-baladin mayyitin fa-anzalnā bihi al-māʾa fa-akhrajnā bihi min kulli al-thamarāti

Translation:

“He sends the winds as bearers of good news before His mercy, until, when they carry heavy clouds, We drive them toward a dead land, send water down through them, and bring forth every kind of fruit and produce.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 continues:

كَذَٰلِكَ نُخْرِجُ الْمَوْتَىٰ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ

kadhālika nukhriju al-mawtā laʿallakum tadhakkarūna

“Thus We bring forth the dead, so that you may remember.”

The land’s revival and growth direct attention to the power of creation and raising again.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 then states the difference between good and poor land.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 uses the same field of nature and growth, while highlighting a new point: lands differ in their capacity to grow and yield.

Is the difference solely in rainfall?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 discusses rain and growth, while Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 compares two kinds of land.

The connection makes the land’s condition and its relation to water and growth important for the example.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 does not say the lands had identical conditions in every respect or received exactly equal water.

The example should therefore not establish a definite theory that every natural factor is equal.

What it makes clear is the difference in the land’s condition and the quality of its yield.

Agricultural experience also shows that yield depends on more than one factor. Soil quality, water, environment, and other conditions can affect growth.

These explanations should remain descriptions of the physical instance, rather than scientific agricultural detail added to nakad’s lexical meaning.

Is the verse a parable about human beings?

The ending says:

كَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَشْكُرُونَ

kadhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāti li-qawmin yashkurūna

“Thus We explain the signs in varied ways for people who give thanks.”

The purpose is therefore more than agricultural information.

Interpretations also connect the verse with differences in accepting guidance and benefiting from divine signs.

A familiar reading compares good land with someone receptive to reminders and guidance who benefits from them, and poor land with someone gaining little from the signs or resisting them.

This fits the ending and the setting of explaining divine signs.

The natural image must nevertheless be distinguished from its application to people.

Nakidan still directly means difficult, meager growth.

Accepting guidance, ingratitude, and resisting truth come from the figurative message and related verses, rather than N K D’s independent meaning.

Is every person with little yield like nakad land?

The parable should not become a hasty judgment of people.

Poverty, illness, disability, or difficult living conditions may result in little material gain, without themselves indicating ingratitude or moral impurity.

Someone may also visibly possess wealth, power, or success while behaving wrongly.

Ṭayyib and khubth initially concern land and the quality of its growth.

Applying the image to moral life requires criteria from verses about faith, action, justice, and gratitude, rather than people’s possessions or outward success.

The verse therefore does not authorize presenting poor land, poor people, or those enduring hardship as morally deficient.

Why does the verse end with gratitude?

Li-qawmin yashkurūn refers to people who give thanks.

Gratitude in context is connected with recognizing blessings and benefiting properly from divine signs.

The connection fits suitable land: with the necessary capacity, it benefits from water and available conditions and yields produce.

One may reflect that people are likewise responsible for how they use guidance and resources provided to them.

That reflection should not become nakad’s lexical meaning.

Nor does gratitude guarantee freedom from shortage, hardship, or failure in worldly life.

The Qur’an connects gratitude and mindfulness with moral responsibility, while natural and everyday difficulties may have many causes.

A yield or condition described as nakad is thus not by itself a final judgment of someone’s relationship with The God.

6. Comparison with related roots

N K D (nakad) and ʿ S R (ʿusr)

ʿUsr concerns difficulty and hardship in Arabic.

Nakad often involves difficulty but may also express a scant or unpleasant result.

An undertaking may be hard yet yield abundant benefit. ʿUsr fits the difficulty, while nakad need not accurately describe the result.

Receiving only a small, unpleasant benefit after much effort may fit nakad more closely.

This matters in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58: poor land faces more than a temporary obstacle to growth; its yield is also described as meager against good land’s yield.

This is a semantic tendency, rather than an exceptionless rule for every use.

Study of ʿ S R

N K D (nakad) and Q L L (qillah)

Q L L concerns a small quantity.

Nakad is more than a small quantity.

A gift can be small yet cheerfully and willingly given. Its amount is little, but it need not be a nakad gift.

A gift may instead come only after repeated requests, with harshness and unpleasantness.

Nakad then conveys something beyond amount.

Rendering nakad in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 merely as “little” therefore explains part of the sense without fully conveying difficulty and scant benefit.

Study of Q L L

N K D (nakad) and KH B TH (khubth)

The roots occur together in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58.

Khabutha describes the land’s poor quality.

Nakidan specifies its growth and yield.

Poor land and difficult, meager growth are connected, but distinct.

Unsuitable conditions may cause a limited crop.

Nakad should therefore not be translated as “impure land” here: the land’s description is expressed separately.

Nor should KH B TH be reduced to a small crop, since it has its own semantic field.

Study of KH B TH

N K D (nakad) and K N D (kanad)

The roots may be confused because of similar letters and certain land-related uses.

N K D, in that consonant order, concerns difficulty and scant benefit.

K N D is another family, connected in Arabic with cutting and severing.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:6 says:

إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ لِرَبِّهِ لَكَنُودٌ

inna al-insāna li-rabbihi la-kanūdun

Translation: “Indeed, the human being is deeply ungrateful to his Lord.”

Kanūd comes from K N D, rather than N K D.

Arabic also records arḍ kanūd for unproductive land, but the similar use does not make the roots identical.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 concerns difficult, meager growth, whereas kanūd in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:6 occurs in a context of human ingratitude.

The resemblance therefore does not make Qur’anic nakad mean ingratitude.

N K D (nakad) and N K R (nukr)

Established uses of N K R concern being unknown, not accepting, and denying.

N K D concerns difficulty, scant benefit, and unpleasantness.

Someone may dislike something because it is unpleasant, but disliking and difficulty remain independent concepts.

Something may also be unknown without involving difficulty or scant benefit.

Similar first consonants therefore establish neither a shared root nor equal meaning.

7. Understanding and translation proposals for Forqan

The central translation issue is preserving distinctions between little quantity, difficulty, and unpleasantness.

All occur in the family, but are not equally prominent in every sentence.

For living conditions, “hard and unpleasant” can be appropriate.

For giving, “scant and reluctantly given” or “an unpleasant gift” is more precise.

For a well, “having little water” may convey the intended sense.

For a harsh person, “disagreeable” or “ungenerous” fits according to context.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:58, however, requires an equivalent fitting growth and the land’s yield.

Translating Al-Aʿrāf 7:58

وَالَّذِي خَبُثَ لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا

wa-alladhī khabutha lā yakhruju illā nakidan

Proposed translation:

“The poor land produces only difficult, meager growth.”

This preserves several features.

First, “poor” concerns the land’s quality and comes from khabutha.

Second, “growth” comes from yakhruju and its connection with nabātuhu in the first part.

Third, “difficult and meager” conveys nakad in context.

Fourth, the sentence does not deny every growth, but describes growth under unfavorable conditions.

A shorter rendering is also possible:

“The poor land yields only with difficulty and scant benefit.”

“It grows no vegetation” and “it grows only harmful plants” are inaccurate: the first removes growth, while the second adds harmfulness without conclusive evidence.

“It grows miserably” or “inauspiciously” is also neither natural nor precise here. Although certain Arabic uses concern ill outcome and unpleasantness, difficult growth and scant benefit are preferable in this verse.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

Brief root meaning: difficulty and hard attainment of benefit, often with a scant or unpleasant result.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:58: difficult, meager growth produced by poor land.

This brief explanation suffices in Forqan’s word box. The different lands and the figurative message belong in a separate reflection.

8. Conclusion

N K D in Arabic concerns difficulty, hard attainment, scant benefit, and unpleasantness.

Established uses include hard living, scant and difficult giving, a disagreeable person, unproductive land, and a well with little water.

Its derivatives do not all mean exactly the same thing. Difficulty is more prominent in some uses, scant benefit or unpleasantness in others.

The specialized uses for certain she-camels also show why small quantity cannot be attributed to the whole family without exception.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as nakidan in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58.

The verse compares good land suited to growth with poor land. Vegetation grows from the first by its Lord’s permission; growth in the second is difficult and meager.

Nakad here is neither a particular land’s name, complete destruction of vegetation, ill omen, nor divine punishment.

It concerns growth’s quality and limited yield.

The surrounding context and the ending about explaining signs to grateful people invite reflection on benefiting from guidance and divine blessings.

That figurative message should not become nakad’s independent meaning.

Nor does poor land’s image establish that someone poor, enduring hardship, or possessing limited resources is morally impure or ungrateful.

Final definition: N K D principally denotes difficulty in attaining or benefiting from something, often with scant benefit or unpleasantness. Hard living, reluctant and scant gifts, and unproductive land exemplify it. Qur’anic nakidan describes growth obtained with difficulty and limited yield from poor land. “Difficult and meager” is therefore suitable for Forqan, while the land’s quality and the figurative message remain distinct from the root’s own meaning.
