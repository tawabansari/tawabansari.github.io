Close companionship, intimate friendship, and a relationship that, in the context of marriage, refers to a sexual bond outside it

In Arabic, KH D N concerns companionship and close friendship. خِدْن (khidn) and خَدِين (khadīn) denote a friend, companion, or someone with whom one has a close relationship. Such companionship may involve conversation, trust, social interaction, and knowledge of one another’s personal affairs.

Early lexicons record مُخَادَنَة (mukhādanah) as companionship and friendship. أَخْدَان (akhdān), from the same family, is a plural denoting friends or close companions. In some uses, however, particularly relations between women and men, khidn denotes a friend or companion in a romantic or sexual relationship.

These two semantic fields must not be confused. Not every friend or companion is a sexual partner; equally, not every relationship called khidn was necessarily an ordinary friendship without an emotional or sexual bond.

The Qur’an uses this root in two verses, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5. Both place أَخْدَان (akhdān) alongside musāfaḥah in the context of marriage and iḥṣān. Akhdān here therefore does not mean ordinary friends and acquaintances alone; it concerns close relationships outside marriage involving sexual intimacy.

The Qur’an does not apply this description only to women. Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 uses مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ (muttakhidhāt akhdān) of women, while Al-Māʾidah 5:5 uses مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ (muttakhidhī akhdān) of men.

This difference shows that the responsibility being discussed is not confined to one gender.

1. Root forms and semantic core

KH D N occurs twice in the Qur’an, solely as أَخْدَانٍ (akhdān). In both verses it is the plural of خِدْن (khidn).

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • خِدْن (khidn): a friend, close companion, or someone in an intimate relationship with another.
  • خَدِين (khadīn): a close friend or intimate companion.
  • أَخْدَان (akhdān): the plural of khidn: friends or close companions.
  • خُدَنَاء (khudanāʾ): another plural for khadīn and khidn.
  • خَادَنَ ـ يُخَادِنُ (khādana–yukhādinu): to establish friendship and close companionship with someone.
  • مُخَادَنَة (mukhādanah): companionship, friendship, and close association; in some uses, a romantic or sexual relationship.
  • مُخَادِن (mukhādin): someone maintaining companionship or close friendship with another.
  • أَخْدَن (akhdan): someone who has friends or companions.
  • خُدَنَة (khudanah): someone with many friends or companions.

These forms are recorded in lexicons, but only akhdān occurs in the Qur’an. Neither the verb khādana nor the verbal noun mukhādanah occurs there.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the two akhdān occurrences in Al-Nisāʾ and Al-Māʾidah

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one underlying principle for this entry:

الْخَاءُ وَالدَّالُ وَالنُّونُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْمُصَاحَبَةُ

al-khāʾu wa-al-dālu wa-al-nūnu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-muṣāḥabatu

That is, KH D N has one underlying principle: companionship and association.

He then states:

فَالْخِدْنُ: الصَّاحِبُ

fa-al-khidnu al-ṣāḥibu

That is, khidn means a companion or friend.

He also quotes Abū Zayd: خَادَنْتُ الرَّجُلَ (khādantu al-rajula) means صَادَقْتُهُ (ṣādaqtuhu), “I befriended that man.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: companionship and the definition of khidn

Lisān al-ʿArab gives a broader explanation: khidn and khadīn describe a friend who accompanies someone in both public and private affairs.

This indicates that a khidn relationship may go beyond passing acquaintance and become close, personal companionship.

Sharing private affairs does not necessarily mean wrongdoing, however. Two friends may know one another’s private circumstances without their relationship being sexual or unethical.

The root’s basic definition should therefore retain companionship and close friendship.

Where a romantic or sexual relationship is intended, that meaning is identified through the kind of companionship and its context.

Is secrecy part of the root’s meaning?

Some dictionaries explain khidn as a “secret friend.”

For example, Al-Fayyūmī writes in Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr:

الْخِدْنُ الصَّدِيقُ فِي السِّرِّ

al-khidnu al-ṣadīqu fī al-sirri

That is, khidn means a friend in secret.

Other sources, including Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, record the general meaning of friendship and companionship.

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah also mentions أَخْدَانِ سُوءٍ (akhdān sūʾ) and أَخْدَانِ صِدْقٍ (akhdān ṣidq): bad companions and true friends.

These uses show that the root alone does not establish that every khidn necessarily involves a secret, illicit relationship.

Secrecy is an established feature of some relationships described with this word, but it does not exhaust the word family’s definition.

Nor are closeness and intimacy inherently blameworthy. The kind of relationship and its boundaries matter.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, Asās al-Balāghah, and Lisān al-ʿArab: general friendship and secret companionship

2. Early Arabic usage: from youthful friends to romantic companions

Friends and companions of youth

The collected poetry of Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, an Arab poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, includes this verse:

تُبَكِّي عَلَى إِثْرِ الشَّبَابِ الَّذِي مَضَى
أَلَا إِنَّ أَخْدَانَ الشَّبَابِ الرَّعَارِعُ

tubakkī ʿalā ithri al-shabābi alladhī maḍā / alā inna akhdāna al-shabābi al-raʿāriʿu

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“You weep over youth that has passed; remember that youth’s companions were the young people growing into their prime.”

Here, أَخْدَانَ الشَّبَابِ (akhdān al-shabāb) refers to the friends and companions of youth.

Arabic also uses الرَّعَارِعُ (al-raʿāriʿ) for young people growing into youth and early maturity.

Akhdān here does not mean secret sexual relationships; it concerns companions and friends.

This witness shows that the Arabic plural akhdān can have a broader meaning than sexual partners.

The verse’s attribution is nevertheless disputed. Labīd’s collected poetry attributes it to him, but Lisān al-ʿArab records Ibn Barrī’s suggestion that it may belong to Al-Baʿīth.

Labīd lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without establishing the date of composition and resolving the attribution, this particular verse cannot be treated as a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

Its value lies in documenting akhdān for companions and friends, a use recorded in literary and lexical sources.

Lisān al-ʿArab, raʿaʿa: the witness attributed to Labīd and its disputed attribution

خِدْنُ الْجَارِيَةِ (khidn al-jāriyah): a young woman’s close companion

Lexicons record the expression خِدْنُ الْجَارِيَةِ (khidn al-jāriyah).

Ibn Fāris and Ibn Manẓūr explain it with مُحَدِّثُهَا (muḥaddithuhā): someone who converses and associates with the young woman.

This expression indicates a personal, close relationship.

Some lexical accounts relate such companionship to emotional bonds and relations between women and men before Islam.

Muḥaddithuhā itself, however, does not mean sexual intercourse. Its direct meaning is someone who talks with her.

This definition alone therefore does not make every man who spoke to a woman her khidn in the sense of sexual partner.

Exegetical accounts of akhdān, on the other hand, show that the same word was also used for close sexual relationships in some relations between women and men.

Together, these observations clarify the breadth of the word’s uses.

Mukhādanah: friendship and a mutual relationship

Arabic uses خَادَنْتُ الرَّجُلَ (khādantu al-rajula) for befriending and associating with someone.

Mukhādanah is therefore not confined to relationships between women and men. Two men may also maintain companionship in its general sense.

In Asās al-Balāghah, Al-Zamakhsharī places أَخْدَانِ سُوءٍ (akhdān sūʾ) and أَخْدَانِ صِدْقٍ (akhdān ṣidq) alongside one another.

This distinction matters.

If khidn inherently meant a corrupt friend or a partner in an illicit relationship, “true friends” would require a different explanation.

The negative force of the Qur’anic usage therefore cannot be transferred to every derivative of the root.

Conversely, the general sense of friendship must not obscure the specific meaning of akhdān in the context of marriage and sexual relations.

The rajaz witness attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes this line to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj:

وَانْصَعْنَ أَخْدَانًا لِذَاكَ الْأَخْدَنِ

wa-inṣaʿna akhdānan li-dhāka al-akhdani

The rajaz context concerns people departing from their former companionship and relationship. Akhdān denotes close companions here, while al-akhdan describes someone who has friends or companions.

The rajaz passage’s authenticity is disputed, however. In Jamharat al-Lughah, Ibn Durayd reports that Al-Aṣmaʿī questioned the attribution of part of it to Ruʾbah and regarded it as fabricated.

This witness therefore cannot independently establish the historical existence of a particular meaning.

Ruʾbah is also an Islamic-era poet; even if the attribution is accepted, his verse is not a pre-Islamic witness.

Clear lexical definitions and independently supported witnesses offer a better basis for identifying KH D N’s basic meaning than reliance on a disputed rajaz passage.

Ibn Durayd, Jamharat al-Lughah, page 406: Al-Aṣmaʿī’s objection to the rajaz attribution

3. Akhdān in relations between women and men: public and secret relationships

Early exegetical sources explain akhdān in Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5 as romantic and sexual relationships outside marriage.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits accounts distinguishing, in some pre-Islamic Arab relationships, public sexual relations from a relationship maintained secretly with a particular companion.

These accounts apply musāfaḥah mainly to public sexual relations or relations without a regular companion, and taking akhdān to a relationship maintained away from public view with a particular friend or companion.

Some reports even state that certain groups disapproved of public sexual relations while tolerating a secret relationship with a particular companion.

Such reports matter for understanding one historical background to the word’s usage, but they have two limitations.

First, they are exegetical reports. Without independent examination, their details must not be treated as certain, uniform facts about every Arab tribe.

Second, the distinction between public and secret relationships does not by itself fully define either word.

Sifāḥ belongs to S F Ḥ and has its own lexical field. Khidn, in turn, fundamentally concerns companionship and association.

A historical explanation identifying musāfaḥah with public relationships and mukhādanah with secret ones must therefore not become an absolute rule for every Arabic usage.

The Qur’an places both descriptions alongside iḥṣān and marriage. This context indicates that the question concerns the kind of sexual relationship and its associated commitment.

Does khidn necessarily mean one person?

Some exegetical reports explain khidn as a particular companion, sometimes a regular sexual partner.

Akhdān itself is plural, however, and its plurality does not establish that each individual necessarily had several partners.

Arabic may use a plural description of a group even where each member has only one instance of the relationship.

Saying that a group of people have friends, for example, does not by itself specify how many friends each person has.

The plural akhdān in Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5 therefore does not prove that each person had multiple sexual partners.

Conversely, a historical report about having one regular companion does not justify always translating akhdān as “only one partner.”

The plural form alone determines neither the number nor the character of the relationships.

Al-Ṭabarī: reports of public relationships, a particular companion, and secrecy

4. Al-Nisāʾ 4:25: the prohibition of women taking akhdān

وَمَنْ لَمْ يَسْتَطِعْ مِنْكُمْ طَوْلًا أَنْ يَنْكِحَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ فَمِنْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ مِنْ فَتَيَاتِكُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِإِيمَانِكُمْ بَعْضُكُمْ مِنْ بَعْضٍ فَانْكِحُوهُنَّ بِإِذْنِ أَهْلِهِنَّ وَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ مُحْصَنَاتٍ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ فَإِذَا أُحْصِنَّ فَإِنْ أَتَيْنَ بِفَاحِشَةٍ فَعَلَيْهِنَّ نِصْفُ مَا عَلَى الْمُحْصَنَاتِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ خَشِيَ الْعَنَتَ مِنْكُمْ وَأَنْ تَصْبِرُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-man lam yastaṭiʿ minkum ṭawlan an yankiḥa al-muḥṣanāti al-muʾmināti fa-min mā malakat aymānukum min fatayātikumu al-muʾmināti wa-allāhu aʿlamu bi-īmānikum baʿḍukum min baʿḍin fa-inkiḥūhunna bi-idhni ahlihinna wa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna bi-al-maʿrūfi muḥṣanātin ghayra musāfiḥātin wa-lā muttakhidhāti akhdānin fa-idhā uḥṣinna fa-in atayna bi-fāḥishatin fa-ʿalayhinna niṣfu mā ʿalā al-muḥṣanāti mina al-ʿadhābi dhālika li-man khashiya al-ʿanata minkum wa-an taṣbirū khayrun lakum wa-allāhu ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Whoever among you lacks the means to marry believing muḥṣan women, then marry from your believing young women who are in your possession. The God knows your faith best; you are of one another. Marry them with their people’s permission and give them their due in a recognized, proper manner, as muḥṣan women, neither engaging in sifāḥ nor taking sexual companions. Then, when they become muḥṣan, if they commit an indecency, they are subject to half the punishment borne by muḥṣan women. This is for anyone among you who fears hardship; if you remain patient, it is better for you. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25

This verse addresses marriage where someone lacks the means to marry the first group of believing women, then considers marriage to believing young women in another status.

It mentions their people’s permission, payment of their due, and the condition of iḥṣān.

In that setting appears مُحْصَنَاتٍ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ (muḥṣanāt ghayr musāfiḥāt wa-lā muttakhidhāt akhdān).

This combination is decisive for identifying the meaning of akhdān.

مُحْصَنَاتٍ (muḥṣanāt): the context of marriage and safeguarding the relationship

Muḥṣanāt belongs to Ḥ Ṣ N and has different Qur’anic uses involving protection, chastity, and marital status.

Here it occurs in the context of marriage, payment of women’s due, and the distinction between the accepted relationship and relationships outside it.

Akhdān must therefore not be examined apart from this construction.

In this context, khidn is not an ordinary friend in a social relationship: the verse is specifying conditions of marital relations.

Conversely, muḥṣanāt itself does not mean akhdān; understanding it requires examining its independent root, Ḥ Ṣ N.

غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ (ghayr musāfiḥāt): the distinction from sifāḥ

In غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ (ghayr musāfiḥāt), musāfiḥāt belongs to S F Ḥ.

In Qur’anic usage concerning marriage, sifāḥ concerns sexual relations outside the framework accepted in the verse.

Akhdān belongs to another root and denotes close companions.

Their conjunction indicates that the Qur’an is not considering only one form of relationship outside marriage.

Historical accounts apply musāfaḥah mainly to public relations or relations with different people, and taking akhdān to a close, secret relationship with a particular person.

This historical explanation fits the context, but the Arabic phrase contains no separate word meaning “publicly” or “secretly.” The absence of a separate adverb does not itself establish that secrecy plays no part in the word’s contextual meaning; several lexicographers and commentators emphasize it in this usage.

A word’s direct meaning should therefore be distinguished from its historical instances.

وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ (wa-lā muttakhidhāt akhdān): close relationships outside marriage

Muttakhidhāt belongs to ʾ KH DH and Form VIII, rather than KH D N.

It refers to women who take or establish akhdān for themselves.

Akhdān denotes those taken in this relationship: the close companions themselves.

In the verse’s context, this companionship concerns sexual relations outside marriage.

“Sexual companions” or “secret sexual relationships” can therefore be considered in translation.

The choice nevertheless requires care.

“Sexual companion” makes the kind of relationship clear.

Secrecy is a feature mentioned in lexical and historical explanations of khidn, but the phrase has no explicit, separate qualification specifying secrecy. Omitting it from the base translation is therefore a translation proposal, rather than a rejection of the sources’ reading “secret lovers.”

A base translation should accordingly preserve the kind of relationship, with the discussion of secrecy presented separately.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: secrecy and companionship involving desire

Does the verse prohibit ordinary friendship between women and men?

This phrase alone cannot establish such a general ruling.

Lexicons also record khidn and mukhādanah as companionship and friendship.

In the Qur’an, akhdān occurs in the specific context of marriage, iḥṣān, and musāfaḥah.

The verse’s subject cannot therefore be extended to all human interaction solely through the general meaning of friendship.

A woman’s conversation with a male colleague, family acquaintance, or scholarly collaboration, for example, does not become “taking akhdān” here merely because a social relationship exists.

Nor does this conclusion imply that every kind of sexual relationship outside marriage is permitted: the same context distinguishes marriage from sexual relationships outside it.

The point is to attach the judgment to the conduct the verse addresses, rather than to every relationship that ordinary language might call friendship.

5. Al-Māʾidah 5:5: the same description applied to men

الْيَوْمَ أُحِلَّ لَكُمُ الطَّيِّبَاتُ وَطَعَامُ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ حِلٌّ لَكُمْ وَطَعَامُكُمْ حِلٌّ لَهُمْ وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ إِذَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ وَلَا مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ وَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالْإِيمَانِ فَقَدْ حَبِطَ عَمَلُهُ وَهُوَ فِي الْآخِرَةِ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

al-yawma uḥilla lakumu al-ṭayyibātu wa-ṭaʿāmu alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba ḥillun lakum wa-ṭaʿāmukum ḥillun lahum wa-al-muḥṣanātu mina al-muʾmināti wa-al-muḥṣanātu mina alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba min qablikum idhā ātaytumūhunna ujūrahunna muḥṣinīna ghayra musāfiḥīna wa-lā muttakhidhī akhdānin wa-man yakfur bi-al-īmāni fa-qad ḥabiṭa ʿamaluhu wa-huwa fī al-ākhirati mina al-khāsirīna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Today wholesome things have been made lawful for you. The food of those given the Book is lawful for you, and your food is lawful for them; also muḥṣan women among the believers and muḥṣan women among those given the Book before you, when you give them their due, yourselves practicing iḥṣān, neither engaging in sifāḥ nor taking sexual companions. Whoever rejects faith, their work has come to nothing, and in the Hereafter they are among the losers.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:5

The verse concerns wholesome things being lawful, the food of the People of the Book, and marriage to muḥṣan women among believers and the People of the Book.

Its marriage passage includes مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ وَلَا مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ (muḥṣinīn ghayr musāfiḥīn wa-lā muttakhidhī akhdān).

The main difference from Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 here lies in the descriptions’ grammatical forms.

In Al-Nisāʾ, muttakhidhāt is feminine plural.

In Al-Māʾidah, muttakhidhī is masculine plural.

The same concept of taking akhdān is therefore applied here to men.

Men’s relationships with akhdān

Al-Māʾidah 5:5 addresses men in the context of marriage to believing women and women of the People of the Book.

Wa-lā muttakhidhī akhdān indicates that a man, too, must not substitute an akhdān relationship for the relationship intended by the verse, or maintain it alongside that relationship.

The linguistic point is clear: muttakhidhī refers to men.

One therefore cannot claim that the Qur’an prohibits this relationship only for women while overlooking the same behavior in men.

Nor is KH D N lexically confined to a particular gender.

A khidn can be a male or female friend or companion.

The Qur’an’s two different constructions also demonstrate this linguistic possibility.

Iḥṣān and payment of women’s due

In this verse, إِذَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ (idhā ātaytumūhunna ujūrahunna) precedes the description of the men.

In the marriage context, it concerns paying women their financial due.

The intended relationship is therefore more than emotional companionship or personal association; it is presented within marriage and its commitments.

Taking akhdān, by contrast, is close sexual companionship outside marriage in this context.

This distinction must not become a merely outward rule about publicly announcing a relationship.

A relationship may be publicly known yet lack the necessary framework and commitment.

Conversely, a couple’s private life may not be known to others while their relationship rests upon marriage.

Secrecy and being outside marriage are therefore related but distinct concepts.

In the Qur’anic use of akhdān, the central question concerns the kind of bond and its relationship to marriage, rather than simply whether others know it exists.

Do the two verses share an ethical standard?

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5 differ in their addressees and marriage conditions.

Al-Nisāʾ concerns women whom the verse identifies in a particular social and legal status.

Al-Māʾidah discusses marriage to believing women and women of the People of the Book.

Both, however, place akhdān alongside musāfaḥah and in contrast with iḥṣān.

This repetition shows the Qur’an addressing the same kind of relationship outside marriage for both women and men.

The shared phrases do not mean that every legal and social detail or marriage condition is identical in the two verses.

Each has its own addressees, setting, and qualifications.

For KH D N, what matters is akhdān’s shared meaning and its use in feminine and masculine constructions.

Al-Ṭabarī: the description of men and the distinction between musāfaḥah and taking akhdān

6. Distinctions from related words

KH D N (khidn) and Ṣ Ḥ B (ṣāḥib): close friend and companion

Ṣāḥib has a broad Qur’anic field, encompassing a companion, associate, fellow traveler, or someone in a relationship with another.

For example, Al-Kahf 18:34 and Al-Kahf 18:37 call two people in conversation ṣāḥib.

Arabic khidn can express a closer kind of companionship, sometimes involving a personal, romantic, or sexual relationship.

This distinction is not absolute, however: ṣāḥib itself can describe very close relationships in some contexts.

In the Qur’an, the main distinction becomes clear because akhdān occurs in the context of marriage and musāfaḥah.

Ṣāḥib must therefore not be understood as a sexual partner in every verse, nor khidn confined to that meaning in every Arabic use.

Study of Ṣ Ḥ B

KH D N (khidn) and KH L L (khalīl): intimacy and close relationships

Arabic khalīl concerns a close, intimate friend.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:125, Abraham is described as khalīlan.

Qur’anic khalīl does not necessarily involve a romantic or sexual relationship.

Khidn, too, can fundamentally mean a close friend, but its Qur’anic use in the two marriage verses acquires a more specific sense.

Commentaries repeatedly explain akhdān with أخلاء (akhillāʾ). This indicates semantic proximity, rather than complete identity across all uses.

If khidn in Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 is described as a kind of khalīl, the context of sexual relations outside marriage must therefore remain clear.

This explanation does not make every Qur’anic khalīl identical to the condemned khidn relationship.

KH D N (khidn) and S F Ḥ (sifāḥ): close companion and sexual relations outside marriage

These two roots occur alongside each other in both Qur’anic uses of akhdān.

Sifāḥ belongs to S F Ḥ and, in the marriage context, denotes sexual relations outside the framework intended by the Qur’an.

Khidn fundamentally concerns companionship and close association.

Historical accounts apply musāfaḥah to public relations or relations without a regular companion, and mukhādanah to a relationship with a close, sometimes secret companion.

This explains the difference between two kinds of instance.

Lexically, however, every musāfaḥah must not be assumed necessarily public, nor every mukhādanah necessarily completely secret.

Their Qur’anic common feature is that both stand in contrast with iḥṣān and marriage.

KH D N (khidn) and ʾ KH DH (ittikhādh): the companion and establishing the relationship

Ittikhādh plays an important role in مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ (muttakhidhāt akhdān) and مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ (muttakhidhī akhdān).

Ittakhadha belongs to ʾ KH DH and, depending on context, may mean taking, choosing, or appointing something for oneself.

Akhdān are the people taken in this relationship.

The sentence therefore describes more than the existence of a chance acquaintance: it concerns establishing or taking a particular kind of relationship for oneself.

Taking someone in such a relationship does not by itself establish deceptive intent, secrecy, or sexual intercourse.

The relationship’s character becomes clear from what is taken and the marriage context.

Study of ʾ KH DH

KH D N (khidn) and W D D (wudd): affection and the kind of relationship

Qur’anic wudd concerns affection and friendship.

Someone may love another person without having a sexual relationship with them.

Conversely, sexual intercourse alone does not establish sincere affection between two people.

Affection, close friendship, and a sexual relationship are therefore three related but distinct concepts.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5 concern a relationship presented within the context of marriage and iḥṣān.

These verses do not establish that affection or attraction toward another person alone amounts to taking akhdān.

Study of W D D

7. Grammar and translation proposals for Forqan

In both verses, أَخْدَانٍ (akhdān) is the broken plural of خِدْن (khidn), on the pattern أَفْعَال (afʿāl), and is an indefinite genitive noun.

It forms a construct phrase with muttakhidhāt or muttakhidhī.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, مُتَّخِذَاتِ (muttakhidhāt) is a feminine plural active participle of Form VIII.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:5, مُتَّخِذِي (muttakhidhī) is a masculine plural active participle whose final plural nūn is omitted because of the construct relationship.

The distinction therefore concerns the grammatical gender of those taking akhdān, rather than a change in the meaning of akhdān itself.

Nor does the plural akhdān specify how many partners each person has.

The challenge of translating أَخْدَان (akhdān)

Several Persian equivalents are available, each with limitations.

Dūstān, “friends,” conveys the general root meaning, but may conceal the kind of relationship in these two verses.

Dūstān-e penhānī, “secret friends,” foregrounds one historical use, but without explanation may encompass any private friendship unrelated to sexual intimacy.

Maʿshūqeh-hā, “mistresses,” approaches the contextual sense when men are addressed, but does not work identically for both genders and may conflate sexual relations with romantic affection.

Sharīkān-e jensī-ye penhānī, “secret sexual partners,” makes the intended relationship clearer, but inserts secrecy as a definite qualification.

Hamrāhān-e jensī, “sexual companions,” is therefore proposed as a more suitable base translation. An explanation can clarify that it concerns sexual relations outside marriage and that lexical and historical accounts frequently associate it with secrecy.

Brief root meaning and the meaning in each verse

أَخْدَانٍ (akhdān): Al-Nisāʾ 4:25

Root: KH D N.

Grammar: the broken plural of خِدْن (khidn), on the pattern أَفْعَال (afʿāl), indefinite and genitive as the construct complement in مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ (muttakhidhāt akhdān).

Brief root meaning: a friend, companion, or someone maintaining close companionship with another.

Meaning in this verse: sexual companions whom women take for themselves in a relationship outside marriage.

أَخْدَانٍ (akhdān): Al-Māʾidah 5:5

Root: KH D N.

Grammar: the broken plural of خِدْن (khidn), on the pattern أَفْعَال (afʿāl), indefinite and genitive as the construct complement in مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ (muttakhidhī akhdān).

Brief root meaning: a friend, companion, or someone maintaining close companionship with another.

Meaning in this verse: sexual companions whom men take for themselves in a relationship outside marriage.

Both uses retain the root’s general sense of companionship, while marriage, iḥṣān, and musāfaḥah identify the particular kind of relationship.

8. Conclusion

Arabic KH D N concerns friendship and close companionship. Khidn and khadīn denote an intimate friend or companion, and akhdān is a plural in this family.

Lexical and literary witnesses show that the word can also describe ordinary friends and associates. In some relations between women and men, however, khidn denotes a romantic or sexual companion, frequently in a secret relationship.

The root occurs in only two Qur’anic verses, both in the context of marriage and iḥṣān.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 applies muttakhidhāt akhdān to women, while Al-Māʾidah 5:5 applies muttakhidhī akhdān to men. The responsibility involved is therefore not restricted to one gender.

Akhdān’s conjunction with musāfaḥah indicates that the subject is more than human friendship and association: it concerns a kind of sexual relationship outside marriage.

Early exegetical accounts explain musāfaḥah mainly as public relations or relations without a regular companion, and mukhādanah as a close, secret relationship. These reports illuminate one historical background, but must not become absolute rules for every use of the root.

Nor does the plural akhdān prove that each person had multiple partners, just as a close relationship alone does not establish deceptive intent or secrecy.

Final definition: KH D N denotes companionship, friendship, and close association. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5, akhdān refers to companions in a sexual relationship presented in the context of marriage and in contrast with iḥṣān. Secrecy is an established feature of some historical instances, but the root’s basic meaning must not be confined to secret friendship or illicit relationships. The construction and context identify the kind of relationship. Brief root meaning: Companionship, friendship, and close association; in the two Qur’anic verses, sexual companionship outside marriage.
