Core meaning: a member or part of a whole; separating something into multiple parts. On the derivation adopted here for the Quranic word عِضِينَ, it describes making the Quran into fragments and treating it as disconnected portions.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The triliteral ع ض و concerns a member, part, or component of a larger whole. Related words express separating, cutting into pieces, and dividing something into multiple parts.

عُضْو or عِضْو names a bodily member of a human or animal. Early lexical explanations particularly describe a part consisting of bone with its surrounding flesh.

Its plural is أَعْضَاء.

Hands, feet, and other bodily parts are members of a larger whole whose components together form the body.

The word also occurs for separated portions of an animal's body.

عَضَّى الذَّبِيحَةَ means to divide a slaughtered animal into separate parts. عَضَّيْتُ الشَّاةَ تَعْضِيَةً likewise means cutting a sheep into multiple portions.

Before division, the animal is one connected body; separating its members and parts produces multiple pieces.

The same field includes تَعْضِيَة, division into parts, and عِضَة, a piece or portion.

When something is divided, each portion may be called عِضَة.

We must distinguish being a member from being separated from the whole.

A natural body has multiple members without being dismembered. تَعْضِيَة concerns the act of separating and turning a whole into multiple portions.

This distinction matters for the Quranic use.

On the division reading, عِضِينَ describes people who make the Quran into separate portions. The issue is not simply its many verses and surahs but their treatment of it.

Some dictionaries also discuss magic, falsehood, and slander in connection with this word. This involves possible derivation from the different root ع ض ه and should not be merged without examination with membership and division.

The basic definition of ع ض و is therefore:

A member or part of a whole, with related verbal and nominal forms expressing separation into multiple components.

This rests on clear lexical uses. Whether Quranic عِضِينَ derives from this family or a neighboring root requires separate examination.

2. Historical evidence and early Arabic usage

Two types of evidence matter: عُضْو for bodily members and تَعْضِيَة for cutting apart and separating a whole's components.

Early dictionaries record عُضْو for a member of a human or animal body: a particular part of a physical whole.

عَضَّى الشَّاةَ and عَضَّى الْجَزُورَ concern cutting up slaughtered sheep and camels.

These uses fit everyday life, where slaughter is followed by division of meat into portions.

A camel may be slaughtered and its meat distributed among several families. A previously unified whole becomes multiple pieces.

تَعْضِيَة itself does not mean fair division, unfair division, or distribution to the poor. Those features depend on how and why the division occurs.

The word itself means separation into parts.

Poetic evidence for division into parts

A familiar example is a poetic line attributed to Ru'bah ibn al-Ajjaj:

وَلَيْسَ دِينُ اللَّهِ بِالْمُعَضَّى

wa-laysa dīnu Allāhi bi-al-muʿaḍḍā

“The religion of The God is not something divided into pieces.”

الْمُعَضَّى is the passive participle of عَضَّى, describing something divided into separate portions.

This illustrates extension beyond material objects to an immaterial subject such as religion.

Religion is pictured as a whole whose components should not be separated so as to destroy their connection.

Ru'bah lived in the Umayyad and early Abbasid periods and died in 145 AH. This is therefore post-Quranic Arabic evidence, not pre-Islamic poetry.

It helps illustrate early usage and semantic extension, but does not independently attest this expression before Islam.

Its closeness to Al-Hijr 15:91 also helps explain its use by lexicographers to elucidate the verse. It illustrates usage in the poet's period, not the pre-Quranic existence of every later interpretation.

Limits of pre-Quranic evidence

The bodily-member and division senses of عُضْو and تَعْضِيَة are established in classical Arabic. If عِضِينَ is derived from ع ض و, the Quran also attests this semantic family in the language of revelation; that attestation should not be assumed independently of the derivational question.

This study has not established sufficient independent, datable poetic or inscriptional evidence to assign every form and sense to the pre-Islamic period.

One therefore cannot assert without evidence that عِضِينَ was already a fixed pre-Islamic term for dividing a religious book.

The better-established lexical senses are member, portion, and division. On the reading adopted here, the verse applies that image to the Quran.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms include عُضْو, عِضْو, أَعْضَاء, عَضَّى, تَعْضِيَة, مُعَضًّى, عِضَة, and عِضِين.

They have different roles.

عُضْو and عِضْو: A member or part of a human or animal body.

أَعْضَاء: The plural of member.

عَضَّى: He divided something into separate parts.

تَعْضِيَة: Division into parts and separation of a whole's components.

مُعَضًّى: Something divided into multiple portions.

عِضَة: A piece or portion; the singular of عِضِين in one morphological analysis.

عِضِين: Separate portions, on the derivation from ع ض و; the condition into which people are said to have made the Quran.

Modern Arabic also uses عُضْو for membership in associations and organizations. This is intelligible from being part of a whole, but modern organizational terminology should not be inserted into Quranic meaning without historical evidence.

The Quranic form

On the derivation from ع ض و, only عِضِينَ is recorded in the Quran under this root:

Al-Hijr 15:91

عِضِينَ is a plural in the accusative, not technically a collective noun.

In the familiar analysis, its singular is عِضَة and its inflection follows the sound masculine plural by attachment to that class.

The nominative is عِضُون and the accusative and genitive are عِضِينَ.

It is not an ordinary sound masculine plural of a masculine adjective referring to rational beings. Its singular عِضَة is feminine and means a piece or portion in this analysis.

A comparable pattern is عِزَة with its plural عِزِينَ; only the plural occurs in Al-Maarij 70:37.

عِزِينَ belongs to ع ز و, whereas عِضِينَ belongs to ع ض و in the analysis under discussion. Similar plural formation does not make their roots or meanings identical.

4. The morphology of عِضِينَ and the missing consonant

The visible form عِضَة contains only ع and ض from the root; its third consonant is not apparent.

Two early analyses address the missing consonant.

First analysis: ع ض و

One reconstruction gives the singular's original form as عِضْوَة.

The و is understood as deleted; grammatical explanations also describe the final ة as compensation for the deleted consonant. Thus عِضَة should not be explained merely by saying that an ordinary feminine ending remained.

On this account, عِضِينَ belongs with عُضْو and تَعْضِيَة.

Its meaning is connected with bodily members and pieces, extending to separate portions.

When an animal is divided into members and pieces, each remains a portion of the original whole.

The Quranic use similarly depicts the Quran made into disconnected portions.

This analysis fits جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ clearly at the semantic level.

Second analysis: ع ض ه

The other analysis reconstructs عِضْهَة, with an original third consonant ه deleted from the form.

عِضِينَ then belongs to ع ض ه rather than ع ض و.

That family includes عَضَهَ and عَضِيهَة for false accusation, slander, improper allegations, and in some uses magic and speech associated with sorcerers.

On this reading, عِضِينَ can concern false descriptions and accusations directed against the Quran.

For example, opponents called it magic, poetry, or fabricated speech.

Which analysis is preferred here?

Both morphological analyses appear in Arabic sources. Classification in a modern index cannot conclusively establish historical derivation.

The connection between الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ and عِضِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:90 and Al-Hijr 15:91 is significant.

الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ belongs to ق س م and, in the reading under discussion, concerns division. Identifying the people and the precise kind of division requires attention to differing contextual interpretations.

The next verse describes people who made the Quran عِضِينَ.

This association supports the reading “separate portions.”

The slander and magic reading also fits descriptions elsewhere of opponents calling revelation magic, poetry, or fabricated speech.

That fit alone does not prove that magic or slander is necessarily the primary sense of عِضِينَ in this verse.

This study prefers division into parts on contextual grounds while retaining the derivation from ع ض ه as a separate lexical possibility. This preference does not conclusively resolve historical derivation.

5. الَّذِينَ جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ

The Quranic occurrence assigned to this root under the adopted analysis is in Surah Al-Hijr.

Understanding it requires examining the nearby expressions.

Al-Hijr 15:87 speaks of the gift given to the Messenger and mentions الْقُرْآنَ الْعَظِيمَ.

Al-Hijr 15:89 then states:

وَقُلْ إِنِّي أَنَا النَّذِيرُ الْمُبِينُ

wa-qul innī anā al-nadhīru al-mubīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:89

Al-Hijr 15:89

“Say: I am indeed the clear warner.”

It continues:

كَمَا أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَى الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ

ka-mā anzalnā ʿalā al-muqtasimīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:90

Al-Hijr 15:90

“Just as We sent down upon those who divided.”

The object of “sent down” is not expressed within this verse. One interpretation supplies punishment in connection with the warning; other interpretations connect it with the preceding gift of Scripture. The following verse states:

الَّذِينَ جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ

alladhīna jaʿalū al-qurʾāna ʿiḍīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:91

Al-Hijr 15:91

“Those who made the Quran into fragments.”

جَعَلُوا belongs to ج ع ل and expresses making or turning something into a particular condition.

الْقُرْآنَ is its first object and عِضِينَ its second.

The syntax does not merely state that the Quran has different parts. It says people made it into a condition described by عِضِينَ.

This distinction matters.

The Quran itself comprises verses and surahs. A book's having different sections is not inherently blameworthy.

The verse instead concerns people making the Quran into separate portions, on the adopted reading.

Their treatment of the Quran is the central issue.

The relationship between الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ and عِضِينَ

الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:90 refers, in this reading, to a group associated with division.

In Al-Hijr 15:91, الَّذِينَ جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ naturally qualifies or explains that group.

The second verse thus identifies the dividers: they made the Quran into fragments.

Another grammatical analysis takes الَّذِينَ as the start of a new sentence whose predicate is found in the next verse. This syntactic difference does not itself settle the lexical derivation of عِضِينَ.

On the division reading, the two verses connect dividing with making into fragments.

These two words alone do not conclusively identify one historical group with a specified membership.

The phrase names neither individuals nor their number.

Accounts linking it to particular people who divided the routes into Mecca should therefore not be imposed as the direct lexical meaning.

6. What does making the Quran into fragments mean?

In the lexical field of ع ض و, fragmentation means separating components from a whole.

Applied to the Quran, however, the image need not mean physically cutting an object into pieces.

The Quran is presented as something people treat in a way that makes it عِضِينَ.

Context and other Quranic uses suggest several behaviors associated with this image.

Varied and conflicting descriptions of the Quran

One established interpretation concerns dividing their descriptions of the Quran into different improper labels.

They might call one part magic, another poetry, and another tales of earlier peoples.

Al-Anbiya 21:5 records successive allegations: confused dreams, fabrication, and calling the Messenger a poet. The verse does not itself require a separate group for each allegation.

Al-Furqan 25:4 and Al-Furqan 25:5 also mention allegations of fabrication and tales of earlier peoples.

The Quran is then not fairly considered as a message but represented through scattered, conflicting labels.

This fits one interpretation of جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ.

The whole meaning should not, however, be restricted to a few specific labels such as poetry and magic. These can be examples rather than the complete definition.

Selective acceptance and rejection

Another reading concerns accepting part of Scripture while rejecting another part.

The Quran addresses such behavior explicitly elsewhere.

Al-Baqarah 2:85 criticizes believing in part of Scripture and rejecting another part.

Al-Nisa 4:150 and Al-Nisa 4:151 discuss people seeking to separate The God from His Messengers, saying they believe in some and reject others.

Selective treatment of divine guidance is thus a recognized Quranic concern.

A conceptual relationship can therefore be drawn with the image of عِضِينَ.

Someone may emphasize a verse serving their interests but disregard or arbitrarily dismiss another verse that constrains their behavior.

The issue is not simply selecting a verse for study, but separating it from related passages to preserve a predetermined conclusion.

Two levels must nevertheless be distinguished.

Al-Baqarah 2:85 explicitly describes accepting part of Scripture and rejecting another part.

Al-Hijr 15:91 uses عِضِينَ, interpreted here as fragments.

Every detail of Al-Baqarah 2:85 should not therefore be declared the lexical meaning of عِضِينَ. Their connection is conceptual within the Quran.

Separating verses from context and connections

A further application for reflection is extracting a statement from its context to support a conclusion the passages together do not warrant.

Someone may quote only part of a verse concerning punishment or warfare while overlooking qualifications in that verse and nearby passages that define its conditions and limits.

The statement is then detached from its network of meaning.

The same problem can arise in discussions of justice, giving, divorce, human choice, and others' rights.

This is a contemporary reflective application of fragmentation, not a claim that عِضِينَ lexically means “quoting a verse out of context.”

Lexical research should not insert a meaning unsupported by the word itself merely to establish an interpretive method.

7. Is every division of the Quran wrong?

Does every division into surahs, verses, topics, or teaching units fall under عِضِينَ?

The context and other verses help clarify the answer.

The Quran has individual verses and refers to its own structure.

Hud 11:1 describes a book whose verses are made firm and then set out in detail.

Fussilat 41:3 also describes a book whose verses are explained in detail.

Al-Isra 17:106 speaks of a Quran divided for recitation to people over time.

Division and detailed exposition are therefore not inherently contrary to the Quran's method.

Careful understanding requires examining individual verses, words, and subjects while also recognizing their connections with other verses.

We must distinguish division for understanding and study from fragmentation that destroys the whole's connections and meaning.

A researcher may concentrate on one verse to understand a word. This is not inherently wrong.

But deliberately excluding qualifying or contrary passages to force that verse to prove a predetermined conclusion goes beyond a neutral study method.

That is selective treatment of the verses.

Al-Hijr 15:91 does not make reading one surah, studying one verse, or writing a topical article automatically an instance of عِضِينَ.

The verse condemns a particular treatment of the Quran, interpreted in terms of divisive treatment or improper allegations; it does not condemn every educational division.

This distinction avoids wrongly condemning verse-by-verse research itself.

8. Responsibility for one's treatment of the Quran

After describing those who made the Quran عِضِينَ, two verses address their responsibility.

فَوَرَبِّكَ لَنَسْأَلَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

fa-wa-rabbika la-nasʾalannahum ajmaʿīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:92

Al-Hijr 15:92

“By your Lord, We will certainly question them all.”

It continues:

عَمَّا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

ʿammā kānū yaʿmalūn

Al-Ḥijr 15:93

Al-Hijr 15:93

“About what they used to do.”

Questioning and accountability concern their actions.

جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ likewise contains the verb جَعَلُوا: it describes conduct performed by people.

This does not automatically make every disagreement over a verse an instance of that conduct.

Two people sincerely seeking truth may differ over a word's meaning or a verse's context.

That differs from knowingly excluding parts of the text or directing improper allegations against it.

Assessment should distinguish misunderstanding, limited knowledge, defensible disagreement, and deliberate selective treatment.

These verses identify particular conduct and recall those people's responsibility.

عِضِينَ should therefore not become a label used to discredit every differing interpretation.

9. Comparison with neighboring roots

ع ض و and ب ع ض

ب ع ض concerns part of a larger whole.

بَعْض can mean a portion or some members of a group without an act of separation or fragmentation.

عُضْو primarily names a bodily member, while تَعْضِيَة concerns dividing a whole into pieces.

“Some people” identifies part of a group without asserting that the group was previously cut apart.

Al-Hijr 15:91 combines جَعَلُوا and عِضِينَ to describe a condition resulting from people's conduct.

عِضِينَ should therefore not simply be equated with بَعْض regardless of context.

ع ض و and ق س م

ق س م concerns division and allocating a share or portion.

الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:90 belongs to this family.

قِسْمَة may describe distribution among several people or determining each person's share.

تَعْضِيَة instead emphasizes separating a whole into pieces or components in the ع ض و branch.

The fields overlap without being identical.

Property can be divided among people without physically cutting it apart. Conversely, cutting up an animal does not necessarily allocate shares to people.

Their association in Al-Hijr 15:90 and Al-Hijr 15:91 matters, but each retains its own contribution.

ع ض و and ف ر ق

ف ر ق concerns separating and distinguishing.

تَعْضِيَة can likewise concern separation, but the image of a piece or bodily member is more prominent in its established uses.

فَرَقَ has a broad range involving people, groups, paths, and other matters.

عِضِينَ, on the ع ض و analysis, depicts a whole made into separate portions.

Every instance of تَعْضِيَة thus involves separation, but not every separation is تَعْضِيَة.

ع ض و and ق ط ع

ق ط ع concerns cutting, separating, and severing.

قَطَعَ can express a physical act of cutting.

عَضَّى, in examples involving slaughtered animals, concerns dividing the whole into members and pieces.

Cutting a rope at one point can be expressed by قطع; cutting up an animal additionally suggests division into multiple parts.

The families can overlap elsewhere, so this distinction should not become an absolute rule.

ع ض و and ع ض ه

This is central to the etymological discussion of عِضِينَ.

ع ض ه includes uses for slander, false allegations, and, in some instances, magic and associated speech.

One lexical analysis derives عِضَة from this root.

عِضِينَ could then concern false descriptions and accusations directed at the Quran.

On the ع ض و analysis, it instead derives from membership and division into parts.

These analyses concern two different roots.

Magic, slander, bodily members, and fragmentation should therefore not all be merged indiscriminately into one definition.

The fragmentation reading fits الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ clearly, while slander remains a possible lexical interpretation of the opponents' conduct.

ع ض و and ع ض ض

ع ض ض concerns biting and pressing with the teeth.

Its forms occur in Al Imran 3:119 and Al-Furqan 25:27.

ع ض و instead concerns a member and division into parts.

Despite shared initial consonants, the families are distinct.

عِضِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:91 should not mean biting the Quran.

Unvocalized spelling may encourage confusion, but morphology and lexical evidence do not support it.

ع ض و and ع ض ل

ع ض ل concerns difficulty, constriction, obstruction, and constraint.

تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ in Al-Baqarah 2:232 and Al-Nisa 4:19 belongs to that root.

The ع ض و reading of the Quranic word instead concerns fragmentation.

Resemblance does not justify transferring obstruction or difficulty into عِضِينَ.

10. Translation proposal for Forqan

On the derivation adopted here, عِضِينَ is this family's sole Quranic form.

This study prefers separate portions for the main translation, as it fits the sentence and context; the alternative derivation remains open.

Word: عِضِينَ

Root: ع ض و, on the derivation from عِضْوَة and تَعْضِيَة.

Word type: Accusative plural of عِضَة, inflected like the sound masculine plural by attachment to that class.

Brief root meaning: A member or part of a whole; separating something into multiple pieces.

Meaning here: Separate, disconnected portions, on the adopted derivation.

Suggested rendering:

الَّذِينَ جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ

alladhīna jaʿalū al-qurʾāna ʿiḍīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:91

“Those who made the Quran into fragments.”

This is concise, natural, and suited to the adopted reading.

جَعَلُوا expresses making, while عِضِينَ names the fragmented condition.

“Made into fragments” therefore renders the combination, not the noun عِضِينَ alone.

“Turned the Quran into separate parts” is also accurate in this reading, though “made into fragments” preserves a stronger image.

“Called the Quran magic” relies on a different lexical analysis and one possible description of the opponents' conduct.

The main translation preferred here should not be confined to that one interpretation.

“Distorted the Quran” is not a precise equivalent: distortion and fragmentation remain distinguishable.

Fragmentation and selective treatment may distort meaning in practice, but عِضِينَ alone does not express the whole concept of distortion.

In a Forqan word box, the adopted sense should remain distinct from the alternative. An additional explanation may note that some lexicographers derive عِضِينَ from ع ض ه and relate it to slander or allegations of magic.

This should not replace the definition of ع ض و itself.

Conclusion

ع ض و concerns a member or part of a whole, with related forms for dividing something into multiple pieces.

عُضْو primarily names part of a human or animal body; تَعْضِيَة concerns cutting up and separating the components of a whole.

The sole Quranic form assigned here to this root is عِضِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:91.

In a familiar morphological analysis, it is the plural of عِضَة and belongs to ع ض و, meaning separate pieces or portions.

Another analysis derives it from ع ض ه and connects it with slander, falsehood, and allegations of magic. That possibility must be acknowledged without merging it with the membership and division branch.

عِضِينَ immediately follows الْمُقْتَسِمِينَ in context, supporting the fragmentation reading.

Fragmenting the Quran does not mean condemning every division for study: the Quran itself has numerous, detailed verses.

The adopted reading condemns disconnected, divisive treatment. False allegations and selective acceptance occur in older interpretations; detaching passages from their semantic connections is a reflective application requiring separate explanation.

ع ض و primarily concerns a member or part of a whole, extending to separation into multiple pieces. On the reading adopted here, عِضِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:91 means separate portions, and جَعَلُوا الْقُرْآنَ عِضِينَ is rendered “made the Quran into fragments.” This preference does not erase the alternative derivation from ع ض ه.

Quranic study should distinguish division for understanding from fragmentation serving selective treatment or improper allegations. This distinction emerges from lexical meaning considered alongside context and related verses, not from the word alone.
