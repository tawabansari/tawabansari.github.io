Central meanings: foam, froth-like material arising from liquids or molten metals, butter, and related uses.

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic root Z B D concerns water foam, milk froth, material arising from molten metals, and butter obtained from milk. Other uses concern gifts and something given from one person’s property to another.

A useful connection among several of these senses is material becoming apparent or separating from something through movement, agitation, heating, or processing. This is a lexical explanation, not a fixed physical definition of every derivative.

An early lexical analysis describes the root’s core as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَوَلُّدِ شَيْءٍ عَنْ شَيْءٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā tawalludi shayʾin ʿan shayʾin

Meaning: “One principle indicating the emergence of one thing from another.”

This is broader than foam on water: foam appears through agitation, butter is obtained by processing milk, and waste material separates during metalworking.

These are nevertheless different. Water foam may be transient and unhelpful, while butter is valuable food. Worthlessness and impermanence are not fixed meanings of every derivative.

Only the noun zabad occurs in the Qur’an. It appears three times in Al-Raʿd 13:17, each concerning foam or material arising from liquids or metals.

2. Forms, derivatives, and Arabic uses

زَبَد — zabad

Zabad means foam or froth-like material appearing on water, milk, or other liquids.

It is used for sea foam, flood foam, and even froth at a camel’s mouth.

An early lexical explanation states:

وَلِلْبَحْرِ زَبَدٌ إِذَا هَاجَ مَوْجُهُ

wa-li-al-baḥri zabadun idhā hāja mawjuhu

Meaning: “The sea has foam when its waves surge.”

Zabad al-maʿādin also denotes dross or impurities arising during the melting of metals and separating from the useful material.

Azbād is recorded as a plural of zabad.

زُبْد and زُبْدَة — zubd and zubdah

Zubd, with ḍammah on the zāy, means butter extracted from milk. Zubdah is a piece or quantity of it.

One early definition describes zubd as the portion of milk obtained through churning.

Another connects zubd and zabad through similarity of color. This explanation should not be treated as conclusive proof of the word’s historical derivation.

Here something separates from another substance, but unlike the discarded foam of the flood illustration, the resulting substance is desirable and useful.

Zubdat al-shayʾ accordingly denotes the pure, selected, or essential part of something.

Zubdat al-kalām, for example, means the essence or summary of speech, not its froth or waste.

أَزْبَدَ — azbada

This verb means to foam or produce foam.

Azbada al-baḥru means that the sea foamed.

Azbada al-sharābu means that the drink produced froth.

It is also used of a person or animal foaming at the mouth.

Azbada al-sidru is recorded for a lote tree blossoming. The occurrence of blossoms is attested, but their precise color should not be made an essential part of the verb’s meaning.

تَزَبَّدَ — tazabbada

To foam, become frothy, or bring forth something resembling foam.

It also describes an angry person when froth or saliva appears around the mouth.

زَبَدَ السِّقَاءَ — zabada al-siqāʾa

This expression refers to churning milk in a skin to extract its butter.

زَبَدَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ سِقَاءَهَا

zabadat al-marʾatu siqāʾahā

Meaning: “The woman churned her milk skin until its butter came out.”

The usage belongs to everyday milk processing in Arab life.

زَبَدَهُ and زَبْد — zabadahu and zabd

The verb zabada is recorded both for feeding someone butter and for giving property. Lexical accounts distinguish these uses through the verb’s inflection and vowel patterns.

Zabd, with a vowelless bāʾ, also means a gift or bestowal.

The root therefore extends beyond natural phenomena to giving and transferring something from one person’s property to another.

These are recorded Arabic usages, but not the forms occurring in the Qur’an.

3. Early usages and linguistic witnesses

Foam, butter, and separation

A recognized Arabic expression is:

قَدْ صَرَّحَ الْمَحْضُ عَنِ الزَّبَدِ

qad ṣarraḥa al-maḥḍu ʿani al-zabadi

Its sense is:

“The pure milk has become clear of the foam.”

As a proverb, it describes a matter becoming clear after ambiguity and confusion.

The foam covers the underlying substance; its removal reveals what lies beneath.

Its recording in dictionaries does not, by itself, establish when the proverb originated or whether it was used before Islam.

Foam and moving water

Foam on water, particularly in floods and surging seas, provides a familiar concrete use of zabad.

Foam can become voluminous and cover the water’s surface while differing from the water beneath in structure, persistence, and function.

Zabad al-māʾ denotes water foam. In a flood, it may be accompanied by light floating matter; not everything floating on water should automatically be called zabad.

Thus the scene need not involve only clean white bubbles. Flood foam may mingle with impurities and floating debris.

Metal dross

Zabad is also recorded for material arising from molten metals.

When metal is heated to make ornaments or tools, impurities and waste can separate from the useful portion.

These differ from water foam in form and function but are described by the same word in connection with their appearance and separation from the main material.

This range directly matters to the Qur’anic usage: a single verse uses zabad both for flood foam and for material separating during metalworking.

4. Qur’anic usage

The root occurs three times, all within one verse.

Al-Raʿd 13:17

أَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا ۚ وَمِمَّا يُوقِدُونَ عَلَيْهِ فِي النَّارِ ابْتِغَاءَ حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْحَقَّ وَالْبَاطِلَ ۚ فَأَمَّا الزَّبَدُ فَيَذْهَبُ جُفَاءً ۖ وَأَمَّا مَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ فَيَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْأَمْثَالَ

anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-sālat awdiyatun bi-qadarihā fa-iḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyan wa-mimmā yūqidūna ʿalayhi fī al-nāri ibtighāʾa ḥilyatin aw matāʿin zabadun mithluhu kadhālika yaḍribu Allāhu al-ḥaqqa wa-al-bāṭila fa-ammā al-zabadu fa-yadhhabu jufāʾan wa-ammā mā yanfaʿu al-nāsa fa-yamkuthu fī al-arḍi kadhālika yaḍribu Allāhu al-amthāla

Al-Raʿd 13:17

Translation:

“He sent water down from the sky, and valleys flowed according to their capacity; the torrent carried rising foam. From what they heat in fire to obtain ornaments or useful goods, there is similar froth. Thus The God illustrates truth and falsehood. The foam goes away, cast aside, while what benefits people remains in the earth. Thus The God sets forth examples.”

First occurrence: زَبَدًا رَابِيًا — zabadan rābiyan

The first phrase reads:

فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا

fa-iḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyan

Al-Raʿd 13:17

“The torrent carried rising foam.”

Zabadan is an indefinite noun and the object of fa-iḥtamala.

Rābiyan describes it as rising, elevated, or increasing.

That adjective comes from R B W, not Z B D.

Rising and increasing should therefore not be derived solely from zabad. The noun names foam; rābiyan describes its raised appearance.

The moving torrent carries foam that may spread visibly over its surface, but the foam does not have all the properties or functions of the water beneath.

Second occurrence: زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ — zabadun mithluhu

The second phrase reads:

وَمِمَّا يُوقِدُونَ عَلَيْهِ فِي النَّارِ ابْتِغَاءَ حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ

wa-mimmā yūqidūna ʿalayhi fī al-nāri ibtighāʾa ḥilyatin aw matāʿin zabadun mithluhu

Al-Raʿd 13:17

“From what they heat in fire to obtain ornaments or useful goods, there is similar froth.”

Zabadun is nominative in this nominal construction, referring to material arising during the heating and melting of metals.

Mithluhu compares this material with the torrent’s foam.

The verse places two different physical phenomena side by side:

In the first, water flows and foam appears on its surface.

In the second, material is heated in fire and impurities or waste separate from the useful portion.

Both produce something distinct from the useful material that remains.

Gold and silver for ornaments, or iron and copper for tools, are intelligible examples. These metals are not individually named in the Arabic verse.

Third occurrence: فَأَمَّا الزَّبَدُ فَيَذْهَبُ جُفَاءً

“The foam goes away, cast aside.”

Al-zabadu is definite and refers back to the foam described in the two preceding images.

Fa-yadhhabu expresses departure or passing away.

Jufāʾan describes being cast aside or carried off. It belongs to J F ʾ and should not be incorporated into zabad’s lexical definition.

By contrast, the verse says:

وَأَمَّا مَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ فَيَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ

wa-ammā mā yanfaʿu al-nāsa fa-yamkuthu fī al-arḍi

Al-Raʿd 13:17

“What benefits people remains in the earth.”

The contrast explains the outcome of the two images: foam and waste are removed, while water and useful material remain.

5. Zabad in the illustration of truth and falsehood

Why two examples?

Al-Raʿd 13:17 uses two phenomena to explain one truth.

First, a torrent carrying foam on its surface.

Second, metals heated so that waste separates and useful material remains.

The examples resemble each other without being identical.

In the water example, foam appears during the torrent’s movement and rests on its surface.

In the metal example, heating and processing separate impurities from the useful portion.

In both, what appears at the surface is not necessarily the most important or beneficial part.

The verse then explicitly states:

كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْحَقَّ وَالْبَاطِلَ

kadhālika yaḍribu Allāhu al-ḥaqqa wa-al-bāṭila

Al-Raʿd 13:17

Meaning: “Thus The God illustrates truth and falsehood.”

The association of foam with falsehood is therefore not merely an etymological invention: the verse itself establishes the comparison.

Impressive appearance and useful reality

Flood foam may be widespread, voluminous, and conspicuous. An observer attending only to the surface may notice it before the water beneath.

Yet water, rather than its passing foam, provides moisture for the land and supports plant growth; suitable water also quenches thirst.

In metalworking, impurities may appear on the surface, but the useful metal is what matters for making ornaments and tools.

These images distinguish outward display from actual worth.

Falsehood may spread for a time, attract great attention, and appear socially dominant. That appearance does not make it true.

Truth may be less conspicuous, but its worth rests in its reality, benefit, and actual effect.

This reading follows the explicit contrast of truth and falsehood and of foam and useful material. It does not make “deception” or “lying” the lexical meaning of zabad.

Usefulness and remaining

A notable feature is the contrast between mā yanfaʿu al-nāsa, “what benefits people,” and al-zabad.

The illustration connects remaining with benefiting others.

Water supports life, and usable metal serves in making tools and ornaments. Foam and waste do not play those same roles in the two processes.

This does not mean that everything long-lasting is true or that everything short-lived is false.

Duration has moral and epistemic significance here when read together with truth, falsehood, and usefulness.

The verse also does not say that human falsehood always disappears immediately. Foam itself may remain for a while before the current carries it aside.

The image therefore supplies neither a timetable nor a simple rule predicting the immediate end of every false practice.

Related verses

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 speaks of truth arriving and falsehood departing.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 also sets truth against falsehood and describes falsehood’s destruction.

These passages relate conceptually to Al-Raʿd 13:17, while each employs its own image.

Al-Raʿd 13:16 contrasts blindness with sight and darkness with light. Al-Raʿd 13:17 then presents truth and falsehood through water and foam.

Al-Raʿd 13:19 contrasts knowing that what the Lord revealed is true with blindness.

These connections place the foam illustration within a wider discussion of recognizing truth, discerning falsehood, and possessing insight.

6. Related words and concepts

Z B D (zabad) and R Gh W (raghw)

Both concern foam and froth-like phenomena.

Raghw is associated with bubbles or froth arising through agitation or boiling of liquids.

Zabad shares this usage but also describes metal dross; the related form zubd means butter.

The semantic fields overlap, but their words cannot be exchanged in every usage.

Z B D (zabad) and Kh B Th (khabath)

This family concerns impurity or something undesirable; khabath is also used concretely for dross from molten metals.

In the second example of Al-Raʿd 13:17, zabad can denote material separated from useful metal during melting.

It is related to khabath in that respect.

But the Z B D family does not invariably mean impurity or uncleanness. Milk butter, zubd, is a clear beneficial use of the same family.

The negative role of zabad in the illustration comes from the particular material, its fate, and the truth–falsehood comparison.

Z B D (zabad) and J F ʾ (jufāʾ)

Zabad names the material appearing on the surface.

Jufāʾ in Al-Raʿd 13:17 describes its being carried off or cast aside. It belongs to J F ʾ, not J F W, the family of jafāʾ meaning estrangement or harshness.

One names a thing; the other describes what happens to it.

Being discarded should not replace zabad’s basic meaning.

Z B D (zabad) and R B W (rabw)

Rābiyan in zabadan rābiyan comes from R B W and describes rising or elevation.

Their combination shows that the verse also characterizes the foam’s appearance.

Zabad names the material, while rābiyan describes its condition in this scene.

The distinction matters for accurate translation.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal natural equivalent of Qur’anic zabad is “foam.”

Arabic usages call for different equivalents:

  • Zabad: water foam, liquid froth, or froth-like material.
  • Zabad al-maʿādin: froth or dross from molten metals.
  • Zubd: butter extracted from milk.
  • Zubdah: a quantity of butter; figuratively, the pure or selected portion of something.
  • Azbada: foamed or produced froth.
  • Tazabbada: became frothy or brought forth foam.
  • Zabd: a gift or bestowal in the relevant lexical usage.

“Rising foam” suits zabadan rābiyan in Al-Raʿd 13:17.

“Similar froth” for zabadun mithluhu preserves the connection between the two images.

“The foam goes away, cast aside” naturally conveys fa-ammā al-zabadu fa-yadhhabu jufāʾan.

The explanation should clarify that the second instance concerns material separating from molten metals. These proposals do not replace the existing verse translation or authored word-box explanations.

Translating zabad itself as “falsehood” is unsuitable. Falsehood is the concept illustrated through the comparison, not the noun’s lexical meaning.

8. Conclusion

Z B D connects with material emerging or separating from something else: foam on water and liquids, dross from molten metals, and butter extracted from milk. Other usages concern gifts and bestowal.

Only the noun zabad occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Raʿd 13:17. All three instances refer to foam or froth-like material.

The crucial distinction is between lexical meaning and the illustration’s conclusion. Zabad does not lexically mean falsehood, but the verse uses foam and dross to illustrate falsehood in relation to truth.

In both images, conspicuous material is cast aside while what is useful remains. The contrast makes the difference between appearance and actual worth tangible.

The lexical definition should preserve the root’s range, while the figurative meaning must be understood from the verse’s words, construction, and explicit contrast of truth and falsehood.
