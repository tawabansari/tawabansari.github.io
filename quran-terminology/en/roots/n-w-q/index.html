The she-camel, its place in early Arabic, and Ṣāliḥ’s she-camel in the Qur’an

The Arabic root N W Q is best known through nāqah, the name for a she-camel. In early Arabian life, this animal mattered for transport, travel, milk, breeding, and wealth. The family also includes various plurals for she-camels, words for training camels, care and refinement in work, and some other recorded uses.

One lexical analysis proposes “height and elevation” as the root’s basic meaning and suggests that nāqah may relate to the animal’s stature. The connection is presented as a possibility, not a certain etymology. The established meaning of nāqah must remain distinct from a proposed explanation of its name’s origin.

N W Q occurs seven times in seven Qur’anic verses, always as the noun nāqah and always in connection with Thamūd and Ṣāliḥ. Neither its plurals nor derived verbs occur in the Qur’an.

This concentration matters: despite the breadth of she-camel vocabulary in Arabic, all Qur’anic uses of this root concern a particular she-camel appointed as a sign for Thamūd. Its literal meaning should be distinguished from the story and interpretations of the animal’s origin, qualities, and fate.

1. Important forms and derivatives

Nāqah: a she-camel

Nāqah is the Arabic name for a female camel. Arabic dictionaries state this explicitly:

النَّاقَةُ: الْأُنْثَى مِنَ الْإِبِلِ

al-nāqatu: al-unthā mina al-ibili

Meaning: “Nāqah is the female of the camels.”

Some lexical reports restrict the name to a particular stage of the animal’s maturity, but its general, familiar definition is a female camel.

Nāqah is an independent name for a she-camel, not simply the grammatical feminine form of jamal. Ibil is a collective name for camels; an individual female can be called nāqah.

One morphological analysis treats nāqah as originally following the pattern faʿalah, with its middle wāw undergoing a sound change. The plural nūq supports that analysis.

Plurals of nāqah

The word has several Arabic plurals, principally:

  • نُوق (nūq): a familiar plural for she-camels.
  • نِيَاق (niyāq): another plural recorded in Arabic and poetry.
  • أَنْوُق, أَوْنُق, and أَيْنُق (anwuq, awnuq, aynuq): plural forms related through sound changes and metathesis.
  • أَيَانِق (ayāniq): a plural of a plural, related to aynuq.
  • نَاقَات (nāqāt): the regular feminine plural.

Two morphological analyses have been proposed for aynuq. In one, wāw changes position and is then replaced by yāʾ. In the other, the middle radical is omitted and yāʾ replaces it elsewhere in the structure.

The details are specialized, but the relevant result is clear: despite its different appearance, aynuq is a plural of nāqah and should not be assigned an independent root.

None of these plurals occurs in the Qur’an.

Nawwaqa, munawwaq, and tanawwaqa

Dictionaries record other derivatives alongside the animal’s name.

نَوَّقَ الْبَعِيرَ concerns training and taming a camel. جَمَلٌ مُنَوَّقٌ describes a camel trained to obey and move as directed. نَاقَةٌ مُنَوَّقَةٌ is likewise explained in terms of being taught how to move.

These uses emphasize training the animal and adapting it to human purposes.

تَنَوَّقَ فِي الْأَمْرِ means taking care, exercising refinement, and striving to do something well. تَنَوَّقَ فِي مَلْبَسِهِ, for example, describes someone taking particular care over dress and appearance.

Al-nīqah also occurs for refinement, elegance, and care in doing something.

Al-nawwāq is recorded in some lexical uses for someone who skillfully arranges or puts affairs right.

The family is therefore not limited to the name of the female camel. Whether all these senses share one historical origin or merely belong together morphologically still requires examination.

2. The semantic core and the origin of the name nāqah

The analysis involving height and elevation states:

النُّونُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْقَافُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى سُمُوٍّ وَارْتِفَاعٍ

al-nūnu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-qāfu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā sumuwwin wa-irtifāʿin

Meaning: “The root denotes height and elevation.”

The explanation points to nīq, a word for a high or projecting part of a mountain.

It then proposes the following possibility for nāqah:

وَمُمْكِنٌ أَنْ يَكُونَ النَّاقَةُ مِنْ هَذَا الْقِيَاسِ، لِارْتِفَاعِ خَلْقِهَا

wa-mumkinun an yakūna al-nāqatu min hādhā al-qiyāsi li-irtifāʿi khalqihā

Meaning: “Nāqah may belong to this analogy because of the height of its body.”

Mumkin, “possible,” matters here. The connection between the she-camel’s name and its stature is a possibility, not an established linguistic fact.

Camels are tall, but male camels also have this feature. Height alone therefore does not explain why the particular name for the female should arise from this root.

The same analysis explains tanawwaqa by noting that female camels were prized possessions and suggesting that care and doing something well may have developed from the animal’s name.

This, too, is a possibility. The connection between a prized she-camel and careful dress or work is understandable, but the available evidence does not establish the historical development with certainty.

Two levels should therefore remain distinct.

First, the independent, established meaning of nāqah as a she-camel and the recorded uses of other derivatives.

Second, lexical attempts to connect height, the animal’s name, taming, and refinement.

The second level cannot replace evidence at the first.

Rare uses

Early dictionaries also record other meanings for some forms in this family.

Al-nawaq describes whiteness tinged with red. Al-nāqah is also a name for a group of stars whose configuration was likened to a she-camel.

Some dictionaries record al-nāq for a particular part or depression of the hand and al-nāqah for a type of skin eruption.

These rare uses are lexical data, but they should not be merged into one definition without sufficient evidence. Naming stars after a camel’s shape does not establish that nāqah in the Qur’an names a constellation.

3. Nāqah in early Arabic and literary witnesses

The she-camel in Arabian life and language

Camels mattered in ancient Arabia’s economic and social life, providing transport, travel, milk, and increased wealth. Females had particular importance for reproduction and milk.

This importance is reflected in the breadth of Arabic vocabulary for camels’ sex, age, bodily features, movement, and training.

That economic role is not itself part of the literal meaning of nāqah. The word still names a female camel, whether used for travel, giving milk, or appearing in another situation.

The saying istanwaqa al-jamal

A familiar witness in Arabic language and literature is:

اسْتَنْوَقَ الْجَمَلُ

istanwaqa al-jamalu

Meaning: “The male camel became a she-camel.”

In literary use, the expression applies to someone who mixes the description of one thing with features of another.

A well-known report set in the pre-Islamic period describes al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas attributing to a male camel a marking conventionally associated with females.

The line attributed to him is:

وَقَدْ أَتَنَاسَى الْهَمَّ عِنْدَ ادِّكَارِهِ
بِنَاجٍ عَلَيْهِ الصَّيْعَرِيَّةُ مُكْدَمِ

wa-qad atanāsā al-hamma ʿinda iddikārihi / bi-nājin ʿalayhi al-ṣayʿariyyatu mukdami

“At times, when sorrow comes to mind, I put it aside with a swift camel bearing the ṣayʿariyyah mark and bite marks.”

Its general sense is that the poet sets aside his grief by riding a swift, strong camel.

Al-ṣayʿariyyah is commonly explained as a mark placed on a she-camel’s neck. According to the report, Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd heard the line and said “istanwaqa al-jamal”: the poet had introduced a female camel’s feature into his description of a male.

Literary and lexical transmissions record this report. Details and the line’s attribution vary, and differing explanations also question whether the mark belonged exclusively to females. The account can therefore illustrate a tradition of linguistic criticism without making every detail a certain historical fact.

The saying matters here because the distinction between jamal and nāqah was familiar enough for mixing their features to become a subject of literary criticism.

Niyāq in poetry and rajaz

One lexical transmission attributes this rajaz passage to al-Qulākh ibn Ḥazn:

أَبْعَدَكُنَّ اللَّهُ مِنْ نِيَاقِ
إِنْ لَمْ تُنَجِّينَ مِنَ الْوِثَاقِ

abʿadakunna allāhu min niyāqi / in lam tunajjīna mina al-withāqi

Meaning: “May The God put you she-camels far away if you do not deliver [me] from bondage.”

Niyāq is a plural of nāqah here, referring to animals in a situation involving movement and escape from confinement.

The passage supports the plural niyāq, but its date is not clear enough to make it certainly pre-Islamic evidence.

Another rajaz passage includes نَاقَةَ الْعَجُوزِ and النِّيَاقَات. These witnesses show that different plurals are recorded, although they were not equally common.

The linguistic evidence thus shows nāqah as a familiar name for a female camel before its role in the Qur’anic narrative. The Qur’an did not create a new name for the animal; it employed the existing name in Ṣāliḥ’s story.

4. Every Qur’anic occurrence

Although the seven occurrences belong to one story, their constructions and roles differ.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:73: the she-camel as a sign

هَٰذِهِ نَاقَةُ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ آيَةً فَذَرُوهَا تَأْكُلْ فِي أَرْضِ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَمَسُّوهَا بِسُوءٍ

hādhihi nāqatu allāhi lakum āyatan fa-dharūhā taʾkul fī arḍi allāhi wa-lā tamassūhā bi-sūʾin

“This she-camel of The God is a sign for you. Let her eat on The God’s land, and do not harm her.”

Ṣāliḥ shows his people a particular animal. Hādhihi points to a specific being, and nāqatu Allāh identifies her.

Two commands follow: allow her to feed on the land and do not harm her.

These verbs, together with the established meaning of nāqah, support reading the passage as concerning an actual animal.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:77: the attack on the she-camel

فَعَقَرُوا النَّاقَةَ وَعَتَوْا عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهِمْ

fa-ʿaqarū al-nāqata wa-ʿataw ʿan amri rabbihim

“They hamstrung the she-camel and defied their Lord’s command.”

Al-nāqah is definite and refers back to the animal already known in the narrative.

ʿAqarū belongs to the separate root ʿ Q R. The action and its semantic range require that root’s study; the story’s context makes clear that attacking the she-camel violated the preceding command.

Hūd 11:64: renewed emphasis on protecting her

وَيَا قَوْمِ هَٰذِهِ نَاقَةُ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ آيَةً فَذَرُوهَا تَأْكُلْ فِي أَرْضِ اللَّهِ وَلَا تَمَسُّوهَا بِسُوءٍ

wa-yā qawmi hādhihi nāqatu allāhi lakum āyatan fa-dharūhā taʾkul fī arḍi allāhi wa-lā tamassūhā bi-sūʾin

“My people, this she-camel of The God is a sign for you. Let her eat on The God’s land, and do not harm her.”

The wording closely resembles Al-Aʿrāf 7:73 and retains the same three elements: presenting her as a sign, allowing her to feed on the land, and prohibiting harm.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:59: the she-camel as a clear sign

وَآتَيْنَا ثَمُودَ النَّاقَةَ مُبْصِرَةً فَظَلَمُوا بِهَا

wa-ātaynā thamūda al-nāqata mubṣiratan fa-ẓalamū bihā

“We gave Thamūd the she-camel as a clear sign, but they acted wrongfully concerning her.”

Mubṣirah belongs to B Ṣ R, not N W Q. A contextual interpretation explains it as the sign being clear and manifest.

The wording therefore should not be taken to mean that the she-camel’s main feature was extraordinary eyesight. The verse concerns sending signs, and mubṣirah fits the clarity of the evidence.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155: a share of water

قَالَ هَٰذِهِ نَاقَةٌ لَهَا شِرْبٌ وَلَكُمْ شِرْبُ يَوْمٍ مَعْلُومٍ

qāla hādhihi nāqatun lahā shirbun wa-lakum shirbu yawmin maʿlūmin

“He said: This is a she-camel. She has a share of water, and you have a share on an appointed day.”

Another aspect of the story appears here: the she-camel has an assigned water share, and the people have their share at a specified time.

Nāqatun is indefinite, unlike nāqatu Allāh or al-nāqah elsewhere. Context nevertheless identifies the same animal.

Al-Qamar 54:28 also says:

وَنَبِّئْهُمْ أَنَّ الْمَاءَ قِسْمَةٌ بَيْنَهُمْ كُلُّ شِرْبٍ مُحْتَضَرٌ

wa-nabbiʾhum anna al-māʾa qismatun baynahum kullu shirbin muḥtaḍarun

“Inform them that the water is divided between them; each drinking turn is attended [by its allotted party].”

Read together, the verses show that dividing the water and respecting the animal’s share were part of the people’s trial.

They do not, without further evidence, establish the precise volume of water, the spring’s capacity, the she-camel’s milk yield, or the number of people using the water.

Al-Qamar 54:27: the she-camel as a trial

إِنَّا مُرْسِلُو النَّاقَةِ فِتْنَةً لَهُمْ فَارْتَقِبْهُمْ وَاصْطَبِرْ

innā mursalū al-nāqati fitnatan lahum fa-irtaqibhum wa-iṣṭabir

“We are sending the she-camel as a trial for them; watch them and remain steadfast.”

Al-nāqah is the genitive complement of mursalū, while fitnatan expresses the trial involved in sending her.

This does not make “trial” part of the lexical meaning of nāqah. That role comes from the sentence, particularly fitnatan lahum.

Al-Shams 91:13: a warning about the she-camel and her water

فَقَالَ لَهُمْ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ نَاقَةَ اللَّهِ وَسُقْيَاهَا

fa-qāla lahum rasūlu allāhi nāqata allāhi wa-suqyāhā

“The messenger of The God said to them: The God’s she-camel and her watering—[respect them].”

Nāqata Allāh is accusative. A familiar grammatical analysis explains it as a warning construction, with a verb such as iḥdharū understood rather than stated.

A natural Persian translation therefore needs some explanatory wording, while keeping clear that the warning verb is not explicitly present in the Arabic.

Suqyāhā concerns watering the she-camel or her share of water, connecting with Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155 and Al-Qamar 54:28.

All seven occurrences thus retain the familiar meaning “female camel.” The animal’s narrative role varies, not the noun’s lexical meaning.

5. Nāqat Allāh: the meaning of attribution to The God

The she-camel is called نَاقَةُ اللَّهِ or نَاقَةَ اللَّهِ in three occurrences: Al-Aʿrāf 7:73, Hūd 11:64, and Al-Shams 91:13.

Grammatically, this is a construct phrase: nāqah is the first noun, and Allāh its genitive complement.

Attributing something to The God does not always have one restricted meaning in Qur’anic Arabic. Here, context gives the she-camel a special relationship with divine command and a divine sign.

The attribution alone does not make the animal a different species or give it an immaterial nature.

The same she-camel feeds on the land in Al-Aʿrāf 7:73 and has a water share in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155. The narrative presents an actual animal in a special position.

Nor should nāqat Allāh mean that The God owns only this animal, in contrast to all others. In the Qur’anic worldview, land and creatures belong to Him; the construct phrase particularly presents this she-camel as a divine sign in Ṣāliḥ’s story.

A literal reading should equally preserve her special status as a sign. The Qur’an itself calls her an āyah and her sending a fitnah.

Both points should remain: nāqah lexically means a female camel, and in the story she is a particular sign for Thamūd.

6. Does the Qur’an explain her origin and extraordinary qualities?

Some interpretive reports say that she emerged from rock or a mountain, was unusually large, or produced enough milk for everyone’s needs.

Such accounts appear in early interpretive transmissions. They must be distinguished from the Qur’an’s explicit text.

The Qur’an clearly states that she was a sign for Thamūd, fed on the land, had a water share, was not to be harmed, and was eventually attacked by the people.

The seven occurrences of N W Q do not state the details of her origin, exact birthplace, size, milk yield, or lifespan.

Those details cannot therefore be made part of the word nāqah in a root study.

The absence of an explanation of her origin also does not disprove every extraordinary aspect of the story. The Qur’an emphasizes her being a sign but does not fully explain the mechanism of her appearance and qualities in these passages.

Linguistic study must keep the Qur’an’s explicit statements distinct from later narrative explanations.

7. Her place in Ṣāliḥ’s story

Access to water and land

The story does more than identify her as a sign: it also assigns her a particular right to use resources.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:73 and Hūd 11:64 command people to leave her free to feed on The God’s land. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155 and Al-Qamar 54:28 concern water allocation.

The people’s treatment of her is therefore practical and observable. They were required to respect a command about a creature living among them.

This follows from the verses together, not from nāqah alone.

The verses may also be examined in terms of respecting limits, access rights, and refraining from harm. Those concepts should not become the etymological meaning of N W Q.

One person’s act and collective responsibility

Al-Qamar 54:29 says:

فَنَادَوْا صَاحِبَهُمْ فَتَعَاطَىٰ فَعَقَرَ

fa-nādaw ṣāḥibahum fa-taʿāṭā fa-ʿaqara

“They called their companion; he undertook the act and hamstrung [her].”

Fa-ʿaqara is singular here.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:77, however, says:

فَعَقَرُوا النَّاقَةَ

fa-ʿaqarū al-nāqata

“They hamstrung the she-camel.”

And Al-Shams 91:14 says:

فَكَذَّبُوهُ فَعَقَرُوهَا

fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-ʿaqarūhā

“They denied him and hamstrung her.”

The narrative emphasizes the direct actor in one passage and the people’s collective behavior in others.

These constructions alone do not establish unreported details about the number involved, each person’s role, or how the act was organized.

The reliable point is that the attack is more than an isolated incident: it concerns the people’s defiance of a previously communicated command.

This belongs to narrative analysis and ʿ Q R, but helps explain the she-camel’s role across the verses.

8. Distinctions from related roots

N W Q and J M L: female and male camels

In familiar Arabic usage, jamal denotes a male camel, whereas nāqah names a female.

The distinction is clear in istanwaqa al-jamal.

Al-jamal also appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40:

حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ

ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi

“Until the camel passes through the eye of the needle.”

This belongs to J M L, not N W Q. Its vocalization and readings have separate discussions outside the present root study.

Study of J M L

N W Q and ʾ B L: an individual name and a collective name

Ibil refers to camels collectively or generically and has no singular formed from the same word.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:17 says:

أَفَلَا يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَى الْإِبِلِ كَيْفَ خُلِقَتْ

a-fa-lā yanẓurūna ilā al-ibili kayfa khuliqat

“Do they not look at the camels, how they were created?”

The subject is camels as a kind, rather than one particular female.

Nāqah in Ṣāliḥ’s story identifies a particular female individual of that kind.

Study of ʾ B L

N W Q and B ʿ R: the camel in general usage

Baʿīr is another familiar camel name and may refer to a male or female according to context.

In Yūsuf 12:65 and Yūsuf 12:72, baʿīr occurs in connection with loads and transported measures.

It belongs to the separate root B ʿ R and should not merge with N W Q merely because both refer to camels.

Study of B ʿ R

N W Q and ʾ N Q: tanawwuq and taʾannuq

This comparison has particular etymological interest.

Tanawwaqa is associated with care and refinement in work. Taʾannaqa has a nearby sense but belongs to ʾ N Q.

Some lexical explanations distinguish the verbs: taʾannuq is more closely related to liking or finding something pleasing, while tanawwuq concerns doing something well and carefully.

Their proximity in meaning does not make their roots identical.

Nor is the historical relationship between every refinement-related use and nāqah clear enough to establish as a certain rule.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Female camel” is the principal, precise equivalent for all seven Qur’anic occurrences of nāqah.

Natural Persian may also retain nāqah, particularly in the familiar recurring phrase “The God’s she-camel.” At first mention, however, readers should understand that it means a female camel.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:73 and Hūd 11:64, nāqatu Allāh may be rendered “The God’s she-camel.” Persian may either retain nāqah or spell out “female camel”; retaining the established term is shorter and more natural.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155, hādhihi nāqatun means “This is a female camel,” or more naturally, “This is a she-camel.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:59, al-nāqah remains “the she-camel,” with mubṣirah explained separately.

In Al-Shams 91:13, the warning construction may require an understood verb in Persian. That grammatical need should not add a new meaning to the noun itself.

“Divine sign,” “miracle,” “means of testing,” or “holder of water rights” cannot independently replace nāqah. These are qualities and roles understood from sentences and context, not the noun’s meaning.

10. Conclusion

In its familiar Arabic use, N W Q includes the word family connected with female camels. Nāqah names the animal, with important plurals nūq, niyāq, and aynuq.

Other recorded branches include training and taming camels, refinement and care in work, and some rare uses.

One lexical analysis treats height and elevation as fundamental and tentatively connects the animal’s name with its stature. That connection remains uncertain within the analysis itself. “Height” should therefore not uncritically replace the established meaning “female camel.”

The root occurs seven times in the Qur’an, only as nāqah. Every occurrence concerns Ṣāliḥ’s she-camel, with no indication that the lexical meaning changes to a human people, a purely symbolic concept, or an immaterial being.

The Qur’an also presents her as a divine sign and a trial for Thamūd. Commands about feeding, water allocation, and avoiding harm clarify her place in the people’s practical conduct.

A brief, usable definition for Forqan is therefore:

N W Q: an Arabic word family best known through nāqah, “female camel.” Other recorded uses concern training and taming camels and care and refinement in work. A possible connection with height and elevation has been proposed, but the historical origin of the animal’s name is uncertain. All seven Qur’anic occurrences denote the female camel appointed as a sign for Thamūd in Ṣāliḥ’s story.
