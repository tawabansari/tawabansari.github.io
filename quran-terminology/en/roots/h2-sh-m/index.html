The Qur’anic root ه ش م (hashama)

Breaking and crumbling: from dry, brittle branches to Qur’anic images of collapse

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral Arabic root ه ش م concerns breaking, crushing, and disrupting the cohesion of something’s parts. The verb is used particularly of dry, brittle, or hollow objects that may break into several pieces.

هَشَمَ الشَّيْءَ means that someone broke or crushed something. اِنْهَشَمَ and تَهَشَّمَ describe its breaking and falling apart. هَشِيم describes something dry, brittle, or broken into fragments, especially plant remains and dry branches.

Classical Arabic records the root for breaking bones, crumbling bread, snapping dry branches, and damaging brittle objects.

A familiar example is هَشَمَ الثَّرِيدَ: he crumbled bread to prepare tharīd, a dish in which broken bread was mixed with broth or a similar preparation.

A verse transmitted in Arabic poetic and historical sources reads:

عَمْرُو الَّذِي هَشَمَ الثَّرِيدَ لِقَوْمِهِ

ʿAmru alladhī hashama al-tharīda li-qawmihi

وَرِجَالُ مَكَّةَ مُسْنِتُونَ عِجَافُ

Wa-rijālu Makkata musnitūna ʿijāfu.

“ʿAmr was the one who crumbled bread for his people’s tharīd, while the men of Mecca were lean and afflicted by famine.”

The verse concerns ʿAmr ibn ʿAbd Manāf, known as Hāshim. Historical accounts explain his epithet through his crumbling bread and providing food for people.

The verse’s attribution varies. Some sources assign it to Maṭrūd ibn Kaʿb al-Khuzāʿī, others to ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā. Parts of its wording also vary. The date of this particular verse therefore cannot confidently be placed before the revelation of the Qur’an.

With that qualification, the verse and associated accounts attest to the recognition of hashm as crumbling something such as bread in the Arabic linguistic tradition. This witness does not establish when the root first arose.

In another usage, هَشَمَ الْعَظْمَ means breaking a bone. هَاشِمَة is recorded for a wound involving a fractured bone.

Lexicons record further usages for forms in this family, including milking a she-camel and seeking someone’s gentleness or goodwill. These do not occur in the Qur’an, and their precise historical relationship to breaking is unclear. All branches should not be forced into an invented common image simply to unify the family.

In Qur’anic usage, the branch concerning fragmented and brittle vegetation is clear.

Its semantic core can be expressed as follows:

Breaking and loss of cohesion among something’s parts, especially when the material is dry or brittle and breaks into small pieces.

Being dry and being broken are different. Something can be dry while remaining whole. Conversely, an object may break under impact without having dried out beforehand.

This distinction matters for understanding هَشِيم in the Qur’an. The word conveys more than lack of moisture: it evokes vegetation that has lost its freshness and firmness and is liable to crumble and scatter.

2. Forms and the Qur’anic semantic core

Arabic recognizes forms including هَشَمَ, هَشَّمَ, اِنْهَشَمَ, تَهَشَّمَ, مَهْشُوم, هَشِيم, هَشِيمَة, and هَاشِمَة.

In the Qur’an, however, the root appears only in the noun هَشِيم.

Its two occurrences, in two verses, are:

  • هَشِيمًا in Al-Kahf 18:45.
  • كَهَشِيمِ in Al-Qamar 54:31.

هَشِيم follows the faʿīl pattern. Here its sense approaches that of a passive participle: something broken, fragmented, or in a brittle and worn condition.

The faʿīl pattern alone does not prove that every instance of hashīm has been reduced to minute pieces by a violent blow. The precise sense follows from usage and the condition of what is described.

In Arabic, hashīm is used especially of dry, brittle vegetation. Plant remains may be dry and worn where they stand, or they may have broken and scattered.

Both Qur’anic occurrences concern such dry, crumbling plant remains, but the verses employ different images and serve different purposes.

3. Al-Kahf 18:45: green vegetation becoming hashīm

وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَاءٍ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقْتَدِرًا

Wa-iḍrib lahum mathala al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-māʾin anzalnāhu mina al-samāʾi fa-ikhtalaṭa bihi nabātu al-arḍi fa-aṣbaḥa hashīman tadhrūhu al-riyāḥu wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqtadirā.

“Set forth for them the likeness of the life of this world: like water We sent down from the sky, with which the vegetation of the earth mingled; then it became dry, broken plant fragments scattered by the winds. The God has power over all things.”

Al-Kahf 18:45

The verse uses the course of vegetation to illustrate the impermanence of worldly life.

Water descends from the sky, plants benefit from it, and the earth produces growth. Vegetation that was once green and alive then loses its freshness and becomes hashīm.

فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا expresses this change of condition.

In this construction, أَصْبَحَ means “became” or “entered a state.” It does not require the entire sequence of growth, drying, and crumbling to occur within a single day and night.

هَشِيمًا is the predicate of أَصْبَحَ, describing the vegetation’s new condition.

The verse continues:

تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ: the winds scatter it.

The verb تَذْرُوهُ belongs to another root, ذ ر و. It describes the scattering of the plant remains. Two aspects must therefore be distinguished: the vegetation becomes hashīm, and the winds scatter it. The sentence describes scattering vegetation that has become hashīm, without establishing a required time interval between the two.

These meanings are related, but distinct.

Hashīm conveys the vegetation’s dryness, brittleness, and fragmentation; tadhrūhu describes its movement and dispersal.

The image is tangible. Vegetation that once had roots, grew, and formed part of a green landscape has become light, brittle remains that wind can carry away.

Hashīm does not itself mean the absolute annihilation of matter. The plant remains still exist, although they have lost their former shape, cohesion, and condition.

Connection with worldly life

The comparison follows the account of the two gardens in Al-Kahf 18:32, Al-Kahf 18:33, Al-Kahf 18:34, Al-Kahf 18:35, Al-Kahf 18:36, Al-Kahf 18:37, Al-Kahf 18:38, Al-Kahf 18:39, Al-Kahf 18:40, Al-Kahf 18:41, Al-Kahf 18:42, Al-Kahf 18:43, and Al-Kahf 18:44. That account concerns possession of wealth and gardens followed by the loss of those resources.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, dry, broken vegetation illustrates the impermanence of worldly conditions. What is green and flourishing today may no longer retain that condition tomorrow.

The following verse, Al-Kahf 18:46, identifies wealth and children as the adornment of worldly life and contrasts them with enduring good deeds.

The image does not deny all value to life, vegetation, wealth, or worldly blessings. It concerns their impermanence and the error of relying on something that does not last forever.

This conclusion follows from the surrounding verses. Hashīm does not lexically mean wealth, worthlessness, or worldly deception.

For the verb تَذْرُوهُ and the wind’s role in this image, see Forqan’s study of the root ذ ر و.

4. Al-Qamar 54:31: the fence builder’s hashīm and the image of collapse

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً فَكَانُوا كَهَشِيمِ الْمُحْتَظِرِ

Innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-kānū ka-hashīmi al-muḥtaẓir.

“We sent a single mighty cry against them, and they became like the dry, broken fragments gathered by a fence builder.”

Al-Qamar 54:31

The verse concerns the fate of Thamūd.

The preceding verses discuss their rejection of the warnings, the she-camel, and the people’s conduct. A single cry and their resulting fate follow.

كَهَشِيمِ combines the comparative kāf with the noun hashīm: “like hashīm.”

Hashīm does not directly name the people here. The Qur’an compares them to something dry, brittle, and fragmented.

The difference from Al-Kahf 18:45 is significant.

In Al-Kahf, vegetation actually becomes hashīm, and that transformation serves as a comparison for worldly life. In Al-Qamar, people are compared to hashīm to portray their devastating collapse and destruction.

The comparison does not require their bodies to have physically fragmented in precisely the manner of dry branches. A simile can highlight an aspect of its image without transferring every material characteristic to its subject.

What is muḥtaẓir?

الْمُحْتَظِرِ belongs to ح ظ ر, a different root from ه ش م.

حَظَرَ concerns preventing and restraining. حِظَار and حَظِيرَة denote a fence or an enclosed place.

In pastoral life, wood, reeds, branches, and thorny bushes could form an enclosure for sheep or other livestock.

In the commonly used reading, مُحْتَظِر is a form VIII active participle, describing someone who builds a fence or encloses a place.

Branches and pieces of wood used in such a fence could gradually become dry, worn, and brittle. Dry branches might also snap into small fragments during collection and construction.

Such dry, broken remains could be called hashīm in Arabic.

Early explanations of كَهَشِيمِ الْمُحْتَظِرِ mention both the broken branches gathered by a fence builder and the remains of a fence that dries out, deteriorates, and breaks apart.

A reading with fatḥah on ẓāʾ, الْمُحْتَظَرِ, is also reported and may refer to the enclosure itself or what has been prepared for fencing. In either case, the main image concerns dry, broken branches and plant remains.

Hashīm must therefore be distinguished from muḥtaẓir itself.

Hashīm describes the dry, brittle fragments; muḥtaẓir specifies their connection to the fence builder or enclosure.

For an independent account of this word, see Forqan’s study of the root ح ظ ر.

Why is this image used for Thamūd?

In Al-Kahf 18:45, hashīm results from vegetation changing and losing its greenness.

In Al-Qamar 54:31, it follows صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً: a single devastating event brings the people to a condition likened to dry, broken fragments.

The comparison emphasizes breaking, collapse, and loss of the previous condition.

Hashīm alone does not mean divine punishment. It also describes vegetation in Al-Kahf. Thamūd’s punishment and destruction are established by the statement and context of Al-Qamar.

The root itself does not specify when or why something breaks. A plant may gradually dry and crumble; another object may break under a single blow.

In Al-Qamar, صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً names the event, while كَهَشِيمِ الْمُحْتَظِرِ portrays its result.

5. Differences from related roots

ه ش م and ح ط م: two related images of fragmentation

ح ط م also concerns breaking, crushing, and disrupting cohesion.

The Qur’an uses حُطَام for dry, broken plant remains. In Al-Zumar 39:21, for example, vegetation becomes ḥuṭām after turning yellow.

This closely resembles hashīm in Al-Kahf 18:45. In both, vegetation loses its former freshness and cohesion and becomes dry, broken remains.

The two roots nevertheless have different ranges of use.

In Al-Naml 27:18, ح ط م concerns the danger of ants being crushed beneath a passing army. In Al-Humazah 104:4 and Al-Humazah 104:5, الْحُطَمَة conveys a crushing quality.

By comparison, the only Qur’anic derivative of ه ش م, hashīm, is connected in both occurrences with dry, brittle vegetation.

This is a tendency in Qur’anic usage, not a rule reserving one root exclusively for vegetation and the other for hard objects.

Nor can ḥaṭm invariably be classified as more violent breaking and hashm as weaker breaking. Lexicons record both for breaking dry and hard objects.

For a wider account, see Forqan’s study of the root ح ط م.

ه ش م and ي ب س: breaking and drying

ي ب س concerns being dry or becoming dry.

يَابِس describes something without moisture or something that has lost its moisture.

Drying and fragmentation are different conditions.

A branch may be dry yet unbroken. Conversely, pressure may break a branch even if it is not completely dry.

In Qur’anic usage, hashīm describes vegetation that is dry, brittle, and liable to crumble, or has become broken fragments.

Translating it merely as “dry” therefore omits part of the image.

Conversely, not every use of yābis should be expanded to “broken and fragmented.” In Ṭā Hā 20:77, يَبَسًا describes a dry path through the sea, without suggesting fragmentation.

This distinction is discussed in Forqan’s study of the root ي ب س.

ه ش م and ه ش ش: two independent roots

ه ش م and ه ش ش resemble one another in sound, but are distinct roots.

ه ش ش occurs in the Qur’an in Moses’ words:

وَأَهُشُّ بِهَا عَلَىٰ غَنَمِي; Ṭā Hā 20:18.

The phrase concerns Moses using his staff to knock leaves from trees to feed his livestock.

أَهُشُّ belongs to ه ش ش and is distinct from هَشِيم, from ه ش م.

Branches and vegetation may feature in both usages, but a shared subject does not make the roots or actions identical.

Moses describes knocking leaves down. Hashīm describes the condition of dry, brittle vegetation or its broken remains.

For further detail, see Forqan’s study of the root ه ش ش.

ه ش م and ك س ر: general breaking and fragmentation

كَسَرَ broadly means breaking something.

هَشَمَ also denotes breaking, with dry, brittle, and certain hard or hollow objects prominent in its recorded usages.

The distinction is not absolute: lexicons record hashm for breaking a variety of things.

In the Qur’an, hashīm describes vegetation’s resulting dry and brittle condition. “Broken vegetation” conveys part of the meaning, but “dry, brittle plant fragments” makes the image clearer.

ه ش م and ذ ر و: fragmentation and scattering

Both roots occur in one sentence in Al-Kahf 18:45.

Hashīm denotes dry, broken plant remains; tadhrūhu describes the winds scattering them.

Something may be broken yet remain in one place. Wind can also scatter soil, seeds, or other light objects without first breaking them.

Hashm concerns breaking and fragmentation; dharw concerns scattering and dispersal.

The distinction is essential when translating Al-Kahf 18:45. Rendering hashīm itself as “vegetation scattered by wind” would absorb the independent contribution of تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ordinary Arabic uses of هَشَمَ, “break,” “crush,” and “break into pieces” are appropriate equivalents.

تَهَشَّمَ may be rendered “crumbled,” “broke,” or “fell apart.” “Shattered into fragments” may also suit the context.

For هَشِيم, the root’s only Qur’anic derivative, suitable equivalents include:

“Dry, broken plant fragments,” “dry, brittle plant remains,” and “dry twig fragments.”

For Al-Kahf 18:45, the proposed rendering is:

“Then it became dry, broken plant fragments scattered by the winds.”

This describes the vegetation’s condition and preserves the winds’ independent role.

For Al-Qamar 54:31, the proposed rendering is:

“And they became like the dry, broken fragments gathered by a fence builder.”

“Like” is essential to preserve the comparative kāf. The explanation of the fence builder should make the image intelligible without importing uncertain historical or technical details into the translation.

“Ash” is not an accurate general equivalent of hashīm: burning is not inherent in the word. Something may dry and crumble without catching fire.

“Rotten” is also not its core meaning, although decay may accompany deterioration in some cases. Rotting can involve decomposition and material change; the Qur’anic image emphasizes dryness, brittleness, and fragmentation.

“Destroyed” states a general outcome without conveying the word’s tangible image.

For Forqan’s basic rendering, retaining “dry, broken fragments” is therefore preferable.

Conclusion

ه ش م concerns breaking and crushing in Arabic, particularly dry, brittle, or hollow objects. Familiar classical usages include breaking bones, crumbling bread, and snapping dry branches.

The Qur’an uses the root only in hashīm; both occurrences connect with dry, brittle plant remains.

In Al-Kahf 18:45, vegetation becomes hashīm after growing, and winds scatter it. This illustrates the impermanence of worldly life.

In Al-Qamar 54:31, Thamūd are likened to the fence builder’s hashīm after a single cry. The comparison portrays their collapse and the loss of their former condition.

Hashīm overlaps with ḥuṭām in the field of dry, broken plant remains, while remaining distinct from yābis and dharw. Dryness, fragmentation, and scattering are three different concepts that may occur successively in a natural process.

Final definition for Forqan:

ه ش م: breaking and crumbling something, especially dry, brittle, or hollow material; in the Qur’anic derivative هَشِيم, vegetation or plant remains that have become dry and brittle and readily crumble or have broken into fragments.

This definition preserves the word’s material image while allowing its figurative use in each verse to be understood in its own context, without restricting the root to absolute annihilation, punishment, or scattering.
