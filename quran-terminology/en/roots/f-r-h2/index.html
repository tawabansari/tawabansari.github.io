Root ف ر ه — Skill, agility, vitality, and exultation

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَارِهِينَ
(fārihīn)
Ḥafṣ reading: skilled and proficient

فَرِهِينَ
(farihīn)
Variant reading: exultation in one interpretation

فَارِه / فَارِهَة
(fārih / fārihah)
Outstanding quality and vigour

The three-consonant Arabic root ف ر ه has two established semantic branches. The first concerns skill, proficiency, outstanding ability, and vitality. In this use, فَارِه describes someone skilled in a task, or an animal possessing agility, strength, and good movement.

The second concerns joy mixed with exultation, conceit, and pride. فَرِه can describe someone excessively elated by their advantages and abilities, and proud of them.

Both branches matter in the Qur’an, since the word is transmitted in two recognised readings: فَارِهِينَ and فَرِهِينَ.

The root occurs only once, in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149, concerning Thamūd carving homes from mountains. One interpretation foregrounds their skill; another, their conceited exultation in their abilities and resources. This division between the readings is not exclusive or definitive: exultation is also reported for the form with an alif.

Skill and exultation are different concepts. People can be highly skilled without becoming arrogant or unjust; they can also boast of their abilities and belittle others. Skill is not blameworthy in itself. Its ethical significance depends on how abilities are used and how people respond to truth and to others.

1. The root in early Arabic

Skill and proficiency

In established Arabic, فَرُهَ is recorded for becoming skilled, proficient, and capable in a task.

رَجُلٌ فَارِهٌ can describe a man skilled in his work.

For example, it may describe someone proficient in carving stone, making tools, or practising a demanding craft.

Here farāhah concerns outstanding ability and good workmanship.

Such ability may result from experience, practice, knowledge, or talent. None of these factors alone is the lexical meaning of فَارِه; the word expresses skill or outstanding capacity.

Animal vitality and movement

فَارِه is also used of animals.

Early dictionaries particularly apply it to mules, donkeys, and certain riding animals, describing their agility, strength, liveliness, and quality of movement.

دَابَّةٌ فَارِهَةٌ may describe a strong, agile animal ready to move.

Farāhah is therefore not confined to intellectual or professional skill. Applied to animals, it also describes outstanding quality and practical capacity.

Human skill in building and animal agility are not identical. Their connection lies in outstanding ability or quality in the activity expected of each.

Pre-Islamic poetic evidence: ʿAdī ibn Zayd

The pre-Islamic Arab poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī describes a horse:

فَصَافَ يُفَرِّي جُلَّهُ عَنْ سَرَاتِهِ
يَبُذُّ الْجِيَادَ فَارِهًا مُتَتَايِعًا

Fa-ṣāfa yufarrī jullahu ʿan sarātihi / yabudhdhu l-jiyāda fārihan mutatāyiʿā

Translation: “It passed the summer, its fatness splitting the covering over its back; vigorous and surging onward, it outstripped swift horses.”

فَارِهًا describes the horse here.

The poet describes the animal’s movement and agility. The word stands beside مُتَتَايِعًا, concerning rapid, energetic forward movement.

فَارِه here does not signify boasting or moral arrogance; it describes the animal’s movement.

Some lexical reports attribute to al-Aṣmaʿī disapproval of using فَارِه for a horse, considering it more appropriate to mules and other animals.

This illustrates disagreement among lexicographers over the word’s range of application.

The poetic evidence nevertheless shows its use for animal vigour and agility in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The verse survives in the collection attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd and in early lexical citations. Despite its historical value, it reaches us through later transmission, without a contemporary document from its composition.

Evidence from al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

أَعْطَى لِفَارِهَةٍ حُلْوٍ تَوَابِعُهَا
مِنَ الْمَوَاهِبِ لَا تُعْطَى عَلَى نَكَدِ

Aʿṭā li-fārihatin ḥulwin tawābiʿuhā / mina l-mawāhibi lā tuʿṭā ʿalā nakadi

Read with the preceding passage, the sense is: “I see none as generous as he in giving a fārihah and the pleasing gifts accompanying it—gifts bestowed without grudging.” One explanation identifies fārihah as an enslaved singing woman; another, as a fine she-camel. It should therefore not be confidently treated as the recipient of the gift.

Lexical explanations identify فَارِهَة here as an enslaved singing woman; some poetic commentaries identify a fine or young she-camel. Beauty and favourable quality are also recorded in other uses.

The evidence concerns favourable quality, but its precise referent depends on the differing explanations.

It does not establish that فَارِه in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 means that the builders themselves were beautiful.

Each use is interpreted according to what it describes.

The referent in al-Nābighah’s verse is disputed; ʿAdī’s verse describes a horse; the Qur’an describes people carving homes from mountains.

These differences illustrate the root’s broad semantic field without explaining every use through one fixed equivalent.

Exultation and conceit

Another Arabic branch relates فَرِهَ to excessive joy, exultation, and pride.

رَجُلٌ فَرِهٌ can describe someone elated with their advantages or ability.

Lexical explanations also use words such as أَشِر and بَطِر for this condition.

أَشِر concerns excessive elation and exuberance.

بَطِر can concern a wrongful response to blessings, with exultation mixed with ingratitude and self-importance.

These meanings are close, but should not be treated indiscriminately as synonyms in every use.

Someone may be pleased with an ability and take pride in it. When that pleasure leads to belittling others, ingratitude, or rebellion, it enters the field of blameworthy exultation.

Early sources sometimes explain فَرِهَ through its proximity to فَرِحَ. Some lexicographers even suggest that hāʾ replaced ḥāʾ in one use.

This alone does not establish a definite historical relationship. ف ر ه and ف ر ح remain distinct families in morphological and Qur’anic analysis.

The evidence for the exultation branch is not equally clear in date. It is established in recognised Qur’anic readings and early lexical reports, but assigning every semantic detail to pre-Islamic Arabic requires independent evidence.

2. Linguistic forms and the central meaning

Important recorded forms include:

  • فَرُهَ: became skilled, or possessed outstanding quality and ability.
  • فَرَاهَة and فَرَاهِيَة: skill, agility, vitality, or outstanding quality.
  • فَارِه: skilled, proficient, agile, or notably capable.
  • فَارِهَة: the feminine form; in some uses, attractive or distinguished by a favourable quality.
  • فَرِهَ: in another branch, became exultant and proud.
  • فَرَه: exultation and excessive elation; elsewhere, liveliness and agility.
  • فَرِه: exultant, conceited, or in some uses lively and capable.
  • أَفْرَهَ: of a she-camel, produced vigorous, fine offspring.
  • اِسْتَفْرَهَ: found or considered something fine and excellent.

Other Arabic forms are recorded concerning beauty, agility, and animal ability.

Not all occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic text contains only one derivative: فَارِهِينَ in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149.

Another recognised reading gives فَرِهِينَ.

Semantically, the two branches should be retained together:

First branch: skill, proficiency, vitality, and outstanding ability.

Second branch: exultation, conceit, and joy mixed with pride.

Sharing root letters does not mean that skill always produces arrogance, or that arrogance is an essential component of skill.

3. Qur’anic use: Thamūd’s skill in carving homes

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149

وَتَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا فَارِهِينَ

Wa-tanḥitūna mina l-jibāli buyūtan fārihīn

Translation: “And you skilfully carve homes from the mountains.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The verse forms part of Ṣāliḥ’s address to Thamūd.

Thamūd possessed considerable building ability. They carved stone and mountains to create living spaces.

تَنْحِتُونَ describes the carving action.

مِنَ الْجِبَالِ identifies its material and location.

بُيُوتًا identifies the outcome.

فَارِهِينَ describes the builders’ condition and qualities while performing the work.

In the widely used Ḥafṣ reading, skill and proficiency fit فَارِهِينَ well.

Does فَارِهِينَ describe the homes?

Grammatically, فَارِهِينَ is an accusative circumstantial qualifier referring to the plural subject in تَنْحِتُونَ.

It therefore describes those carving the homes.

The verse does not call the homes themselves fārih; it characterises the people while they carve them.

The distinction matters.

Beautiful, luxurious homes may result from the builders’ skill, but فَارِهِينَ does not directly qualify the homes.

Renderings such as “you built luxurious homes” may convey part of the general picture of prosperity, but do not precisely preserve the word’s syntactic role.

“You skilfully carve homes” or “you carve homes from mountains while proficient in the task” better reflects the construction.

Is carving mountain homes blameworthy?

Building homes or carving stone is not blameworthy in itself.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 reminds Thamūd that they have settled in the land, build on its plains, and carve mountains into homes.

The same verse calls them to remember God’s blessings and avoid corruption.

Building ability and access to the earth’s resources can therefore be genuine blessings.

The problem arises when people use those blessings for arrogance, injustice, and corruption, or become so attached to their power and resources that they forget their responsibilities.

The condemnation of Thamūd does not establish that architectural skill, solid homes, or the use of natural resources is inherently wrong.

The verse mentions their real ability within a call to God-consciousness and improved conduct.

4. The readings فَارِهِينَ and فَرِهِينَ

A central feature of this root is the variant reading at its sole Qur’anic occurrence.

The reading فَارِهِينَ

In Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, and several other recognised readings, the word has an alif after fāʾ:

فَارِهِينَ

This is the sound masculine plural of فَارِه.

فَارِه can be an active participial or adjectival form derived from فَرُهَ.

In this interpretation, skill and proficiency are prominent.

The sentence consequently means:

“You carve homes from mountains, being skilled and proficient in this work.”

This fits the subject: carving homes within mountains requires familiarity with stone, tools, measurement, and practical ability.

Technical architectural details cannot, however, be extracted from the word itself.

فَارِهِينَ does not mean possession of specific iron tools, a modern named engineering discipline, or a particular building method.

It expresses ability and proficiency; their technical implementation is another matter.

The reading فَرِهِينَ

Other recognised readings, including those of Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, and Abū ʿAmr, omit the alif:

فَرِهِينَ

This is the plural of فَرِه.

Many lexical and exegetical explanations understand this reading as exultant, conceited people proud of their abilities and prosperity.

On that interpretation, the sentence may mean:

“You carve homes from mountains, being exultant and conceited.”

This also relates to Thamūd’s narrative context.

In al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:146, Ṣāliḥ asks whether they think they will be left indefinitely amid their present blessings and security.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:147 and al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148 recall their gardens, springs, crops, and date palms.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 then mentions carving homes from mountains.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:150 commands God-consciousness; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:151 and al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:152 forbid following transgressors who corrupt the land rather than setting things right.

Exultation and conceit are therefore intelligible within the context.

Are the readings completely separate?

Some lexicographers distinguish the forms:

They explain فَارِهِينَ as skilled and proficient,

and فَرِهِينَ as exultant and proud.

Others consider them close or shared in meaning. The presence of alif therefore does not alone establish skill, nor does its absence alone establish arrogance.

Arabic farāhah concerns vitality and ability, while farah (فَرَه)can also approach liveliness and elation.

This proximity can allow overlap between the forms.

Overlap does not mean complete identity, however.

Proficiency is an ability; exultation and conceit concern conduct and attitude.

Thamūd may have been both skilled builders and proud of their ability and prosperity.

That possibility should not lead to combining both meanings without distinction into a long primary translation.

A primary rendering should be selected, while the other recognised reading is presented in the lexical and contextual explanation.

5. Skill, security, prosperity, and Thamūd’s corruption

Three matters should be distinguished: building ability, a sense of security, and ethical conduct.

Building ability

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 describes carving homes in mountains.

This indicates practical skill and material resources.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74 likewise mentions both buildings on plains and homes carved in mountains.

Thamūd are therefore not portrayed as lacking resources or ability.

A sense of security

Al-Ḥijr 15:82

وَكَانُوا يَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا آمِنِينَ

Wa-kānū yanḥitūna mina l-jibāli buyūtan āminīn

Translation: “They used to carve homes from the mountains, feeling secure.”

The same carving activity appears here, but the final description differs.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 has:

فَارِهِينَ

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 has:

آمِنِينَ

آمِنِينَ concerns security and being safe.

فَارِهِينَ, in the skill interpretation of the common reading, concerns proficiency.

The concepts are related, but not synonyms.

Someone may be skilled at building a home that nevertheless has safety problems.

Someone else may live in a secure home without being a skilled builder.

Thamūd’s story presents these features in two separate verses.

فَارِهِينَ should therefore not simply be rendered as آمِنِينَ.

Ethical conduct

In al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:151 and al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:152, Ṣāliḥ forbids following transgressors who corrupt the land without setting things right.

The ethical issue is explicit here.

This corruption should not automatically become the lexical definition of فَارِهِينَ.

Even when فَرِهِينَ is understood as exultation and pride, corruption and excess remain distinct words and behaviours mentioned subsequently.

A society can possess technical ability, natural resources, solid homes, and comfortable living conditions while falling into social and ethical injustice and corruption.

Building ability alone proves neither righteousness nor, conversely, moral corruption.

Society must be assessed by its actual conduct.

6. Morphology and syntax

ف ر ه is a sound three-consonant root.

In فَارِهِينَ, the singular فَارِه follows فَاعِل, with the plural فَارِهُونَ or فَارِهِينَ.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 uses the accusative فَارِهِينَ.

Its accusative case follows from its circumstantial role, or ḥāl.

A ḥāl describes the subject’s condition or quality during an action.

Here the subject is the plural pronoun in تَنْحِتُونَ.

فَارِهِينَ therefore describes the people carving homes from mountains.

In the reading فَرِهِينَ, the singular is فَرِه, whose plural likewise occupies the circumstantial position.

The variant reading does not change the basic syntactic role: both forms describe the builders.

Their morphological pattern and semantic range can nevertheless differ.

The other words’ roles

تَنْحِتُونَ belongs to ن ح ت and expresses carving and shaping material.

مِنَ الْجِبَالِ uses مِنْ to relate the action to the mountains.

بُيُوتًا gives the outcome: homes created by carving stone.

فَارِهِينَ describes the builders’ state.

Skill is thus understood from فَارِهِينَ, carving from تَنْحِتُونَ, and the outcome’s being homes from بُيُوتًا.

These contributions should not be merged.

مِنَ الْجِبَالِ alone does not specify the number of homes, building sizes, or amount of stone removed.

The verse describes carving mountain homes, not every detail of their construction.

7. Comparison with neighbouring roots

ف ر ه (فَارِه) and ف ر ح (فَرِحَ)

Some uses concerning joy and liveliness bring these roots close together.

Qur’anic ف ر ح concerns joy and gladness.

Such joy may be praised or condemned: compare joy in God’s bounty in Yūnus 10:58 with Korah’s exultation in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76.

One branch of ف ر ه approaches exultation and conceit, but it also has a branch of skill and proficiency.

Similar letters do not make فَارِهِينَ a derivative of ف ر ح.

Nor is every happy person necessarily فَرِه in the sense of conceited and exultant.

ف ر ه (فَارِه) and م ه ر (مَهَرَ)

مَهَرَ concerns skill and proficiency in Arabic.

فَارِه can likewise describe a skilled, capable person.

They are close within the field of skill.

ف ر ه also has uses concerning vitality, animal agility, and human exultation.

“Skilled” is therefore appropriate for فَارِه in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149, but does not cover the root’s entire field.

ف ر ه (فَارِه) and ن ح ت (نَحَتَ)

The roots share a sentence in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149.

Naḥt is carving and shaping material.

Farāhah, in the skill interpretation, characterises the person performing it.

Someone may carve stone without much skill.

A proficient person, by contrast, can produce the intended shape with greater precision and ability.

تَنْحِتُونَ and فَارِهِينَ therefore do not perform the same semantic task.

ف ر ه (فَرِه) and ب ط ر (بَطِرَ)

Baṭar concerns exultation, ingratitude, and responding wrongly to blessings.

فَرِه, understood as exultation, can approach this condition.

Not every advantage or outstanding ability is baṭar.

Someone may use skill to build a safe home, serve people, or improve their surroundings.

Another may use the same ability to belittle others or display power.

The latter conduct may approach exultation and baṭar.

Context must nevertheless establish that this condition is actually present.

ف ر ه (فَرِه) and ف خ ر (فَخَرَ)

Fakhr concerns boasting of advantages and asserting superiority.

The exultation branch of فَرِه can accompany conceit and pride.

The meanings are not identical.

Someone may inwardly exult in an ability without speaking of it.

Someone may also continually flaunt advantages and behave boastfully.

The exultation interpretation in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 can concern Thamūd’s pride, but cannot itself specify every form their boasting took.

ف ر ه (فَارِه) and ص ن ع (صَنَعَ)

Qur’anic ṣanʿ concerns doing and making something.

The skill interpretation of فَارِه describes the quality of the maker’s ability.

Making can be highly skilled or poorly executed.

Ṣanʿ and farāhah thus describe different aspects: performing work and possessing outstanding ability in it.

Thamūd’s building skill accompanies ethical responsibility, but technical ability alone does not establish moral value.

8. Difficulties in understanding and translation

The first difficulty is equating skill with arrogance.

The skill interpretation of فَارِهِينَ has clear lexical support.

Rendering فَارِهِينَ as “arrogant” also has exegetical support. It should not, however, be inferred merely from Thamūd’s condemnation, nor should the skill sense be considered impossible.

Conversely, attending only to skill and ignoring فَرِهِينَ would omit a recognised reading.

Both meanings should therefore be recognised in their appropriate place.

The second difficulty is condemning the construction of large, solid homes.

The Qur’an mentions Thamūd’s carving ability and identifies their misconduct in other verses.

Building homes, using stone, and advancing technical skills do not themselves constitute corruption.

The third difficulty is making فَارِهِينَ a description of the homes.

Grammatically it describes the agents circumstantially.

“Magnificent homes” may be a possible result of people’s skill or prosperity, but is not a direct equivalent of this word.

The fourth difficulty is equating فَارِهِينَ with آمِنِينَ.

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 describes security; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 describes skill or, in another interpretation, exultation.

The verses do not simply repeat one feature in different words; each highlights a particular aspect of Thamūd’s condition.

The fifth difficulty is reducing all exultation to ordinary happiness.

Qur’anic joy can arise from God’s bounty and mercy and be praiseworthy.

Exultation mixed with ingratitude and self-importance is another matter.

That distinction matters when فَرِهِينَ is understood as exultant.

Finally, a word’s morphology cannot produce a historical account of Thamūd’s entire life.

The Qur’an mentions their ability, prosperity, conduct, and fate; the root itself does not reveal every detail of their architecture, building tools, or social organisation.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

In the skill branch, فَارِه can be “skilled,” “proficient,” or “capable,” and for an animal, “agile and vigorous.”

In the exultation branch, فَرِه can be “exultant,” “conceited,” or “proud of one’s advantages.”

For al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149, translation should consider the reading, the recorded semantic range, and the context together.

For فَارِهِينَ, a suitable proposal is:

“And you skilfully carve homes from the mountains.”

This is concise, natural, and close to the construction.

Another possibility is:

“And you carve homes from the mountains, being proficient in the work.”

This makes the circumstantial role more explicit, but is longer.

For فَرِهِينَ, one may say:

“And you carve homes from the mountains, being exultant and conceited.”

This conveys the ethical sense prominent in that interpretation.

For Forqan’s base text following Ḥafṣ, “skilfully” is a clear proposal fitting the carving action; it is not the only possible meaning of فَارِهِينَ.

The lexical explanation and reflection should also present exultation and conceit, identifying the variant reading and its relationship to Thamūd’s context.

A brief lexical explanation can retain these distinctions:

Word form: فَٰرِهِينَ

Root: ف ر ه

Brief root meaning: skill, proficiency, and vitality; in another branch, exultation and conceit.

Meaning here: skilled and proficient at carving mountain homes; exultation and conceit are also recorded interpretations and are not confined to the reading without alif.

This distinction allows readers to understand the chosen text while recognising the valid variation in readings.

10. Conclusion

Arabic ف ر ه has two established semantic branches.

The first concerns skill, proficiency, vitality, and outstanding capacity. Pre-Islamic poetic evidence illustrates its application to vigorous animals or persons with favourable qualities.

The second concerns exultation, conceit, and joy mixed with pride, known in early lexical reports and the recognised reading فَرِهِينَ.

The root occurs only in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149.

The skill interpretation of the common فَارِهِينَ reading highlights Thamūd’s proficiency in carving mountain homes.

Exultation and pride are also defensible meanings for فَرِهِينَ.

The narrative shows a people with natural resources, security, and technical ability, yet responsible for responding to the call to God-consciousness and reformed conduct.

The family’s definition must retain both branches: skill and outstanding capacity on one side, exultation and conceit on the other.

The branches are semantically related, but neither should be made an obligatory meaning of the other.

People can be highly skilled at building, learning, and improving their surroundings while remaining humble, just, and responsible. They can also use the same ability for self-importance, corruption, or belittling others.

Skill therefore proves neither righteousness nor corruption. Moral value becomes clear through the use of ability, respect for others’ rights, and conduct towards truth.

In the proposed translation, فَارِهِينَ describes the builders’ skill. Exultation is explained with attention to the reading, context, and differing interpretations, without treating either as the only possible meaning.
