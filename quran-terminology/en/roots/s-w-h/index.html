Courtyard, open space, the grounds around dwellings, and an event reaching people's own quarters

The Arabic root S W Ḥ is associated with open, spacious ground beside houses, between dwellings, or around a settlement. Its principal word is sāḥah: a courtyard, square, open space, or area belonging to people's homes and living quarters.

In broader usage, sāḥah can also denote a district or the area occupied by a group. Thus, something reaching or descending upon a people's sāḥah can mean its arrival at the place where they themselves live and are present.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as bi-sāḥatihim in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177. The verse concerns people who demand that divine punishment be hastened, but are warned that when it reaches their quarters they will experience its unhappy outcome.

Here sāḥah denotes neither travel nor a battlefield specifically designated for combat. Its basic meaning is open ground and the area where people live: a place whose being reached by an event makes that event's presence among the people themselves apparent.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Sāḥah: the open space of a house or settlement

Arabic dictionaries explain sāḥatu al-dār as a house's open space, courtyard, or grounds beside its built structures.

This space may lie in front of the entrance, between buildings, or within a residential compound where no roof or enclosed structure covers it.

Sāḥah is also recorded for open ground among the dwellings of a neighborhood or tribe.

In Arab social life, homes did not necessarily consist of enclosed buildings isolated from their surroundings. Tents, houses, and other dwellings could stand together, with the open ground between them forming part of everyday life.

Such ground might serve for passing through, gathering, keeping animals, or performing daily tasks.

These functions, however, are not fixed components of the word's meaning. Sāḥah primarily identifies a kind of place; what people do there is determined by the situation and context.

Dictionaries also explain al-sāḥah by al-nāḥiyah: a region or part of an area.

This usage shows that sāḥah is not always limited to a small domestic courtyard. It can encompass the open space of a settlement or the area inhabited by a community.

Lexical forms of sāḥah

Several plural or collective forms of sāḥah are recorded in Arabic:

Sāḥāt: courtyards, open spaces, and squares.

Sūḥ: open grounds and the courtyards of dwellings.

Sāḥ: another form recorded as a plural or collective noun.

The diminutive of sāḥah, suwayḥah, is also attested in Arabic.

This diminutive is significant morphologically because it supports analysis under a root containing wāw. Many lexicographers therefore discuss sāḥah under S W Ḥ; this morphological evidence does not by itself establish a complete history of the word.

Some morphological analyses reconstruct the underlying form as sawaḥah, with a vowel on the wāw, and explain its change to alif by the combination of a vowel-bearing wāw and a preceding fatḥah.

This analysis agrees with the plural sūḥ and diminutive suwayḥah.

Lexical sources also contain another opinion relating sāḥah to S Y Ḥ. Its derivational history must therefore be examined separately from its clear, established meaning.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says in a poem:

وَقَالَ مَاشِيهِمْ سِيَّانِ سَيْرُكُمُ
وَأَنْ تُقِيمُوا بِهِ وَاغْبَرَّتِ السُّوحُ

wa-qāla māshīhim siyyāni sayrukumu / wa-an tuqīmū bihi wa-ighbarrati al-sūḥu

“Their livestock owner said: traveling on and remaining there are alike for you, while the open grounds have grown dusty.”

In this line, al-sūḥ is the plural of sāḥah.

The poet describes a region whose open grounds have become dusty through drought and lack of rain.

In such conditions, moving on and staying make little difference to people who need pasture and feed for livestock.

The evidence matters because sūḥ refers here to actual places and open ground, rather than to traveling or an abstract concept.

Some grammatical works transmit a different form of the line:

فَكَانَ سِيَّانِ أَنْ لَا يَسْرَحُوا نَعَمًا
أَوْ يَسْرَحُوهُ بِهَا وَاغْبَرَّتِ السُّوحُ

fa-kāna siyyāni an lā yasraḥū naʿaman / aw yasraḥūhu bihā wa-ighbarrati al-sūḥu

“It was alike whether they did not send livestock out to graze or sent it out there, while the open grounds had grown dusty.”

In the commentary on this poetic evidence, this version is identified as a combination of two separate lines. The separately transmitted line is therefore preferable for establishing lexical usage.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived into the Islamic period. The precise date of this poem is not sufficiently established to identify the line confidently as pre-Quranic.

It is evidence of early usage of sūḥ, but does not establish a pre-Islamic date for this particular line.

The evidence considered here does not establish an independent example of this word whose attribution and pre-Quranic date are both secure.

Historical claims should therefore remain within what can be established: in early Arabic and Quranic usage, sāḥah denotes open ground associated with homes and habitation. The precise antiquity of every plural form and the details of the naming process are not independently established.

2. Semantic core and word structure

In its established usage, the semantic core of S W Ḥ is open, spacious ground associated with a house, residential compound, or inhabited area.

Two features should be retained in this definition.

First, the openness and relative spaciousness of the place.

Second, its connection with dwellings, a settlement, or an area where people are present.

Sāḥah need not mean an enormously extensive space. A small courtyard may also be called a sāḥah.

Nor is it necessarily ground entirely devoid of objects, trees, or human activity. The point is the openness of the space in relation to enclosed, built portions.

From a domestic courtyard to a people's quarters

When used of a house, sāḥatu al-dār can mean its courtyard or adjacent open space.

When used of a people or group, its scope can broaden to their living area or the ground they occupy.

For example, nazala bi-sāḥati al-qawm need not mean merely entering a building's courtyard; it can mean a person or group arriving where those people live.

This extension is readily understandable in ordinary language.

A house's open space forms part of the area occupied by its inhabitants. Applied to a collection of houses or a community, its name can bring their surrounding living area into view.

This extension does not, however, make sāḥah a designation for all the territory of a country or a defined political jurisdiction.

The extent of the sāḥah in any instance is determined by context.

Distinguishing a place from what happens there

A sāḥah may host gatherings, commerce, passage, games, conflict, or combat.

The word alone, however, expresses none of these activities.

If people are said to gather in a house's courtyard, gathering is conveyed by the relevant verb.

If an enemy reaches a people's grounds, arrival and the possible danger are understood from the verb and the enemy's circumstances.

Sāḥah thus names a place; what happens there belongs to another part of the sentence and its context.

This distinction is particularly important in the root's Quranic usage.

3. Bi-sāḥatihim: the sole Quranic occurrence

S W Ḥ occurs only once in the Quran:

بِسَاحَتِهِمْ — Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177

The word consists of three components.

Bi- is a preposition.

Sāḥah is a feminine singular noun meaning courtyard, open space, or inhabited grounds.

Hum is an attached third-person masculine plural pronoun referring to the group under discussion.

Bi-sāḥatihim therefore means “in their courtyard,” “within their living quarters,” or “upon the open ground that belongs to them.”

Here the preposition bāʾ accompanies nazala and indicates the place of descent or arrival.

Quranic and other lexical forms

This is the only form from S W Ḥ found in the Quran.

Sāḥah, sāḥāt, sūḥ, sāḥ, and suwayḥah are known in Arabic, but do not occur in the Quran in these unattached forms.

Bi-sāḥatihim is a single written Quranic word, with a preposition and pronoun attached to its noun.

The bāʾ and hum must therefore not be counted as independent derivatives of the root.

Forms such as fa-sīḥū in Al-Tawbah 9:2, al-sāʾiḥūn in Al-Tawbah 9:112, and sāʾiḥāt in Al-Taḥrīm 66:5 belong to S Y Ḥ. Siyāḥah is also known in that family, but does not itself occur in the Quran. These forms should not be added to S W Ḥ without justification.

4. Fa-idhā nazala bi-sāḥatihim in the context of Al-Ṣāffāt

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:176 states:

أَفَبِعَذَابِنَا يَسْتَعْجِلُونَ

a-fa-bi-ʿadhābinā yastaʿjilūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:176

“Do they then seek to hasten Our punishment?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177 then states:

فَإِذَا نَزَلَ بِسَاحَتِهِمْ فَسَاءَ صَبَاحُ الْمُنْذَرِينَ

fa-idhā nazala bi-sāḥatihim fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177

“Then, when it descends upon their courtyard, how evil will be the morning of those who were warned!”

These two verses are directly connected.

The first concerns people who demand that divine punishment be hastened.

The next speaks of the time when that punishment reaches their quarters.

What descends is identified by the preceding verse: the punishment whose arrival they were demanding.

Nazala thus concerns the arrival of punishment, not the physical movement of the courtyard or a building descending.

Why “their quarters”?

The verse could simply have spoken of punishment reaching them. Bi-sāḥatihim, however, creates a more distinct image.

Punishment reaches the place where the people themselves live and are present.

What was previously a subject of warning and speech is now present in their own surroundings.

This contrast marks the difference between hearing news of an event and encountering its occurrence.

To clarify the image, one may imagine a dangerous force approaching a settlement's homes. While it remains distant, people might dismiss the danger; once it reaches their grounds and entrances, it is no longer merely distant news.

This illustration helps explain “descending upon their quarters,” but the verse itself does not necessarily describe an actual army, military siege, or troops entering a particular city.

The subject of the verb is understood from the context of punishment; the image of descent emphasizes that punishment reaching the addressees themselves.

Does sāḥah mean battlefield?

In contemporary Persian, meydān can mean both a public open space and a place of battle.

In Arabic too, a sāḥah may be somewhere that fighting or confrontation occurs.

Lexically, however, sāḥah is not a name reserved for a battlefield.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177, the warning and punishment context can evoke danger reaching people's homes, but sāḥah itself does not incorporate the meanings of warfare, killing, or battle.

The Persian rendering meydānashān is therefore suitable if understood as their open grounds or quarters, rather than necessarily a military battlefield.

“Their living grounds” and “their residential quarters” also clarify the sentence's meaning.

The relation between descent and sāḥah

The Arabic verb nazala can express descending, arriving, and settling in a place.

Saying that someone descended upon the grounds of a house or settlement can mean that the person arrived and stayed in that area.

The Quranic passage uses this construction for punishment.

This usage should not be turned into a physical theory about punishment moving downward from above.

Nazala bi-sāḥatihim here expresses punishment arriving and taking place within their quarters.

The manner in which that punishment occurs is not supplied by sāḥah itself.

Fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn

The second part of Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177 says:

فَسَاءَ صَبَاحُ الْمُنْذَرِينَ

fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177

“How evil will be the morning of those who were warned!”

Sāʾa belongs to S W ʾ and expresses badness or unpleasantness.

Ṣabāḥ belongs to Ṣ B Ḥ and concerns morning.

Al-mundharīn belongs to N DH R and means those who have been warned.

These three words therefore do not share the root S W Ḥ, although they contribute to the same image.

In Arabic, an evil morning can express a harsh, distressing event occurring at the beginning of the day.

In the setting of earlier confrontations, danger suddenly arriving in the morning was a familiar image.

The phrase alone does not establish that the punishment must occur at a particular hour after sunrise.

Morning can highlight the moment of confronting the unhappy outcome in this image.

The main point is the arrival of what had been warned about and the result becoming apparent to the addressees.

Warning and the event's occurrence

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:174, the messenger is told to turn away from them for a time.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:175 also states:

وَأَبْصِرْهُمْ فَسَوْفَ يُبْصِرُونَ

wa-abṣirhum fa-sawfa yubṣirūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:175

“And watch them; soon they will see.”

After Al-Ṣāffāt 37:176 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177, the commands to turn away and watch recur in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:178 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:179.

This repetition places Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177 within a sequence of warning, respite, and the result becoming apparent.

The addressees seek to hasten punishment, whereas the Quran speaks of the time when the threatened outcome reaches them themselves.

In this construction, bi-sāḥatihim identifies the place where the event becomes manifest.

The essential point is that punishment reaching people's quarters follows the warning's fulfillment; sāḥah itself expresses neither the reason for punishment nor its precise timing.

The cause and the people's responsibility are understood from the wider passages and their conduct.

5. Distinctions from related roots and words

S W Ḥ (sāḥah) and S Y Ḥ (siyāḥah): open grounds and traveling through the land

These roots resemble one another in form but are treated separately in Quranic morphological indexing.

S W Ḥ appears in Quranic usage through sāḥah, meaning open ground and a people's living quarters.

S Y Ḥ concerns movement and traveling through the land.

Al-Tawbah 9:2 states:

فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ

fa-sīḥū fī al-arḍi arbaʿata ashhur

Al-Tawbah 9:2

“Travel through the land for four months.”

Fa-sīḥū is a verb of movement through the land.

Al-sāʾiḥūn in Al-Tawbah 9:112 and sāʾiḥāt in Al-Taḥrīm 66:5 also belong to this family. Their meaning must be examined in their respective contexts and in light of the lexical uses of siyāḥah.

These occurrences should not be added to the Quranic derivatives of S W Ḥ.

Some lexical works discuss sāḥah alongside siyāḥah and suggest a relationship between open space and the possibility of movement.

Another morphological analysis, however, uses sūḥ and suwayḥah to assign sāḥah to a root containing wāw.

The semantic proximity between open space and movement within it is understandable, but this resemblance alone does not establish a common historical origin.

Sāḥah and fināʾ: courtyard and open ground beside a house

In its spatial use, fināʾ denotes open ground or a yard adjoining a house.

Sāḥah can likewise refer to a house's courtyard and open space.

The two are very close in some uses.

Sāḥah can nevertheless also denote open ground among a people's dwellings or part of a district.

It should therefore not always be restricted to a single building's courtyard.

Fanāʾ, meaning perishing or coming to an end, is a different sense and must not be confused with spatial fināʾ.

S W Ḥ (sāḥah) and ʿ R Ṣ (ʿarṣah): open ground and yards

ʿArṣah is recorded in Arabic for open ground, including the part of a house without buildings.

Sāḥah likewise denotes the open space of a house or settlement.

Both words occur in descriptions of a house's open ground. One cannot draw a fixed boundary between them by claiming that only sāḥah relates to dwellings whereas ʿarṣah is always separate from habitation.

Their uses overlap, and the precise extent of the place in each expression should be determined from context.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177, attaching the people's pronoun to sāḥah clarifies its connection with the place they inhabit or occupy.

S W Ḥ (sāḥah) and D W R (dār): open grounds and dwelling

Dār denotes a house, dwelling, and sometimes the wider residential area or territory of a group.

Sāḥah can be the open portion of that house or residential area.

Something reaching a people's sāḥah can thus be understood as reaching where they live; this extension does not make sāḥah and dār interchangeable in every sentence.

Dār can emphasize the dwelling as a whole, and sāḥah the open ground or area associated with it.

S W Ḥ (sāḥah) and N Z L (nuzūl): location and event

Nazala and sāḥah appear together in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177.

Nazala is the verb of arriving or descending.

Sāḥah is the place or area upon which descent is expressed.

Sāḥah therefore does not itself mean descent.

Someone may enter a courtyard, stop there, or pass through it. The action is identified by the verb and context.

The same distinction holds in the Quranic verse: nazala expresses the punishment's arrival, and bi-sāḥatihim its destination.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root's sole Quranic form is bi-sāḥatihim in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177.

The original phrase:

فَإِذَا نَزَلَ بِسَاحَتِهِمْ

fa-idhā nazala bi-sāḥatihim

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177

A suitable rendering:

“Then, when it descends upon their courtyard”

This conveys the spatial image preserved in Forqan's current Persian translation, which uses meydānashān, “their open grounds.”

“Then, when it reaches their living quarters” also clarifies the contextual meaning, but is more explanatory than the Arabic form.

“Then, when it descends into the courtyards of their houses” conveys the concrete image, but may restrict a people's sāḥah too closely to particular domestic courtyards.

“Then, when it reaches their territory” is broader, but may suggest a large geographical or political jurisdiction, whereas sāḥah more directly evokes the people's open grounds and immediate surroundings.

“Upon their courtyard” or “upon their grounds” is therefore suitable for a direct rendering; the broader sense can be explained in the word notes.

For the complete phrase:

فَإِذَا نَزَلَ بِسَاحَتِهِمْ فَسَاءَ صَبَاحُ الْمُنْذَرِينَ

fa-idhā nazala bi-sāḥatihim fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177

The corresponding rendering is:

“Then, when it descends upon their courtyard, how evil will be the morning of those who were warned!”

This preserves both the image of descent and the connection between their quarters and the warning's outcome.

Important forms of this family

Sāḥah: courtyard, a house's open ground, square, or open space in a settlement.

Sāḥāt: courtyards and squares.

Sūḥ: plural of sāḥah; open grounds and domestic yards.

Sāḥ: a recorded plural or collective form of sāḥah.

Suwayḥah: diminutive of sāḥah.

Sāḥatu al-dār: a house's open ground or courtyard.

Sāḥatu al-qawm: a people's open grounds and living quarters.

Barīʾu al-sāḥah: figuratively, a person considered free of the accusation or impurity in question.

In the last expression, sāḥah extends from a tangible place to a person's standing or reputation. This figurative use should not be imposed upon the direct meaning of bi-sāḥatihim in the Quran.

Conclusion

In established Arabic usage, S W Ḥ is associated with sāḥah: a house's open ground, a square, or a people's living quarters.

This may be a domestic courtyard, open space between buildings, or the area occupied by a community.

Lexical sources record forms such as sūḥ, sāḥ, and sāḥāt; the diminutive suwayḥah also supports analysis under a root containing wāw.

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī's poetry provides evidence for sūḥ as the open grounds of a region. Because he lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods and the line's precise date is unknown, it should not be presented as securely pre-Quranic evidence.

The root occurs in the Quran only once, as bi-sāḥatihim in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177.

The context shows people demanding that divine punishment be hastened, but being warned that when it reaches their own living quarters they will experience its unhappy outcome.

Sāḥah names the location; nazala expresses the event reaching it; and fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn expresses the unhappy outcome for those warned.

The final definition is:

In its established Quranic and lexical usage, S W Ḥ denotes open, spacious ground associated with a house, residential compound, or a people's living area. Sāḥah can be a courtyard, square, or open yard; in bi-sāḥatihim, it expresses the people's own quarters and surroundings.

This definition keeps the place's meaning distinct from the event occurring there.

Sāḥah does not inherently mean a place of warfare, punishment, or judgment; in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:177, however, it becomes the place reached by the threatened punishment.

The verse's image draws its force from this distinction: what was previously a matter of warning now reaches the very homes of those who had not taken its occurrence seriously.

The direct meaning of sāḥah is thus open ground associated with habitation; punishment arriving and the warning's outcome becoming apparent are understood from the whole sentence and its context.
