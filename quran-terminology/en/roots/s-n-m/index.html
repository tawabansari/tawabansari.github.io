Height, prominence, rising above, and Tasnīm in the heavenly drink

The Arabic root S N M concerns height, prominence, and rising above one's surroundings. In its concrete usage, sanām names the hump on a camel's back. The same semantic field includes raised ground, raising something, flames rising, and occupying a higher position.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as Tasnīm in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27. The verse describes the special drink of the righteous, whose admixture is from Tasnīm. The following verse connects Tasnīm with a spring from which those brought near drink.

The lexical meaning of tasnīm relates to elevation. In the Quranic context, the word can name or describe a spring whose drink is mixed with the heavenly raḥīq.

The essential distinction is that height belongs to the root's meaning; the spring and drink are its Quranic referents. The spring's exact location, elevation, flow, and qualities cannot be extracted from the root alone.

1. The root in early Arabic and evidence attributed to pre-Quranic poetry

Sanām: the hump on a camel's back

In early Arabic, sanām names the hump on a camel's back. Its shape and position place it above the surrounding parts of the animal's back.

Its plural is asnimah.

Sanima al-baʿīru means that the camel's hump grew large; nāqatun sanimatun describes a female camel with a large, prominent hump.

Height and prominence are related here. A hump is not simply something at the body's highest point; it is a particular projection rising above the surrounding surface.

Arabic also uses this concrete image to describe other phenomena.

For example, raised sand, a hill's elevated part, or something projecting like a hump can be described with words from this family.

The semantic field therefore extends beyond the camel's body, although its hump is among the clearest concrete examples.

A poetic example: the dunes of Asnumah

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

ضَحَّوْا قَلِيلًا قَفَا كُثْبَانِ أَسْنُمَةٍ
وَمِنْهُمُ بِالْقَسُومِيَّاتِ مُعْتَرَكُ

ḍaḥḥaw qalīlan qafā kuthbāni asnumatin / wa-minhumu bi-al-qasūmiyyāti muʿtaraku

“They briefly pastured their camels in the morning behind the dunes of Asnumah; at al-Qasūmiyyāt, too, was a place where they halted and made their camels kneel.”

In this line, Asnumah occurs alongside kuthbān, meaning sand dunes.

The commentary identifies Asnumah here as a place-name. It must be distinguished from asnimah, the plural of sanām; variations in vocalization are also recorded.

The line thus illustrates a geographical name in early poetry. It does not by itself establish that the poet used a common noun meaning sandy elevations.

A geographical name's precise derivation cannot be established without examining its naming history.

The dunes supply a concrete geographical setting, but they do not alone prove the place-name's derivation from the camel's hump.

A poetic example of Tasnīm

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas reads:

كَأَنَّ بِرِيقَتِهَا لِلْمِزَاجِ
مِنْ ثَلْجِ تَسْنِيمَ شِيبَتْ عُقَارَا

ka-anna bi-rīqatihā li-al-mizāji / min thalji tasnīma shībat ʿuqārā

“As though her saliva were wine mixed with snow from Tasnīm.”

The explanation identifies Tasnīm as a mountain whose snow contributes coolness and pleasantness to the poet's image of the drink.

The example matters historically because the form Tasnīm appears in poetry attributed to a pre-Quranic poet.

However, the line survives in later lexical and explanatory works, and its date and original wording require independent examination. This report alone cannot establish that a particular geographical name was widely current throughout pre-Islamic Arabic.

If the attribution and reading are accepted, it suggests that Tasnīm could already have named an elevated place before the Quran.

This suits the meaning of height, but does not establish that Quranic Tasnīm is the geographical location mentioned by the poet.

2. Semantic core and principal derivatives

The forms of S N M in Arabic lexicography principally concern height and prominence.

Sanām: a camel's hump; more broadly, the high or prominent part of something.

Asnimah: the plural of sanām; in descriptions of terrain, sandy elevations or comparable shapes.

Sanima: for an animal's hump to grow large and prominent.

Sanīm: high, prominent, or figuratively elevated in status.

Sannama: raised something or made it project above its surroundings.

Musannam: something made raised or prominent.

Tasannama: mounted something, climbed it, or occupied its top.

Tasnīm: the verbal noun of form II, meaning raising or making prominent; in the Quran, the name or description of a spring associated with the heavenly drink.

These forms differ in structure and use.

Sanām names a projection; sannama expresses raising or making prominent; tasannama can express climbing or occupying a position above something.

Tasnīm in structures and tangible objects

Sannama al-qabra means raising the surface of a grave, especially its middle, so that it is not entirely flat.

Sannama al-ināʾa also describes filling a vessel until its contents rise above its rim in a mound.

These usages share the idea of rising above the surrounding surface.

The precise shape depends on the object: contents rise above a vessel's rim, earth forms a mound over a grave, and part of a camel's back projects upward.

Sharing a root does not give all these objects an identical geometric shape.

Sanām as high rank

Figuratively, sanām can denote the highest position or outstanding part of something.

Sanām al-majd, for example, can signify the summit of glory or an outstanding position of greatness.

Here sanām is no longer a bodily part: physical height extends to rank and standing.

This extension is intelligible in Arabic, but does not mean every use of S N M inherently expresses a positive value.

A hill's prominence or an object's elevation is not itself a moral virtue. Value arises where the context concerns rank, honor, or worth.

3. The Quranic form Tasnīm

The root S N M occurs only once in the Quran:

Tasnīmin — Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

Its structure corresponds to the verbal noun of form II.

Sannama is attested for raising or making prominent, and its verbal noun is tasnīm.

In the verse, the word follows the preposition min and is genitive:

وَمِزَاجُهُ مِنْ تَسْنِيمٍ

wa-mizājuhu min tasnīm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

“And its admixture is from Tasnīm.”

Here Tasnīm refers to what is mixed with the drink under discussion.

The grammatical form alone does not decide whether Tasnīm is exclusively a proper name or a description derived from elevation.

Two linguistic possibilities deserve consideration.

First, Tasnīm may name a particular heavenly spring or drink. The root might then illuminate the background of the name, without requiring the name to be translated literally in each occurrence.

Second, the word may retain descriptive force, referring to water coming from a height or associated with an elevated position.

Both possibilities have been discussed in lexical and grammatical interpretation.

The following verse, however, supplies an important clue: “ʿaynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūn.” This directly connects Tasnīm with a spring to drink from.

Its contextual meaning must therefore be understood through that spring, rather than merely through the general act of raising.

4. “Wa-mizājuhu min tasnīm” in the context of Al-Muṭaffifīn

The neighboring verses are essential for understanding this usage.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25

يُسْقَوْنَ مِنْ رَحِيقٍ مَخْتُومٍ

yusqawna min raḥīqin makhtūm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25

“They are given a sealed, pure drink.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

خِتَامُهُ مِسْكٌ وَفِي ذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَتَنَافَسِ الْمُتَنَافِسُونَ

khitāmuhu miskun wa-fī dhālika fa-lyatanāfasi al-mutanāfisūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

“Its seal is musk; for this let those who compete compete.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

وَمِزَاجُهُ مِنْ تَسْنِيمٍ

wa-mizājuhu min tasnīm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

“And its admixture is from Tasnīm.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28

عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا الْمُقَرَّبُونَ

ʿaynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28

“A spring from which those brought near drink.”

These verses describe the drink of the righteous through several features.

First comes sealed raḥīq. Its seal or final taste is then associated with musk. Tasnīm is introduced as the source of its admixture, followed by a spring from which those brought near drink.

Tasnīm is therefore not an abstract moral concept here. It has a particular role in describing a drink.

The role of mizājuhu

Mizāj belongs to M Z J and concerns mixing one thing with another, or the substance used for mixing.

The suffix -hu in mizājuhu refers to the previously mentioned drink, raḥīq.

The verse thus says that this drink is mixed with something from Tasnīm.

Tasnīm is neither the name of raḥīq itself nor a synonym for mizāj. Raḥīq is the drink described, mizāj its admixture, and Tasnīm the source of that admixture.

These three concepts should not be collapsed in translation.

Does mixture with Tasnīm imply impurity?

Ordinarily, mixing substances can sometimes reduce quality, but need not do so.

Something may be added to improve a drink's taste, fragrance, or other qualities.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn, the admixture from Tasnīm appears in praise of heavenly blessing. Mizāj therefore does not establish that the original raḥīq was inferior or impure.

The description “sealed” in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25 and the mention of musk in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26 are separate features of the drink.

The Quran presents a collection of desirable qualities here, and Tasnīm belongs to that description.

Is Tasnīm the name of a spring?

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28 says:

عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا الْمُقَرَّبُونَ

ʿaynan yashrabu bihā al-muqarrabūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28

“A spring from which those brought near drink.”

ʿAynan here denotes a spring of water.

Several grammatical analyses have been proposed: an accusative governed by an implied verb, an explanatory expression for Tasnīm, or a connection with a construction understood from the preceding sentence.

Despite differences in grammatical analysis, the semantic connection is clear: Tasnīm relates to a spring from which those brought near drink.

Reading Tasnīm as the name of a heavenly spring therefore has strong contextual support.

Nevertheless, treating it as a proper name does not establish all its possible qualities from the root's meaning.

A name derived from elevation, for example, does not specify a numerical height, direction of flow, or geographical location.

5. Does Tasnīm flow from above?

One familiar lexical explanation connects the name with water descending from a high place.

As a linguistic image, this fits the family S N M.

Tasnīm relates structurally to raising; tasannama can express climbing above something; sanām names an elevated projection.

If a spring flows from a height, a name from this family is consequently intelligible.

Three propositions must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, the root concerns height and prominence: this is a clear lexical proposition.

Second, Quranic Tasnīm relates to a drinking spring: this follows from Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28.

Third, its exact source and elevation are separate questions that these two propositions do not conclusively answer.

Some interpretations, for example, describe Tasnīm as flowing from the highest part of Paradise or pouring down upon drinkers from above heavenly dwellings.

This image can suit the root, but the verse itself gives no specific elevation, precise source, or course of flow.

“Water pouring from the highest point of Paradise” therefore exceeds the word's direct meaning.

Physical elevation or high rank?

Elevation in Arabic may be literal and spatial or figuratively express rank and standing.

Sanām al-majd exemplifies physical height used to describe outstanding status.

For Tasnīm, too, one may ask whether its name concerns only spatial height or also the drink's high standing and special worth.

The surah concerns the blessings of the righteous and the drinking of those brought near, clearly associating Tasnīm with an outstanding blessing.

That association does not make “the highest spiritual station” the lexical definition of Tasnīm.

High standing, if inferred here, is a contextual interpretation; the root's lexical field remains elevation and prominence.

6. Tasnīm and those brought near

In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21, al-muqarrabūn appears in connection with the record of the righteous.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28, the same designation describes those drinking from the spring of Tasnīm.

Its repetition indicates its significance within the surah.

Muqarrabūn is the plural of the passive participle of form II from Q R B: “those brought near.” It does not belong to S N M.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28 identifies those brought near as drinkers from the spring.

A common interpretation holds that they drink Tasnīm unmixed, while the drinks of other righteous people are mixed with it.

This interpretation combines Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27, and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28. Yet these verses do not use a separate term such as “pure” or “unmixed” for their drinking.

Two relationships are explicit: the admixture of raḥīq comes from Tasnīm, and those brought near drink from the associated spring.

Differences in enjoyment can therefore be examined contextually, without presenting every detail as Tasnīm's literal meaning.

Are those brought near its only drinkers?

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28 names a particular group, those brought near.

Naming one group alone does not establish that no other group drinks from the spring under any circumstances.

Exclusivity requires evidence beyond the mere naming of a group.

Conversely, naming them is significant: the Quran connects them with the spring as part of their heavenly blessing.

Their stated distinction should be retained without inventing unstated restrictions.

7. Tasnīm alongside other heavenly springs

Al-Insān also describes drinks mixed with another substance or spring.

Al-Insān 76:5 mentions a cup whose admixture is camphor.

Al-Insān 76:6 mentions a spring from which the servants of God drink.

Al-Insān 76:17 introduces another drink with ginger as its admixture.

Al-Insān 76:18 mentions a spring named Salsabīl.

These descriptions relate to Tasnīm in Al-Muṭaffifīn: drink, admixture, and spring appear together in several heavenly scenes.

The differences among the words nevertheless matter.

Camphor and ginger have their established Arabic meanings; Salsabīl has been associated with pleasant drinking; Tasnīm belongs to the family of elevation and prominence.

Their appearance within one subject does not make them the same spring or substance.

Tasnīm and Salsabīl

Salsabīl in Al-Insān 76:18 and Tasnīm in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27 both occur in descriptions of heavenly drink and springs.

Their linguistic forms and roots, however, differ.

Tasnīm foregrounds elevation; lexical explanations of Salsabīl emphasize the drink's smoothness and pleasantness.

This helps distinguish the imagery without establishing a definitive theory of the springs' physical structure.

Naming the springs does not itself disclose their water's chemical composition, temperature, numerical elevation, or circulation.

The context concerns heavenly blessings described through desirable qualities.

8. Comparison with neighboring roots

S N M (sanām) and S M K (samk): prominence and structural height

S M K concerns height, the elevation of a structure, and its vertical extent.

S N M also concerns height, but its concrete image in sanām is a projection above the surrounding surface.

A wall's height may exemplify samk, while a hill's prominence above surrounding land is closer to the image of sanām.

This is a tendency in usage. Both relate to height, but their derivatives are not freely interchangeable.

S N M (sanām) and S M W (sumuww): prominence and elevation

S M W has a broad field of elevation and loftiness.

Concrete uses of S N M particularly concern prominence, projection, and rising above something.

Figuratively, both can relate to high rank and honor.

Semantic proximity does not establish historical kinship; each root's letters and structure require separate examination.

S N M (sanām) and R F ʿ (rafʿ): elevation and raising

Rafʿ concerns the act of lifting or raising.

Sannama can also mean raising, but its attested uses include making something prominent or hump-shaped.

Tasnīm generally concerns elevation, while not every act expressed by rafaʿa creates a raised, hump-like shape.

Lifting an object from the ground does not itself mean forming a projection on its surface.

S N M (sanām) and ʿ L W (ʿuluww): height and superiority

ʿUluww may express spatial height or, in other contexts, superiority and standing.

Sanām can likewise figuratively describe the most outstanding level of something.

ʿUluww is broader, however, and does not depend on a specific projection.

Elevation can help explain Tasnīm without making these families wholly synonymous.

S N M (sanām) and N B ʿ (nabʿ): height and springing water

Nabʿ concerns water emerging and welling from its source.

Quranic Tasnīm relates to a spring, but S N M does not itself mean water welling up.

This distinction matters.

A spring may well from the ground or flow from a height; Tasnīm should not without evidence be translated simply as water welling up.

The spring is the subject of the verse; Tasnīm concerns its name or characteristic.

S N M (sanām) and Ṣ N M (ṣanam): separate families

Ṣ N M is used in the Quran for idols and manufactured objects of worship.

S N M concerns prominence, elevation, and Tasnīm.

The roots differ only in their first letter, but occupy distinct Quranic fields.

The sound resemblance of sanām and ṣanam does not establish a shared derivation.

S N M consequently yields no definition of an idol, statue, or its worship.

9. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The root's sole Quranic form is tasnīmin in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27.

Arabic text:

وَمِزَاجُهُ مِنْ تَسْنِيمٍ

wa-mizājuhu min tasnīm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

A suitable direct translation is:

“And its admixture is from Tasnīm.”

This preserves Tasnīm because the following verse links it with a particular spring, whose name it may be.

“Its admixture is from elevation” substitutes the root meaning too directly for the drink's name or description, obscuring what is mixed with it.

“Its admixture is from the water of an elevated spring” is an explanatory reading worth examining, but “water” and “spring” come from the next verse, not from tasnīm itself in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27.

“Its admixture is from Paradise's loftiest drink” is a further interpretation. It may contribute to discussion of Tasnīm's standing, but is not a direct equivalent.

It is therefore preferable to preserve the name and explain it in the lexical commentary.

Suggested word information for Forqan:

Word in the verse: tasnīmin.

Root: S N M.

Brief root meaning: height, prominence, raising, and rising above.

Meaning here: Tasnīm, the name or description of a heavenly spring providing the drink's admixture and drunk from by those brought near.

Further explanation may note that the root supports a connection between the name and elevation, without making its precise character clear from the verse.

Principal forms in this family

Sanām: the hump on a camel's back.

Asnimah: humps or comparable projections.

Sanima: for the hump to grow large and prominent.

Sannama: raised or made prominent.

Tasannama: climbed or occupied the top of something.

Musannam: raised or made prominent.

Tasnīm: raising or making prominent; in Quranic usage, the name or description of the spring associated with the heavenly drink.

These forms illuminate the lexical family, but only Tasnīm occurs in the Quran.

Conclusion

S N M concerns height, prominence, and rising above. Its clearest concrete example is the camel's hump, a projection above the surrounding parts of its back.

The same field includes raised terrain, lifting objects, rising flames, and occupying a height.

Evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry includes Asnumah in Zuhayr's poem and Tasnīm in a line attributed to al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas. These relate to the semantic field, while variant readings, place-names, and transmission history must remain distinct from certain claims about origins.

The Quran uses the root only once, as Tasnīm in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27.

Its context connects Tasnīm with the drink of the righteous; the following verse mentions a spring from which those brought near drink.

Tasnīm therefore names or describes a heavenly spring. Its lexical connection with height is defensible, but the root alone cannot determine the spring's exact elevation, location, or flow.

The resulting definition is:

S N M denotes height, prominence, and rising above the surroundings. Its verbal forms also express raising or occupying a position above something. Tasnīm in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27 relates to a spring whose name or description belongs to this field of elevation and prominence.

Spatial height, bodily prominence, and high rank can be linguistically related without being identical.

In the Quranic description, the root supplies an image of elevation; the spring's reality and qualities extend beyond the details expressed by the word itself.

Preserving Tasnīm in direct translation and explaining its semantic field in lexical commentary thus respects the Quranic wording while avoiding unsupported additions to the verse.
