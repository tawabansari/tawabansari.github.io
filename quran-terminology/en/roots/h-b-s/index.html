The Arabic root ح ب س concerns holding something and preventing its release, movement, or progress. حَبَسَ (ḥabasa) is used when a person, animal, or thing is prevented from leaving, being released, or continuing on its way. This may involve keeping someone in a place, restraining an animal, obstructing water, or an event being delayed.

In modern usage, ḥabs is often associated with prison and deprivation of freedom. Its Arabic range is broader: holding is not always imprisonment. It can mean keeping someone for a task, stopping a flow, or holding back an event.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an. Al-Māʾidah 5:106 uses تَحْبِسُونَهُمَا (taḥbisūnahumā) for holding two witnesses during testimony and an oath. Hūd 11:8 uses يَحْبِسُهُ (yaḥbisuhu) in a question about what holds back a delayed punishment.

Both involve holding back or preventing progress, but with different objects: people in one case, and the arrival of punishment in the other.

1. Forms and Derivatives

Ḥ B S consists of ḥāʾ, bāʾ, and sīn and is a sound Arabic root, with no weak consonant, hamzah, or doubled radical.

Important forms include:

  • حَبَسَ ـ يَحْبِسُ (ḥabasa–yaḥbisu): held, detained, or prevented movement or release.
  • حَبْس (ḥabs): holding or detention; as a noun, also a detention place.
  • حَبِيس (ḥabīs): a person or thing held or prevented from release.
  • مَحْبُوس (maḥbūs): detained; someone or something with restricted freedom of movement.
  • حَابِس (ḥābis): one holding or restraining.
  • مَحْبَس / مَحْبِس (maḥbas/maḥbis): a holding or detention place; in some forms and uses, also a verbal noun or a name of an instrument or place.
  • حُبْسَة (ḥubsah): stoppage or impediment, including difficulty in speaking.
  • حَبَّسَ ـ يُحَبِّسُ (ḥabbasa–yuḥabbisu): held or detained; in some uses with greater emphasis.
  • أَحْبَسَ ـ يُحْبِسُ (aḥbasa–yuḥbisu): held back or dedicated something to a particular use.
  • اِحْتَبَسَ ـ يَحْتَبِسُ (iḥtabasa–yaḥtabisu): remained behind, stayed, or held oneself; sometimes reserved something for oneself.
  • تَحَبَّسَ (taḥabbasa): restrained oneself from something or confined oneself to a task.
  • حَبِيسَة (ḥabīsah): the feminine of ḥabīs; something held or dedicated.
  • تَحْبِيس (taḥbīs): holding or dedicating; legally, retaining property’s principal and dedicating its benefit.
  • حِبْس (ḥibs): in an early use, a basin or structure collecting and retaining water.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Only the Form I verb ḥabasa–yaḥbisu occurs, in taḥbisūnahumā at Al-Māʾidah 5:106 and yaḥbisuhu at Hūd 11:8.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two transitive Form I verbs

Both are active, transitive imperfect verbs. The verbal noun ḥabs, the nouns ḥabīs and maḥbūs, and the forms aḥbasa, ḥabbasa, and iḥtabasa do not occur in these forms in the Qur’anic text.

2. Semantic Core and Early Arabic Uses

Holding as opposed to release

Lisān al-ʿArab contrasts حَبْس (ḥabs) with تَخْلِيَة (takhliyah).

Takhliyah here concerns release and leaving the way open; ḥabs prevents free departure or release.

حَبَسَهُ عَنْ وَجْهِهِ (ḥabasahu ʿan wajhihi) means preventing someone from proceeding toward their destination.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris records حَبَسْتُهُ حَبْسًا (ḥabastuhu ḥabsan) and discusses something held or stopped.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: holding and a water-retaining structure

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise explains ḥabs in Al-Mufradāt as preventing movement or progress.

The basic meaning is therefore holding something in a condition that prevents its movement, departure, release, or progress.

Lisān and Al-Rāghib: restraint versus release and prevention of movement

This restraint can be brief or prolonged; the root does not specify its duration.

Nor is it restricted to people. Animals, water, property, and the occurrence of an event can also be its objects.

Holding a caravan: a witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah al-Yashkurī, a pre-Islamic poet, says:

فَحَبَسْتُ فِيهَا الرَّكْبَ أَحْدُسُ فِي
جُلِّ الْأُمُورِ وَكُنْتُ ذَا حَدْسِ

fa-ḥabastu fīhā al-rakba aḥdusu fī / julli al-umūri wa-kuntu dhā ḥadsi

Meaning: “I held the caravan there, considering most matters by conjecture; I was a man of discernment and conjecture.”

Ḥabastu means holding the caravan and preventing its onward movement.

The poet describes a place retaining traces of an earlier dwelling. He halts the caravan to reflect on those traces and past events.

This differs from imprisonment.

The caravan is held from continuing, without any mention of prison, punishment, or crime.

The opening of the same poem contains الْحَبْس (al-Ḥabs), but as a place-name. That geographical use should not be equated with the action in fa-ḥabastu.

The witness shows ḥabs as a halt during movement without necessarily involving judicial deprivation of liberty. Its transmission in the collected poems establishes an attribution, rather than independently fixing the exact date of this line.

Dīwān of Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah: holding the caravan

Holding and restraining animals

Early Arabic uses إِبِلٌ مُحَبَّسَةٌ (ibilun muḥabbasah) for camels kept near their holding place rather than released freely to pasture.

Tethering, confinement within an area, or human supervision may restrict the animal’s free movement.

Lexical uses of maḥbas also concern a place for holding or feeding animals.

The core remains prevention of release or free movement.

The keeping is not necessarily punishment or injury. An animal may be held for care, use, or another purpose.

Ḥabs itself proves neither kindness nor cruelty. The purpose and conditions are separate from the root’s meaning.

Ḥibs: restraining the flow of water

Holding water is another concrete use.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records حِبْس (ḥibs) for a structure or place collecting and retaining water.

Lexical sources give أَحْبَاس (aḥbās) as its plural, referring to water-retaining basins or structures.

A barrier holds water that could otherwise flow onward.

Ḥabs thus applies beyond living beings: a barrier can stop or restrict water’s flow.

The purpose may be to store water for later use.

Here it does not mean destroying water or permanently denying people access to it; it concerns preventing unrestricted flow.

The water-retaining structure meaning should not replace every occurrence of ḥabs. The object and context identify the intended use.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and lexical sources: ḥibs and aḥbās

Ḥabīs and dedicating property

Another branch concerns dedicating property.

حَبِيس (ḥabīs) can denote property whose owner removes its principal from unrestricted transfer and dedicates its use to a particular purpose.

Lexical sources use أَحْبَسَ فَرَسًا (aḥbasa farasan) for dedicating a horse to a particular use.

In developed legal terminology, تَحْبِيس (taḥbīs) concerns retaining the principal of property and dedicating its benefit.

This relates to وَقْف (waqf), but the entire law of endowment is not inherent in the root.

The semantic core is withholding property from certain unrestricted transactions and retaining it for a specified use.

Rules of ownership, transfer, inheritance, and benefit belong to the relevant legal systems, rather than the root alone.

This use does not make either Qur’anic occurrence a reference to endowing or dedicating property.

Lexical sources: dedicating a horse or property and taḥbīs

Ḥubsah: difficulty and interruption in speech

Lexical sources also record حُبْسَة (ḥubsah) for difficulty or impediment in speaking.

When speech does not flow easily, its progress can be described as stopped or restricted.

The use concerns the tongue and expression, rather than imprisoning a person.

Asās al-Balāghah also records ḥubsah as difficulty in expression.

The image of restraint can therefore extend from bodily movement to the flow of speech.

Such resemblance does not establish the historical origin of every derivative with certainty. Each form requires evidence of its use.

Asās al-Balāghah: ḥubsah and difficulty in expression

3. Qur’anic Uses

Al-Māʾidah 5:106: holding two witnesses for an oath

Arabic verse:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا شَهَادَةُ بَيْنِكُمْ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ حِينَ الْوَصِيَّةِ اثْنَانِ ذَوَا عَدْلٍ مِنْكُمْ أَوْ آخَرَانِ مِنْ غَيْرِكُمْ إِنْ أَنْتُمْ ضَرَبْتُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَأَصَابَتْكُمْ مُصِيبَةُ الْمَوْتِ تَحْبِسُونَهُمَا مِنْ بَعْدِ الصَّلَاةِ فَيُقْسِمَانِ بِاللَّهِ إِنِ ارْتَبْتُمْ لَا نَشْتَرِي بِهِ ثَمَنًا وَلَوْ كَانَ ذَا قُرْبَىٰ وَلَا نَكْتُمُ شَهَادَةَ اللَّهِ إِنَّا إِذًا لَمِنَ الْآثِمِينَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū shahādatu baynikum idhā ḥaḍara aḥadakumu al-mawtu ḥīna al-waṣiyyati ithnāni dhawā ʿadlin minkum aw ākharāni min ghayrikum in antum ḍarabtum fī al-arḍi fa-aṣābatkum muṣībatu al-mawti taḥbisūnahumā min baʿdi al-ṣalāti fa-yuqsimāni bi-Allāhi ini irtabtum lā nashtarī bihi thamanan wa-law kāna dhā qurbā wa-lā naktumu shahādata Allāhi innā idhan la-mina al-āthimīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“You who believe, testimony among you when death approaches one of you, at the time of making a bequest, is by two just persons from among you, or two others from outside your group if you have traveled through the land and the calamity of death has reached you. You hold the two after ṣalāh; if you doubt, they swear by The God: We take no price for it, even where a relative is concerned, and we do not conceal the testimony of The God, for then we would be among the sinful.”

Taḥbisūnahumā is an active Form I imperfect verb.

Taḥbisūna addresses a plural “you”: you hold or detain.

Humā refers to the two people introduced earlier as witnesses.

The object is therefore two people, rather than property, a written will, or testimony itself.

This matters for translation: the verse speaks of holding two witnesses, rather than seizing property or preventing execution of a bequest.

Taḥbisūnahumā and testimony

The context concerns a bequest, death, travel, and testimony.

After introducing two witnesses, the verse says to hold them after ṣalāh so that, if there is doubt, they swear an oath.

The holding keeps them available for the oath and clarification of the testimony.

Taḥbisūnahumā alone does not name a place of detention.

The verse does not say that they must enter a prison or receive a sentence.

Ḥabs can nevertheless mean keeping someone from leaving. It should not be weakened into a merely optional invitation to converse.

The more precise contextual sense is holding the witnesses for a stage in examining the testimony.

A particular purpose is stated. The word alone does not establish indefinite detention, long imprisonment, or deprivation of other rights.

Min baʿdi al-ṣalāh: the timing

مِنْ بَعْدِ الصَّلَاةِ (min baʿdi al-ṣalāh) specifies the relevant time.

It does not belong to Ḥ B S, but helps situate the verb.

Some early commentaries identify a particular prayer, especially the afternoon prayer, here.

The Arabic phrase itself, however, does not name ʿaṣr.

The direct translation should preserve “after ṣalāh,” with any account of its particular timing or kind examined separately.

Min baʿdi relates the holding to a particular time, but does not itself specify the full duration.

Al-Ṭabarī: holding the two and differing accounts of ṣalāh

Oaths and doubt

The continuation says فَيُقْسِمَانِ بِاللَّهِ إِنِ ارْتَبْتُمْ (fa-yuqsimāni bi-Allāhi ini irtabtum).

This connects the witnesses’ oath with doubt.

Irtabtum belongs to R Y B and concerns doubt or suspicion.

Yuqsimāni belongs to Q S M and concerns swearing an oath.

Three distinct elements are present: holding the witnesses, doubt about testimony, and their oath.

They should not all be merged into taḥbisūnahumā.

Ḥabs expresses the holding. Oath and doubt clarify its role within the testimony procedure.

The witnesses go on to swear that they take no price for the testimony and do not conceal it.

The verse’s overall purpose concerns trustworthiness and preventing loss of rights in a bequest.

The lexical meaning of ḥabs does not supply the entire law of testimony and bequests.

The relationship with the following verses

Al-Māʾidah 5:107 addresses the discovery of wrongdoing by the witnesses. Al-Māʾidah 5:108 concerns giving testimony properly and avoiding wrongful oaths.

Al-Māʾidah 5:106 is thus part of a process for examining testimony.

Taḥbisūnahumā should be understood within that process: holding the witnesses for the oath and examination of testimony.

Detached from context, it might become a general command to imprison people. The verse itself specifies the people, subject, and situation.

Conversely, “merely waiting” can understate the act of holding them back.

“You hold the two” preserves the basic meaning; contextual explanation identifies the purpose.

Hūd 11:8: what holds the punishment back?

Arabic verse:

وَلَئِنْ أَخَّرْنَا عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابَ إِلَىٰ أُمَّةٍ مَعْدُودَةٍ لَيَقُولُنَّ مَا يَحْبِسُهُ أَلَا يَوْمَ يَأْتِيهِمْ لَيْسَ مَصْرُوفًا عَنْهُمْ وَحَاقَ بِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

wa-la-in akhkharnā ʿanhumu al-ʿadhāba ilā ummatin maʿdūdatin la-yaqūlunna mā yaḥbisuhu a-lā yawma yaʾtīhim laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“If We delay their punishment until a numbered term, they will certainly say: What holds it back? Be aware: on the day it reaches them, it will not be turned away, and what they mocked will encompass them.”

Yaḥbisuhu is a third-person masculine singular active imperfect verb.

The pronoun hu refers to الْعَذَابَ (al-ʿadhāb), the punishment.

The thing held back is therefore punishment.

Unlike Al-Māʾidah 5:106, the object is an event whose occurrence has been foretold, rather than a person.

Holding back and delaying

The verse opens with أَخَّرْنَا (akhkharnā).

This belongs to ʾ KH R and concerns postponing or delaying.

The opponents then ask مَا يَحْبِسُهُ (mā yaḥbisuhu): what holds it back?

The expressions are close but distinct.

Akhkharnā expresses postponement of the punishment’s occurrence.

Yaḥbisuhu, in their question, raises what prevents its arrival.

Here ḥabs does not mean keeping punishment in a physical location.

The verse names no container or place in which it is imprisoned. The issue is its not occurring at the time raised by the questioners.

“What holds it back?” and the more explanatory “What prevents its coming?” are therefore suitable.

Who asks the question?

Mā yaḥbisuhu is spoken by those mocking the punishment.

It is not a direct Qur’anic statement that God lacks power or that an independent barrier obstructs His will.

The verse first says that if We delay it, they will speak this way.

Delay is attributed to God; the question about its restraint belongs to the opponents.

This distinction matters.

Their mocking question should not be used as established evidence of an actual barrier before God.

Their mistaken or mocking intention does not change yaḥbisu’s lexical meaning.

They still use a word concerning restraint and prevention of occurrence.

Laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum: the punishment is not turned away

The continuation says لَيْسَ مَصْرُوفًا عَنْهُمْ (laysa maṣrūfan ʿanhum).

This belongs to Ṣ R F and concerns turning away or removing.

At the beginning punishment is delayed; when it arrives, it is not turned away from them.

These are different conditions.

Holding back or delaying an event does not necessarily cancel it definitively.

Its arrival and not being averted are stated separately.

“Canceling forever” should therefore not be inserted into ḥabasa’s definition.

In Hūd 11:8, ḥabs concerns delayed punishment in the opponents’ question; the continuation shows that delay does not prevent fulfillment at the appointed time.

Al-Ṭabarī: the opponents’ question and punishment held back

4. Differences from Related Roots

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and M N ʿ (manaʿa): holding and preventing

Study of M N ʿ

Manaʿa concerns preventing arrival, occurrence, action, or access.

Many uses of ḥabasa likewise involve prevention.

Ḥabs often emphasizes holding or stopping something, while manʿ can prevent an occurrence or access without holding something at a location.

Someone can be denied entry without being detained elsewhere.

If they are held from continuing on their way, ḥabs is more evident.

The distinction is not absolute: both verbs can describe the same conduct in some situations.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and M S K (amsaka): holding and retaining

Amsaka concerns taking hold and retaining.

Lexical sources also explain ḥabasa through amsaka.

Imsāk has a broader range of taking, retaining, and preventing release.

Ḥabs is especially apparent when a person or thing is prevented from leaving, being freed, or progressing.

Qur’anic amsaka has varied objects, including holding birds and preventing release.

The overlap does not make every instance of imsāk imprisonment or judicial detention.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and S J N (sajana): holding and imprisonment

Sijn appears in the Qur’anic story of Joseph.

Yūsuf 12:33 mentions prison, and later verses describe Joseph’s entry and life there.

Sajana concerns imprisonment in that context.

Ḥabasa is lexically broader: briefly holding someone, stopping a caravan, or holding back an event need not involve a prison.

Al-Māʾidah 5:106 concerns two witnesses, without the word itself specifying a prison.

Hūd 11:8 concerns punishment being held back, rather than a person being imprisoned.

Ḥabs can overlap with sajn, but not every act of holding is imprisonment.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and ʾ KH R (akhkhara): holding back and postponing

Study of ʾ KH R

The two families occur together in Hūd 11:8.

Akhkharnā concerns delaying punishment; yaḥbisuhu raises the restraining factor in the opponents’ question.

Delay emphasizes the event’s temporal relation; ḥabs emphasizes prevention of progress or arrival.

An event held back may occur later, but the overlap does not make every use identical.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and Ṣ B R (ṣabara): restraint and perseverance

Study of Ṣ B R

Lexical sources also explain ṣabr through holding or restraining oneself.

Qur’anic ṣabr concerns perseverance, firmness, and self-restraint under pressure and difficulty.

Ḥabs denotes the holding itself without necessarily having a positive moral value.

A person might be unjustly detained or might restrain themselves from wrongdoing.

Praised Qur’anic ṣabr, in contrast, concerns steadfastness on the right course.

Shared restraint does not make ḥabs and ṣabr identical.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣara): holding and restriction

Study of Ḥ Ṣ R

Some Arabic uses of ḥaṣr concern restriction, confinement, or being prevented from movement or access.

This is close to ḥabs.

Ḥaṣr can emphasize constraint or a restricted condition, while ḥabs more prominently expresses holding and preventing progress.

Qur’anic Ḥ Ṣ R appears in various settings, including being prevented from acting and conditions of restriction.

This proximity does not transfer the rules concerning iḥṣār in pilgrimage verses into the two occurrences of Ḥ B S without evidence.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and W Q F (waqafa): stopping and holding

Waqafa concerns standing or stopping.

Ḥabasa can cause someone to stop and prevent further movement.

Waqf can express the condition of standing or stopping itself, while ḥabs often emphasizes the act of holding or restraining.

In developed legal usage, waqf and taḥbīs also overlap concerning the dedication of property.

This connection does not make taḥbisūnahumā in Al-Māʾidah 5:106 the endowment of two witnesses or their dedication to an institution.

Ḥ B S (ḥabasa) and Ḥ S B (ḥasiba): holding and counting

Study of Ḥ S B

The letters resemble one another, but bāʾ and sīn change position.

Ḥ B S concerns holding and restraining.

Ḥ S B has established meanings of counting, accounting, and supposing.

Yaḥbisuhu in Hūd 11:8 means “holds it back,” not “counts it.”

Root study must preserve radical order; phonetic similarity should not merge the lexical families.

5. Interpretive Challenges and Semantic Development

Does ḥabs always mean a prison sentence?

No.

Imprisonment is one kind of ḥabs, rather than its entire meaning.

Holding a caravan, restraining water, keeping someone for a testimony procedure, and an event being held back can all fall within its range.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence explicitly sentences someone to imprisonment.

Al-Māʾidah 5:106 concerns holding two witnesses.

Hūd 11:8 asks what holds punishment back.

The modern penal sense should not be imposed on these verses.

Is every restraint wrong?

The root itself does not determine whether restraint is right or wrong.

Keeping someone for protection or an essential inquiry may be necessary; unjust detention can violate freedom and constitute oppression.

Al-Māʾidah 5:106 places holding the witnesses within testimony and a bequest.

It does not grant unlimited authority to deprive people of liberty.

Ḥabs alone does not establish that every kind of detention is lawful or unlawful.

Moral and legal judgment requires purposes, circumstances, limits, and the verses concerning justice and human rights.

Does ḥabs always mean a complete stop?

Many uses prevent movement or release, but the root specifies neither degree nor duration.

A caravan may be held briefly.

Water may be stored and used later.

A person may be kept for a task and released afterward.

Hūd 11:8 delays punishment but explicitly speaks of its arrival.

Ḥabs therefore does not imply destruction, permanent cancellation, or definitive impossibility of occurrence.

Ḥabs and dedicating property

Arabic legal taḥbīs can concern retaining the principal of property and dedicating its benefit.

This relates to waqf, but its complete rules do not come from the root alone.

The lexical use does not mean Al-Māʾidah 5:106 or Hūd 11:8 concerns endowments.

The object and context clarify each use.

Voluntary stopping and being held by someone else

Ḥabasahu and forms such as iḥtabasa differ structurally.

Ḥabasahu generally expresses someone holding another person or thing.

Iḥtabasa can express remaining behind or holding oneself.

Not every use should therefore be assumed to involve external coercion by someone else.

Both Qur’anic occurrences are transitive Form I: the addressees hold two witnesses in Al-Māʾidah, and the questioners ask what holds punishment back in Hūd.

Agency and the subject–object relation must be understood from the actual sentence.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For taḥbisūnahumā in Al-Māʾidah 5:106, “you hold the two” is the most suitable direct rendering.

It preserves the dual object and restraint without adding prison or punishment.

“You hold the two back” is lexically sound, though its Persian equivalent may raise the question of what they are prevented from doing. “You keep them” is more natural in the testimony and oath context.

“You imprison the two” is unsuitable because the word alone specifies neither that method nor location.

For mā yaḥbisuhu in Hūd 11:8, “what holds it back?” follows the Arabic structure closely.

“What has prevented its coming?” clarifies the context but is more explanatory: coming is understood from the continuation and the subject of punishment.

“Why has it been delayed?” conveys the question’s implication while preserving less of the distinction between akhkharnā and yaḥbisuhu.

“Holding, detaining, and preventing movement or progress” is suitable as Forqan’s brief root meaning. Brief root meaning: Holding, detaining, and preventing movement or progress.

Each verse’s contextual meaning should remain separate.

For Al-Māʾidah 5:106, “holding two witnesses for an oath” clarifies the use.

For Hūd 11:8, “holding back or preventing the arrival of punishment” is suitable.

Added contextual explanations should not replace the word itself in the base translation.

7. Conclusion

Ḥ B S concerns holding and preventing movement, release, or progress.

It can concern people, animals, water, property, and even events.

Early Arabic attests ḥabasa for holding a caravan and its movement. Ḥibs can name a water-retaining structure, and ḥabīs something held, whether a person, animal, or object.

Legal uses connect this family with prison, detention, and dedication of property, but these technical senses do not exhaust its range. Their attestation does not by itself date the emergence of every legal sense.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

Taḥbisūnahumā in Al-Māʾidah 5:106 concerns holding two witnesses during testimony and an oath, in the setting of bequest and doubt, rather than sentencing them to prison.

Mā yaḥbisuhu in Hūd 11:8 is the opponents’ question about delayed punishment. Delay is not permanent cancellation: the verse also announces its arrival.

The clear meaning of Ḥ B S is holding or restraining against release or progress.

The restraint may be material, as with people and animals, or concern time and an event’s occurrence, as with punishment being held back.

Forqan’s translation should preserve that core. “Keeping” in Al-Māʾidah and “holding back” in Hūd are natural renderings.

Understanding the root alone is insufficient: the object, verbal construction, and context matter too. This preserves restraint and prevention without imposing the modern prison sense on every use.
