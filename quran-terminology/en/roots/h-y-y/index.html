Linguistic Note

The core meaning of this root is life in contrast to death, dryness, lifelessness, and closedness. A living thing is not merely something biologically moving; in Qur’anic use, life is tied to effect, wakefulness, responsiveness, growth, freshness, and the ability to receive.

From this field, the branch of haya is also understood. Haya is not merely social embarrassment. It is a living ethical sensitivity: a person gathers himself or herself and holds back before an act that would break dignity. Haya is therefore a sign of a living conscience and living inward boundaries, not blind fear or unhealthy shame.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of Ḥ Y Y is life and living responsiveness. Dead land receives rain and becomes alive; the human heart, too, can receive the message of truth and come out of deadness and numbness.

In the Qur’an, this root extends from bodily life to ethical life, from reviving the earth to reviving the heart, from keeping alive to haya, and from greeting to the divine name الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy).

Material Image

The material image is land lying dry and silent, then receiving water, moving, becoming green, and showing the effect of life. Life means that something emerges from stillness, dryness, and lifelessness and gains flow, growth, and responsiveness.

At the bodily level, life is tied to breath, movement, warmth, growth, and reception. At the ethical level, haya resembles this inward life: the person is not numb toward boundaries; something inside responds and keeps him or her from breaking honor.

Classical Language Note

In classical Arabic, ḥayāh stands opposite mawt and is used for human and animal life as well as the reviving of land and plants. Haya is also used for healthy shame, restraint, and holding the self back from an ugly act.

These two branches are not unrelated. Both concern sensitivity, responsiveness, and not losing a living boundary. In Qur’anic reading, the immediate context remains the main guide.

Al-Ḥayy: Divine Life

In 2:255, the Qur’an says: اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ ۚ لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ (Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa al-Ḥayyu al-Qayyūm lā ta'khudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawm).

الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy) appears beside الْقَيُّوم (al-Qayyūm). The life of The God is not like the life of a creature. Created life is dependent, limited, and exposed to sleep, weakness, and death; divine life is self-standing, needless, undecaying, and the source of life for others.

In 25:58, the Qur’an says: وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى الْحَيِّ الَّذِي لَا يَمُوتُ (wa-tawakkal ʿalā al-Ḥayyi alladhī lā yamūt). Here the Qur’an itself clarifies the boundary: al-Ḥayy, when used of The God, is the Living who does not die.

The Definite Al in Divine Names

When the Qur’an says of The God الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy), الْقَيُّوم (al-Qayyūm), الْعَلِيم (al-ʿAlīm), or similar names, the definite ال (al) is not merely a simple grammatical marker. In this setting, the attribute is directed toward its known and unique perfection. The God is not one living being among other living beings; He is the true Living, upon whom the life of all living beings depends.

Divine attributes in the Qur’an use words known in human language, but their meaning for The God must be purified from creaturely deficiency. The God is ḥayy, but not by breath, blood, body, sleep, or death.

The God Gives Life and Causes Death

In 2:28, the Qur’an says: كَيْفَ تَكْفُرُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَكُنتُمْ أَمْوَاتًا فَأَحْيَاكُمْ ۖ ثُمَّ يُمِيتُكُمْ ثُمَّ يُحْيِيكُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ (kayfa takfurūna bi-Allāhi wa-kuntum amwātan fa-aḥyākum thumma yumītukum thumma yuḥyīkum thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn).

Life and death come in sequence: absence of life, being made alive, death, being made alive again, and return. أَحْيَاكُمْ (aḥyākum) shows the power of The God to bring a being from lifelessness into life.

In 30:19, the Qur’an says: يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَيُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتَ مِنَ الْحَيِّ وَيُحْيِي الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا (yukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti wa-yukhriju al-mayyita mina al-ḥayyi wa-yuḥyī al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā). The reviving of the earth after its death becomes a witness for resurrection.

Worldly Life and True Life of the Next Life

In 29:64, the Qur’an says: وَمَا هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا لَهْوٌ وَلَعِبٌ ۚ وَإِنَّ الدَّارَ الْآخِرَةَ لَهِيَ الْحَيَوَانُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ (wa-mā hādhihi al-ḥayātu al-dunyā illā lahwun wa-laʿib wa-inna al-dāra al-ākhirata lahiya al-ḥayawān law kānū yaʿlamūn).

الْحَيَوَان (al-ḥayawān) points to fuller, more serious, and more lasting life. The next life is not merely temporal continuation; the truth of life is more fully manifest there. “True life” or “real life” is more fitting here than a stiff unfamiliar rendering.

Revelatory Life and the Living Heart

In 8:24, the Qur’an says: يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اسْتَجِيبُوا لِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ إِذَا دَعَاكُمْ لِمَا يُحْيِيكُمْ (yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū istajībū li-Allāhi wa-li-l-rasūli idhā daʿākum li-mā yuḥyīkum).

يُحْيِيكُمْ (yuḥyīkum) here points to ethical, faith-filled, and human life. A person may be bodily alive while dead or half-alive in receiving truth, taking responsibility, waking up, and moving rightly.

Istiḥyā': Keeping Alive

In Pharaoh’s story, 2:49 says: يُذَبِّحُونَ أَبْنَاءَكُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيُونَ نِسَاءَكُمْ (yudhabbiḥūna abnā'akum wa-yastaḥyūna nisā'akum).

يَسْتَحْيُونَ (yastaḥyūn) here does not mean “they felt shame.” It means they kept your women alive. This keeping alive was not mercy; it took place within domination, selection, and exploitation. This example shows why forms from this root must not be translated mechanically.

Haya: Ethical Restraint

In 2:26, the Qur’an says: إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَسْتَحْيِي أَن يَضْرِبَ مَثَلًا مَّا بَعُوضَةً فَمَا فَوْقَهَا (inna Allāha lā yastaḥyī an yaḍriba mathalan mā baʿūḍatan fa-mā fawqahā).

The meaning is not that The God has or lacks a human psychological feeling of embarrassment. The point is that The God does not refrain from giving even a small example when it is right and true. Mentioning a mosquito as an example, when it serves truth, is not beneath divine dignity.

In 28:25, the Qur’an says: فَجَاءَتْهُ إِحْدَاهُمَا تَمْشِي عَلَى اسْتِحْيَاءٍ (fa-jā'athu iḥdāhumā tamshī ʿalā istiḥyā'). This haya does not erase a woman’s presence from public life; she comes, speaks, and conveys her father’s message. Haya here is the quality of presence, not the negation of presence.

Taḥiyyah: Life, Peace, and Honor

In 4:86, the Qur’an says: وَإِذَا حُيِّيتُم بِتَحِيَّةٍ فَحَيُّوا بِأَحْسَنَ مِنْهَا أَوْ رُدُّوهَا (wa-idhā ḥuyyītum bi-taḥiyyatin fa-ḥayyū bi-aḥsana minhā aw ruddūhā).

Taḥiyyah is not a cold verbal greeting. It is a speech-act that recognizes the life and honor of another. Responding with something better means increasing that honor, not diminishing it.

In 10:10, the Qur’an says: دَعْوَاهُمْ فِيهَا سُبْحَانَكَ اللَّهُمَّ وَتَحِيَّتُهُمْ فِيهَا سَلَامٌ (daʿwāhum fīhā subḥānaka Allāhumma wa-taḥiyyatuhum fīhā salām). Taḥiyyah in the next-life setting is joined with salām: peace, safety, and mutual honor.

Distinguishing Qur’anic Uses

In 2:255 and 25:58, الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy) is a divine name and points to the complete, deathless life of The God. In 2:28 and 30:19, giving life concerns revival after lifelessness or death. In 29:64, الْحَيَوَان (al-ḥayawān) points to the true life of the next life.

In 8:24, life has an ethical and guidance-related meaning. In 2:49, يَسْتَحْيُونَ (yastaḥyūn) means they keep alive. In 2:26 and 28:25, haya concerns restraint and dignity. In 4:86 and 10:10, taḥiyyah is tied to peace, honor, and the wish of life and safety.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ (al-Ḥayyu al-Qayyūm), two definite descriptions appear together. الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy) indicates complete life, and الْقَيُّوم (al-Qayyūm) indicates self-standing and sustaining others. Together they show that The God is not merely living; He is the Living upon whom the existence of others depends.

In لَا يَسْتَحْيِي أَن يَضْرِبَ مَثَلًا (lā yastaḥyī an yaḍriba mathalan), the structure with أَن (an) after the verb points to refraining from an act. In يَسْتَحْيُونَ نِسَاءَكُمْ (yastaḥyūna nisā'akum), the direct object and the contrast with killing the sons show that the meaning is keeping alive.

Difference From Nearby Roots

Ḥ Y Y is close to ʿ Y SH, but not the same. ʿAysh concerns living, livelihood, provisions, and ordinary conditions of life; ḥayāh is more basic: being alive and responsive.

Ḥ Y Y differs from B Q Y and KH L D. Baqā' means remaining; ḥayāh means being alive. Khuld is lastingness and endurance in a state or place; ḥayāh is the living flow itself.

Ḥ Y Y also differs from R W Ḥ and N F S. Rūḥ is tied to breath, divine command, life-force, and animation; ḥayāh is the state of being alive. Nafs points to self, soul, person, and the center of desire and responsibility.

Haya is not the same as khawf or embarrassment. Khawf is fear of harm or consequence; embarrassment may be social or psychological shame; healthy haya is dignity and ethical boundary-keeping.

Forqan Translation Method

In Forqan translation, Ḥ Y Y must be translated by context. حَيَاة (ḥayāh) is usually life, but الْحَيَوَان (al-ḥayawān) in 29:64 is better rendered “true life” or “real life.”

الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy), when used of The God, should not be reduced to “living” if the text allows fuller wording; “the Ever-Living,” “the Living who does not die,” or “the truly Living” is more precise. يَسْتَحْيُونَ نِسَاءَكُمْ (yastaḥyūna nisā'akum) must be translated “they kept your women alive,” not “they felt shame before your women.” But لَا يَسْتَحْيِي (lā yastaḥyī) points to not refraining from giving a true example.

Translation Suggestions

  • For الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy): the Ever-Living; the Living who does not die; the truly Living, in root explanation.
  • For الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ (al-Ḥayyu al-Qayyūm): the Ever-Living, the Self-Standing Sustainer.
  • For الْحَيَاة الدُّنْيَا (al-ḥayāt al-dunyā): worldly life; the near and passing life, in explanation.
  • For الْحَيَوَان (al-ḥayawān): true life; real life.
  • For فَأَحْيَاكُمْ (fa-aḥyākum): He gave you life; He brought you to life.
  • For لِمَا يُحْيِيكُمْ (li-mā yuḥyīkum): to what gives you life; to what gives you life and wakefulness.
  • For يَسْتَحْيُونَ نِسَاءَكُمْ (yastaḥyūna nisā'akum): they kept your women alive; they left your women alive.
  • For لَا يَسْتَحْيِي (lā yastaḥyī): He does not refrain; or, He does not have haya, with explanation that He does not refrain from a true act.
  • For تَمْشِي عَلَى اسْتِحْيَاءٍ (tamshī ʿalā istiḥyā'): walking with haya; coming with dignity and restraint; walking while preserving her boundary.
  • For تَحِيَّة (taḥiyyah): greeting; greeting and honoring; salutation carrying a wish of peace and life.

Summary

The root Ḥ Y Y in the Qur’an points to life, being alive, freshness, responsiveness, and the flow of life. It extends from bodily life and the reviving of the earth to ethical human life and the true life of the next life. Against death, dryness, and numbness, life means the capacity to grow, receive, move, and respond.

In the divine name الْحَيّ (al-Ḥayy), this root reaches its highest transcendent use. The God is living, but not like a creature; His life is needless, deathless, and the source of life for others. The behavioral branch of the root, haya, is not separate from life; it is the sign of living inward boundaries.
