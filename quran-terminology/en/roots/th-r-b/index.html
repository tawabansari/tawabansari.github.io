Qur’anic Root TH R B

Reproach, blame, and condemnatory reminders of wrongdoing: from calling someone to account to forbearance without humiliation or taunting

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(tathrība)

(tharaba)

In one principal Arabic branch, TH R B concerns reproaching someone for a fault or wrongful conduct. This can involve recalling an offence, finding fault, rebuking, and calling the person to account, particularly when a speaker confronts someone with a past wrong and blames them for it.

This use involves more than informing someone of a mistake. A person may recognise and even admit the wrong, while someone else continues repeating it to reproach them or deepen their shame.

This distinction matters in the root’s only clear Qur’anic occurrence.

In Yūsuf 12:91, Joseph’s brothers recognise the truth and admit their wrongdoing. In reply, Joseph says in Yūsuf 12:92:

«لَا تَثْرِيبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْيَوْمَ»

lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawma

That is, there is no reproach or taunting against you today.

The brothers’ wrong is not denied; the truth of their past conduct has become clear. Refraining from tathrīb here does not mean their conduct was right, but concerns Joseph’s treatment of people who have now admitted their wrongdoing.

Arabic tharb also names a thin layer of fat covering parts of an animal’s internal organs. Some lexicographers propose a derivational connection between this physical sense and reproach, whereas Ibn Fāris treats them as two distinct bases.

Study of this root must therefore distinguish the attested meaning of reproach from proposed explanations of its origin.

1. The semantic core in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris writes about this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الثَّاءُ وَالرَّاءُ وَالْبَاءُ كَلِمَتَانِ مُتَبَايِنَتَا الْأَصْلِ، لَا فُرُوعَ لَهُمَا»

al-thāʾu wa-al-rāʾu wa-al-bāʾu kalimatāni mutabāyinatā al-aṣli, lā furūʿa lahumā

The family contains two uses with different bases, which should not be regarded as branches of one meaning.

He then explains the first branch:

«فَالتَّثْرِيبُ اللَّوْمُ وَالْأَخْذُ عَلَى الذَّنْبِ»

fa-al-tathrību al-lawmu wa-al-akhdhu ʿalā al-dhanbi

Tathrīb means blaming and calling someone to account for an offence or wrong.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: two bases and the definition of tathrīb

This definition has two important aspects.

First is lawm, reproach or blame.

Second is al-akhdh ʿalā al-dhanb, calling someone to account for a wrong attributed to them.

Tathrīb is more than expressing dissatisfaction: the reproaching person makes someone else’s past conduct the object of blame.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains it through rebuke, fault-finding, and pursuing blame thoroughly.

Tathrīb can therefore go beyond ordinary reproach to repeated or severe censure.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī calls it al-taqrīʿ wa-al-taqrīr bi-al-dhanb: overt rebuke and confronting someone with their wrongdoing.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: rebuke and confronting wrongdoing

The established meaning of this branch can thus be expressed as follows:

Reproaching or calling someone to account by bringing up their wrongful conduct, particularly when the reminder becomes blame, fault-finding, or humiliation.

Does tathrīb concern only an actual wrong?

Lexical definitions commonly associate tathrīb with dhanb, a wrong attributed to someone.

Establishing the truth nevertheless requires distinguishing an actual wrong from an allegation.

Someone may have acted wrongly and be reproached for that action.

Conversely, an innocent person may be blamed because others think they committed a wrong.

The reproaching speech can still have the form of tathrīb even when its allegation is untrue.

The use of a verb meaning reproach does not by itself establish that an accusation is true.

Joseph’s narrative differs, since the brothers’ wrong is already clear in the story and they themselves acknowledge it.

The issue there is continued reproach after the truth has emerged, rather than whether the wrong was real or invented.

2. Physical tharb and the question of derivational connection

Lexical sources use tharb for a thin layer of fat spread over the stomach, intestines, and certain internal organs of an animal.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad records this sense in Kitāb al-ʿAyn, with the plural thurūb.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains tharb as thin fat covering the intestines and stomach.

Shātun tharbāʾ describes a sheep with abundant fat of this kind.

Here tharb names a physical substance.

Does reproach derive from removing a layer of fat?

Some lexical explanations attempt to connect tathrīb meaning reproach with tharb meaning fat.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī proposes such a connection, comparing other words that extend from the name of a layer or covering to broader senses.

Later explanations suggest that severe reproach figuratively strips away a person’s reputation or dignity, exposing a fault.

This is intelligible as a literary image.

Repeatedly bringing up someone’s wrong can damage their reputation before others.

The similarity does not, however, establish that tharraba historically developed from the name of a layer of fat.

Ibn Fāris regards the two as having distinct bases.

Stripping off fat should therefore not be made the definitive original meaning of tathrīb.

Nor should “I will not tear away the covering of your reputation” directly replace lā tathrība ʿalaykum in a Qur’anic translation.

It may explain an effect of reproach, but the word in the verse concerns blame and censure.

Subsidiary uses

Some sources also record tharaba al-marīḍa for removing a sick person’s clothing.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: fat, forms, and subsidiary uses

Al-tharabāt is also applied to fingers or their extremities.

Uses concerning a type of ground and certain place names are recorded as well.

These meanings do not occur in the Qur’an through tathrīb.

Their shared letters do not justify merging all these branches into the definition of reproach.

3. Morphological forms

Early Arabic records several verbal forms for reproach.

Tharaba

Tharabahu and tharaba ʿalayhi concern reproaching and blaming someone.

In some uses, a person recounts another’s wrongful conduct to them and condemns them for it.

Tharb is also recorded as a Form I verbal noun in this branch.

Physical tharb, however, names fat; context is needed to distinguish the uses.

Tharraba and tathrīb

Tharraba is Form II.

Its verbal noun is tathrīb.

Here the verb can concern reproach, fault-finding, and condemnatory reminders of wrongdoing.

Form II sometimes emphasises intensity or repetition, but not every occurrence necessarily means prolonged, repeated reproach.

Tathrīb can be severe censure, while the sentence determines its extent and duration.

Only this verbal noun occurs as a clear derivative in the Qur’an.

Athraba

Athraba is Form IV.

As transmitted by Thaʿlab and other lexicographers, Ibn al-Aʿrābī records tharaba, tharraba, and athraba for rebuking and reproaching.

Three verbal patterns can thus operate in the same semantic field.

Their morphological differences are real, however; their intensity and usage should not be assumed identical without examination.

Thārib and mutharrib

Thārib is the Form I active participle and denotes someone reproaching another.

Mutharrib is the Form II active participle, describing someone who blames and finds fault with another.

Some Arabic uses also have muthrib from Form IV.

The geminated and ungeminated forms must be distinguished, since both are recorded in lexical sources.

Tathrīb

Tathrīb is the Form II verbal noun and names the act of reproaching or blaming.

In Qur’anic lā tathrība ʿalaykum, it is a noun, rather than a prohibitive verb.

This syntactic distinction matters.

Joseph does not say lā tutharribū, “Do not reproach.”

He says lā tathrība ʿalaykum, “There is no reproach or taunting against you.”

The statement negates reproach against the brothers, rather than merely ordering them how to speak to other people.

4. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim: forbearance without reproach

A verse transmitted in lexical sources and collections of Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim’s poetry reads:

فَعَفَوْتُ عَنْهُمْ عَفْوَ غَيْرِ مُثَرِّبٍ
وَتَرَكْتُهُمْ لِعِقَابِ يَوْمٍ سَرْمَدِ

fa-ʿafawtu ʿanhum ʿafwa ghayri mutharribin / wa-taraktuhum li-ʿiqābi yawmin sarmadi

Translation: “I pardoned them with a pardon free of reproach, and left them to the punishment of an enduring day.”

This witness is particularly relevant because it places ʿafw and tathrīb together.

The poet says he pardoned them without reproaching or taunting them.

The second hemistich nevertheless speaks of leaving them to punishment.

Refraining from reproach in language does not therefore necessarily deny a wrong or negate every possible consequence.

Someone may refrain from directly censuring another while still regarding the past conduct as wrong.

Mutharrib here describes the person doing the reproaching, rather than the wrong itself.

The verse is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim. Some early sources, however, attribute it to Tubbaʿ, and the second hemistich has textual variants.

It can illuminate early usage of mutharrib, but its attribution and precise wording should not be presented as certain without acknowledging the source differences.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Bishr’s witness and alternative attribution to Tubbaʿ

Nuṣayb: hurtful speech and blame

A poem by Nuṣayb says:

إِنِّي لَأَكْرَهُ مَا كَرِهْتَ مِنَ الَّذِي
يُؤْذِيكَ سُوءُ ثَنَائِهِ لَمْ يَثْرِبِ

innī la-akrahu mā karihta mina alladhī / yuʾdhīka sūʾu thanāʾihi lam yathribi

A close rendering is: “I too dislike what you dislike, concerning one whose ill-speaking hurts you, without having reproached.” The transmitted wording and syntactic relationships require caution; lam yathrib alone establishes neither “not yet” nor “overtly.”

Here yathrib occurs alongside hurtful speech.

The use suggests a close connection between disparagement, condemnation, and reproach.

They do not all have the same meaning, however.

Someone may say something unpleasant about another without directly confronting them with a past wrong.

Tathrīb is generally closer to reproaching someone by recalling their conduct or wrongdoing.

Nuṣayb was an Umayyad poet. This is therefore evidence of classical Arabic after the period of Qur’anic revelation and should not be called pre-Islamic poetry.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Nuṣayb witnesses and muthrib

Reminding a recipient of a favour after giving

Another verse quoted in lexical explanations of athraba describes someone with possessions who gives little and then reminds the recipient of the favour.

In explaining this use, muthrib describes someone whose reminders of a gift place another in a position of shame or blame.

This use has social significance.

Reproach need not concern only an obvious offence: a person may use financial superiority or status to humiliate someone who needed help.

Mann and tathrīb must nevertheless be distinguished.

Reminding someone of a favour can be a particular form of reproaching conduct, but the words are not synonymous throughout the language.

Their distinction should also be preserved in Qur’anic study.

5. The setting of the sole clear Qur’anic derivative: Joseph and his brothers

Understanding lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawm requires attention to its setting.

Joseph’s brothers had previously made a wrongful decision concerning him.

Yūsuf 12:9 and Yūsuf 12:10 discuss removing him and separating him from his father.

In Yūsuf 12:15, they place him in the depths of the well.

In Yūsuf 12:16, Yūsuf 12:17, and Yūsuf 12:18, they return to their father weeping and present an untrue account of Joseph’s fate.

Their wrongdoing was therefore more than a simple family disagreement.

They contributed to Joseph’s separation from his father, the invented story, and the suffering that followed.

Later, circumstances change.

Joseph attains a position in which his brothers come to him for food and assistance, initially without recognising him.

Eventually, the time comes for his identity to be revealed.

Yūsuf 12:89

Verse text:

قَالَ هَلْ عَلِمْتُمْ مَا فَعَلْتُمْ بِيُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ إِذْ أَنْتُمْ جَاهِلُونَ

qāla hal ʿalimtum mā faʿaltum bi-Yūsufa wa-akhīhi idh antum jāhilūna

Translation:

“He said: Do you know what you did to Joseph and his brother when you were ignorant?”

This verse is highly relevant to understanding tathrīb.

Before saying lā tathrīb, Joseph asks the brothers about their past conduct.

He does not hide the truth or pretend that no wrong occurred.

He asks them to attend to what they did to Joseph and his brother.

The question can involve recalling wrongdoing and bringing the truth to light.

This reminder must nevertheless be distinguished from continuing reproach after admission.

If every reminder of wrongdoing necessarily constituted tathrīb, Joseph’s question would appear inconsistent with his subsequent statement.

One can instead distinguish confronting someone with the past truth from continuing blame and taunting after the truth emerges.

Yūsuf 12:91

Verse text:

قَالُوا تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَإِنْ كُنَّا لَخَاطِئِينَ

qālū ta-Allāhi la-qad ātharaka Allāhu ʿalaynā wa-in kunnā la-khāṭiʾīna

Translation:

“They said: By The God, The God has certainly preferred you over us, and we were indeed at fault.”

The brothers themselves acknowledge their wrongdoing here.

This admission matters for understanding Joseph’s reply.

He addresses people who now know the truth and regard their past conduct as wrong.

Lā tathrīb follows the disclosure of truth and admission of wrongdoing.

This sequence does not establish that every admission, in every matter and before every person, automatically removes all consequences of harmful conduct.

The narrative concerns Joseph’s particular treatment of his brothers.

It illustrates the value of forbearance and avoiding fruitless reproach; other people’s rights and separate responsibilities require their own relevant evidence.

6. Lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawm: Yūsuf 12:92

Verse text:

قَالَ لَا تَثْرِيبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْيَوْمَ ۖ يَغْفِرُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ ۖ وَهُوَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ

qāla lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawma yaghfiru Allāhu lakum wa-huwa arḥamu al-rāḥimīna

Translation:

“He said: There is no reproach or taunting against you today. May The God forgive you; He is the most merciful of the merciful.”

This verse contains the root’s only clear Qur’anic derivative.

Tathrība occurs in a sentence negating reproach against the brothers.

Since this follows their admission, its meaning should be considered alongside that admission and the rest of Joseph’s statement.

The meaning of tathrīb

Here tathrīb concerns reproach, rebuke, and taunting.

Al-Ṭabarī’s explanation presents three aspects: refraining from fault-finding, preserving the sanctity of brotherhood, and pardoning.

Thaʿlab likewise explains that their wrongs will not again be made the subject of condemnatory reminders.

In the report transmitted by Al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Isḥāq understands it as the absence of rebuke for their past conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī: reproach, pardon, and forbearance

These explanations connect tathrīb here with condemnatory censure over an acknowledged wrong.

Translation should not nevertheless compress all its consequences into a single word.

Lā tathrīb itself does not mean “complete restoration of trust,” “erasure of every responsibility,” or “removal of every harm.”

These are separate matters that may arise during reconciliation and repair.

The direct meaning is the absence of reproach and taunting.

The function of lā

In lā tathrība, lā is the particle negating the entire category.

Tathrīb is the noun governed by lā, built on fatḥah in the accusative position.

The construction negates the existence of reproach within the statement’s scope.

“There is no reproach against you” is therefore appropriate.

It differs from “Do not reproach.”

Joseph does not order his brothers not to reproach someone else; he speaks of reproach and censure against them in this situation.

The function of ʿalaykum

ʿAlaykum identifies those against whom the reproach would be directed.

Arabic ʿalā can occur in statements about responsibility, burdens, or something directed against a person.

Here reproach against the brothers is negated.

“There is no reproach against you” thus closely follows the construction.

In natural Persian, a wording equivalent to “No reproach or taunting is directed at you today” would also fit.

The meaning of al-yawm

Al-yawm here denotes today or the present time.

Does this adverb limit Joseph’s forbearance to one day?

In the common syntactic analysis, al-yawm relates to the statement negating tathrīb.

The wording thus directly states the absence of reproach in this situation.

Arabic and Persian can nevertheless use “today” to announce the beginning of a new state rather than its ending tomorrow.

For example, “From today I will no longer reproach you” does not ordinarily restrict that conduct to the same day.

Early explanations also suggest that al-yawm marks the beginning of an end to reproach, with forbearance continuing afterwards.

Conversely, al-yawm alone does not constitute an explicit verbal pledge concerning every future situation.

The translation should therefore retain “today,” while explanation can note that the reconciliation context and Joseph’s subsequent conduct support an end to continued reproach.

Al-Qurṭubī: today and the beginning of an end to reproach

Does lā tathrīb deny the offence?

No.

In the preceding verse, the brothers admit being at fault.

Joseph has also already recalled their conduct.

Lā tathrīb does not therefore deny the past.

This is one of the root’s most important semantic boundaries.

A wrong may actually have occurred while the injured person gives up continued taunting and blame.

Approving an action and refraining from reproaching its agent are different matters.

Joseph’s restraint does not endorse his brothers’ conduct; it shows that after the truth emerged and they admitted the wrong, he chose another response.

7. Reproach, forbearance, and a request for forgiveness

After lā tathrīb, the same verse continues:

«يَغْفِرُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ»

yaghfiru Allāhu lakum

May The God forgive you.

This phrase derives from the independent root GH F R.

In the common analysis, Joseph prays for his brothers’ forgiveness.

The verse thus presents two related but distinct matters.

First is Joseph’s conduct towards his brothers.

Second is requesting their forgiveness from The God.

Does Joseph himself confer divine forgiveness?

Yaghfiru Allāhu lakum is constructed with an imperfect verb whose subject is Allāh.

The God is the one to whom the forgiving is attributed.

If understood as a prayer, its natural rendering is “May The God forgive you.”

This reading accords with the context and early explanations.

Al-Ṭabarī: prayer for the brothers’ forgiveness

The sentence does not say that Joseph has independent authority to erase his brothers’ sins before The God.

He refrains from personal reproach and speaks of divine forgiveness.

These should not be conflated.

Someone may waive a personal claim, while judgment of every aspect of conduct and responsibility before The God remains a separate matter.

The difference from Jacob’s statement

Later, the brothers also acknowledge their wrongdoing before their father.

Yūsuf 12:97

Verse text:

قَالُوا يَا أَبَانَا اسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا خَاطِئِينَ

qālū yā abānā istaghfir lanā dhunūbanā innā kunnā khāṭiʾīna

Translation:

“They said: Our father, ask forgiveness for our sins; we were indeed at fault.”

Yūsuf 12:98

Verse text:

قَالَ سَوْفَ أَسْتَغْفِرُ لَكُمْ رَبِّي ۖ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

qāla sawfa astaghfiru lakum rabbī innahu huwa al-ghafūru al-raḥīmu

Translation:

“He said: I will soon ask my Lord to forgive you; He is indeed the Forgiving, the Merciful.”

The brothers ask Jacob in these two verses to seek forgiveness for them from The God.

Jacob says he will do so.

The request resembles Joseph’s statement, but the two situations have different constructions.

Joseph refrains from reproaching his brothers and asks divine forgiveness for them.

Before Jacob, the brothers explicitly request istighfār.

Tathrīb itself should therefore not be treated as synonymous with istighfār or ghufrān.

Tathrīb concerns reproach.

Istighfār denotes requesting forgiveness from The God.

Ghufrān is a separate subject to be examined in the study of GH F R.

Does the verse make repairing harm unimportant?

Refraining from reproach is compatible with accepting responsibility and repairing harm.

Someone suffering financial harm may refrain from humiliating the person responsible while still seeking the return of their property.

Where a false statement has damaged someone’s reputation, forbearance from taunting can coexist with correcting that statement.

This distinction has ethical and social significance.

Yūsuf 12:92 does not, however, provide a catalogue of general rules for repairing harm.

Its direct subject is Joseph’s treatment of his brothers in that situation.

Forbearance cannot therefore be invoked to erase all legal responsibilities, nor responsibility invoked to justify endless humiliation and reproach.

Each matter requires its own relevant evidence.

8. Yathrib: a place name and the question of derivation

Al-Aḥzāb 33:13 contains the name Yathrib.

Historical and lexical sources relate it to the area in which Medina was situated.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:13

Verse text:

وَإِذْ قَالَتْ طَائِفَةٌ مِنْهُمْ يَا أَهْلَ يَثْرِبَ لَا مُقَامَ لَكُمْ فَارْجِعُوا ۚ وَيَسْتَأْذِنُ فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمُ النَّبِيَّ يَقُولُونَ إِنَّ بُيُوتَنَا عَوْرَةٌ وَمَا هِيَ بِعَوْرَةٍ ۖ إِنْ يُرِيدُونَ إِلَّا فِرَارًا

wa-idh qālat ṭāʾifatun minhum yā ahla Yathriba lā muqāma lakum fa-irjiʿū wa-yastaʾdhinu farīqun minhumu al-nabiyya yaqūlūna inna buyūtanā ʿawratun wa-mā hiya bi-ʿawratin in yurīdūna illā firāran

Translation:

“When a group of them said: People of Yathrib, there is no place for you to remain, so return. A group of them sought the Prophet’s permission, saying: Our houses are exposed, although they were not exposed. They wanted only to flee.”

Yathrib is a place name here.

It should not be translated as “he reproaches.”

Morphological analysis recognises it as a proper name.

Does Yathrib derive from TH R B?

Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī suggests that Yathrib may belong to this family, with its initial yāʾ added.

Some historical accounts also relate the name to a person who formerly lived in the region.

These accounts do not establish a certain naming history.

A proper name’s resemblance to a familiar Arabic root does not suffice to prove derivation.

Even a place name originally drawn from an ordinary word can become a proper name that no longer directly expresses that word’s meaning.

Qur’anic study should therefore state two matters separately.

First, tathrīb in Yūsuf 12:92 is the root’s only clear Qur’anic derivative.

Second, Yathrib in Al-Aḥzāb 33:13 is a place name whose derivational connection with this root remains uncertain and open to study.

Al-Rāghib and lexical sources: a proposed derivation of Yathrib

The shared letters alone do not establish that the Qur’an intends reproach, corruption, or fault-finding through the name Yathrib.

Nor does its occurrence in the speech of a condemned group establish that the name itself necessarily has a negative lexical meaning.

The condemnation of their conduct comes from context, rather than an unproven meaning of the place name.

9. Related roots and words

TH R B and L W M

Study of L W M

Lawm is among the closest words to tathrīb.

Both concern reproach and blame.

Lexical explanations of tathrīb, however, foreground recalling wrongdoing and calling a person to account for it.

Lawm can have a broader range, condemning or reproaching conduct without necessarily repeating a past offence or humiliating the person.

An absolute boundary should not be imposed whereby every tathrīb is severe and every lawm mild.

Early sources directly explain tathrīb through lawm.

They thus share a semantic field, while established uses of tathrīb especially associate it with condemnatory censure of a wrong.

TH R B and ʿ Y R

Study of ʿ Y R

Taʿyīr concerns fault-finding and taunting over a person’s fault or conduct.

Lisān al-ʿArab and exegetical explanations gloss tathrīb through taʿyīr.

This proximity matters.

Someone may repeatedly recall another’s past wrongdoing to shame them.

Such conduct can be both taʿyīr and tathrīb.

Taʿyīr may especially emphasise exposing a fault and humiliating someone, whereas tathrīb also concerns blame and censure over wrongdoing.

TH R B and W B KH

Tawbīkh concerns severe reproach and explicit rebuke.

As transmitted in early sources, Ibn al-Aʿrābī explains tharaba, tharraba, and athraba through wabbakha.

Tathrīb can therefore be tawbīkh.

Not every rebuke necessarily concerns repeating an old wrong, however.

Someone may act wrongly at that moment and be rebuked for it.

Joseph’s verse has the particular setting of a past wrong no longer becoming the subject of continued reproach after admission.

TH R B and ʿ T B

Study of ʿ T B

ʿAtb concerns dissatisfaction, complaint, and blame.

Someone hurt by another’s conduct may express that hurt through ʿitāb.

Tathrīb can also accompany ʿitāb.

Some uses of ʿitāb express complaint while seeking to repair a relationship, whereas tathrīb can convey more severe censure over someone’s wrongdoing.

Their relative intensity is not fixed in every sentence; context is decisive.

TH R B and ʿ F W

Study of ʿ F W

ʿAfw concerns pardoning and waiving censure or a particular consequence.

Tathrīb concerns reproach and blame.

The poem attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim combines them: pardon without tathrīb.

In Joseph’s narrative, avoiding reproach likewise belongs to a setting of reconciliation and forbearance.

The words do not have identical meanings, however.

ʿAfw can concern waiving a claim or punishment; refraining from tathrīb concerns giving up blame and taunting.

Someone may outwardly waive a claim while repeatedly humiliating another through reminders of the past wrong.

Waiving a claim and refraining from reproach must therefore be distinguished.

TH R B and Ṣ F Ḥ

Study of Ṣ F Ḥ

Ṣafḥ concerns turning away from a wrong and passing over it.

Al-Ṭabarī’s explanation of Yūsuf 12:92 places ṣafḥ alongside ʿafw.

Refraining from tathrīb can thus form part of forgiving conduct.

Ṣafḥ is nevertheless an independent word requiring study through its own Qur’anic root occurrences.

Translation of lā tathrīb should preferably preserve reproach and taunting, while explanation can address the contextual forbearance.

TH R B and GH F R

Study of GH F R

Ghafr concerns forgiveness and, within its semantic field, covering and passing over a wrong.

Yūsuf 12:92 contains both tathrīb and forgiveness.

They play different roles, however.

Joseph says there is no reproach against the brothers.

He then speaks of divine forgiveness.

Refraining from human reproach should therefore not automatically be equated with divine forgiveness.

TH R B and B H T

Study of B H T

In accusation-related usage, buhtān denotes a false statement and wrongful allegation that distorts the truth.

Tathrīb concerns reproaching.

A person may reproach and humiliate another through an invented accusation, combining buhtān and tathrīb in one act.

Someone may also be reproached for an actual wrong. That reproach need not be buhtān, although its intensity, timing, or manner may be inappropriate.

The falsity of an accusation and the manner of blame are separate matters.

TH R B and TH B R

Study of TH B R

TH B R is a different root whose Qur’anic uses concern destruction and ruin.

Qur’anic TH R B concerns reproach and censure.

Their similar letters must not lead to conflating their meanings.

In Al-Furqān and Al-Inshiqāq, thubūr names the destruction being cried out; the cry is understood from the verb and context.

Tathrīb in Yūsuf means reproach and taunting.

Lā tathrīb does not therefore mean “you will not perish”; it negates reproach and blame in that situation.

10. Proper inquiry and fruitless taunting

The Qur’anic use illustrates an important distinction in human conduct.

Sometimes a wrong needs to be brought to light.

When someone has harmed another, repairing the situation may require establishing what happened, who was responsible, and what needs repair.

Stating the truth and examining responsibility can then be necessary.

After the wrong is clear and responsibility accepted, continued humiliation and taunting do not necessarily assist repair.

Joseph’s narrative clearly places these two stages together.

First Joseph asks what the brothers did to him and his brother.

They then acknowledge their wrongdoing.

Joseph then declares there is no reproach against them.

Ending tathrīb does not therefore necessarily remove the stage of establishing the truth.

Nor does the story establish that an injured person must immediately restore the former relationship with the same trust in every circumstance.

Trust, safety, repairing harm, and continuing a relationship are separate matters.

Refraining from reproach can coexist with caution about future conduct.

In other circumstances, trust may gradually be rebuilt.

TH R B directly conveys reproach and condemnatory censure. Questions of pardon, repair, and rebuilding relationships require the fuller evidence relevant to those subjects.

11. Proposed equivalents for Forqan

For tharaba concerning wrongdoing, “reproached,” “blamed,” or “censured for a wrong” fits.

For tharraba, “reproached,” “taunted,” or “confronted someone with their wrongdoing” can fit.

Where intense blame is prominent, “reproached severely” is possible; intensity should not be added to every use without evidence.

For athraba concerning reproach, “rebuked” or “taunted” is appropriate.

Thārib can be rendered “one who reproaches”; mutharrib as “one who taunts” or “a reproachful fault-finder.”

The main equivalents for tathrīb are “reproach,” “blame,” and “taunting.”

Physical tharb can be rendered “a thin layer of fat around the internal organs.”

That physical branch is not present in the Qur’anic occurrence and should not enter the translation of Yūsuf 12:92.

Proposed translation of the Qur’anic occurrence

Yūsuf 12:92

Phrase:

«لَا تَثْرِيبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْيَوْمَ»

lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawma

There is no reproach or taunting against you today.

Proposed translation:

“There is no reproach or taunting against you today.”

This rendering preserves several features.

First, lā expresses negation of reproach.

Second, tathrīb retains its established meaning of blame and condemnatory censure.

Third, ʿalaykum identifies the people against whom reproach would be directed.

Fourth, al-yawm retains its place.

For the whole verse, the proposed rendering is:

“He said: There is no reproach or taunting against you today. May The God forgive you; He is the most merciful of the merciful.”

Equivalents requiring caution

“You committed no offence” is unsuitable, since the brothers admit their wrongdoing in the preceding verse.

“All your obligations and responsibilities have lapsed” also exceeds the lexical meaning of tathrīb.

“We will never speak about the past again” may interpret Joseph’s forgiving orientation, but this entire temporal commitment is not explicit in the wording.

“I forgive you” is understandable as an overall message, but replacing lā tathrīb with it alone removes the distinction between pardon and refraining from reproach.

“There is no reproach or taunting against you” both preserves the wording and conveys the Persian naturally and clearly.

12. Conclusion

Arabic TH R B has two established branches.

In the first, tathrīb concerns reproaching, blaming, and censuring someone for a wrong or improper conduct.

In the second, tharb names a thin layer of fat covering certain internal organs of an animal.

Ibn Fāris treats these as having distinct bases. Although some lexicographers connect physical covering with stripping away the covering of reputation, that derivation is insufficiently established to define all the uses fundamentally.

In Arabic poetic witnesses, mutharrib describes someone reproaching and finding fault with another.

The poem attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim combines pardon with avoiding tathrīb. Nuṣayb’s poetry likewise associates this family with hurtful speech and blame.

Only tathrība in Yūsuf 12:92 occurs as a clear Qur’anic derivative.

The word comes at the close of Joseph’s encounter with his brothers.

They had harmed him, separated him from his father, and invented an untrue account of his fate.

At the narrative’s close, Joseph recalls the past truth and they admit their wrongdoing.

He then declares that there is no reproach or taunting against them and asks divine forgiveness for them.

Refraining from tathrīb is thus distinct from denying a wrong.

It also does not mean that every responsibility or need to repair harm disappears in every situation.

The verse nevertheless shows that, once truth is clear and wrongdoing acknowledged, an injured person can refrain from continued blame and humiliation.

Final definition in Qur’anic usage: TH R B in its branch concerning human conduct denotes reproach and condemnatory censure for wrongdoing, particularly when the wrong is recalled and the person subjected to fault-finding or taunting.

Tathrīb names this act; Yūsuf 12:92 declares its absence towards the brothers.

The distinctive feature is blame and confronting someone reproachfully with a wrong, rather than merely knowing the truth, every discussion of responsibility, or forgiveness and pardon themselves.

“Reproach and taunting” is therefore the most suitable basic equivalent for tathrīb.

Lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawm directly states that there is no reproach or taunting against the brothers today; the broader narrative supplies the setting of pardon, admission of wrongdoing, and requested divine forgiveness.

Yathrib in Al-Aḥzāb 33:13 must likewise be examined as a proper name. Its derivational connection with TH R B is uncertain, and reproach should not be attributed to it without evidence.
