The Qur’anic Root F T N

Fatana: trial, pressure, and exposure to a change of direction

The root ف ت ن, F T N, has a broad, layered family in the Qur’an. Its forms occur in fields including trial, pressure and persecution, deception and diversion, social disturbance, and punishment. This breadth also underlies the many meanings fitnah acquired in later periods, from trial and adversity to civil war and political turmoil.

None of these meanings alone, however, defines the Qur’anic root.

In the Qur’an, wealth and children can be fitnah; good and evil can be fitnah; people can be fitnah for one another; someone can subject another person to fitnah and seek to turn them from their course; and in some ayahs, fatana is directly associated with fire and punishment.

A core that brings a substantial portion of these uses together is:

Placing someone, or being placed, in a situation of trial, pressure, or influence in which human steadfastness, direction, and response are put to the test, and a person may turn from their course.

This definition deliberately avoids reducing fitnah to “testing” alone. In some ayahs, testing is central; in others, pressure and persecution intended to redirect someone; and in still others, punishment itself.

The root also has no fixed moral value. The Qur’an explicitly says that both evil and good can be a trial. Fitnah is therefore not inherently “evil”: it is something in relation to which a person is tested or influenced.

Fire and testing: an old image, not a definition of every use

Arabic attests the expression فَتَنَ الذَّهَبَ بِالنَّارِ, fatana al-dhahaba bi-l-nār, for putting gold into fire to assess its quality. The metal is exposed to heat, and its quality becomes apparent under those conditions.

This image helps explain the proximity of “testing,” “pressure,” and “fire” within the family. Something has a certain condition under ordinary circumstances; when exposed to difficult conditions, what it actually produces becomes apparent.

Nevertheless, metalworking cannot confidently be declared the definitive historical origin of every meaning of F T N. The lexical use is real, but does not by itself establish the root’s earliest history.

The Qur’an itself also uses the branch involving fire:

يَوْمَ هُمْ عَلَى النَّارِ يُفْتَنُونَ ۝ ذُوقُوا فِتْنَتَكُمْ هَٰذَا الَّذِي كُنْتُمْ بِهِ تَسْتَعْجِلُونَ

Yawma hum ʿalā n-nāri yuftanūn. Dhūqū fitnatakum hādhā lladhī kuntum bihi tastaʿjilūn.

“On the day they are subjected to fitnah upon the Fire: ‘Taste your fitnah. This is what you used to seek to hasten.’”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:13–14

Here يُفْتَنُونَ, yuftanūn, approaches being punished and burned upon the Fire. The connection between fatana and fire is therefore not merely an external metaphor: the Qur’an itself preserves this branch.

But not every fitnah is fire. Wealth, children, good, evil, and other people are also called fitnah. The image of fire helps explain one branch, rather than defining every use.

Fitnah as trial

One of the clearest uses occurs at the beginning of Al-ʿAnkabūt:

أَحَسِبَ النَّاسُ أَنْ يُتْرَكُوا أَنْ يَقُولُوا آمَنَّا وَهُمْ لَا يُفْتَنُونَ ۝ وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ فَلَيَعْلَمَنَّ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا وَلَيَعْلَمَنَّ الْكَاذِبِينَ

A-ḥasiba n-nāsu an yutrakū an yaqūlū āmannā wa-hum lā yuftanūn. Wa-laqad fatannā lladhīna min qablihim fa-la-yaʿlamanna llāhu lladhīna ṣadaqū wa-la-yaʿlamanna l-kādhibīn.

“Do people suppose they will be left simply to say, ‘We believe,’ without being tested? We certainly tested those before them; The God will certainly know those who have spoken truthfully, and He will certainly know the liars.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:2–3

The context distinguishes a spoken claim from undergoing conditions of trial. Saying آمنا, āmannā, “we believe,” is not the end of the matter: a situation arises in which the truth or falsehood of that claim takes practical form in human behavior.

This need not be understood as meaning that The God did not know something before the trial and acquired knowledge afterward. At the level of human life, fitnah brings a person’s claim into the sphere of choice and action.

Ṭā Hā also says of Mūsā:

وَقَتَلْتَ نَفْسًا فَنَجَّيْنَاكَ مِنَ الْغَمِّ وَفَتَنَّاكَ فُتُونًا

Wa-qatalta nafsan fa-najjaynāka mina l-ghammi wa-fatannāka futūnā.

“You killed a person, and We rescued you from distress and put you to the test.”

Ṭā Hā 20:40

In this context, Mūsā’s life includes a series of difficult stages. In one analysis, فُتُونًا, futūnan, is a verbal noun functioning as an emphatic cognate accusative, reinforcing the act of testing. An analysis as a plural of fitnah, referring to various trials, is also recorded. Multiple trials thus fit the context, but neither a specific number nor a particular intensity should be inferred from the form alone.

Fatana also approaches testing in the accounts of Dāwūd in Ṣād 38:24 and Sulaymān in Ṣād 38:34:

وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ

Wa-ẓanna Dāwūdu annamā fatannāh.

And:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ

Wa-laqad fatannā Sulaymān.

One of the most important ayahs for defining fitnah’s boundaries, however, is:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ ۗ وَنَبْلُوكُمْ بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً ۖ وَإِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ

Kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu l-mawti wa-nablūkum bi-sh-sharri wa-l-khayri fitnatan wa-ilaynā turjaʿūn.

“Every soul will taste death. We test you with evil and good as a trial, and to Us you will be returned.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35

The Qur’an explicitly places evil and good together.

A blessing can therefore be a setting for trial just as hardship can. The ayah does not by itself support a psychological theory that evil necessarily makes a person “contract” while good makes them “complacent”; human responses vary. The linguistic point is simpler and stronger:

Fitnah is not determined by whether something is desirable or undesirable. Something can be good and still serve as a trial for a person.

The same is said of wealth and children:

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَأَوْلَادُكُمْ فِتْنَةٌ

Wa-ʿlamū annamā amwālukum wa-awlādukum fitnah.

“Know that your wealth and your children are a trial.”

Al-Anfāl 8:28

And:

إِنَّمَا أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَأَوْلَادُكُمْ فِتْنَةٌ

Innamā amwālukum wa-awlādukum fitnah.

Al-Taghābun 64:15

These ayahs do not say that wealth and children are bad. They can provide situations in which human choice and direction are tested.

Even people can play this role for one another:

وَجَعَلْنَا بَعْضَكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ فِتْنَةً أَتَصْبِرُونَ

Wa-jaʿalnā baʿḍakum li-baʿḍin fitnatan a-taṣbirūn.

“We have made some of you a trial for others. Will you be patient?”

Al-Furqān 25:20

The question أَتَصْبِرُونَ, a-taṣbirūn, “will you be patient?”, shows that the human response to this situation matters.

In this branch, fitnah therefore names no particular object: it is the role a situation comes to play in testing a person.

When trial becomes pressure and persecution

In some ayahs, fitnah is more than the general conditions of testing: another human being deliberately applies pressure.

Al-Naḥl states:

ثُمَّ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لِلَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا فُتِنُوا ثُمَّ جَاهَدُوا وَصَبَرُوا

Thumma inna rabbaka li-lladhīna hājarū min baʿdi mā futinū thumma jāhadū wa-ṣabarū.

“Then your Lord, toward those who emigrated after being subjected to persecution, then strove and remained patient …”

Al-Naḥl 16:110

Emigration and patience show that فُتِنُوا, futinū, here involves serious pressure.

In Yūnus:

عَلَىٰ خَوْفٍ مِنْ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَلَئِهِمْ أَنْ يَفْتِنَهُمْ

ʿAlā khawfin min Firʿawna wa-malaʾihim an yaftinahum.

“In fear of Pharaoh and their chiefs, lest he subject them to persecution.”

Yūnus 10:83

The agent of fitnah here is a human power.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt, the ayah itself makes the connection between fitnah and harm explicit:

فَإِذَا أُوذِيَ فِي اللَّهِ جَعَلَ فِتْنَةَ النَّاسِ كَعَذَابِ اللَّهِ

Fa-idhā ūdhiya fī llāhi jaʿala fitnata n-nāsi ka-ʿadhābi llāh.

“When he is harmed for the sake of The God, he treats people’s persecution as though it were The God’s punishment.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:10

Before فتنة الناس, fitnat al-nās, the ayah explicitly says أُوذِيَ, ūdhiya, “he was harmed.” In this context, “people’s fitnah” therefore concerns pressure and harm that can target a person’s steadfastness.

Al-Burūj likewise states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَتَنُوا الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا فَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ الْحَرِيقِ

Inna lladhīna fatanū l-muʾminīna wa-l-muʾmināti thumma lam yatūbū fa-lahum ʿadhābu jahannama wa-lahum ʿadhābu l-ḥarīq.

“Those who persecuted believing men and women and then did not repent will have the punishment of Jahannam and the punishment of burning.”

Al-Burūj 85:10

The preceding context concerns أصحاب الأخدود, aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd, the people of the trench, and fire. Here فَتَنُوا, fatanū, can therefore activate the field of persecution and torture while overlapping with the branch involving fire.

It is better not to translate it solely as “burned” or solely as “tested” without justification. The context itself concerns severe persecution of the believers.

Al-fitnatu ashaddu mina l-qatl: a context that must not be removed

Two well-known expressions occur in Al-Baqarah:

وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ

Wa-l-fitnatu ashaddu mina l-qatl.

“Fitnah is more severe than killing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:191

And:

وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ

Wa-l-fitnatu akbaru mina l-qatl.

“Fitnah is graver than killing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:217

Removing these expressions from their context opens the way to very different meanings, from “political turmoil” to “any disagreement in belief.” The ayahs themselves, however, supply further elements.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 states:

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا

Wa-qātilū fī sabīli llāhi lladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū.

“Fight in The God’s way those who fight you, and do not transgress.”

Then:

وَأَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَخْرَجُوكُمْ

Wa-akhrijūhum min ḥaythu akhrajūkum.

“Expel them from where they expelled you.”

And afterward:

وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ

Wa-l-fitnatu ashaddu mina l-qatl.

Fitnah thus appears within a setting of warfare, expulsion, and pressure.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 is still more explicit:

وَصَدٌّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَكُفْرٌ بِهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَإِخْرَاجُ أَهْلِهِ مِنْهُ أَكْبَرُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ ۚ وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ

Wa-ṣaddun ʿan sabīli llāhi wa-kufrun bihi wa-l-masjidi l-ḥarāmi wa-ikhrāju ahlihi minhu akbaru ʿinda llāhi wa-l-fitnatu akbaru mina l-qatl.

“Obstructing The God’s way, rejecting Him, [obstructing access to] the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people from it are graver before The God; and fitnah is graver than killing.”

The same ayah continues:

وَلَا يَزَالُونَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَرُدُّوكُمْ عَنْ دِينِكُمْ إِنِ اسْتَطَاعُوا

Wa-lā yazālūna yuqātilūnakum ḥattā yaruddūkum ʿan dīnikum ini staṭāʿū.

“They will continue fighting you until they turn you back from your dīn, if they can.”

This continuation clarifies fitnah’s field: pressure, warfare, expulsion, and attempts to turn people back from their dīn—their religious allegiance and way—are present in the same context.

In this passage, fitnah therefore cannot simply be translated as “unrest.” “Persecution and pressure intended to turn people from their religion” is closer to the context.

Yet the ayah should not generate a theory such as “killing destroys the body, but fitnah destroys the soul.” The Qur’an gives no such philosophical division. The expression states the text’s own judgment: this kind of fitnah, in this context, is more severe or graver than killing.

Nor should “shirk” become the fixed, comprehensive definition of fitnah. Older interpretations of these ayahs do explain fitnah as shirk, associating partners with The God; this is a contextual interpretive reading, not an equivalent applicable to every occurrence of F T N. In this article’s reading, pressure to turn people from their religion is more prominent in the sequence of ayahs.

The same caution is necessary with:

وَقَاتِلُوهُمْ حَتَّىٰ لَا تَكُونَ فِتْنَةٌ وَيَكُونَ الدِّينُ لِلَّهِ

Wa-qātilūhum ḥattā lā takūna fitnatun wa-yakūna d-dīnu li-llāh.

Al-Baqarah 2:193

And:

وَقَاتِلُوهُمْ حَتَّىٰ لَا تَكُونَ فِتْنَةٌ وَيَكُونَ الدِّينُ كُلُّهُ لِلَّهِ

Wa-qātilūhum ḥattā lā takūna fitnatun wa-yakūna d-dīnu kulluhu li-llāh.

Al-Anfāl 8:39

The meaning of الدين لله, al-dīnu li-llāh, “dīn belongs to The God,” must be examined in its own context. The word fitnah alone cannot yield an unrestricted ruling to fight every different belief.

Fitnah as diverting someone from their course

In some ayahs, the preposition عن, ʿan, “away from,” makes the action’s direction particularly clear.

Al-Māʾidah states:

وَاحْذَرْهُمْ أَنْ يَفْتِنُوكَ عَنْ بَعْضِ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ إِلَيْكَ

Wa-ḥdharhum an yaftinūka ʿan baʿḍi mā anzala llāhu ilayk.

“Beware of them, lest they divert you from part of what The God has sent down to you.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:49

Here:

يَفْتِنُوكَ عَنْ

Yaftinūka ʿan.

is a construction for turning someone away from something.

Al-Isrāʾ similarly states:

وَإِنْ كَادُوا لَيَفْتِنُونَكَ عَنِ الَّذِي أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ

Wa-in kādū la-yaftinūnaka ʿani lladhī awḥaynā ilayk.

“They almost diverted you from what We revealed to you.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:73

In this use, fatana therefore means more than testing: it involves an effort to change someone’s direction.

There is no need to compare this relationship to a “gravitational field” or an “orbit.” The preposition ʿan is clear enough: a person stands in relation to something, and an attempt is made to turn them away from it.

Al-Aʿrāf also states:

يَا بَنِي آدَمَ لَا يَفْتِنَنَّكُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ كَمَا أَخْرَجَ أَبَوَيْكُمْ مِنَ الْجَنَّةِ

Yā banī Ādama lā yaftinannakumu sh-shayṭānu ka-mā akhraja abawaykum mina l-jannah.

“Children of Ādam, do not let Satan lead you astray, as he drove your parents out of the Garden.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:27

Here deception and a change of direction are present in the semantic field.

Al-Ṣāffāt likewise states:

مَا أَنْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ بِفَاتِنِينَ ۝ إِلَّا مَنْ هُوَ صَالِ الْجَحِيمِ

Mā antum ʿalayhi bi-fātinīn. Illā man huwa ṣāli l-jaḥīm.

“You cannot lead anyone astray [toward what you worship], except one who will burn in the blazing Fire.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:162–163

فاتنين, fātinīn, approaches exerting influence to draw someone away and mislead them. There are different analyses of the pronoun’s referent in عليه, ʿalayhi. The rendering above chooses its connection with ما تعبدون, mā taʿbudūn, “what you worship,” in the preceding ayah; this should not be treated as the only possible analysis.

One important branch of F T N is therefore:

Subjecting someone to an influence that may change their direction.

Social fitnah and the later sense of turmoil

Al-Tawbah states:

لَوْ خَرَجُوا فِيكُمْ مَا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا وَلَأَوْضَعُوا خِلَالَكُمْ يَبْغُونَكُمُ الْفِتْنَةَ

Law kharajū fīkum mā zādūkum illā khabālan wa-la-awḍaʿū khilālakum yabghūnakumu l-fitnah.

“Had they gone out with you, they would have added only disorder and hurried about among you, seeking fitnah for you.”

Al-Tawbah 9:47

And:

لَقَدِ ابْتَغَوُا الْفِتْنَةَ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَقَلَّبُوا لَكَ الْأُمُورَ

Laqadi btaghawu l-fitnata min qablu wa-qallabū laka l-umūr.

“They had already sought fitnah before and turned matters upside down for you.”

Al-Tawbah 9:48

In such a context, fitnah approaches creating division, disorder, and instability within a community.

Al-Anfāl also says:

وَاتَّقُوا فِتْنَةً لَا تُصِيبَنَّ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْكُمْ خَاصَّةً

Wa-ttaqū fitnatan lā tuṣībanna lladhīna ẓalamū minkum khāṣṣah.

“Guard against a fitnah that will not strike only those among you who have acted unjustly.”

Al-Anfāl 8:25

Here fitnah can have a widespread social consequence.

These uses overlap with the familiar meaning of fitnah as political turmoil and civil war. The precise historical course by which that meaning developed, however, cannot be established from these ayahs alone.

The later meaning should not be read back into every Qur’anic ayah. Wealth, children, good, evil, and persecution targeting a person’s faith are all expressed through this family.

Thus:

Social turmoil is one branch of usage, not the definition of the root.

Fire, punishment, and the outcome of fitnah

Al-Dhāriyāt states:

يَوْمَ هُمْ عَلَى النَّارِ يُفْتَنُونَ

Yawma hum ʿalā n-nāri yuftanūn.

“On the day they are punished upon the Fire.”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:13

Then, in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:14:

ذُوقُوا فِتْنَتَكُمْ

Dhūqū fitnatakum.

“Taste your fitnah.”

Here fitnah has reached the sense of punishment.

In Al-Burūj 85:4–5, the setting of fire is also clear:

قُتِلَ أَصْحَابُ الْأُخْدُودِ ۝ النَّارِ ذَاتِ الْوَقُودِ

Qutila aṣḥābu l-ukhdūd. An-nāri dhāti l-waqūd.

Then, in Al-Burūj 85:10:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَتَنُوا الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ

Inna lladhīna fatanū l-muʾminīna wa-l-muʾmināt.

The proximity of these words allows the burning branch to be taken seriously here, without restricting فَتَنُوا, fatanū, to “burned” without justification. Persecution and pressure against believers are also present in the surah’s construction.

Fire is therefore a real part of the family’s field, but does not govern every use of fitnah.

Uses that rule out a single fixed translation

Al-Anʿām states:

ثُمَّ لَمْ تَكُنْ فِتْنَتُهُمْ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا وَاللَّهِ رَبِّنَا مَا كُنَّا مُشْرِكِينَ

Thumma lam takun fitnatuhum illā an qālū wa-llāhi rabbinā mā kunnā mushrikīn.

“Then their fitnah was nothing but that they said: ‘By The God, our Lord, we were not people who associated partners with Him.’”

Al-Anʿām 6:23

Immediately beforehand, they are questioned about the partners they used to claim. In this setting of interrogation, their answer is what emerges from them in the fitnah.

For this reason, فتنتهم, fitnatuhum, has sometimes been explained as their “plea,” “excuse,” or “answer.” These explanations are recorded in the interpretive tradition and should not be denied. Connecting them with the outcome of trial and interrogation is a semantic analysis: the response that becomes apparent from them in that situation, not proof that fitnah means “answer” in every use.

The contextual outcome should therefore remain distinct from the root’s meaning.

The same caution is also needed with:

بِأَيِّكُمُ الْمَفْتُونُ

Bi-ayyikumu l-maftūn.

Al-Qalam 68:6

Maftūn occurs in the context of an accusation of madness. In one analysis, it is a passive participle describing someone afflicted by trial or disturbance; in another, it is a verbal noun on the passive-participle pattern, understood as fitnah or madness. This disagreement also affects the analysis of بأيكم, bi-ayyikum. Neither one morphological analysis nor the equation of maftūn with “mad” in every use should therefore be treated as certain.

F T N (fitnah) and B L W (balāʾ): two overlapping fields of trial

Fitnah and balāʾ overlap in the field of testing; the Qur’an even places them in a single construction:

وَنَبْلُوكُمْ بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً

Wa-nablūkum bi-sh-sharri wa-l-khayri fitnah.

“We test you with evil and good as a trial.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35

نبلوكم, nablūkum, expresses the act of testing. In a common grammatical analysis, فتنة, fitnatan, is an accusative verbal noun, or cognate accusative, drawn from the meaning of that verb: it explains and reinforces the testing. The construction itself therefore does not establish a fixed distinction between an “act of testing” and a “situation of testing.”

The Qur’an itself supplies another witness to this overlap:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ

Wa-laqad fatannā lladhīna min qablihim.

Fatana, too, can therefore directly mean testing.

A difference in tendency may be stated as follows:

Balāʾ places greater emphasis on exposure to a trial;

Whereas fitnah has greater scope for pressure, attraction, deception, or influence that can challenge a person’s direction during the trial.

The two fields nevertheless overlap in many uses, and no absolute boundary should be drawn between them.

Translation proposals for Forqan

No single fixed equivalent suits fitnah.

For testing:

وَهُمْ لَا يُفْتَنُونَ

Wa-hum lā yuftanūn.

“While they are not tested.”

And:

أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَأَوْلَادُكُمْ فِتْنَةٌ

Amwālukum wa-awlādukum fitnah.

“Your wealth and children are occasions for trial.”

For pressure and persecution:

مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا فُتِنُوا

Min baʿdi mā futinū.

“After they had been subjected to pressure and persecution.”

And:

فِتْنَةَ النَّاسِ

Fitnata n-nās.

“People’s persecution and pressure.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:191 and 2:217, depending on the translation of the complete passage:

“Pressure and persecution intended to turn people from their religion”

is closer to the context than generic “unrest.”

For diversion:

أَنْ يَفْتِنُوكَ عَنْ بَعْضِ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ

An yaftinūka ʿan baʿḍi mā anzala llāh.

“Lest they divert you from part of what The God has sent down.”

And:

لَا يَفْتِنَنَّكُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ

Lā yaftinannakumu sh-shayṭān.

“Do not let Satan deceive you and turn you from your course.”

For punishment:

يَوْمَ هُمْ عَلَى النَّارِ يُفْتَنُونَ

Yawma hum ʿalā n-nāri yuftanūn.

“On the day they are punished upon the Fire.”

And in social uses, depending on the construction:

“Disorder,” “division,” or “fitnah”

may be suitable.

Retaining fitnah in every ayah is not a precise solution either: English readers may find it opaque, or associate the borrowed term primarily with turmoil and political conflict, whereas many ayahs concern trial, pressure, or diversion.

Conclusion

In the Qur’an, F T N moves through the field of trial, pressure, and influence. A person or community enters conditions in which their steadfastness and direction are tested; sometimes another human deliberately applies that pressure to turn them from their course. Testing metal in fire is an image compatible with this field, and the fire branch appears in the Qur’an itself, but it should not be presented as the certain origin of every use.

Fitnah is not inherently evil, unrest, shirk, or civil war. Good and evil, wealth and children, and other people can all be fitnah. Conversely, fatana can also involve persecution, deception, and diversion. In الفتنة أكبر من القتل, al-fitnatu akbaru mina l-qatl, the context itself speaks of obstruction, expulsion, warfare, and attempts to turn people from their religion. This fitnah cannot therefore be reduced to general unrest, just as its gravity compared with killing should not be explained through notions such as “ontological death” that the text does not state.

The main caution is not to cover the root’s many branches with one fixed equivalent. Divine testing, human pressure, deception and changed direction, social disorder, and punishment by fire belong to one family, but construction and context determine the precise contribution of meaning in each ayah.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

F T N: placing someone, or being placed, in a situation of trial, pressure, or influence in which human steadfastness and direction are tested and a person may be turned from their course. Testing, persecution and pressure, deception and diversion, and in certain contexts punishment and burning emerge within this field; the precise sense is determined by each ayah’s construction and context.
