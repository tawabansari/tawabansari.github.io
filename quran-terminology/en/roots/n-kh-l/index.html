The date palm and its products: from cultivation and human provision to sifting and selection in Arabic

1. Introducing the root and its semantic branches

The Arabic root «ن خ ل» has two prominent semantic branches. One concerns the date palm; the other concerns sifting, separating the components of a substance, and choosing a desirable part from among other things.

«نَخْل» is the familiar name of the date palm, whose fruit, trunk, leaves, and other parts had many uses in Arab life. «نَخْلَة» denotes one date palm, while «نَخِيل» also refers to date palms collectively.

In the other branch, «نَخَلَ» means to sift or pass something through a sieve. «مُنْخُل» names the sifting tool, and «نُخَالَة» denotes bran or material separated from flour and grain.

Within this semantic field, «اِنْتَخَلَ» and «تَنَخَّلَ» are recorded for selecting, choosing, and taking the best part of something.

Sifting and the date palm appear to be different meanings. Some lexical explanations propose a connection, but shared consonants do not prove that one historically developed from the other.

One lexical analysis treats selection as the entry’s central meaning and suggests that the date palm was named nakhl because it was considered a choice, valuable tree among those with trunks.

This is a lexical proposal, not an established history of the tree’s name. Independent historical evidence would be needed to accept it as a definite origin.

For Qur’anic study, it is therefore preferable to distinguish nakhl as the date palm from nakhl as sifting and selection.

Only the date-palm branch occurs in the Quran.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs 20 times in the Quran in three nominal forms:

  • «نَخْل»: 11 occurrences; the date palm as a collective generic noun.
  • «نَخِيل»: seven occurrences; date palms or a collection of them.
  • «نَخْلَة»: two occurrences; one date palm.

These occurrences are distributed across 20 verses in 16 surahs.

The forms occur as follows:

«نَخْل»: Al-Anʿām 6:99 and Al-Anʿām 6:141; Al-Kahf 18:32; Ṭā Hā 20:71; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148; Qāf 50:10; Al-Qamar 54:20; Al-Raḥmān 55:11 and Al-Raḥmān 55:68; Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7; ʿAbasa 80:29.

«نَخِيل»: Al-Baqarah 2:266; Al-Raʿd 13:4; Al-Naḥl 16:11 and Al-Naḥl 16:67; Al-Isrāʾ 17:91; Al-Muʾminūn 23:19; Yā Sīn 36:34.

«نَخْلَة»: Maryam 19:23 and Maryam 19:25.

The Quran does not use verbs such as «نَخَلَ», «يَنْخُلُ», «تَنَخَّلَ», or «اِنْتَخَلَ». Likewise, «مُنْخُل» and «نُخَالَة» are attested lexical forms, but are not Qur’anic derivatives.

This matters because sifting or selection should not replace the meaning “date palm” in the verses merely because the forms share a root.

2. The date palm in early Arabic and everyday life

The date palm: the familiar meaning of nakhl

In early Arabic, nakhl named the date palm. In hot, dry regions, particularly oases and areas with water, this tree had economic and subsistence importance.

Dates could be eaten fresh or dried, stored, and transported. The trunk was used in construction, while parts of the leaves and fibres were used in weaving and making various objects.

The date palm was therefore more than a source of fruit; in some regions it formed part of agriculture and everyday life.

This economic importance should not be incorporated into the word’s lexical meaning. Nakhl names the tree; its usefulness, produce, and uses are known from the tree’s characteristics and its relationship with people.

Nor does the name imply that date palms everywhere are equally productive, tall, resilient, or useful.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: a grove and its arrangement

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

وَحَدِّثْ بِأَنْ زَالَتْ بِلَيْلٍ حُمُولُهُمْ
كَنَخْلٍ مِنَ الْأَعْرَاضِ غَيْرِ مُنَبَّقِ

wa-ḥaddith bi-an zālat bi-laylin ḥumūluhum / ka-nakhlin mina al-aʿrāḍi ghayri munabbaqi

Translation:

“Tell how their laden caravan departed by night, like date palms of Al-Aʿrāḍ, not arranged in a straight row.”

The poet compares the caravan and its loads to date palms.

One lexical explanation uses «مُنَبَّق» for trees planted in an even line. Thus «غَيْرِ مُنَبَّقِ» can refer to trees not arranged in an orderly row.

The image shows that groves and the arrangement of date palms were familiar sights for the poem’s audience.

«مُنَبَّق», however, belongs to «ن ب ق», not «ن خ ل». The arrangement or lack of arrangement comes from that description, not from the meaning of nakhl.

The line is preserved in versions of Imruʾ al-Qays’s collected poetry and cited in early lexical material. Despite variations in wording and explanation, the use of nakhl for date palms is clear.

Another witness: tall palms and bunches of dates

Another poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

أَوِ الْمُكْرَعَاتِ مِنْ نَخِيلِ ابْنِ يَامِنٍ
دُوَيْنَ الصَّفَا اللَّائِي يَلِينَ الْمُشَقَّرَا

awi al-mukraʿāti min nakhīli ibni yāminin / duwayna al-ṣafā allāʾī yalīna al-mushaqqarā

And continues:

سَوَامِقَ جَبَّارٍ أَثِيثٍ فُرُوعُهُ
وَعَالَيْنَ قِنْوَانًا مِنَ الْبُسْرِ أَحْمَرَا

sawāmiqa jabbārin athīthin furūʿuhu / wa-ʿālayna qinwānan mina al-busri aḥmarā

A rendering close to the sense:

“Or like Ibn Yāmin’s palms rooted in water near Al-Ṣafā and Al-Mushaqqar: tall, mighty trees with luxuriant branches, holding aloft bunches of red dates not yet at the soft-ripe stage.”

Several features of the date palm appear together: tall trunks, abundant branches, and bunches of dates.

The witness also introduces associated words, including «قِنْوَان» for bunches of dates and «بُسْر» for a stage in the fruit’s development.

These words also help in understanding Qur’anic descriptions of date palms.

Not all these characteristics belong to the name nakhl itself. A palm can be tall or short, productive or unproductive. Height, density, and fruit colour in the poem come from separate descriptions.

Sifting: the root’s other branch

In Arabic, «نَخَلَ الدَّقِيقَ» means “he sifted the flour.”

The material passes through a sieve, separating its components by size or another characteristic.

«مُنْخُل» names the sifting tool; «نُخَالَة» denotes bran or material separated out.

Some lexical explanations assign nukhālah to what remains in the sieve, others to material separated from flour. The substance and sifting method can affect what remains and what passes through.

The central action is clear: separating the components of a material.

From this field comes «اِنْتَخَلَ الشَّيْءَ», selecting the best part of something.

Likewise, «تَنَخَّلَ» can refer to careful selection, taking what is desirable from among alternatives.

Selecting a pleasing statement or poem from other utterances, for example, can be expressed through this family.

The extension is understandable: sifting separates one part of a material from another; selection chooses something from among other things.

This does not mean that every kind of choice in Arabic uses this root, or that every act of selection necessarily chooses what is morally best.

Was the date palm named from sifting?

In this lexical analysis, selection is treated as the root’s central meaning, and the tree’s name is explained through its superior standing among trees with trunks.

The explanation is conceivable, since the date palm had considerable value in some Arab societies.

A tree’s cultural value, however, does not by itself establish the history of its name.

Even if such a historical relationship were proved, the name’s meaning in Qur’anic Arabic would remain “date palm,” not “chosen tree” or “something that has been sifted.”

The proposed connection between sifting and the tree’s name should therefore remain a historical possibility rather than enter its Qur’anic definition.

3. Nakhl, nakhīl, and nakhlah: morphological and linguistic distinctions

Nakhl and nakhīl: collective forms

«نَخْل» is a collective generic noun in Arabic: it can refer to date palms as a group.

«نَخِيل» likewise refers to date palms and is recorded as a plural or collective form of nakhl.

In many uses the two can refer to the same things.

The Quran sometimes uses nakhl alongside gardens and other plants, and sometimes uses nakhīl in that setting.

This difference in form does not mean that nakhl always denotes one tree while nakhīl always denotes a large grove.

Nor does nakhīl necessarily mean very tall palms and nakhl small ones.

Both Qur’anic forms concern the date palm or a collection of date palms.

Nakhlah: one date palm

«نَخْلَة» is the singular unit form and denotes one date palm.

This form occurs twice in Maryam.

The distinction matters: Maryam’s story concerns a particular tree beside whose trunk she had come.

Verses about gardens and groves, by contrast, generally use nakhl or nakhīl.

This numerical difference does not make nakhlah inherently special or supernatural. The tree’s particular identity comes from the story’s context, not the word alone.

Grammatical gender

One linguistic feature is the use of both masculine and feminine agreement with collective nakhl.

Some early Arabic dialects treated it as feminine, others as masculine.

The Quran shows this in several constructions.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 says:

وَمِنَ النَّخْلِ مِنْ طَلْعِهَا

wa-mina al-nakhli min ṭalʿihā

The pronoun «ها» uses feminine agreement with nakhl.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148 likewise has «طَلْعُهَا», while Al-Raḥmān 55:11 has «ذَاتُ الْأَكْمَامِ».

Al-Qamar 54:20, however, has «نَخْلٍ مُنْقَعِرٍ» with a masculine description, while Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 has «نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ» with a feminine description.

These differences accord with the grammatical possibilities of a collective generic noun.

The adjective’s masculine or feminine form therefore does not establish a new kind of tree, the palms’ biological sex, or an independent moral meaning.

Date palms do have male and female trees biologically, but grammatical gender in these verses does not by itself identify their biological sex.

4. Date palms in verses about creation, cultivation, and provision

Many Qur’anic occurrences of the root appear alongside water, gardens, cultivated crops, grapes, and other fruits.

The date palm belongs here to plant growth and human use of the earth.

A. Al-Anʿām 6:99: palms and low-hanging bunches

وَمِنَ النَّخْلِ مِنْ طَلْعِهَا قِنْوَانٌ دَانِيَةٌ

wa-mina al-nakhli min ṭalʿihā qinwānun dāniyatun

Translation:

“And from the date palm, from its emerging blossom, come bunches hanging near.”

Al-Anʿām 6:99, part of the verse

The verse describes the sending down of water, plant growth, grains, and various fruits.

Three words matter particularly in the palm-related passage.

«النخل» names the date palm.

«طلع» concerns the early emergence of the palm’s flower cluster, when the flowers and reproductive structures appear from their covering.

«قنوان» is the plural of «قنو», referring to bunches of dates.

«دانية» describes their being near or hanging low.

These qualities should not all be derived from nakhl itself.

The palm is the subject, while blossom, bunches, and their proximity come from separate words.

The verse continues with grapes, olives, pomegranates, and differences among fruits, inviting the audience to look at fruit as it develops and ripens.

The passage presents attention to growth and fruiting as part of recognising signs of creation.

It is not a complete botanical account, however, and cannot supply every stage of date-palm pollination, growth, and ripening.

B. Al-Anʿām 6:141: produce and its due at harvest

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَ جَنَّاتٍ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَغَيْرَ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَالنَّخْلَ وَالزَّرْعَ مُخْتَلِفًا أُكُلُهُ

wa-huwa alladhī anshaʾa jannātin maʿrūshātin wa-ghayra maʿrūshātin wa-al-nakhla wa-al-zarʿa mukhtalifan ukuluhu

Translation:

“He is the One who brought into being gardens raised on supports and gardens without supports, date palms, and crops differing in their produce.”

Al-Anʿām 6:141, part of the verse

Date palms appear alongside gardens and cultivated crops.

«مُخْتَلِفًا أُكُلُهُ» concerns differences in the produce and food obtained.

The same verse goes on to command:

وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ وَلَا تُسْرِفُوا

wa-ātū ḥaqqahu yawma ḥaṣādihi wa-lā tusrifū

Translation:

“Give its due on the day of its harvest, and do not be excessive.”

This connects the production of food with human responsibility for the harvest and its use.

That responsibility comes from the independent command «آتُوا حَقَّهُ», not from the lexical meaning of nakhl.

This phrase does not specify the exact amount of that due. A particular percentage or legal ruling should not be inserted into the meaning of nakhl or the verse’s translation without examining further evidence.

C. Al-Raʿd 13:4: palms sharing a base and those growing separately

وَفِي الْأَرْضِ قِطَعٌ مُتَجَاوِرَاتٌ وَجَنَّاتٌ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَزَرْعٌ وَنَخِيلٌ صِنْوَانٌ وَغَيْرُ صِنْوَانٍ يُسْقَىٰ بِمَاءٍ وَاحِدٍ وَنُفَضِّلُ بَعْضَهَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي الْأُكُلِ

wa-fī al-arḍi qiṭaʿun mutajāwirātun wa-jannātun min aʿnābin wa-zarʿun wa-nakhīlun ṣinwānun wa-ghayru ṣinwānin yusqā bi-māʾin wāḥidin wa-nufaḍḍilu baʿḍahā ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-ukuli

Translation:

“On the earth are adjoining plots, gardens of grapes, crops, and date palms sharing a base or growing separately, watered with the same water; We favour some over others in produce.”

Al-Raʿd 13:4, part of the verse

Here nakhīl appears with two descriptions:

«صِنْوَان» and «غَيْرُ صِنْوَان».

In the date-palm context, «صنوان» denotes palms growing from one shared base or origin.

«غير صنوان» denotes palms without this shared-base connection.

The distinction concerns growth and the relationship between the trees’ bases.

The verse then mentions the same irrigation water and differences in produce.

The palms’ shared or separate bases do not establish an independent moral or social value.

One can reflect on diversity in creation through the trees’ growth and produce. What the verse directly describes, however, concerns land, growth, and yield.

There is also recitational variation in the case endings of «زَرْع» and «نَخِيل». One reading syntactically connects them with the grapes in the gardens; another places them alongside the gardens as vegetation on the earth.

The variation changes their syntactic relationship with the preceding sentence elements, not the meaning of nakhīl itself.

D. Al-Naḥl 16:11 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:19: gardens of palms and grapes

Al-Naḥl says:

يُنْبِتُ لَكُمْ بِهِ الزَّرْعَ وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالنَّخِيلَ وَالْأَعْنَابَ وَمِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ

yunbitu lakum bihi al-zarʿa wa-al-zaytūna wa-al-nakhīla wa-al-aʿnāba wa-min kulli al-thamarāti

Translation:

“With it He causes crops, olives, date palms, grapes, and every kind of fruit to grow for you.”

Al-Naḥl 16:11, part of the verse

Al-Muʾminūn also says:

فَأَنْشَأْنَا لَكُمْ بِهِ جَنَّاتٍ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ لَكُمْ فِيهَا فَوَاكِهُ كَثِيرَةٌ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

fa-anshaʾnā lakum bihi jannātin min nakhīlin wa-aʿnābin lakum fīhā fawākihu kathīratun wa-minhā taʾkulūna

Translation:

“With it We brought into being gardens of date palms and grapes for you; in them you have abundant fruits, and from them you eat.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:19

In both verses, palms appear alongside other plants.

The main subject is plants growing through water and people benefiting from their produce.

Nakhīl refers to date palms collectively and cannot be restricted to one particular tree or variety of dates.

The pairing of dates and grapes does not mean that they always grow in the same climate or under the same cultivation methods. The verses give examples of plants and sources of food.

E. Al-Naḥl 16:67: products of dates and grapes

وَمِنْ ثَمَرَاتِ النَّخِيلِ وَالْأَعْنَابِ تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْهُ سَكَرًا وَرِزْقًا حَسَنًا إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-min thamarāti al-nakhīli wa-al-aʿnābi tattakhidhūna minhu sakaran wa-rizqan ḥasanan inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yaʿqilūna

Translation:

“From the fruits of date palms and grapes you make an intoxicant and good provision. In this is a sign for people who use their reason.”

Al-Naḥl 16:67

This occurrence matters because it concerns products made from dates and grapes.

The principal disputed meaning here belongs to «سَكَرًا», not nakhīl.

Nakhīl means date palms, while «ثمرات» refers to their fruit and produce.

An intoxicating drink is a well-established lexical and interpretive explanation of «سكر». Other explanations concerning foods or drinks made from dates and grapes have also been proposed.

Syntactically, «سَكَرًا» and «رِزْقًا حَسَنًا» are distinct expressions.

Their juxtaposition therefore does not make them identical or establish equal moral and legal approval of every product mentioned.

Nor should a ruling about permissibility or prohibition be derived from the word nakhl itself.

For this root study, the point is that the verse concerns products of date palms and grapes. Sakar requires a separate study of that word and the relevant verses together.

5. Palms in gardens, Maryam’s story, and descriptions of produce

A. Al-Baqarah 2:266 and Al-Kahf 18:32: a garden and a human test

Al-Baqarah says:

أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ تَكُونَ لَهُ جَنَّةٌ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ

a-yawaddu aḥadukum an takūna lahu jannatun min nakhīlin wa-aʿnābin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru

Translation:

“Would any of you wish to have a garden of date palms and grapes, with rivers flowing beneath it?”

Al-Baqarah 2:266, part of the verse

The verse continues with the garden’s destruction by a fiery whirlwind when its owner has grown old and has dependent children.

The garden represents property and a source of livelihood whose loss can have severe consequences.

Nakhl itself does not mean wealth, security, or human dependence on property. Those ideas come from the entire image and context.

Al-Kahf 18:32 also says:

جَعَلْنَا لِأَحَدِهِمَا جَنَّتَيْنِ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ وَجَعَلْنَا بَيْنَهُمَا زَرْعًا

jaʿalnā li-aḥadihimā jannatayni min aʿnābin wa-ḥafafnāhumā bi-nakhlin wa-jaʿalnā baynahumā zarʿan

Translation:

“For one of them We made two gardens of grapes, surrounded them with date palms, and placed crops between them.”

«حَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ» concerns placing date palms around the two gardens.

The picture concerns gardens and produce, while the following story examines the owner’s behaviour, pride, and assumption that his possessions would endure.

The date palm should therefore not become an inherent symbol of arrogance or hoarding wealth.

In both verses the palm is part of the garden’s physical reality; the moral message comes from human behaviour and the story’s outcome.

B. Al-Isrāʾ 17:91: demanding a garden of palms and grapes

أَوْ تَكُونَ لَكَ جَنَّةٌ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَعِنَبٍ فَتُفَجِّرَ الْأَنْهَارَ خِلَالَهَا تَفْجِيرًا

aw takūna laka jannatun min nakhīlin wa-ʿinabin fa-tufajjira al-anhāra khilālahā tafjīran

Translation:

“Or have a garden of date palms and grapes, and make rivers flow abundantly through it.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:91

This is part of the demands made by the Prophet’s opponents.

They set having a garden and making rivers flow among their conditions for believing.

Nakhīl again means date palms, joined with grapes to picture a garden supplied with water and produce.

This demand does not mean that the Quran makes owning a palm garden a requirement for prophethood.

The verse reports the opponents’ words; it does not endorse their demand as a criterion of truth.

C. Maryam 19:23 and Maryam 19:25: the trunk of one date palm

Maryam’s story says:

فَأَجَاءَهَا الْمَخَاضُ إِلَىٰ جِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ قَالَتْ يَا لَيْتَنِي مِتُّ قَبْلَ هَٰذَا وَكُنْتُ نَسْيًا مَنْسِيًّا

fa-ajāʾahā al-makhāḍu ilā jidhʿi al-nakhlati qālat yā laytanī mittu qabla hādhā wa-kuntu nasyan mansiyyan

Translation:

“The pains of childbirth drove her to the trunk of the date palm. She said: If only I had died before this and become something forgotten.”

Maryam 19:23

«النخلة» denotes one date palm, while «جذع» denotes its trunk.

The passage continues:

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyan

Translation:

“Shake the trunk of the date palm towards yourself, and it will drop fresh, ripe dates upon you.”

Maryam 19:25

These verses concern a particular tree beside which Maryam was situated.

«رُطَبًا» denotes fresh dates at a ripening stage distinct from dried dates.

«جَنِيًّا» describes readiness for picking or being freshly picked.

The tree, its trunk, and its fruit therefore need to be distinguished.

Nakhlah names the tree, jidhʿ its trunk, ruṭab the fruit’s type and condition, and janiyy its description.

Was the tree dry or fruitful?

Some interpretive reports describe a dry tree, or a bare, fruitless trunk, on which dates then appeared miraculously.

Other accounts envisage an existing, fruit-bearing tree.

The verse itself does not explicitly say that the tree was dry or previously had no fruit.

The Quran states that Maryam was beside the trunk and was commanded to shake it so that fresh dates would fall.

Dryness, sudden greening, or stages of miraculous growth should therefore not enter the translation without an explicit Qur’anic statement.

Nor can the presence of fresh dates definitively establish the birth’s precise date or season: the verse gives no calendar date.

Does shaking the trunk belong to the meaning of nakhl?

No.

The verb «هُزِّي» belongs to «ه ز ز» and means “shake.”

«جذع» likewise belongs to «ج ذ ع».

Study of J DH ʿ

Nakhlah simply names the tree whose trunk is shaken.

The verse does not establish that nakhl originally means shaking or causing fruit to fall.

Other words in the sentence express those actions and features.

D. Qāf 50:10: tall palms and ordered clusters

وَالنَّخْلَ بَاسِقَاتٍ لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ

wa-al-nakhla bāsiqātin lahā ṭalʿun naḍīdun

Translation:

“And tall date palms, with clusters arranged one upon another.”

Qāf 50:10

This verse describes palms with two expressions.

«بَاسِقَات» concerns their height and upward growth.

«طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ» concerns clusters or blossoms whose components lie one upon another.

Height and the arrangement of clusters are thus two separately stated qualities.

Nakhl should not itself always be defined as a very tall tree: a date palm’s height varies with its growth stage.

Nor should naḍīd become part of nakhl’s meaning.

The verse belongs to a passage about growth and the earth’s revival; what follows concerns provision for servants and bringing the earth to life after its death.

This connection with resurrection does not alter nakhl’s lexical meaning.

Study of B S Q

E. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148: palms and «طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ»

وَزُرُوعٍ وَنَخْلٍ طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ

wa-zurūʿin wa-nakhlin ṭalʿuhā haḍīmun

Translation:

“And crops, and date palms whose emerging clusters are soft and closely packed.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148

This verse belongs to Thamūd’s story and describes some of their agricultural resources.

There are several explanations of «هضيم».

Some concern softness and ripeness of the produce; others emphasise the close arrangement of a cluster’s components.

Some also describe a blossom or cluster whose parts separate easily when touched.

The variation concerns haḍīm and the produce’s developmental stage, not the tree’s name.

The next verse mentions carving houses in the mountains.

Alongside cultivated land and palm groves, the story therefore also describes the ability to construct stone houses.

Possessing these resources is not itself corruption or arrogance. Judgement of the people’s behaviour comes from other verses and their response to Ṣāliḥ’s message.

F. Al-Raḥmān 55:11 and Al-Raḥmān 55:68: palms on earth and in the Garden

Al-Raḥmān says:

فِيهَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَالنَّخْلُ ذَاتُ الْأَكْمَامِ

fīhā fākihatun wa-al-nakhlu dhātu al-akmāmi

Translation:

“In it are fruit and date palms with sheaths around their clusters.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:11

«أكمام» is the plural of «كِمّ»; here it denotes coverings enclosing the emerging flowers and clusters of the date palm.

It should not be confused in translation with a garment’s sleeve or other coverings expressed through the same Arabic family.

The palm is described through a particular feature of its clusters and their coverings.

Another part of the same surah says:

فِيهِمَا فَاكِهَةٌ وَنَخْلٌ وَرُمَّانٌ

fīhimā fākihatun wa-nakhlun wa-rummānun

Translation:

“In both are fruit, date palms, and pomegranates.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:68

Here palms occur among the Garden’s blessings.

Their separate mention after the general term for fruit does not establish that dates and pomegranates are not considered fruit in Arabic.

A particular item may follow a general category for emphasis without necessarily falling outside it.

Nakhl again denotes the date palm.

The precise nature of the Garden’s trees and produce should not be embellished beyond the text or identified completely with the limitations of earthly trees.

G. Yā Sīn 36:34 and ʿAbasa 80:29: palms alongside other plants

Yā Sīn says:

وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا جَنَّاتٍ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ وَفَجَّرْنَا فِيهَا مِنَ الْعُيُونِ

wa-jaʿalnā fīhā jannātin min nakhīlin wa-aʿnābin wa-fajjarnā fīhā mina al-ʿuyūni

Translation:

“We placed in it gardens of date palms and grapes, and caused springs to gush forth in it.”

Yā Sīn 36:34

ʿAbasa also says:

وَزَيْتُونًا وَنَخْلًا

wa-zaytūnan wa-nakhlan

Translation:

“And olives and date palms.”

ʿAbasa 80:29

Both verses concern plant growth and the provision of human food.

Yā Sīn emphasises the connection between gardens and springs; ʿAbasa places palms alongside plants from which people and livestock benefit.

In all these cases, nakhl names the familiar tree.

The verses may concern signs of creation, provision, water, or human responsibility, but none of those ideas is an independent lexical meaning of nakhl.

6. Palms in images of death, destruction, and punishment

Two important Qur’anic uses concern neither produce nor cultivation, but compare destroyed people to date-palm trunks.

A. Al-Qamar 54:20: uprooted trunks

تَنْزِعُ النَّاسَ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ مُنْقَعِرٍ

tanziʿu al-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿirin

Translation:

“It tears people away, as though they were trunks of uprooted date palms.”

Al-Qamar 54:20

The verse concerns the devastating wind sent against ʿĀd.

«أَعْجَاز» is the plural of «عَجُز» and here denotes the lower parts or trunks of date palms.

«مُنْقَعِر» belongs to «ق ع ر» and concerns being uprooted or torn from the ground.

The comparison pictures people cast down by the wind’s destructive force.

The root «ن خ ل» does not itself mean uprooting, destruction, or death.

The palm supplies the object of comparison; uprooting comes from the independent description «منقعر».

B. Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7: hollow, fallen trunks

Al-Ḥāqqah says:

فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyatin

Translation:

“You see the people lying fallen in it, as though they were trunks of hollow date palms.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, part of the verse

ʿĀd is again compared to palm trunks, but the final description differs.

Al-Qamar uses «مُنْقَعِر», emphasising uprooting.

Al-Ḥāqqah uses «خَاوِيَة», a word connected with emptiness, being hollow, or falling down.

The images are close but not identical.

The difference does not introduce two different types of date palm.

Nor does masculine munqaʿir versus feminine khāwiyah establish a difference in the trees’ biological sex.

Both constructions accord with the grammar of collective nakhl.

Study of KH W Y

Do palms symbolise death in these verses?

These two verses use the palm in a comparison concerning ʿĀd’s destruction.

Elsewhere the same tree appears alongside life, fruit, gardens, and blessings.

Nakhl is therefore neither inherently a symbol of life nor inherently a symbol of death.

In ʿĀd’s story, the trunk offers an apt image for fallen human bodies; in agricultural verses, the fruitful tree illustrates provision and growth.

The difference comes from context and accompanying descriptions.

A word’s stable meaning should not be changed on the basis of one figurative use.

7. Related roots and translation issues

N KH L and N Ḥ L: the date palm and honey bees

These roots are close in writing and sound but have independent meanings and uses.

«نَخْل» names the date palm.

«نَحْل» names honey bees.

The different middle consonants, khāʾ in nakhl and ḥāʾ in naḥl, must not be overlooked or allowed to blur the two roots.

The Quran uses nakhl for date palms, while naḥl in Al-Naḥl 16:68 refers to honey bees.

Bees may benefit from parts of plants, but that biological relationship does not establish a historical relationship between the names for the date palm and honey bees.

Study of N Ḥ L

N KH L and GH R B L: sifting and separation

In the second branch, «نَخَلَ» comes close to «غَرْبَلَ».

Both describe separating components of a substance with a sieve.

Sifting, however, is not the meaning of Qur’anic nakhl.

None of the 20 Qur’anic occurrences concerns sifting flour, selecting speech, or purifying a substance.

A proposed lexical core therefore cannot replace a word’s established contextual meaning.

N KH L and ʿ N B: dates and grapes

Palms and grapes repeatedly appear together, as in Al-Baqarah 2:266, Al-Naḥl 16:67, and Yā Sīn 36:34.

This association does not make the plants or their products identical.

Nakhl names the date palm; ʿinab names grapes or the vine’s produce.

Some verses connect dates and grapes with cultivation and food; another concerns products made from their fruits.

Their difference must be preserved in translation.

Repeated association does not mean that they carry an identical symbolic or moral meaning in every verse.

N KH L and SH J R: the date palm and the tree

Shajar is a broader term for trees and certain kinds of vegetation.

Nakhl names a particular kind of tree.

Every date palm is therefore a tree, but not every tree is a date palm.

The Quran sometimes mentions palms independently and sometimes alongside other trees and plants.

Separate mention shows the palm’s importance in that context without removing it from the category of trees.

Study of SH J R

N KH L and TH M R: the tree and its produce

Nakhl names the tree; thamar concerns fruit or yield.

Al-Naḥl 16:67 makes the distinction clear:

ثَمَرَاتِ النَّخِيلِ

thamarāti al-nakhīli

Meaning the fruits of date palms.

Nakhl should therefore not be translated directly as “dates” when the tree itself is intended.

Dates are the palm’s fruit; the date palm is their source.

Persian may sometimes use a tree’s name figuratively for its produce, but a precise Qur’anic translation should preserve the distinction wherever possible.

Study of TH M R

Translation proposals for Forqan

“Date palm” is a suitable base equivalent for nakhl and nakhlah in their Qur’anic uses.

For nakhīl, “date palms” or “a palm grove” may be appropriate according to context.

“Palm grove” should not replace nakhīl everywhere: it can refer to a collection of palms without necessarily specifying a bounded garden.

In Persian, the familiar word nakhl can sometimes make a phrase more fluent, particularly when several descriptions accompany it.

Agricultural explanations can discuss the palm’s economic importance, but “wealth,” “blessing,” “growth,” and “provision” should not be presented as direct meanings of nakhl.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root «ن خ ل» has two important semantic branches.

The first, and the only one found in the Quran, names the date palm. Nakhl and nakhīl refer to date palms, while nakhlah denotes one date palm.

The second concerns sifting, separating components of a material, and selecting a desirable part. Munkhul, nukhālah, intikhāl, and tanakhkhul belong here.

Explaining the palm’s name through its being a choice tree is an etymological possibility, not an established historical origin.

The root occurs in 20 Qur’anic verses, all concerning the date palm.

Some verses place palms alongside gardens, crops, water, grapes, and other produce. These uses concern growth, provision, use of the land, and human responsibility for blessings.

Others describe features such as clusters, blossom sheaths, tall trunks, and different patterns of growth.

Maryam’s story concerns one particular tree and its trunk. The verse reports fresh, ripe dates falling after the trunk is shaken, without fully explaining the tree’s type or previous condition.

In ʿĀd’s story, palm trunks provide a comparison for fallen, destroyed people. The image differs from fruitful gardens, while nakhl retains the same meaning.

Al-Raḥmān likewise mentions date palms among blessings and descriptions of the earth and the Garden.

Together these uses show that nakhl names a real, familiar tree; height, fertility, usefulness, destruction, security, and moral value do not come from the name itself. Those features must be understood from accompanying descriptions, sentence relationships, and each verse’s context.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

In Qur’anic usage, «ن خ ل» concerns the date palm. «نَخْل» and «نَخِيل» name date palms or a collection of them, while «نَخْلَة» denotes one date palm.

In another Arabic branch, «نَخَلَ» means sifting, separating material components, and, in extended uses, selecting something desirable. Its historical relationship with the tree’s name is uncertain.

In the Quran, nakhl, nakhīl, and nakhlah all refer to the date palm, with no occurrence meaning sifting or selection.

“Date palm” is therefore a suitable brief Qur’anic definition. Distinctions between one tree, a collection of trees, their produce, and their outward features must be preserved in each verse’s translation and explanation.
