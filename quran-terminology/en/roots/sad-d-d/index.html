Turning away, hindering, and diverting from a path: from changing one’s own direction to obstructing others

Ṣ D D concerns turning away, changing direction, and preventing someone from something. A person can turn away from a path, statement, or individual, or prevent another from reaching a destination, following a path, or acting. These uses share a semantic family but differ in subject, object, and responsibility.

The Qur’anic expression ṣaddun ʿan sabīli allāh is particularly important. Many verses concern people hindering others from entering or following God’s path; elsewhere the verb concerns someone turning away themselves.

Alongside ṣadd and ṣudūd, the family includes ṣadīd, wound discharge. Yaṣiddūna in Al-Zukhruf 43:57 also expresses an uproar, with differences in recitation and lexical analysis.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus indexes 42 occurrences in 41 verses: 37 verbs under Form I, one under Form II, two ṣadd verbal nouns, one ṣudūd, and one ṣadīd. Its Form II label for wa-ṣuddū in Al-Raʿd 13:33, however, does not fit the form: ṣuddū is the passive of Form I ṣadda; the passive of ṣaddada would be ṣuddidū. This article therefore retains the total of 42 but analyzes 38 Form I verbs and four nominal or verbal-noun uses, explicitly recording the corpus’s differing label.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: counts and the reported Form II label

1. The semantic core in Arabic

Turning away and changing direction

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الصَّادُ وَالدَّالُ مُعْظَمُ بَابِهِ يَئُولُ إِلَى إِعْرَاضٍ وَعُدُولٍ

al-ṣādu wa-al-dālu muʿẓamu bābihi yaʾūlu ilā iʿrāḍin wa-ʿudūlin

Most of this family’s uses concern turning away or changing direction.

He explains al-ṣadd through al-iʿrāḍ, then notes that the verb can also describe diverting someone else from an activity.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: aversion and changing direction

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records:

الصَّدُّ: الإِعْرَاضُ وَالصُّدُوفُ

al-ṣaddu: al-iʿrāḍu wa-al-ṣudūfu

That is, ṣadd concerns aversion or turning aside.

Someone changes direction away from what faces them or previously held their attention.

This can be physical, such as turning one’s face away, or concern conduct, decision, or belief, such as withdrawing from a statement or path.

Turning away does not inherently mean hostility, disbelief, or sin. Turning away from something wrong can be good; its object and context establish the moral value.

Hindering or diverting someone else

Another branch is transitive: the action affects a person or thing.

Arabic records:

صَدَدْتُ فُلَانًا عَنِ الأَمْرِ

ṣadadtu fulānan ʿani al-amri

That is, I prevented someone from an activity or diverted them from it.

The subject does not simply change their own direction but affects another’s movement, decision, or behavior.

This distinction is fundamental.

Ṣadda ʿanhu can mean turning away from him; ṣaddahu ʿanhu means preventing him from it.

In the former, someone determines their own course or conduct. In the latter, another person or factor intervenes in their path.

Hindering need not involve physical force. Threats, deception, social pressure, promised gain, or practical obstacles can also be involved.

Someone can also use speech or reasoning to dissuade another from wrongdoing. The verb therefore does not have a negative moral value in every use.

Where the Qur’anic object is God’s path, God’s signs, or the Sacred Mosque, the hindrance is condemned because of its object, rather than because every prevention is inherently wrong.

The role of ʿan

Many uses contain the preposition ʿan.

It identifies what someone moves away from or what another is made to leave.

In ṣadda ʿani al-sabīli, the path is the object of aversion or hindrance.

In ṣaddahu ʿani al-sabīli, the object pronoun establishes that the subject hinders someone else from the path.

In ṣadda ʿanhu, without a direct object, the person’s own aversion can be prominent.

An unstated object does not invariably mean there is nobody being hindered. Some sentences omit an object evident from context.

The complete construction must therefore be examined to distinguish personal aversion from obstructing others.

2. Uses in early Arabic

Turning the face or gaze away

Al-Quṭāmī, a poet of the early Islamic period, says:

أَبْصَارُهُنَّ إِلَى الشُّبَّانِ مَائِلَةٌ
وَقَدْ أَرَاهُنَّ عَنْهُمْ غَيْرَ صُدَّادِ

abṣāruhunna ilā al-shubbāni māʾilatun / wa-qad arāhunna ʿanhum ghayra ṣuddādi

Translation: “Their eyes incline toward the young men, and I see them showing no aversion to them.”

Ṣuddād concerns turning away or indifference here.

The poet describes the direction of the gaze, contrasting inclination toward someone with turning away.

This shows Ṣ D D being used for the direction of attention and sight.

Ṣuddād itself does not mean affection or interest. Those emerge from the contrast between māʾilah, inclining, and ghayra ṣuddādi, not turning away.

The verse belongs to the Islamic period and should not be presented as a securely pre-Qur’anic witness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Quṭāmī’s witness with ʿanhum

Hindering through the force of the sword

Dhū al-Rummah says:

أُنَاسٌ أَصَدُّوا النَّاسَ بِالسَّيْفِ عَنْهُمُ
صُدُودَ السَّوَاقِي عَنْ رُءُوسِ الْمَخَارِمِ

unāsun aṣaddū al-nāsa bi-al-sayfi ʿanhumu / ṣudūda al-sawāqī ʿan ruʾūsi al-makhārimi

Approximate translation: “People who held others back from themselves with the sword, as watercourses are prevented from reaching the mountain’s elevated extremities.” In Ibn Barrī’s explanation, water cannot rise to those heights; this is not simply water choosing another course.

Lexicons transmit another ending as well. Ibn Barrī prefers the wording ruʾūsi al-makhārimi.

Aṣaddū concerns hindering others; bi-al-sayfi specifies the sword as the means.

The sword is therefore not part of the root’s own meaning but comes from the accompanying expression.

The poet compares holding people back with water being unable to reach a height. The comparison concerns prevention and failure to reach; it does not establish that ṣadd always means water voluntarily changing course.

Dhū al-Rummah is also an Islamic-period poet. His verse witnesses classical Arabic usage without independently proving a pre-Qur’anic date.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Ibn Barrī: watercourses and heights

Ṣadad: a facing direction or side

Lexicons also record ṣadad for a side, facing direction, or proximity.

For example:

هَذِهِ الدَّارُ عَلَى صَدَدِ هَذِهِ

hādhihi al-dāru ʿalā ṣadadi hādhihi

That is, “This house stands opposite that house.”

Al-ṣaddān also denotes the two sides of a valley or mountain pass.

Ibn Fāris relates these uses to changing direction or inclining toward one side.

This connection is understandable, but it does not prove that every facing-direction or side meaning historically arose directly from aversion.

The uses establish that direction and sides also belong to the family’s lexical range.

Ṣadīd: wound discharge

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains al-ṣadīd as:

الدَّمُ الْمُخْتَلِطُ بِالْقَيْحِ فِي الْجُرْحِ

al-damu al-mukhtaliṭu bi-al-qayḥi fī al-jurḥi

That is, blood mixed with pus in a wound.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records a thin fluid emerging from a wound, potentially mixed with blood.

Aṣadda al-jurḥu describes a wound developing pus and discharge.

This differs semantically from aversion and hindrance.

Ibn Fāris also includes ṣadīd among uses not easily subsumed under the aversion core.

One should not insist, to preserve a single definition, that wound discharge necessarily derived from hindering or diverting.

Different branches within a lexical family do not require all its forms to have an identical meaning.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: aversion, clamor and wound discharge

3. Hindering others from God’s path in the Qur’an

A large proportion of the root’s Qur’anic uses concern God’s path.

Hindrance can accompany obstacles, threats, pressure, deception, misleading promotion, or the use of power.

These means are not fixed components of the root’s definition. Each context identifies the agent, means, and object.

Āl ʿImrān 3:99: hindering believers and seeking crookedness for the path

قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لِمَ تَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ مَنْ آمَنَ تَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا وَأَنْتُمْ شُهَدَاءُ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ

qul yā ahla al-kitābi lima taṣuddūna ʿan sabīli allāhi man āmana tabghūnahā ʿiwajan wa-antum shuhadāʾu wa-mā allāhu bi-ghāfilin ʿammā taʿmalūna

Translation: Say, “O people of Scripture, why do you hinder whoever believes from the path of The God, seeking to make it crooked, while you yourselves are witnesses? The God is not unaware of what you do.”

Taṣuddūna is an imperfect verb addressing a plural audience.

Man āmana identifies the person or people being hindered from God’s path.

The verb is clearly transitive: a group intervenes in believers’ course.

Tabghūnahā ʿiwajan follows, connecting their conduct with seeking crookedness for the path.

Representing the path as crooked is not taṣuddūna’s own lexical meaning; it comes from the following clause.

The verse gives two related aspects: hindering people from God’s path, and seeking to distort or make that path appear crooked.

This is not necessarily blocking a physical road. Its religious and moral object allows hindrance in belief, invitation, and social conduct.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:86: sitting on roads and threatening

Shuʿayb tells his people:

وَلَا تَقْعُدُوا بِكُلِّ صِرَاطٍ تُوعِدُونَ وَتَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ مَنْ آمَنَ بِهِ وَتَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا

wa-lā taqʿudū bi-kulli ṣirāṭin tūʿidūna wa-taṣuddūna ʿan sabīli allāhi man āmana bihi wa-tabghūnahā ʿiwajan

Translation: “Do not sit on every road threatening and hindering whoever believes in Him from the path of The God, seeking to make it crooked.”

Hindering accompanies sitting on roads and making threats.

The context therefore concerns conduct more organized than personal aversion.

Tūʿidūna expresses threatening, and taṣuddūna hindering. The actions are related but distinct.

Bi-kulli ṣirāṭin evokes traversable roads, while sabīli allāhi identifies the religious and moral object of hindrance.

Threats and fear can thus be means of preventing people from following their chosen path.

Ṣadd itself does not make every instance involve an explicit threat.

Al-Anfāl 8:36: spending wealth to hinder

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ لِيَصُدُّوا عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَسَيُنْفِقُونَهَا ثُمَّ تَكُونُ عَلَيْهِمْ حَسْرَةً ثُمَّ يُغْلَبُونَ وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ يُحْشَرُونَ

inna alladhīna kafarū yunfiqūna amwālahum li-yaṣuddū ʿan sabīli allāhi fa-sa-yunfiqūnahā thumma takūnu ʿalayhim ḥasratan thumma yughlabūna wa-alladhīna kafarū ilā jahannama yuḥsharūna

Translation: Those who disbelieve spend their wealth to hinder from the path of The God. They will spend it, then it will become a regret for them, then they will be overcome. Those who disbelieve will be gathered toward Hell.

The lām in li-yaṣuddū expresses purpose: they spend in order to hinder.

Hindering is therefore an objective to which resources are devoted.

Ṣadd can extend beyond an individual response into an undertaking supported by financial resources.

The root does not itself mean spending money; that connection comes from yunfiqūna amwālahum together with li-yaṣuddū.

Nor does the verse make every expenditure on communication or invitation an instance of hindering God’s path. The objective and nature of the funded activity are decisive.

Al-Tawbah 9:34: misusing wealth and religious standing

The verse states:

إِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْأَحْبَارِ وَالرُّهْبَانِ لَيَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ النَّاسِ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

inna kathīran mina al-aḥbāri wa-al-ruhbāni la-yaʾkulūna amwāla al-nāsi bi-al-bāṭili wa-yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli allāhi

Translation: “Many scholars and monks consume people’s wealth wrongfully and hinder from the path of The God.”

It describes many scholars and monks consuming people’s wealth wrongfully and hindering from God’s path.

Kathīran, “many,” matters: the verse does not generalize this conduct to every scholar and monk.

In this context, hindering God’s path accompanies financial misconduct and religious standing.

Religious standing can therefore accompany conduct that obstructs rather than opens God’s path.

The root itself does not imply that every religious officeholder hinders people from that path.

Judgments about individuals and institutions require their actual conduct and specific evidence.

Other examples of hindering from God’s path

Al-Aʿrāf 7:45 and Hūd 11:19 connect hindrance with seeking to make the path crooked.

Ibrāhīm 14:3 places preference for worldly life over the hereafter alongside hindrance.

Al-Anfāl 8:47 connects hindrance with ostentation and arrogance.

Al-Tawbah 9:9 places exchanging God’s signs for a small price alongside hindering His path.

Al-Naḥl 16:88, Al-Naḥl 16:94, Muḥammad 47:1, Muḥammad 47:32, and Muḥammad 47:34 also use the expression in contexts of disbelief, opponents’ conduct, and consequences.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:16 and Al-Munāfiqūn 63:2, oaths serve as a shield or cover, followed by hindrance from God’s path.

The same action can therefore involve different motives and means while remaining hindrance from God’s path.

These motives and means do not all belong to ṣadd’s definition. The verb expresses hindrance; context establishes the conduct involved.

4. Hindering access to the Sacred Mosque: practical obstruction and violated rights

An important use concerns preventing people from accessing the Sacred Mosque.

Hindering here extends beyond inward belief or conduct to denying entry to a place and preventing intended acts.

Al-Baqarah 2:217: hindering God’s path and the Sacred Mosque

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ قِتَالٍ فِيهِ قُلْ قِتَالٌ فِيهِ كَبِيرٌ وَصَدٌّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَكُفْرٌ بِهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَإِخْرَاجُ أَهْلِهِ مِنْهُ أَكْبَرُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ وَلَا يَزَالُونَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَرُدُّوكُمْ عَنْ دِينِكُمْ إِنِ اسْتَطَاعُوا وَمَنْ يَرْتَدِدْ مِنْكُمْ عَنْ دِينِهِ فَيَمُتْ وَهُوَ كَافِرٌ فَأُولَٰئِكَ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-shahri al-ḥarāmi qitālin fīhi qul qitālun fīhi kabīrun wa-ṣaddun ʿan sabīli allāhi wa-kufrun bihi wa-al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-ikhrāju ahlihi minhu akbaru ʿinda allāhi wa-al-fitnatu akbaru mina al-qatli wa-lā yazālūna yuqātilūnakum ḥattā yaruddūkum ʿan dīnikum ini istaṭāʿū wa-man yartadid minkum ʿan dīnihi fa-yamut wa-huwa kāfirun fa-ulāʾika ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūna

Translation: They ask you about fighting in the sacred month. Say, “Fighting in it is a grave offense; but hindering from the path of The God, disbelieving in Him, barring access to the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people are graver before The God. Persecution is graver than killing. They will continue fighting you until, if they can, they turn you back from your religion. Whoever among you turns back from their religion and dies a disbeliever: their deeds become futile in this world and the hereafter. They are the people of the Fire, remaining there.”

Ṣaddun is a verbal noun expressing hindrance.

Ṣaddun ʿan sabīli allāhi occurs together with al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi and expelling its people in the same discussion.

Different grammatical analyses connect al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi with the preceding expressions. One familiar reading coordinates it with sabīli allāhi, conveying hindrance from the Sacred Mosque.

This fits clear uses such as Al-Anfāl 8:34 and Al-Fatḥ 48:25, explicitly describing barring access to the Sacred Mosque.

The context therefore concerns more than personal aversion to a belief: it concerns obstructing others’ access to a path and place of worship.

The verse continues with expulsion and efforts to turn people back from their religion.

Hindrance can therefore accompany deprivation, expulsion, pressure, and attempts to force a change of religious direction.

These acts should be identified through their own wording rather than merged indiscriminately into ṣadd’s definition.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2: hostility does not authorize aggression

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُحِلُّوا شَعَائِرَ اللَّهِ وَلَا الشَّهْرَ الْحَرَامَ وَلَا الْهَدْيَ وَلَا الْقَلَائِدَ وَلَا آمِّينَ الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ وَرِضْوَانًا وَإِذَا حَلَلْتُمْ فَاصْطَادُوا وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ أَنْ صَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ أَنْ تَعْتَدُوا وَتَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْبِرِّ وَالتَّقْوَىٰ وَلَا تَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tuḥillū shaʿāʾira allāhi wa-lā al-shahra al-ḥarāma wa-lā al-hadya wa-lā al-qalāʾida wa-lā āmmīna al-bayta al-ḥarāma yabtaghūna faḍlan min rabbihim wa-riḍwānan wa-idhā ḥalaltum fa-iṣṭādū wa-lā yajrimannakum shanaʾānu qawmin an ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi an taʿtadū wa-taʿāwanū ʿalā al-birri wa-al-taqwā wa-lā taʿāwanū ʿalā al-ithmi wa-al-ʿudwāni wa-ittaqū allāha inna allāha shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

Translation: O you who believe, do not violate the sacred observances of The God, the sacred month, the sacrificial offerings, the garlanded offerings, or those approaching the Sacred House seeking bounty and approval from their Lord. When you leave consecration, you may hunt. Do not let hostility toward people who barred you from the Sacred Mosque lead you to aggression. Cooperate in goodness and mindfulness, and do not cooperate in sin and aggression. Be mindful of The God; indeed, The God is severe in punishment.

Ṣaddūkum is a past-tense verb with the object pronoun kum, “you.”

Its subject is the group that barred the addressees from the Sacred Mosque.

The sense is clear: they prevented your access.

After recalling this conduct, the Qur’an prohibits the addressees from aggression.

Barring access is condemned, but a history of injustice does not itself authorize reciprocal injustice.

This comes from the complete verse. Ṣadd still means hindering; the prohibition of aggressive retaliation comes from the separate command wa-lā yajrimannakum ... an taʿtadū.

Al-Anfāl 8:34 and Al-Fatḥ 48:25

Al-Anfāl 8:34 states:

وَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

wa-hum yaṣuddūna ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi

That is, “While they hinder access to the Sacred Mosque.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 also states:

هُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

humu alladhīna kafarū wa-ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi

That is, “They are those who disbelieved and barred you from the Sacred Mosque.”

In Al-Fatḥ, kum explicitly identifies those barred.

In Al-Anfāl, the verb has no following explicit object, but the context concerns denying people access to the Mosque.

Al-Ḥajj 22:25 likewise places hindrance from God’s path and from the Sacred Mosque together.

Ṣadd can therefore concern practical obstacles to accessing a place of worship.

It does not itself mean mosque, pilgrimage, or visitation. Those are the objects of hindrance in these verses.

5. Personal aversion and indirect hindrance

Al-Nisāʾ 4:61: yaṣuddūna ʿanka ṣudūdan

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ وَإِلَى الرَّسُولِ رَأَيْتَ الْمُنَافِقِينَ يَصُدُّونَ عَنْكَ صُدُودًا

wa-idhā qīla lahum taʿālaw ilā mā anzala allāhu wa-ilā al-rasūli raʾayta al-munāfiqīna yaṣuddūna ʿanka ṣudūdan

Translation: When they are told, “Come to what The God has sent down and to the Messenger,” you see the hypocrites turning resolutely away from you.

Yaṣuddūna is an imperfect verb, followed by its accusative verbal noun ṣudūdan.

The verbal noun emphasizes their turning away.

Here the individuals themselves reject the invitation. Unlike Āl ʿImrān 3:99, the sentence contains no direct object identifying another person whom they hinder.

Personal aversion is therefore clearer in this context.

The verse demonstrates that ṣadd does not always mean obstructing others.

Someone can turn away from divine instruction and the Messenger without the same sentence describing obstruction of anyone else.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:55: acceptance and aversion

The verse states:

فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ آمَنَ بِهِ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ صَدَّ عَنْهُ

fa-minhum man āmana bihi wa-minhum man ṣadda ʿanhu

That is, “Some of them believed in it, and some turned away from it.”

Ṣadda ʿanhu is contrasted with believing.

The person’s own aversion to the matter is therefore an appropriate reading.

It does not establish that everyone turning away necessarily uses force or threats to hinder others.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:5: aversion with arrogance

The verse states:

لَوَّوْا رُءُوسَهُمْ وَرَأَيْتَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ وَهُمْ مُسْتَكْبِرُونَ

lawwaw ruʾūsahum wa-raʾaytahum yaṣuddūna wa-hum mustakbirūna

That is, “They twist their heads, and you see them turning away arrogantly.”

Twisting their heads is physical conduct; yaṣuddūna describes their aversion.

Mustakbirūna separately expresses arrogance.

Their aversion accompanies arrogance here, without arrogance being ṣadd’s fixed meaning.

Al-Naml 27:24: Satan and hindering the path

The verse states:

وَزَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ فَصَدَّهُمْ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ

wa-zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu aʿmālahum fa-ṣaddahum ʿani al-sabīli

That is, “Satan adorned their deeds for them and hindered them from the path.”

Satan is the subject and hum the object of fa-ṣaddahum.

The transitive hindering meaning is clear.

Adorning deeds is also connected with hindering the path.

Hindrance can thus work by changing judgment and making wrongful conduct attractive.

Ṣadd does not itself mean adorning or deceiving; that aspect comes from zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu aʿmālahum.

The same pattern appears in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:38.

Al-Naml 27:43: wa-ṣaddahā mā kānat taʿbudu

وَصَدَّهَا مَا كَانَتْ تَعْبُدُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ إِنَّهَا كَانَتْ مِنْ قَوْمٍ كَافِرِينَ

wa-ṣaddahā mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni allāhi innahā kānat min qawmin kāfirīna

Translation following the first analysis: What she worshipped apart from The God had hindered her, for she belonged to a disbelieving people.

The verse concerns the ruler of Sheba.

In a familiar grammatical analysis, mā kānat taʿbudu is ṣaddahā’s subject: what she worshipped had obstructed her guidance.

On this reading, religious habit and following her community constitute the hindering factor.

Al-Ṭabarī also proposes two other possibilities: Solomon prevented her former worship, or God turned her away from it.

In those two readings, mā kānat taʿbudu occupies an object position.

The difference demonstrates the importance of grammar in identifying subject and object.

In all three possibilities, ṣadda retains hindering or diverting. The difference concerns who or what hinders, and from what the person is hindered.

A grammatical possibility should not be turned into the root’s definitive meaning.

6. Yaṣiddūna: uproar, laughter, and aversion

Al-Zukhruf 43:57

وَلَمَّا ضُرِبَ ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ مَثَلًا إِذَا قَوْمُكَ مِنْهُ يَصِدُّونَ

wa-lammā ḍuriba ibnu maryama mathalan idhā qawmuka minhu yaṣiddūna

Translation following the uproar reading: When the son of Mary was presented as an example, your people suddenly raised an uproar about it.

This is one of the root’s distinctive uses.

The Ḥafṣ recitation has yaṣiddūna, with kasrah on the ṣād.

Other recitations have yaṣuddūna, with ḍammah on the ṣād.

Lexical and recitation sources differ over their relationship and meaning.

First reading: uproar and raising a clamor

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records ṣadda yaṣiddu for loud laughter and clamor.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits yaḍijjūna from Ibn ʿAbbās and other early interpreters: they make an uproar.

The verse accordingly describes a noisy, argumentative response to the example concerning Jesus.

This fits Al-Zukhruf 43:58, which states that they used the example for argument.

Second reading: turning away

Some lexical accounts distinguish yaṣiddūna with kasrah from yaṣuddūna with ḍammah.

On that analysis, the former concerns noise and clamor, and the latter aversion.

Not all lexicographers or reciters consider this semantic distinction definitive.

After examining the readings, Al-Ṭabarī recognizes both and favors the uproar and protest meaning for the verse’s context.

The vowel difference should therefore not immediately become two invariably separate meanings.

Nor does this verse make every Qur’anic ṣadda mean laughing or raising a clamor.

Most verses clearly concern aversion or hindering a path; the auditory use in Al-Zukhruf 43:57 also has sound lexical support.

The family’s definition and the verse’s translation should retain this distinction.

Al-Ṭabarī: variant recitations and clamor

7. Ṣadīd: the wound-discharge branch

Ibrāhīm 14:16

مِنْ وَرَائِهِ جَهَنَّمُ وَيُسْقَىٰ مِنْ مَاءٍ صَدِيدٍ

min warāʾihi jahannamu wa-yusqā min māʾin ṣadīdin

Translation: Ahead of him is Hell, and he is given foul, pus-like water to drink.

Ṣadīd concerns an unpleasant, purulent substance.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains it as blood mixed with wound pus.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records thin fluid emerging from an inflamed wound.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the verse through pus and blood, transmitting early accounts identifying ṣadīd as bodily or wound discharge.

Is ṣadīd the same as ṣadd and hindering?

Morphological indexes and dictionaries place it under Ṣ D D, but it does not mean the same as aversion or hindering.

Ṣadīd names or describes material associated with wounds and their discharge.

Ṣadd, by contrast, is an action in which someone turns away or hinders another.

Shared consonants do not prove a historical derivation of wound pus from deviation off a path.

Lexicons also record aṣadda for a wound developing ṣadīd.

This branch occurs in the Qur’an only in Ibrāhīm 14:16.

Māʾin ṣadīdin: the liquid given to drink

Ṣadīdin follows māʾin in the genitive.

The construction can describe or explain the kind of liquid.

The verse concerns being given an unpleasant, purulent liquid to drink.

The word alone specifies neither its chemical composition, precise temperature, nor physical origin in the afterlife.

Ibrāhīm 14:17 continues with the difficulty of drinking and the person’s condition.

Ṣadīd’s direct meaning should therefore remain, while the punishment’s details come from context.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣadīd in Ibrāhīm 14:16

8. Morphological forms and grammatical roles

The root occurs in several important Qur’anic constructions.

Ṣadda and its inflections can be intransitive or transitive: someone turns away themselves, or hinders another from something.

Ṣadd is a verbal noun for aversion or hindering. Al-Baqarah 2:217 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:160 particularly concern hindering from God’s path.

Ṣudūd is also a verbal noun. Alongside yaṣuddūna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:61, it emphasizes aversion.

Ṣadīd is an adjectival or nominal form associated with wound discharge, used in Ibrāhīm 14:16.

The passive: when the agent is unstated

Al-Raʿd 13:33 states:

وَصُدُّوا عَنِ السَّبِيلِ

wa-ṣuddū ʿani al-sabīli

That is, “And they were hindered from the path.” This is a passive Form I construction; the corpus’s different label is explained in the introduction.

Ghāfir 40:37 also states:

وَصُدَّ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ

wa-ṣudda ʿani al-sabīli

That is, “And he was hindered from the path.”

These are passive constructions.

The passive primarily foregrounds the condition of the person hindered, rather than naming the hindering agent.

It does not establish that the person had no agency or responsibility.

Conduct, deception, choice, and hindrance must be considered through the full context.

Al-Ṭabarī: active and passive readings of ṣaddū

Ṣadda and ṣudda without vowel marks

Active and passive verbs can look alike in unvocalized writing.

Ṣadda, with fatḥah on the ṣād, is active.

Ṣudda, with ḍammah on the ṣād, is passive.

The distinction can determine whether someone turns away themselves, hinders another, or is hindered.

Vowels and grammatical construction therefore matter for Qur’anic study and translation.

Taṣaddā and differing derivational analyses

ʿAbasa 80:6 states:

فَأَنْتَ لَهُ تَصَدَّىٰ

fa-anta lahu taṣaddā

Translation: “Then you attend to him.”

The Quranic Arabic Corpus indexes this under Ṣ D Y.

Some early lexicographers derive it from tataṣaddadu, explaining a dāl changing into yāʾ.

In that analysis, taṣaddā relates to ṣadad, facing or attending to someone.

Other analyses connect it with Ṣ D Y.

A morphological index’s classification does not invariably settle a word’s historical derivation.

This article keeps the corpus’s root-occurrence count while acknowledging taṣaddā’s possible lexical relationship to this family.

9. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ D D (ṣadda) and M N ʿ (manaʿa)

Manaʿa expresses preventing, prohibiting, or withholding something.

Transitive ṣadda can likewise express hindrance.

The two are very close in some sentences.

Ṣadda also has an independent personal-aversion branch, with changed direction and departure from a path prominent.

Manaʿa can concern withholding money, permission, or opportunity without necessarily evoking a changed course.

The roots overlap without being completely synonymous.

Study of M N ʿ

Ṣ D D (ṣadda) and Ṣ R F (ṣarafa)

Ṣarafa concerns turning, redirecting, or turning something back.

Transitive ṣadda can likewise describe diverting someone else.

Ṣarf has a broader family involving redirection, transformation, and circulation; ṣadd particularly emphasizes aversion or prevention from a specified path or object.

This does not make ṣarf invariably positive and ṣadd invariably negative.

Moral value comes from the object and context.

Ṣ D D (ṣadda) and ʿ R Ḍ (aʿraḍa)

Iʿrāḍ concerns turning away or withdrawing.

Intransitive ṣadda ʿanhu can be very close to aʿraḍa ʿanhu.

Ṣaddahu ʿani al-sabīli is clearly transitive, describing hindering someone else.

Aversion alone therefore cannot translate every ṣadd instance.

Where an object or context clearly establishes hindering others, “hinder” or “prevent” is more precise.

Study of ʿ R Ḍ

Ṣ D D (ṣadda) and S D D (sadda)

These roots are distinct despite their resemblance in sound and writing.

S D D concerns closing gaps, making a barrier, and making something firm or correct.

Saddan in Al-Kahf 18:94 denotes a barrier or wall placed between two groups.

Ṣ D D concerns aversion and hindrance.

A physical sadd can serve as a means of ṣadd, but a barrier’s name and the act of preventing are distinct concepts.

Sadīd in qawlan sadīdan comes from S D D and must not be confused with Ṣ D D’s ṣadīd.

Qawlan sadīdan means sound, firm speech; māʾin ṣadīdin in Ibrāhīm 14:16 describes purulent material.

A single different consonant distinguishes entirely different semantic families.

Ṣ D D (ṣadda) and Ṣ D Y (taṣaddā)

Some lexical analyses propose a historical relationship or sound change between the families.

The corpus indexes taṣaddā in ʿAbasa 80:6 and taṣdiyah in Al-Anfāl 8:35 under Ṣ D Y.

Taṣaddā in ʿAbasa concerns turning toward and attending to a person.

Taṣdiyah in Al-Anfāl concerns clapping.

These uses should not be indiscriminately converted into hindering God’s path throughout Ṣ D D’s verses.

10. Challenges in understanding and translation

The main difficulty is the proximity, in some constructions, between turning away oneself and hindering another.

Yaṣuddūna ʿanka ṣudūdan in Al-Nisāʾ 4:61 describes the hypocrites’ aversion.

Taṣuddūna ... man āmana in Āl ʿImrān 3:99 concerns hindering believers.

Ṣaddūkum in Al-Māʾidah 5:2 directly describes barring the addressees from the Sacred Mosque.

Fa-ṣaddahum in Al-Naml 27:24 describes Satan hindering them from the path.

These cannot all be translated identically.

Does ṣadd ʿan sabīli allāh always mean hindering others?

In many verses, yes: an object, means, or context clearly establishes obstruction of others.

Some constructions lack a direct object and also allow personal aversion.

In Muḥammad 47:1 and Muḥammad 47:34, for example, ṣaddū ʿan sabīli allāhi appears alongside disbelief.

Arabic allows both turning away from God’s path and hindering others. Choosing between them requires context and related verses.

ʿAn sabīli allāhi alone therefore does not prove one identical behavior in every verse.

Must hindrance be physical?

No.

Al-Anfāl 8:36 describes wealth spent on hindrance.

Al-Naml 27:24 connects adorning wrongful deeds with hindering the path.

Āl ʿImrān 3:99 connects hindrance with misrepresenting the path.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 and Al-Fatḥ 48:25 concern preventing access to the Sacred Mosque.

Hindrance can therefore take practical, social, financial, or intellectual forms.

Disagreement, criticism, or intellectual opposition is not automatically ṣadd ʿan sabīli allāh.

Such a judgment requires showing that the conduct actually obstructed others’ access, choice, or action, or was undertaken toward that end.

Is ṣadd always intentional?

Transitive ṣadda can describe a conscious act but does not invariably establish the agent’s intention and awareness independently.

Some verses, such as Āl ʿImrān 3:99, supply clues about the agents’ awareness and purpose.

Elsewhere habit, deception, or following others may make someone an agent of hindrance.

On the first analysis of Al-Naml 27:43, the ruler of Sheba’s religious habit and former worship are the hindering factor.

Hindrance occurring, the agent’s knowledge of the truth, and their intention must therefore be distinguished.

This particularly matters in moral judgments about people: a harmful outcome and its being knowingly intended are separate questions.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

For intransitive ṣadda, “turn away” or “become averse” is appropriate.

For the transitive use, “hinder,” “prevent,” or, in some contexts, “divert” is preferable.

In path and movement expressions, “hinder from the path” conveys the core meaning naturally.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 — وَصَدٌّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

“And hindering from the path of The God.”

The hindrance is connected with the Sacred Mosque, expulsion, and religious pressure.

Āl ʿImrān 3:99 — لِمَ تَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ مَنْ آمَنَ

“Why do you hinder whoever believes from the path of The God?”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:61 — يَصُدُّونَ عَنْكَ صُدُودًا

“They turn resolutely away from you.”

“They hinder others from you” does not fit the direct construction here.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 — أَنْ صَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

“That they barred you from the Sacred Mosque.”

Al-Naml 27:24 — فَصَدَّهُمْ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ

“So he hindered them from the path.”

Al-Naml 27:43 — وَصَدَّهَا مَا كَانَتْ تَعْبُدُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ

Following the first grammatical analysis:

“What she worshipped apart from The God had hindered her.”

Other analyses understand a different subject; the full translation must follow the chosen grammar.

Al-Zukhruf 43:57 — إِذَا قَوْمُكَ مِنْهُ يَصِدُّونَ

Preferred rendering following Ḥafṣ and the uproar interpretation:

“Your people suddenly raised an uproar about it.”

The aversion interpretation is also recorded in lexical and recitation sources and should remain in the study’s explanation.

Ibrāhīm 14:16 — مِنْ مَاءٍ صَدِيدٍ

“From foul, pus-like water.”

A lexical note can explain ṣadīd as wound pus and discharge, sometimes mixed with blood.

Ghāfir 40:37 — وَصُدَّ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ

“And he was hindered from the path.”

The passive must be preserved here.

12. Conclusion

Ṣ D D’s main Arabic branch concerns turning away and hindering. Intransitively, a person turns away; transitively, another agent hinders them from a path, decision, or activity.

Many Qur’anic uses concern hindering God’s path. Threats, expenditure, deception, misuse of religious standing, or barring access to the Sacred Mosque may accompany it, without belonging to ṣadd’s lexical meaning itself.

Personal aversion is clearer in verses such as Al-Nisāʾ 4:61 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:55. Hindering others is explicit in Āl ʿImrān 3:99, Al-Māʾidah 5:2, and Al-Fatḥ 48:25.

The auditory yaṣiddūna in Al-Zukhruf 43:57 shows a different branch involving clamor. Ṣadīd in Ibrāhīm 14:16 concerns wound pus and discharge and cannot be reduced to aversion or obstruction.

Short definition: Ṣ D D’s main branch expresses turning away or hindering and diverting someone else. Other recorded branches concern loud clamor and wound discharge, which should not be forced into the same meaning. Brief root meaning: Turning away; hindering or diverting another; also clamor and wound discharge in distinct branches.

The central distinction is between a person choosing to turn away and another agent intervening to hinder them.

Translation should identify these situations through subject, object, ʿan, and context. Ṣadd ʿan sabīli allāh can consequently concern personal aversion in some verses and obstructing others’ progress in others.

This distinction matters for lexical accuracy and for understanding human responsibility in the verses.
