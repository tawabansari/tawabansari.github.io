From accumulation and firmness to the seat of rule

The Arabic root K R S concerns the gathering, accumulation, joining, and consolidation of parts. Classical dictionaries record kirs for compacted collections, accumulated layers, a building’s foundation, and even a group of people. Its principal Quranic noun is kursiyy, a seat or throne that may be associated with rule.

Two questions must be distinguished: the Arabic meanings of K R S and the historical origin of kursiyy itself. Arabic lexicographers connect them, while comparative scholarship has proposed borrowing from Aramaic. This proposal should not be presented as a settled history based on resemblance alone. Nor can every meaning of kursiyy be derived directly from karasa.

The Quran uses only the noun kursiyy from this family, in two inflected forms:

  • «كُرْسِيُّهُ» in al-Baqarah 2:255, concerning the Seat of The God.
  • «كُرْسِيِّهِ» in Ṣād 38:34, concerning Solomon’s throne.

Verbs such as karasa, karrasa, and takarrasa and nouns such as kirs and kurrāsah do not occur in the Quran, although they help describe the Arabic lexical family.

1. Historical meanings and early usages

In dictionary records, a principal meaning of K R S is gathering parts that adhere or accumulate, forming something compact or firm.

Several concrete usages illustrate this meaning.

Accumulation in livestock enclosures

Kirs named compacted layers of animal waste and soil where livestock stayed. When animals remained in one place, this material accumulated, adhered to the ground, and gradually formed a dense layer.

The point is not merely collecting several things: adhesion and compaction also matter.

A building’s foundation

Kirs al-bināʾ denotes a building’s foundation or firm base. Takarrasa uss al-bināʾ describes its foundation becoming firm.

This branch emphasizes the result of accumulation or settlement: something cohesive and strong enough to support a structure.

Connected collections

Kirs and its plural akrās are also recorded for collections of assembled components. In descriptions of necklaces, the word can denote a strand or collection of connected beads and ornaments.

Akārīs is also recorded for groups of people. Here the emphasis is on gathering into a group, without necessarily implying physical compaction.

Kurrāsah and gathered leaves

Kurrāsah denotes a collection of written or connected leaves; its plural is karārīs. Lexicographers explain its connection with K R S through separate leaves forming one collection.

This is a lexical explanation linking assembled material to a writing object; the semantic connection alone does not establish a dated history of the word’s formation.

Poetry and the limits of historical evidence

A verse attributed to al-ʿAjjāj, an Arab poet who lived into the Umayyad period, reads:

يَا صَاحِ، هَلْ تَعْرِفُ رَسْمًا مُكْرَسَا؟

yā ṣāḥi hal taʿrifu rasman mukrasā

“Companion, do you recognize a trace left compacted and layered?”

Mukras here describes a site retaining accumulated material and traces of livestock habitation. It illustrates the family’s use for accumulation and adhesion.

The date of this verse has not independently been established as pre-Islamic, so it cannot be treated as a certain pre-Islamic witness. Similar caution applies to other quotations preserved only in later dictionaries.

The evidence examined here does not establish that every recorded use predates the Quran. What is clear is this semantic field in Classical Arabic and kursiyy itself in Quranic language.

2. Was kursiyy formed directly from K R S?

Arabic lexicographers generally place kursiyy under K R S, connecting the gathering and firmness of parts with the solid structure of a seat or throne.

The semantic resemblance is understandable: a throne ordinarily has a firm base and connected parts. Resemblance alone, however, does not establish historical origin.

Aramaic, including Syriac, has forms resembling kursyā for a seat or throne, alongside related forms in other ancient regional languages. This phonetic and semantic comparison underlies the proposal of Aramaic borrowing, but does not by itself settle the route and date of transmission.

Two considerations therefore need to remain distinct:

First, Arabic K R S has a semantic family concerning accumulation, density, and firmness.

Second, kursiyy as a name for a seat or throne has close counterparts in neighboring languages, and borrowing from Aramaic is a historical proposal concerning its origin.

It is therefore unwarranted to insist that kursiyy originally meant “things piled together” or that Arabs named the throne solely after its construction.

Even if borrowed, a word becomes part of Arabic through established usage. Its presence in the Quran attests its use within Arabic at the time of revelation.

Its Quranic meaning should therefore be determined from the word’s established usage and the two passages, rather than an unproven historical derivation.

3. Solomon’s throne: a seat of rule

In Ṣād, kursiyy refers directly to Solomon:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ

wa-la-qad fatannā sulaymāna wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan thumma anāb

Ṣād 38:34

“We certainly tested Solomon and cast a body upon his throne; then he turned back.”

Ṣād 38:34

This is the clearest Quranic occurrence for understanding the word’s ordinary sense.

Kursiyyihi comprises kursiyy and the attached pronoun -hi, meaning “his seat” or “his throne.” It follows the preposition ʿalā.

The construction “We cast a body upon his throne” describes a body being placed on a seat. ʿAlā expresses the relationship of something being upon a place; the ordinary sense of throne or seat fits naturally.

Yet Solomon’s throne is not merely an everyday sitting object. The Quran presents him as a ruler, and his throne may also represent his governing position and authority.

The next verse says:

قَالَ رَبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي وَهَبْ لِي مُلْكًا لَا يَنْبَغِي لِأَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِي إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

qāla rabbi ighfir lī wa-hab lī mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī innaka anta al-wahhāb

Ṣād 38:35

“He said: My Lord, forgive me and grant me a kingdom that will not be fitting for anyone after me. You are indeed the Great Bestower.”

Ṣād 38:35

The proximity of the throne and the request for a kingdom reinforces its association with Solomon’s rule and governing power.

This association does not make “rule” the lexical meaning of kursiyy. A throne may signify rule, but what an object signifies differs from the meaning of its name.

The verse itself does not identify the body, explain how it was cast on the throne, or fully specify Solomon’s trial. Additional stories about another person occupying his throne are not part of the word’s lexical meaning.

The central point is a particular seat within Solomon’s life and reign: a real throne that may also carry an association with his authority in the narrative.

4. The Seat in al-Baqarah 2:255: encompassing the heavens and earth

The other occurrence is in the passage known as the Verse of the Seat:

وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ

wasiʿa kursiyyuhu al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth.”

Al-Baqarah 2:255

Kursiyyuhu is the subject of wasiʿa, and “the heavens and the earth” are its objects. The pronoun -hu refers to The God.

Wasiʿa belongs to W S ʿ and expresses capacity, breadth, or encompassing. Encompassing the heavens and earth comes from this verb and the sentence, not from kursiyy alone.

This distinction matters: kursiyy alone does not mean infinite extent, universal encompassing, or unlimited power. The sentence attributes the Seat to The God and states that it encompasses the heavens and earth, without specifying spatial measurements or boundaries.

Does the Seat mean divine knowledge here?

An interpretation of kursiyy as divine knowledge is transmitted in exegetical accounts and appears in some dictionaries. The surrounding discussion of knowledge is relevant to that reading, but cannot alone establish it as the word’s sole meaning.

Before mentioning the Seat, the same verse says:

يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ

yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum wa-lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shayʾin min ʿilmihi illā bi-mā shāʾ

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“He knows what is before them and what is behind them; they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills.”

Al-Baqarah 2:255

Divine knowledge is thus explicitly mentioned. Yet the preceding statement about knowledge does not by itself prove that kursiyy in the next clause lexically means knowledge.

Translating kursiyy directly as “knowledge” selects one transmitted interpretation. Although that gloss occurs in lexical and exegetical records, using it as the only translation conceals the interpretive choice.

Another reading understands the Seat as signifying the scope of divine rule, authority, and encompassing, with knowledge as one dimension of that encompassing.

This reading draws on the whole verse: divine life and sustaining power, ownership of the heavens and earth, intercession dependent on permission, encompassing knowledge, and preservation without weariness.

Knowledge and power should therefore be discussed as interpretations in this passage, not asserted as the single historical meaning of kursiyy or used to erase its ordinary sense of seat or throne.

A real entity or an expression of rule?

Alongside the symbolic reading is the possibility that the Seat refers to a reality within creation whose extent encompasses the heavens and earth.

The verse’s wording does not exclude this possibility merely because its nature is unknown. It describes encompassing, but does not explain material, construction, shape, spatial location, or mechanism.

Nor does kursiyy establish that The God sits on a material throne as a human does. No verb meaning divine sitting occurs in this verse.

Three matters must remain distinct: the word’s established meaning, the scope attributed to it, and the actual nature of what is intended in creation or in the expression of divine rule.

Language supplies the first; the verse states the second; a physical image or a definitive cosmological theory cannot establish the third.

The Seat and the preservation of the world

After mentioning the Seat, the verse continues:

وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ

wa-lā yaʾūduhu ḥifẓuhumā wa-huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“Preserving the two does not weary Him; He is the Exalted, the Great.”

Al-Baqarah 2:255

The dual pronoun in ḥifẓuhumā refers to the heavens and earth. The clause expresses divine ability to preserve both.

The verse thus places the Seat’s extent alongside divine ownership, knowledge, preservation, and greatness.

This juxtaposition does not establish that the Seat is a physical mechanism for preserving the world. The God is the preserver in meaning; grammatically, ḥifẓuhumā is the subject of yaʾūduhu. The relationship between the Seat and creation is not further specified here.

5. Kursiyy and ʿarsh in the Quran

Kursiyy and ʿarsh overlap in some uses because both may concern a seat of rule. They are nevertheless distinct words.

Kursiyy ordinarily denotes a seat or throne.

ʿArsh has a wider field including an elevated structure, covering, or royal throne. In the Quran it may denote a ruler’s throne or occur in descriptions of divine greatness and rule.

For example, the account of the queen of Sheba says:

وَلَهَا عَرْشٌ عَظِيمٌ

wa-lahā ʿarshun ʿaẓīm

Al-Naml 27:23

“And she has a great throne.”

Al-Naml 27:23

In the same account Solomon asks those present:

أَيُّكُمْ يَأْتِينِي بِعَرْشِهَا قَبْلَ أَنْ يَأْتُونِي مُسْلِمِينَ

ayyukum yaʾtīnī bi-ʿarshihā qabla an yaʾtūnī muslimīn

Al-Naml 27:38

“Which of you will bring me her throne before they come to me in submission?”

Al-Naml 27:38

Here ʿarsh denotes a particular ruler’s throne, presented as transportable.

Solomon’s account in Ṣād uses kursiyy instead of ʿarsh. Both can relate to rule, but the Quran uses the two names in different expressions.

Both words also occur concerning The God, yet these passages do not explicitly define their physical relationship.

The different names alone do not establish that the Throne is one particular cosmic layer and the Seat another beneath it, or assign either a measured place in a cosmological system.

Neither should the two automatically receive identical translations.

For Forqan, retaining “Seat” for kursiyy in al-Baqarah 2:255 and “seat” or “throne” for Solomon helps preserve the contextual distinction.

6. Comparison with related roots

K R S and R K M

Rakama also concerns gathering and heaping things. The Quran uses rukāman for piled clouds, as in al-Nūr 24:43.

There is therefore overlap between the dictionary fields of K R S and R K M.

Recorded K R S usages additionally emphasize adhesion, compaction, and consolidation. This is not an absolute boundary for every use of either family.

In particular, this resemblance does not justify translating Quranic kursiyy as merely “a mass of piled components.”

K R S and ʿ R SH

ʿArsh may denote an elevated structure or royal throne, whereas kursiyy names a seat or throne.

Their Quranic distinction must be understood from actual usage and context, rather than an invented system in which one must be higher, greater, or more sacred.

K R S and M L K: mulk

Mulk concerns rule, possession, and authority; kursiyy names a seat or object that may signify that rule.

Solomon’s throne is associated with his kingdom and authority. In the Verse of the Seat, its extent appears alongside divine ownership and rule.

This conceptual relationship nevertheless does not authorize translating every kursiyy as mulk.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the word’s established meaning from what its context adds.

In Ṣād 38:34:

“And We cast a body upon his throne.”

“Throne” is natural here because the passage concerns Solomon’s seat and a body placed upon it. Commentary can explain its governing association without adding that explanation to the translation.

In al-Baqarah 2:255:

“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth.”

In Persian, retaining kursī is preferable because “throne” may immediately suggest a familiar human object. The verse concerns the divine Seat and says it encompasses the heavens and earth, without supplying a physical image. English “Seat” likewise retains the term’s distinction.

For the base translation, replacing the word with “knowledge,” “power,” “sovereignty,” or “Throne” selects an interpretation or substitutes another term; such proposals can be discussed separately.

The scope of divine rule and encompassing belongs in reflection on the verse, rather than being inserted into the lexical translation of kursiyy.

8. Summary

Classical Arabic K R S concerns gathering, joining, accumulation, and firmness. Compacted layers in livestock enclosures, building foundations, necklace strands, and gathered leaves in a kurrāsah illustrate this field.

Aramaic borrowing has been proposed for kursiyy. Its historical origin cannot be established merely from resemblance to Arabic K R S, nor should the borrowing proposal be presented as a completely settled transmission history.

The Quran uses kursiyy twice: Solomon’s throne, associated with his rule, and the Seat of The God in al-Baqarah 2:255, encompassing the heavens and earth.

The former illustrates an ordinary throne; the latter describes a scope far beyond a human seat. Context supplies this distinction, not an arbitrary change of meaning.

The divine Seat should not be reduced without evidence to a throne like a human one. Nor should the interpretations “knowledge” and “power” become the sole translation. These themes occur in the verse and illuminate it, while the Seat’s nature and full relationship to creation remain unspecified.

A defensible ordinary meaning of kursiyy is a seat or throne. Its divine use describes an encompassing scope associated with the greatness and rule of The God, without allowing its actual nature to be determined from the word alone.
