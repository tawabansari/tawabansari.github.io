Qur’anic Root A L W

Falling short and exerting effort, oaths and blessings: from لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا to the Lord’s ālāʾ

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَأْلُونَكُمْ
(yaʾlūnakum)
Form I masculine plural active imperfect, indicative; under negation in Āl ʿImrān 3:118

يُؤْلُونَ
(yuʾlūna)
Form IV masculine plural active imperfect, indicative; Al-Baqarah 2:226

يَأْتَلِ
(yaʾtali)
Form VIII masculine singular active imperfect, jussive under prohibition; Al-Nūr 24:22

آلَاء
(ālāʾ)

(alā yaʾlū)

(aliyyah / alāyā)

آلَى يُؤْلِي / إِيلَاء
(ālā yuʾlī / īlāʾ)
Form IV verb and verbal noun; inflected yuʾlūna occurs in the Qur’an

ائْتَلَى / ائْتِلَاء
(iʾtalā / iʾtilāʾ)

(alw / uluww)

(alan / ilan / ilyun)

(ilwah)

Arabic A L W has several semantic branches. One concerns falling short, failing to carry out an undertaking, and, conversely, applying one’s ability and effort. Another concerns an oath through which someone commits to doing or refraining from something. A third appears in آلاء, ālāʾ: blessings, gifts, and great acts of beneficence, used in the Qur’an especially for the Lord’s gifts and signs.

The Qur’an uses this family in four principal forms. In Āl ʿImrān 3:118, يَأْلُونَكُمْ in a negative clause means sparing no effort in causing harm. In Al-Baqarah 2:226, يُؤْلُونَ concerns men who swear to abstain from marital intercourse with their wives. In Al-Nūr 24:22, يَأْتَلِ appears amid withholding assistance from relatives and the needy, with two recognized lexical and syntactic analyses. Ālāʾ, by contrast, denotes divine blessings and recurs frequently in Al-Raḥmān.

These uses show why all A L W derivatives cannot initially be reduced to one meaning, such as effort, abstention, or blessing. Each branch has an established meaning, while the precise historical relationship among them is not equally clear.

Another distinction is essential: sparing no effort is not inherently ethically positive. Someone may use all their ability for good, or spare no effort in harming another. The action’s value comes from its purpose and context, rather than from أَلَا itself.

1. Qur’anic forms and semantic field

The Qur’anic morphological index based on the Ḥafṣ reading records A L W 37 times in 37 verses, distributed among four principal forms.

يَأْلُونَكُمْ in Āl ʿImrān 3:118 is an imperfect verb from basic Form I أَلَا يَأْلُو, alā yaʾlū. Under negation, it means sparing no effort in bringing harm and corruption upon the addressees.

يُؤْلُونَ in Al-Baqarah 2:226 is an imperfect verb from آلَى يُؤْلِي, ālā yuʾlī, with the verbal noun إِيلَاء, īlāʾ. It denotes swearing an oath; the verse concerns an oath associated with abstaining from marital intercourse.

يَأْتَلِ in Al-Nūr 24:22 is a jussive Form VIII imperfect verb. One lexical analysis relates it to swearing an oath; another relates it to falling short or withholding.

Ālāʾ occurs 34 times. This broken plural denotes divine blessings and gifts: twice in Al-Aʿrāf, once in Al-Najm, and 31 times in Al-Raḥmān.

Arabic sources record singular forms for ālāʾ such as أَلًى, إِلًى, and إِلْيٌ. Variation in the singular’s vocalization does not make Qur’anic ālāʾ several different words.

Arabic also recognizes forms such as أَلْو, أُلُوّ, أَلِيَّة, إِيلَاء, ائْتِلَاء, and تَأَلِّي within these lexical domains.

Not all of them occur in the Qur’an. The proximity of A L W and A L Y in certain derivatives, especially those concerning oaths, also requires separate attention.

Three principal branches should preferably remain distinct:

Effort and falling short: carrying out or neglecting an undertaking in relation to ability and the effort applied.

Oath and commitment: binding oneself through an oath to do or refrain from something.

Blessing and gift: ālāʾ as gifts, blessings, and beneficent provisions.

Some Arabic uses show semantic relationships among the branches, but these alone do not establish a definite shared historical origin.

2. Early Arabic: falling short and applying one’s ability

أَلَا يَأْلُو: falling short and withholding effort

Early Arabic records alā yaʾlū for falling short, being slow, and withholding part of one’s effort.

أَلَا فِي الْأَمْرِ can mean falling short in an undertaking.

Conversely, the negative لَمْ يَأْلُ جُهْدًا means that someone spared no effort and applied what ability they had.

This distinction matters. Negation changes the sentence from falling short to not falling short.

Arabic also recognizes مَا أَلَوْتُ جُهْدًا for sparing no effort.

It does not imply unlimited ability: it means that the person withheld none of their effort in the undertaking being discussed.

An early witness: وَمَا ائْتَلَى

A line by Al-Nābighah Al-Jaʿdī, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, reads:

وَأَشْمَطَ عُرْيَانٍ يُشَدُّ كِتَافُهُ
يُلَامُ عَلَى جَهْدِ الْقِتَالِ وَمَا ائْتَلَى

wa-ashmaṭa ʿuryānin yushaddu kitāfuhu / yulāmu ʿalā jahdi al-qitāli wa-mā iʾtalā

“And a grey-haired, unclothed man whose bonds are being tightened: he is blamed for his exertion in battle, although he did not fall short.”

وَمَا ائْتَلَى here appears in a setting of effort and perseverance in battle.

The man is blamed despite the effort he made. مَا ائْتَلَى indicates that he did not fall short in his exertion.

This matters for the first branch because it shows that Arabic also records Form VIII for falling short, or under negation for applying one’s full ability.

The possibility that وَلَا يَأْتَلِ in Al-Nūr 24:22 relates to this branch should therefore not be dismissed without evidence.

Al-Nābighah Al-Jaʿdī lived before and after Islam, but this line’s date has not been independently established. It is an early Arabic witness, rather than proof of composition before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Can alā also mean exerting effort?

Some Arabic accounts record أَلَوْتُ فِي حَاجَتِهِ for making an effort to meet someone’s need.

Some uses of أَلَوْتُ الشَّيْءَ also concern being able to do something.

Early dictionaries therefore record falling short, effort, and ability together for alw and related forms.

Some lexicographers classify the family among words with opposed uses: one construction expresses falling short, another exerting effort.

These differences should not be resolved by arbitrarily changing the meaning of each verse.

One must first identify negation or affirmation, the verb’s object, any accompanying preposition, and its context.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:118, negation and the complement خَبَالًا make the sentence clear: the group under discussion spares no effort in causing harm.

“Applying all one’s ability” in that verse therefore arises from the negative construction and context. It does not show that the verb inherently denotes commendable effort in every use.

3. The oath branch: aliyyah and īlāʾ

Early Arabic records أَلِيَّة, aliyyah, and إِلْوَة, ilwah, for an oath.

آلَى يُؤْلِي إِيلَاءً means to swear an oath.

ائْتَلَى, iʾtalā, and تَأَلَّى, taʾallā, also occur for swearing and binding oneself to something.

An oath in this branch is speech through which someone commits to an undertaking, whether doing something or refraining from it.

The general meaning of īlāʾ should not initially be restricted to abstaining from marital intercourse. That specific meaning arises in Al-Baqarah 2:226.

Arabic allows an oath about many different subjects.

A poetic witness for oaths

Lexical sources transmit the following line and attribute it to Kuthayyir ʿAzzah:

قَلِيلُ الْأَلَايَا حَافِظٌ لِيَمِينِهِ
وَإِنْ سَبَقَتْ مِنْهُ الْأَلِيَّةُ بَرَّتْ

qalīlu al-alāyā ḥāfiẓun li-yamīnihi / wa-in sabaqat minhu al-aliyyatu barrat

“He swears few oaths and guards his word; if an oath escapes him, it is fulfilled.”

Al-alāyā here is a plural of an oath noun, while al-aliyyah has the same sense.

Their juxtaposition with yamīn makes the meaning of an oath clear.

The poet praises someone who does not swear frequently without cause and remains faithful to an oath once spoken.

The witness confirms the oath meaning within the lexical family associated with A L W.

The line also appears in transmissions of the Umayyad poet Kuthayyir ʿAzzah’s poetry. The second hemistich has variants including فَإِنْ سَبَقَتْ مِنْهُ الأَلِيَّةُ بَرَّتْ and فَإِنْ بَدَرَتْ مِنْهُ الأَلِيَّةُ بَرَّتْ. The form quoted here is a lexical transmission. This post-revelation poem is comparative evidence for oath usage, rather than independent proof that this line was used in the pre-Islamic period.

Did the oath sense develop from falling short?

Some lexical explanations connect oaths with withholding or falling short in an undertaking.

Someone may, for example, swear not to do something. The oath then causes them to refrain from that action.

This is semantically intelligible.

Yet not every oath concerns abstention: someone can swear to carry out an action.

Abstention therefore cannot be treated as the necessary historical meaning of every oath form.

Qur’anic īlāʾ accompanies abstention, but that association is also established by the verse’s subject matter.

4. لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا: sparing no effort to cause harm

Āl ʿImrān 3:118 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَتَّخِذُوا بِطَانَةً مِنْ دُونِكُمْ لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ قَدْ بَدَتِ الْبَغْضَاءُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَمَا تُخْفِي صُدُورُهُمْ أَكْبَرُ قَدْ بَيَّنَّا لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tattakhidhū biṭānatan min dūnikum lā yaʾlūnakum khabālan waddū mā ʿanittum qad badati al-baghḍāʾu min afwāhihim wa-mā tukhfī ṣudūruhum akbaru qad bayyannā lakumu al-āyāti in kuntum taʿqilūn

“You who believe, do not take as intimates from outside your own circle people who spare no effort in corrupting your affairs. They wish you hardship. Hatred has become apparent from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater. We have made the signs clear to you, if you reason.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:118

لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا is one of the clearest uses of alā.

Yaʾlūnakum is a plural imperfect verb; kum refers to the addressees.

Khabāl concerns ruin, corruption, and harmful disorder.

The sentence as a whole means sparing no effort to bring corruption and harm upon the addressees.

Why does لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ not mean “they do not help you”?

Taken without khabālan, the verb under negation could concern not falling short or not withholding effort in an undertaking.

Here, however, the undertaking is khabāl.

They therefore spare no effort in causing corruption and harm.

“They do not help you” changes the action’s purpose. The verse describes more than withholding assistance: it describes efforts to corrupt the addressees’ situation.

Khabāl should conversely not be absorbed into the lexical definition of alā. The root concerns effort and falling short; corruption is expressed by the accompanying word.

Biṭānah and trust

The verse opens with biṭānah: people admitted into an individual’s or group’s circle of trust and private affairs.

This word belongs to another root and relates to inwardness and closeness.

The prohibition here concerns people who, despite hostility and a desire to harm, might gain a position of trust and knowledge of private affairs.

The verse says more than that their beliefs differ: it describes their harmful conduct and intentions.

وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ shows that they wish the addressees hardship and difficulty.

قَدْ بَدَتِ الْبَغْضَاءُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ likewise describes hostility becoming apparent in their words.

This should therefore not become the claim that everyone with a different belief or identity necessarily intends harm.

Āl ʿImrān 3:113 also says that the People of the Book are not all alike. Extending a hostile group’s traits to everyone under one religious designation conflicts with the sūrah’s own context.

Āl ʿImrān 3:118 condemns entrusting one’s affairs to people whose conduct reveals hostility and efforts to harm.

Is wholehearted effort always positive?

لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا importantly shows that great effort is not inherently a virtue.

Someone may spare no effort in helping another; that effort can be commendable.

They may instead apply just as much effort to corruption and harm.

Effort and not falling short remain present in both situations, while the action’s direction determines its ethical value.

The root should therefore not be defined solely as “good effort” or “endeavor for good.”

5. يُؤْلُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ: an oath and marital abstention

Al-Baqarah 2:226 states:

لِلَّذِينَ يُؤْلُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ تَرَبُّصُ أَرْبَعَةِ أَشْهُرٍ فَإِنْ فَاءُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

li-lladhīna yuʾlūna min nisāʾihim tarabbuṣu arbaʿati ashhurin fa-in fāʾū fa-inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīm

“For those who swear to abstain from marital intercourse with their wives, there is a waiting period of four months. If they return, indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:226

Yuʾlūna derives from ālā yuʾlī and the verbal noun īlāʾ.

The verb generally denotes swearing an oath, but the oath here concerns marital intercourse.

مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ also matters.

The preposition min here accompanies a sense of distance and abstention. In context, therefore, the oath concerns abstaining from relations with wives.

Arabic īlāʾ does not necessarily mean divorce. Divorce is addressed separately in the next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:227.

Four months: the waiting period, not the lexical meaning of īlāʾ

The verse assigns تَرَبُّصُ أَرْبَعَةِ أَشْهُرٍ to those making this oath.

The duration comes from the expression for waiting and four months, rather than from yuʾlūna itself.

Īlāʾ should therefore not be lexically defined as inherently meaning “a four-month oath.”

Īlāʾ denotes the oath; the verse sets a time limit for the specific resulting situation.

The continuation expresses returning from this abstention with فَإِنْ فَاءُوا.

Al-Baqarah 2:227 separately addresses deciding on divorce.

This sequence distinguishes three related matters: oath, return, and divorce.

Is īlāʾ simply abstention without an oath?

No. Yuʾlūna denotes the oath itself.

Marital intercourse may cease for a time for various reasons. Lexically, however, that interval without the relevant oath is not itself īlāʾ.

Conversely, the general meaning of an oath does not supply every legal and familial detail of this situation.

The Qur’an describes a specific situation, giving a waiting period and the possibility of return. Its detailed implications require examining the verses and sentence construction together, rather than the verb’s morphological pattern alone.

An oath and responsibility toward someone else

The verse shows that an oath can affect another person’s life.

The husband’s oath here is more than an inward decision or private statement: it affects the marriage and his wife’s situation.

The value of fulfilling an oath therefore cannot alone justify disregarding the other party’s rights and position.

Equally, A L W itself does not mean harming a wife. Harm or difficulty arises from the particular oath and how it is carried out.

A lexical study must preserve this distinction.

6. وَلَا يَأْتَلِ: swearing to withdraw help or falling short in giving?

Al-Nūr states:

وَلَا يَأْتَلِ أُولُو الْفَضْلِ مِنْكُمْ وَالسَّعَةِ أَنْ يُؤْتُوا أُولِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَالْمُهَاجِرِينَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلْيَعْفُوا وَلْيَصْفَحُوا أَلَا تُحِبُّونَ أَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-lā yaʾtali ulū al-faḍli minkum wa-l-saʿati an yuʾtū ulī al-qurbā wa-l-masākīna wa-l-muhājirīna fī sabīli allāhi wa-l-yaʿfū wa-l-yaṣfaḥū a-lā tuḥibbūna an yaghfira allāhu lakum wa-allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm

“Those among you with bounty and means should not withhold giving to relatives, the needy, and emigrants in the way of The God. Let them pardon and overlook. Do you not love for The God to forgive you? The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Nūr 24:22

Yaʾtali is a jussive Form VIII imperfect, preceded by prohibitive lā.

Its precise meaning has two recognized lexical analyses.

First analysis: not swearing to withdraw assistance

One analysis places yaʾtali in the oath branch: those with bounty and means should not swear to cut off assistance to the needy and relatives.

This reading concerns an oath spoken from hurt or anger, through which someone intends to withhold assistance.

Two syntactic explanations relate أَنْ يُؤْتُوا to this reading: an omitted negation, yielding an oath not to give; or an understood sense of “out of aversion to giving.” The oath analysis therefore does not depend on one syntactic explanation alone.

It also fits historical accounts of the verse’s setting, describing a decision to stop financial support after the slander episode in Al-Nūr.

Historical accounts and syntactic analysis nevertheless remain separate matters. Even without settling every participant’s identity, the verse itself calls for continued giving, pardon, and overlooking.

Second analysis: not falling short in providing assistance

The other analysis connects yaʾtali with alā yaʾlū, “to fall short.”

On this reading, those with bounty and affluence should not fall short in giving to relatives, the needy, and emigrants.

This has linguistic support: early Arabic records the family’s Form VIII within the field of falling short.

Here, أَنْ يُؤْتُوا relates directly to giving, without requiring an understood negation.

The prominence of the oath reading should therefore not alone exclude this possibility.

The shared outcome of both analyses

Both analyses lead to a similar practical outcome.

One prohibits an oath to withdraw assistance; the other prohibits falling short in giving.

In both, a person of means should not use hurt and disagreement as a reason to deprive relatives and the needy of assistance.

The verse then gives two commands: وَلْيَعْفُوا وَلْيَصْفَحُوا.

Pardoning and overlooking establish the sentence’s ethical direction.

The analyses nevertheless differ lexically: the first foregrounds an oath, the second falling short.

A root study should recognize both without concealing their distinction.

Connection with the slander verses in Al-Nūr

Al-Nūr 24:22 follows verses about spreading slander and the responsibility of those who speak and repeat it.

The command to pardon therefore appears amid wrongdoing, hurt, and damage to people’s reputations.

This shows that pardon and continued beneficence can be called for even after wrongdoing.

It does not establish that every injured person in every situation must unconditionally support someone who continues to harm them, or abandon their right to seek redress.

The verse has specific addressees, recipients with stated characteristics, and a command within a setting of pardon and giving.

Conversely, the pardon encouraged here should not be weakened into merely refraining from revenge. The verse itself speaks of giving and continued assistance.

In either analysis, A L W here relates to a practical concern: not abandoning beneficence because of hurt and hostility.

7. Ālāʾ: the Lord’s blessings and gifts

The most frequent Qur’anic form of this root is ālāʾ.

Early Arabic uses ālāʾ for blessings and beneficent gifts.

It can denote benefits received from another person or favors and gifts bestowed upon someone.

Qur’anic ālāʾ is attributed to The God and concerns His blessings and gifts.

This does not make every ālāʾ a possession, food, or a direct material benefit.

Health, ability, life, guidance, safety, opportunity, and other benefits can fall within blessing, while each verse’s precise referents come from its context.

An early witness: al-ālāʾ wa-l-niʿam

Lexical transmission attributes this line to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah Al-Dhubyānī:

هُمُ الْمُلُوكُ وَأَبْنَاءُ الْمُلُوكِ لَهُمْ
فَضْلٌ عَلَى النَّاسِ فِي الْآلَاءِ وَالنِّعَمِ

humu al-mulūku wa-abnāʾu al-mulūki lahum / faḍlun ʿalā al-nāsi fī al-ālāʾi wa-l-niʿami

“They are kings and sons of kings, surpassing other people in beneficence and gifts.”

Al-ālāʾ appears alongside al-niʿam.

Both concern gifts and benefits.

Lexical sources use this wording as a witness for ālāʾ meaning blessings and gifts. The same line also has the variant فِي اللَّأْوَاءِ وَالنِّعَمِ, “in hardship and blessing.” Its use as evidence for ālāʾ therefore depends on the selected wording; the transmission variant should not be concealed.

Mentioning the two words separately does not establish a complete, fixed boundary between them.

The poet may place two related expressions together to emphasize abundant and substantial giving.

Nor does the line alone prove that ālāʾ is always greater or more spiritual than niʿam.

“Blessings and great gifts” can convey the expression’s field of meaning, but context must determine each gift’s nature and extent.

Is ālāʾ related to sparing no effort?

Some early uses also connect alw with giving and gifts.

One can accordingly envisage a relationship between striving to meet someone’s needs and providing a gift.

This alone does not prove that ālāʾ developed from a verb of striving or sparing no effort.

Nor can the oath sense be made the origin of blessing without evidence.

Each branch is better understood through its own witnesses, avoiding an invented common origin where historical evidence is absent.

Qur’anic ālāʾ as the Lord’s blessings and gifts has independent linguistic and contextual support.

8. فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ: recalling blessings in the accounts of ʿĀd and Thamūd

Al-Aʿrāf uses ālāʾ allāhi twice in the speech of Prophets to their peoples.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:69: ability and succession after Noah’s people

أَوَعَجِبْتُمْ أَنْ جَاءَكُمْ ذِكْرٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ عَلَىٰ رَجُلٍ مِنْكُمْ لِيُنْذِرَكُمْ وَاذْكُرُوا إِذْ جَعَلَكُمْ خُلَفَاءَ مِنْ بَعْدِ قَوْمِ نُوحٍ وَزَادَكُمْ فِي الْخَلْقِ بَصْطَةً فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

a-wa-ʿajibtum an jāʾakum dhikrun min rabbikum ʿalā rajulin minkum li-yundhirakum wa-udhkurū idh jaʿalakum khulafāʾa min baʿdi qawmi nūḥin wa-zādakum fī al-khalqi baṣṭatan fa-udhkurū ālāʾa allāhi laʿallakum tufliḥūn

“Do you marvel that a reminder from your Lord has come to you through a man from among you, to warn you? Remember when He made you successors after Noah’s people and increased you in physical stature and capacity. Recall the blessings of The God, so that you may prosper.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:69

This appears in Hūd’s address to ʿĀd.

He describes their succeeding Noah’s people and receiving increased capacity and stature in creation.

He then commands: فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ.

Ālāʾ here denotes blessings the people received, including ability, position, and the means of life.

These benefits do not by themselves establish ethical superiority.

A people may possess extensive power and resources yet use them wrongly.

The reminder of blessings consequently accompanies an invitation to prosper.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:74: building and dwelling in the land

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, Ṣāliḥ reminds Thamūd that they succeeded ʿĀd and were established in the land.

The verse describes building palaces on plains and carving homes into mountains.

فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ then recurs, accompanied by a prohibition of corruption in the land.

This also shows that divine blessings can include the means of life, building capacity, habitation, and use of the land.

The verse does not establish that large buildings, technology, or power alone prove The God’s approval of their possessors.

The same context commands the people to abandon corruption.

Receiving a blessing and using it rightly are therefore different matters.

Remembering blessings and human responsibility

In both Al-Aʿrāf verses, ālāʾ accompanies the imperative udhkurū, “remember.”

Remembering here means more than listing blessings: one verse connects it with prosperity, the other with avoiding corruption.

This entire ethical implication should nevertheless not be placed within the noun ālāʾ itself.

Ālāʾ denotes blessings, udhkurū commands remembrance, and the verse’s other components establish the direction of human action.

9. فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكَ تَتَمَارَىٰ: blessing and doubt in Al-Najm

Al-Najm 53:55 states:

فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكَ تَتَمَارَىٰ

fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbika tatamārā

“Which, then, of your Lord’s blessings do you doubt and dispute?”

Al-Najm 53:55

Ālāʾ here is plural and stands in a possessive construction with rabbika, “your Lord.”

تَتَمَارَىٰ belongs to M R Y and here concerns doubt, disputation, and questioning the truth.

Doubt and disputation therefore do not come from ālāʾ itself.

Ālāʾ amid life, death, and the fate of peoples

The verses preceding Al-Najm 53:55 describe divine acts and power.

Al-Najm 53:43 concerns causing laughter and weeping, Al-Najm 53:44 causing death and life, and Al-Najm 53:47 bringing about another creation.

Al-Najm 53:50 and the following verses recall the destruction of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and other earlier peoples.

The question about ālāʾ then follows.

The sequence shows that the verse appears amid more than pleasant material blessings: power, creation, human outcomes, and warning also form its setting.

This does not justify abandoning the established blessing sense and translating ālāʾ solely as “punishment” or “power.”

The Lord’s ability, the order of creation, and warnings about the fate of wrongdoers can direct people toward blessings, responsibility, and truth.

A wrongdoer’s punishment, viewed from the position of the person undergoing it, is nevertheless not necessarily a pleasant blessing.

The lexical meaning of ālāʾ must therefore remain distinct from all the topics mentioned in its context.

Rabbika and the singular addressee

Rabbika and tatamārā in this verse address one person.

Al-Raḥmān asks about ālāʾ with a dual address and tukadhdhibāni, “you two deny.” Besides the number of addressees, the verb of the question therefore differs from tatamārā.

This real grammatical difference should not be flattened in translation.

Al-Najm uses singular address to an individual, whereas Al-Raḥmān addresses two groups.

Ālāʾ itself retains the same meaning in both constructions.

10. فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ: blessings in Al-Raḥmān

The best-known occurrence of ālāʾ is in Al-Raḥmān:

فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān

“Which, then, of your Lord’s blessings do you two groups deny?”

Al-Raḥmān 55:13

This verse recurs 31 times in Al-Raḥmān.

Bi-ayyi asks which of the blessings is meant.

Ālāʾ is plural, denoting blessings and gifts.

Rabbikumā contains a dual pronoun, while tukadhdhibāni is an imperfect verb addressing two.

The sentence therefore addresses two addressees or two groups.

Who are the addressees of rabbikumā?

Al-Raḥmān describes the creation of the human being and the jānn.

Al-Raḥmān 55:14 and Al-Raḥmān 55:15 describe their creation separately.

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 also addresses يَا مَعْشَرَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ, “company of jinn and humans.”

The stronger reading of this sequence identifies the two addressees as humans and jinn.

“You two groups” can therefore be used in translation.

Ālāʾ itself nevertheless does not mean blessings exclusive to humans and jinn. Those groups are identified through the pronoun and context.

Why repeat the question 31 times?

Al-Raḥmān describes many subjects.

It speaks of teaching the Qur’an, creating the human being, the order of sun and moon, establishing the balance, the earth, food, and natural blessings.

It then recalls seas, the boundary between waters, life and death, the accountability of humans and jinn, and the outcome in the hereafter.

Later sections also describe scenes of punishment and then the blessings of the Garden.

The question about ālāʾ returns repeatedly among these subjects.

After each section, the repetition confronts the addressees with a question of denying or acknowledging what they have heard.

Without independent evidence, the number 31 should nevertheless not become a numerical code or a definite system concerning the number of the Garden’s gates, stages of creation, or levels of the universe.

The recurrence count is a textual fact; interpreting its number requires separate evidence.

Why does ālāʾ also follow verses about punishment?

A difficult question concerning this root is the refrain’s recurrence amid descriptions of punishment.

Al-Raḥmān 55:43 identifies the Hell that the guilty denied.

Al-Raḥmān 55:44 describes their movement between it and scalding water.

Al-Raḥmān 55:45 then repeats the question:

فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān

“Which, then, of your Lord’s blessings do you two groups deny?”

Al-Raḥmān 55:45

This shows that the sūrah’s structure cannot be confined to a list of pleasant blessings in this world and the hereafter.

Warning and reckoning also form part of the Lord’s address to humans and jinn.

Warning about the outcome of wrongful conduct can itself offer an opportunity to recognize truth and correct one’s course.

The manifestation of The God’s justice, power, and judgment also has a place within the sūrah as a whole.

This difficulty nevertheless should not be resolved by asserting without evidence that suffering and punishment are necessarily a pleasant blessing for whoever undergoes them.

Nor does this juxtaposition alone establish that Arabic ālāʾ never meant blessings and only denotes power or signs.

Some earlier explanations also interpret ālāʾ in this context through divine power. This can illuminate part of the sūrah’s contextual breadth, but cannot independently replace the attested meaning of blessing and gift.

The basic translation should therefore preferably retain “the Lord’s blessings and gifts,” explaining that alongside direct gifts the Qur’anic address also concerns power, justice, and warning about human outcomes.

Denial and ingratitude

Study of K DH B

Al-Raḥmān uses tukadhdhibāni.

This verb belongs to K DH B and concerns denying or declaring something false.

Ālāʾ itself nevertheless does not mean ingratitude.

Someone can recognize a blessing without showing gratitude for it. They can also deny the blessing’s existence or its attribution to the Lord.

These responses are related, but not identical.

Al-Raḥmān’s question directly expresses denial.

“For which blessing do you fail to give thanks?” therefore changes the verb’s precise meaning.

“Which of your Lord’s blessings do you deny?” is closer to the Arabic construction.

11. A L W and related roots and concepts

A L W (alā) and Q Ṣ R (qaṣṣara)

Study of Q Ṣ R

Arabic qaṣṣara concerns shortening and falling short.

One use of alā likewise denotes falling short.

Early Arabic accounts use the verbs to explain each other.

Q Ṣ R nevertheless has a broader family, also concerning shortness in size, restriction, or shortening something.

In its effort branch, A L W concerns withholding or sparing effort.

Under negation in Āl ʿImrān 3:118, it means sparing no effort in causing harm.

A L W (alā) and J H D (jahd)

Study of J H D

Jahd concerns effort and applying one’s ability.

The Arabic expression مَا أَلَوْتُ جُهْدًا places the two concepts together.

Jahd expresses the effort and ability expended, while alā in the falling-short branch concerns withholding or not withholding effort.

They therefore cannot be treated as synonymous in every sentence.

A L W (īlāʾ) and Q S M (qasam)

Study of Q S M

Qasam concerns an oath.

Arabic īlāʾ also denotes swearing an oath.

In Al-Baqarah 2:226, however, īlāʾ appears in the specific setting of marital relations.

That specific usage should not be extended to every oath.

Qasam and īlāʾ also belong to independent lexical families, although they can overlap in the general meaning of an oath.

A L W (īlāʾ) and Y M N (yamīn)

Study of Y M N

One Arabic use of yamīn denotes an oath.

Aliyyah occupies the same semantic field.

The alāyā poetic witness contains both words within one line.

This confirms that an oath is a real meaning within the family.

Yamīn nevertheless also denotes the right hand and has other uses, which should not all be transferred to īlāʾ.

A L W (ālāʾ) and N ʿ M (niʿam)

Study of N ʿ M

Niʿmah and its plural niʿam concern a gift or benefit received.

Qur’anic ālāʾ likewise denotes the Lord’s blessings and gifts.

Early poetry juxtaposes them, and they are close across a substantial part of their semantic field.

This does not establish that ālāʾ always means only great blessings and niʿam only small ones.

Such a fixed division has not been established across Arabic usage.

“Blessings” is a natural, clear equivalent for ālāʾ; each verse’s setting must identify the kind of gift or benefit intended.

A L W (ālāʾ) and F Ḍ L (faḍl)

Study of F Ḍ L

Qur’anic faḍl can concern increase, superiority, or a gift beyond an ordinary measure or entitlement.

Ālāʾ also belongs within gifts and benefits.

Every faḍl is not necessarily confined to a material blessing, nor does every ālāʾ mean an increase relative to other people’s shares.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:69 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, ability and resources accompany remembrance of ālāʾ.

Faḍl and ālāʾ can in such cases approach the same reality from different perspectives while retaining their own meanings.

A L W (ālāʾ) and A Y Y (āyah)

Study of A Y Y

Qur’anic āyah concerns a sign.

Ālāʾ denotes the Lord’s blessings and gifts.

A blessing can also be a sign of The God’s power, mercy, or ordering.

Creation, provision, and the means of life can, for example, be both blessings and signs.

This overlap does not make āyah and ālāʾ identical.

In Al-Raḥmān, blessings appear among signs of divine power and mercy, but ālāʾ itself should not simply be translated as “signs.”

Ālāʾ, āl, and ulū

Qur’anic ālāʾ names blessings.

Āl, however, in “the family of Abraham” or “the people of Pharaoh,” denotes a household or associated group and belongs to a different family in the morphological index.

Ulū means possessors or people endowed with something, as in أُولُو الْفَضْلِ in Al-Nūr 24:22.

Its grammatical function and meaning differ from ālāʾ.

Ilā, إِلَىٰ, is also a preposition denoting direction, destination, or limit. It should not be confused with إِلًى among the recorded singulars of ālāʾ.

Similar-looking spellings do not justify equating their roots or meanings.

12. Is there one historical core for all these branches?

A L W has three principal fields: effort and falling short, oaths, and blessings.

A conceptual relationship can be envisaged between effort and withholding: someone who falls short does not use their full ability.

An oath can also bind someone to act or refrain from acting.

Some Arabic uses similarly associate giving with striving to meet another person’s need.

These relationships nevertheless do not establish one historical origin.

In particular, ālāʾ as blessings should not be defined through the argument that The God spares no effort and His blessings are therefore called ālāʾ.

Such an explanation may be imaginable as a literary connection, but lacks independent evidence for the word’s historical origin.

Early Arabic records each branch with its own forms and witnesses.

The final definition should therefore retain clear branches beside one another, rather than reducing them all to a vague statement such as “applying force to obtain a result.”

13. Translation proposals for Forqan

Each Qur’anic A L W form should be translated according to its own structure and context.

لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا — Āl ʿImrān 3:118

Proposed translation:

“They spare no effort in corrupting your affairs.”

“They spare no effort to harm you” is also clear and natural.

The verb alone should not directly become “they cause corruption,” since خَبَالًا supplies the corruption sense.

يُؤْلُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ — Al-Baqarah 2:226

Proposed translation:

“Those who swear to abstain from marital intercourse with their wives.”

Yuʾlūna supplies the oath; the construction and context supply the subject of abstention.

“They divorce” is not the verb’s direct equivalent.

وَلَا يَأْتَلِ — Al-Nūr 24:22

To preserve the shared outcome of the lexical analyses, the proposed basic rendering is:

“Those among you with bounty and means should not withhold giving to relatives and the needy.”

An explanation can clarify that one analysis concerns swearing to stop assistance, while the other concerns falling short in giving.

If the oath analysis forms the basis of translation, “they should not swear to cut off their assistance” is appropriate.

The analyses should not be merged into one sentence so that their syntactic distinction disappears.

آلاء الله — Al-Aʿrāf 7:69 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:74

Proposed translation:

“The blessings of The God.”

For فَاذْكُرُوا آلَاءَ اللَّهِ, a natural rendering is:

“Recall the blessings of The God.”

آلاء ربك — Al-Najm 53:55

Proposed translation:

“Your Lord’s blessings.”

For the full sentence:

“Which, then, of your Lord’s blessings do you doubt and dispute?”

آلاء ربكما — Al-Raḥmān

Proposed translation:

“The blessings of the Lord of you both.”

For the full sentence:

“Which, then, of your Lord’s blessings do you two groups deny?”

Retaining “you two groups” makes the Arabic dual address clear. Identifying the groups as humans and jinn is an explanation based on the sūrah’s context.

None of these uses should confine ālāʾ to financial provision, bodily comfort, or material resources. The context identifies the blessings intended.

14. Conclusion

Arabic A L W is a family with several branches.

One concerns falling short, withholding effort, and, under negation, sparing no effort.

Another has īlāʾ and related forms for swearing an oath and committing to an action or abstention.

In the third, ālāʾ denotes blessings, gifts, and beneficence.

The Qur’an uses this root 37 times.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:118, لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا concerns people who spare no effort in causing harm. This shows that great effort is not inherently ethically positive.

In Al-Baqarah 2:226, yuʾlūna describes an oath accompanying abstention from marital intercourse. It connects the oath with responsibility toward a wife and a waiting period, but does not itself mean divorce.

In Al-Nūr 24:22, yaʾtali concerns not abandoning giving and pardon. Both lexical analyses, not swearing to stop assistance and not falling short in giving, should be recognized: their similar practical outcome does not erase differences in structure and derivational analysis.

Ālāʾ occurs 34 times, most famously in Al-Raḥmān, recalling the Lord’s blessings and gifts to humans and jinn.

In Al-Aʿrāf, these blessings relate to ability, succession, and the means of life. Al-Najm asks about ālāʾ following creation, life, death, and the fate of peoples. In Al-Raḥmān, it recurs among blessings, signs of power, warning, and reckoning.

Ālāʾ should therefore not be confined to material blessings or immediate pleasures. Equally, its appearance amid punishment does not make suffering and recompense necessarily a pleasant blessing for whoever undergoes them.

The precise historical relationship among effort, oath, and blessing is uncertain. A root definition should keep the branches clear and independent, avoiding an unsupported common image.

Forqan’s final definition of A L W:

“A lexical family with three prominent branches: first, falling short or sparing no effort, according to the verb’s construction and context; second, swearing an oath and committing oneself to do or refrain from something; third, ālāʾ as blessings, gifts, and beneficence. In the Qur’an, يَأْلُونَكُمْ concerns sparing no effort in causing harm, يُؤْلُونَ an oath, يَأْتَلِ an oath or falling short in giving, and ālāʾ the Lord’s blessings. These branches are gathered under one lexical entry, but a definite shared historical development should not be assumed without evidence.”
