Qur’anic Root Q Ṭ Ṭ

Cutting and separating; a share, portion, and written grant

Qur’anic forms

قِطَّنَا

Qiṭṭanā

Our share; sole occurrence, 38:16

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The Arabic root Q Ṭ Ṭ denotes cutting and separating something. In material uses, it particularly describes cutting across an object and separating a portion of it.

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, uses quṭūṭ for written grants. Later lexical works and commentaries transmit this witness; its exact date of composition has not been independently established here. Classical dictionaries also record transverse cutting, but the available evidence does not establish the chronological order in which these meanings arose.

Qur’anic form examined: qiṭṭanā, “our share” or “our portion,” and, in another interpretation, “our record of account.” These are two semantic interpretations of the same form, not different recitations of the verse.

Other Arabic forms include qaṭṭa, “to cut”; qaṭṭ, “cutting”; taqṭīṭ, “cutting and shaping”; and quṭūṭ, the plural of qiṭṭ meaning shares or written grants. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Material imagery and early Arabic evidence

The Arabic expression qaṭṭa al-qalama means trimming and preparing the tip of a reed pen for writing. Cutting across wood, hide, and other objects also belongs to the root’s material uses.

The relationship between cutting and a share can be illustrated by dividing property among several people. A portion is separated and designated for each person; that portion is their share.

In economic usage, qiṭṭ also designated a written document recording a person’s grant or share. Some lexicographers connect this sense with cutting pieces of hide or other writing materials. The explanation is semantically intelligible, but it does not establish the word’s historical origin with certainty.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, speaks of King al-Nuʿmān:

وَلَا الْمَلِكُ النُّعْمَانُ يَوْمَ لَقِيتُهُ / بِإِمَّتِهِ يُعْطِي الْقُطُوطَ وَيَأْفِقُ

“Nor King al-Nuʿmān, when I met him in his prosperity and splendor, granting written awards and setting affairs right.”

Here al-quṭūṭ is the plural of qiṭṭ and means written grants. The translation above follows al-Naḥḥās’s explanation of yaʾfiqu as setting right; excelling is also reported as an explanation of the verb. This difference does not remove the evidence for al-quṭūṭ as written grants.

The witness demonstrates qiṭṭ in the domain of written grants. By itself, it establishes neither the date of the word’s emergence nor a definite historical development from cutting to an allotted share.

Classical Arabic also records qiṭṭ meaning a cat. This formally identical use should not be merged with cutting and allotment without derivational evidence.

3. The Qur’anic use of qiṭṭ

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Ṣād, in the words of people who did not take divine warnings about reckoning and retribution seriously:

وَقالوا رَبَّنا عَجِّل لَنا قِطَّنا قَبلَ يَومِ الحِسابِ

Wa-qālū rabbanā ʿajjil lanā qiṭṭanā qabla yawmi l-ḥisāb.

“And they said, ‘Our Lord, hasten our share for us before the Day of Reckoning.’”

Sad 38:16

Qiṭṭanā consists of the noun qiṭṭ and the attached pronoun nā, “our,” and is the object of ʿajjil. This verb, from the root ʿA J L, means to hasten or bring something forward. Qabla yawmi l-ḥisāb specifies the time requested by the speakers: before the Day of Reckoning.

Their intention must be understood in context. The opening of the surah describes people who respond to the divine message with defiance and opposition and have not yet tasted divine punishment.

إِن كُلٌّ إِلّا كَذَّبَ الرُّسُلَ فَحَقَّ عِقابِ

In kullun illā kadhdhaba l-rusula fa-ḥaqqa ʿiqāb.

“All of them denied the Messengers, so My punishment became due.”

Sad 38:14

In this context, asking for their share before the Day of Reckoning expresses a challenge to the promised reckoning and punishment, rather than a sincere desire for divine judgment. Instead of taking the warning seriously and reconsidering their conduct, they demand an early realization of what they have been warned about.

A share and portion, or a record of account?

Two established Arabic meanings of qiṭṭ are relevant to interpreting this verse.

In the first interpretation, qiṭṭ is a share or portion: what a person receives as the outcome and recompense of their actions. In light of the preceding verses, the share here points particularly to the promised punishment.

In the second interpretation, qiṭṭ is a written record of account: the speakers mockingly demand their record before the Day of Reckoning.

This interpretation is compatible with the lexical sense of a document and the Qur’anic depiction of recorded deeds. The following verse illustrates that depiction; it does not independently prove that qiṭṭ must mean a record of account in Ṣād:

اقرَأ كِتٰبَكَ كَفىٰ بِنَفسِكَ اليَومَ عَلَيكَ حَسيبًا

Iqraʾ kitābaka kafā bi-nafsika l-yawma ʿalayka ḥasībā.

“Read your book; today you yourself suffice as a reckoner against yourself.”

Al-Isra 17:14

A person’s share and the document specifying that share or account are semantically related, but they are not identical. One denotes what belongs to the person; the other denotes the writing that records or specifies it.

Given the context of Ṣād and its discussion of the deniers’ punishment, “our share” is the preferred translation here. The sense of a record of account remains a lexically supported alternative interpretation.

Qiṭṭ does not itself mean punishment. The connection between the requested share and punishment is inferred from context, not from the word’s independent meaning.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṭ Ṭ (qaṭṭ) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭʿ): cutting and severing a connection

Both roots express cutting and separating. Qaṭʿ nevertheless has a broader range: severing material connections, breaking relationships, ending a flow, and separating parts of a whole.

Transverse cutting and separating part of an object are prominent material uses of qaṭṭ. The noun qiṭṭ also has the senses of a share and the document assigning it.

Q Ṭ Ṭ (qiṭṭ) and Q S M (qasm): a share and division

In its division-related branch, Q S M denotes dividing something and specifying its shares.

Qiṭṭ more particularly denotes the share allotted to a person or the document concerning it.

For example, when property is divided among several people, the act of division can be expressed through Q S M, while qiṭṭ can name a person’s allotted share.

Q Ṭ Ṭ (qiṭṭ) and N Ṣ B (naṣīb): a share and portion

In the Qur’an, naṣīb is a share or portion received by a person, whether of inheritance, earnings, reward, or the consequences of deeds.

Qiṭṭ overlaps with naṣīb in the sense of a share, while its use for a written allocation document adds another feature to its semantic range.

Qiṭṭ must also be distinguished from qaṭ, “enough,” and qaṭṭu, “ever before,” in negative Arabic constructions referring to the past. These forms differ in meaning and grammatical function.

5. Translation proposed for Forqan

For qiṭṭanā in Ṣād 38:16, “our share” or “our portion” is proposed.

“Our share of punishment” fits the context, but punishment is not part of qiṭṭ’s direct meaning. That explanation belongs in the reflection, while the base translation should preserve the word’s semantic scope.

“Our record of account” is also defensible under the second interpretation.

6. Conclusion

In material use, Q Ṭ Ṭ denotes cutting and separating, especially cutting across something. The noun qiṭṭ is established in Arabic for a share, a portion, and a written document allocating a share.

In the Qur’an, qiṭṭ occurs in the speech of people who challenge the divine promise by demanding their share before the Day of Reckoning. The negative force comes from the context, not the word itself.

Proposed Forqan definition: Cutting and separating; in the noun qiṭṭ, a person’s allotted share or portion and the written document specifying their share or account.
