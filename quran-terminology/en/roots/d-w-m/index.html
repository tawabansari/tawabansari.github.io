Remaining, continuity, and persistence: from a lasting condition and steady rain to perseverance in action and sustained circling

Arabic D W M concerns remaining, continuing, and maintaining a condition over time. Something may stay stable for a period, an action recur regularly, a situation continue while a condition holds, or a blessing remain available without interruption.

This family includes dāma, to continue or remain established; dawām and daymūmah, continuity; dāʾim, lasting or continuing; mudāwamah, persistently pursuing an action; and dīmah, gentle, continuing rain.

Early Arabic also records standing water, a bird’s circling flight, a spinning top, and calming a pot’s boiling. Some concern remaining in a state; others concern motion and circling.

D W M occurs nine times in nine Qur’anic verses: seven inflections of dāma and two active-participle forms of dāʾim.

The verses are Āl ʿImrān 3:75, Al-Māʾidah 5:24, Al-Māʾidah 5:96, Al-Māʾidah 5:117, Hūd 11:107, Hūd 11:108, Al-Raʿd 13:35, Maryam 19:31, and Al-Maʿārij 70:23.

The central point is that continuity does not itself mean eternity. Something may last an hour, a day, a lifetime, or without a specified end. Its subject, sentence conditions, and context determine the duration.

Nor does an action’s continuity necessarily mean performing it without a break at every moment. An action repeated at specified times can also involve perseverance and continuity.

1. The semantic core: stillness and adherence

Ibn Fāris on the root’s principle

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى السُّكُونِ وَاللُّزُومِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-sukūni wa-al-luzūmi

Meaning that the root has one principle indicating becoming still and adhering or remaining established.

He continues:

دَامَ الشَّيْءُ يَدُومُ، إِذَا سَكَنَ

dāma al-shayʾu yadūmu, idhā sakana

Meaning that dāma applies when something becomes still or remains in its condition.

This appears somewhat different from contemporary dawām, usually understood as continuing through time, whereas Ibn Fāris emphasizes stillness and adherence.

Yet the connection is clear.

Something remaining in a condition has not departed from or abandoned it.

Continuity can therefore concern maintaining a condition or remaining established.

This does not make everything enduring physically motionless. Rain can continue falling, or a person regularly carry out an activity.

What continues is the flow or action itself, rather than bodily stillness.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: stillness, adherence, and calming

Dāma al-shayʾ: a condition remains established

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

دَامَ الشَّيْءُ يَدُومُ وَيَدَامُ

dāma al-shayʾu yadūmu wa-yadāmu

“The thing continued; imperfect forms: yadūmu and yadāmu.”

It records the verbal nouns dawm, dawām, and daymūmah.

In ordinary use, dāma al-shayʾ means something remained or a condition continued.

If calm is said to endure, it remained established for a period.

If rain endures, rainfall continues.

If friendship endures, the friendly bond remains over time.

The things enduring differ, but all continue and do not disappear during the relevant period.

Does continuity always involve stillness?

No.

Lexical sources record dāma both for stillness and for movement or circling.

Alongside continuity and staying, the Cairo Arabic Language Academy lists turning, moving, and becoming still among the verb’s uses.

This initially seems contradictory.

Yet something may remain in one place while moving in another respect.

A top may rotate around its axis while its overall location changes little.

A bird can also circle in one area of the sky, keeping its wings spread and relatively steady for a time.

Maintaining a condition and movement are therefore not always opposed.

This explanation should not, however, become a definite claim that every circling use developed from stillness. Early sources offer different accounts of their relationship.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Tāj al-ʿArūs and Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: remaining and movement

2. Physical uses in early Arabic

Dāʾim water: standing water

Arabic uses māʾun dāʾimun for water that does not flow and remains standing in place.

Ibn Fāris takes this as evidence for stillness.

Dāʾim here does not necessarily mean water that will never run out.

Water can stand in a pond for a time and then evaporate or diminish.

The relevant feature is therefore standing water, rather than its eternity.

Nor does standing still itself establish whether water is clean or polluted.

Those are separate properties.

Dīmah: gentle, continuing rain

Dīmah describes rain falling gently without a noticeable break.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes it as rain accompanied by calm.

Some lexicographers specify a day and night or more; others include several days.

Khālid ibn Janbah, quoted by Ibn Manẓūr, describes it as rain continuing for a period without thunder or lightning.

Its familiar plural is diyam.

Gentleness and continuity are connected here.

Yet sources do not all specify the same amount, intensity, or exact duration.

Dīmah should therefore not be restricted to a fixed volume or duration of rain.

Nor does D W M mean that every prolonged rainfall must be beneficial. Its effects depend on land conditions, water amounts, and living beings’ needs.

Daymūmah: an extensive desert

Arabic sources also record daymūmah for a vast, remote desert.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes very level terrain without a visible route, water, or clear landmark.

Lexicographers connect the name with the distance and time needed to cross it.

The traveler crosses a great distance and experiences the route as long.

Daymūmah here does not mean a literally limitless desert.

Every desert has boundaries; the traveler experiences its extent and the difficulty of reaching its end.

Lisān al-ʿArab: standing water, dīmah and the daymūmah desert

Dawwama al-ṭāʾir: a bird circles or sustains its flight

Early Arabic uses dawwama al-ṭāʾir for a bird circling in the air or keeping its wings spread and steady for a time while flying.

Lisān al-ʿArab records two explanations.

In one use, the bird circles within an area of sky.

In the other, it keeps its wings open without continuously flapping them.

Al-Layth ibn al-Muẓaffar explains tadwīm through circling and flight in the air.

The movement image can be compared with ṣāffāt in Al-Mulk 67:19, where birds spread and then fold their wings.

But dawwama and ṣaffa are different roots, whose full meanings should not be transferred to one another.

The first concerns a sustained posture or circling; the second highlights spreading the wings in a particular state.

Duwwāmah: a spinning top and sustained turning

Duwwāmah names a top children spin with a string or another means.

Some lexical explanations connect the name with rotational movement.

Others say that rapid spinning makes it seem calm and fixed in place, explaining the name that way.

Ibn Manẓūr records these different accounts.

Both turning and relative maintenance of a condition have therefore featured in explaining the word.

This does not mean that Qur’anic dāʾim means rotating or circling.

The top is one particular physical use of the family.

Lisān al-ʿArab: bird flight and different accounts of the spinning top

Adāma al-qidr: calming a pot’s boiling

Arabic uses adāma al-qidr for calming boiling with water or another means.

Ibn Fāris relates this to stillness and settling.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī says:

تَفُورُ عَلَيْنَا قِدْرُهُمْ فَنُدِيمُهَا
وَنَفْثَؤُهَا عَنَّا إِذَا حَمْيُهَا غَلَا

tafūru ʿalaynā qidruhum fa-nudīmuhā / wa-nafthaʾuhā ʿannā idhā ḥamyuhā ghalā

Rendering: “Their pot boils against us, so we calm its boiling and settle it away from us when its heat rises.”

Fa-nudīmuhā concerns calming the pot’s boiling.

This shows that the stillness and calming branch had actual early uses.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; without dating this particular verse, it cannot be described as certainly preceding Qur’anic revelation.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: attribution of the pot witness to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

3. Continuity, waiting, and deliberation in pre-Islamic poetry

Qays ibn Zuhayr’s witness

A poem attributed to Qays ibn Zuhayr al-ʿAbsī, a pre-Islamic poet and warrior, states:

فَلَا تَعْجَلْ بِأَمْرِكَ وَاسْتَدِمْهُ
فَمَا صَلَّى عَصَاكَ كَمُسْتَدِيمِ

fa-lā taʿjal bi-amrika wa-istadimhu / fa-mā ṣallā ʿaṣāka ka-mustadīmi

Approximate rendering: “Do not hurry your affair; take time and consider it, for none straightens your stick over the fire like a patient, deliberate person.”

Istadimhu belongs to D W M.

Here it means more than “continue it forever”: it concerns pausing, avoiding haste, and proceeding thoughtfully.

The lexical commentary explains istidāmat al-amr through al-anāh: calm deliberation in doing something.

The second hemistich pictures preparing or straightening wood through heat.

Haste may spoil such work, which requires attention and time.

On this attribution, the witness suggests that pre-Islamic D W M concerned more than continuing: it also concerned taking time and staying with a task toward its outcome.

It does not make istadāma mean delaying a task in every use.

Here deliberation emerges from the verb and the advice against haste.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: the witness attributed to Qays ibn Zuhayr

Persistence compared with delay

Arabic mudāwamah concerns persistently pursuing a task.

Some early uses of istidāmah also involve waiting, watching, and deliberating.

The uses are close, since neither immediately abandons the matter.

But repeatedly performing an action and waiting for its outcome are not identical acts.

Someone may work at something daily and persevere in it.

Another may pause over an important decision and assess circumstances before acting.

Both avoid haste or abruptly dropping the subject, but their conduct differs.

4. Mā dāma: a condition’s duration in the Qur’an

Most Qur’anic uses occur in the construction mā dāma.

Arabic grammar includes this among kāna and its sisters; it expresses how long a condition holds.

For example, mā dumtu ḥayyan means “as long as I am alive.”

Mā with dāma establishes a temporal relationship, and the state following the verb defines the continuity’s scope.

Mā dāma should therefore not always be translated “forever.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:75: sustained pursuit of a trust’s return

وَمِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِقِنْطَارٍ يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِدِينَارٍ لَا يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ إِلَّا مَا دُمْتَ عَلَيْهِ قَائِمًا ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا لَيْسَ عَلَيْنَا فِي الْأُمِّيِّينَ سَبِيلٌ وَيَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-min ahli al-kitābi man in taʾmanhu bi-qinṭārin yuʾaddihi ilayka wa-minhum man in taʾmanhu bi-dīnārin lā yuʾaddihi ilayka illā mā dumta ʿalayhi qāʾiman dhālika bi-annahum qālū laysa ʿalaynā fī al-ummiyyīna sabīlun wa-yaqūlūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba wa-hum yaʿlamūna

Translation: Among the People of the Book are those who, if you entrust them with great wealth, return it; and among them are those who, if you entrust them with a dinar, do not return it unless you remain persistently on their case. That is because they say, “We bear no responsibility toward the unlettered,” and knowingly attribute lies to The God.

Dumta is a perfect verb in the temporal construction mā dumta.

ʿAlayhi qāʾiman specifies the addressee’s state during pursuit.

Continuity here concerns persisting in demanding and pursuing a right.

But dumta itself does not mean demanding: that comes from qāʾiman ʿalayhi and the context of entrusted property.

Nor must qāʾim mean physically standing before the debtor.

Arabic standing over a matter can concern overseeing, pursuing, and insisting on its completion.

The phrase describes someone who recovers the entrusted property only through sustained pursuit.

Continued pursuit compared with trustworthiness

The verse distinguishes two kinds of conduct among the People of the Book.

One group returns even a large trust.

Another fails to return even a small one without sustained pursuit.

The verse therefore does not describe everyone in a religious community as equally trustworthy.

Continuity concerns the claimant’s conduct here; neither betrayal nor trustworthiness is the root’s own meaning.

The verse then reports the second group’s wrongful justification.

This ethical discussion comes from the full context and should not be added to the definition of dāma.

Al-Māʾidah 5:24: a group remaining in the land

قَالُوا يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنَّا لَنْ نَدْخُلَهَا أَبَدًا مَا دَامُوا فِيهَا فَاذْهَبْ أَنْتَ وَرَبُّكَ فَقَاتِلَا إِنَّا هَاهُنَا قَاعِدُونَ

qālū yā mūsā innā lan nadkhulahā abadan mā dāmū fīhā fa-idhhab anta wa-rabbuka fa-qātilā innā hāhunā qāʿidūna

Translation: They said, “Moses, we will never enter it while they remain there. Go, you and your Lord, and fight; we are sitting here.”

Dāmū concerns a group’s continuing presence in a land.

Fīhā specifies their location.

Mā dāmū fīhā thus means “as long as they are in it.”

This construction alone does not say they will remain there forever.

The speakers emphasize their refusal to enter with lan nadkhulahā abadan in the preceding clause.

But abadan and mā dāmū differ in meaning and role.

Abadan emphasizes their refusal’s force and temporal scope, while mā dāmū fīhā makes the other group’s presence a condition of that refusal.

Dāmū itself therefore does not establish that the group’s presence is eternal.

Al-Māʾidah 5:96: a ruling continues during consecration

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَحْرِ وَطَعَامُهُ مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِلسَّيَّارَةِ وَحُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَرِّ مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ الَّذِي إِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

uḥilla lakum ṣaydu al-baḥri wa-ṭaʿāmuhu matāʿan lakum wa-li-al-sayyārati wa-ḥurrima ʿalaykum ṣaydu al-barri mā dumtum ḥuruman wa-ittaqū allāha alladhī ilayhi tuḥsharūna

Translation: Sea game and its food are made lawful for your benefit and travelers’, while land game is forbidden to you as long as you are in consecration. Be mindful of The God, to whom you will be gathered.

Dumtum appears with ḥuruman here.

Mā dumtum ḥuruman expresses the duration of consecration.

Continuity thus concerns a state with a beginning and end.

The verse relates the ruling to the period of being in that state.

Dumtum alone does not determine how consecration begins or ends, or every ruling concerning hunting.

Those require the verse’s separate expressions and related verses.

This use clearly shows that continuity need not mean endlessness, since it concerns a limited state.

Al-Māʾidah 5:117: Jesus’s witness during his presence among the people

مَا قُلْتُ لَهُمْ إِلَّا مَا أَمَرْتَنِي بِهِ أَنِ اعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ رَبِّي وَرَبَّكُمْ وَكُنْتُ عَلَيْهِمْ شَهِيدًا مَا دُمْتُ فِيهِمْ فَلَمَّا تَوَفَّيْتَنِي كُنْتَ أَنْتَ الرَّقِيبَ عَلَيْهِمْ وَأَنْتَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدٌ

mā qultu lahum illā mā amartanī bihi ani uʿbudū allāha rabbī wa-rabbakum wa-kuntu ʿalayhim shahīdan mā dumtu fīhim fa-lammā tawaffaytanī kunta anta al-raqība ʿalayhim wa-anta ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīdun

Translation: I told them only what You commanded: “Worship The God, my Lord and your Lord.” I was a witness over them while I remained among them. When You took me, You Yourself watched over them; You witness everything.

Mā dumtu fīhim expresses the duration of Jesus’s presence among the people.

The witness described is thus connected to that period of presence.

Fa-lammā tawaffaytanī follows, after which The God’s watchfulness is emphasized.

This sequence distinguishes Jesus’s presence from the period after tawaffī.

D W M itself does not determine the meaning of tawaffī, death, raising, or the manner of departure from the people.

These concern other expressions and verses and need independent study.

Continuity’s direct meaning here is the persistence of presence among a group.

Maryam 19:31: responsibility throughout life

وَجَعَلَنِي مُبَارَكًا أَيْنَ مَا كُنْتُ وَأَوْصَانِي بِالصَّلَاةِ وَالزَّكَاةِ مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا

wa-jaʿalanī mubārakan ayna mā kuntu wa-awṣānī bi-al-ṣalāti wa-al-zakāti mā dumtu ḥayyan

Translation: He made me blessed wherever I may be and enjoined ṣalāh and zakat upon me as long as I live.

Mā dumtu ḥayyan expresses the duration of being alive.

It relates to the injunction concerning ṣalāh and zakat.

The responsibility continues throughout the speaker’s lifetime.

It does not follow that someone must perform one particular outward action at every moment, day and night, without stopping.

The continuing injunction of zakat, for example, does not mean a person must be handing over wealth every moment of life.

Likewise, the continuing responsibility of ṣalāh does not itself determine the manner and schedule of all its forms.

Mā dumtu ḥayyan clearly shows that the injunction is not limited to one day or a short period.

A responsibility’s continuity can thus accompany action at appropriate times and circumstances, rather than uninterrupted performance of the same behavior.

5. Mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍ: continuity and very long duration

Hūd 11:107

خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا مَا دَامَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ رَبُّكَ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ فَعَّالٌ لِمَا يُرِيدُ

khālidīna fīhā mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍu illā mā shāʾa rabbuka inna rabbaka faʿʿālun li-mā yurīdu

Translation: Remaining there as long as the heavens and earth endure, except as your Lord wills; your Lord does whatever He wills.

This continues the description of a group in the Fire.

Dāmat concerns the heavens and earth remaining established.

Grammatically, mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍ connects duration with their endurance.

Arabic can use such expressions for very long duration, even the unending continuity intended by a speaker.

Al-Ṭabarī explains that Arabs expressed very long or permanent duration through the endurance of the heavens and earth and the succession of night and day.

This phrase should therefore not be reduced to an astronomical calculation of the lifetime of today’s familiar heavens and earth.

Dāmat and khālidīn

The verse contains both khālidīn and mā dāmat.

The words belong to different roots.

Khālidīn belongs to KH L D and concerns remaining in a condition or place.

Dāmat belongs to D W M and expresses how long something holds.

Khālidīna fīhā describes remaining, while mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍ expresses its temporal scope.

Dāmat itself does not independently decide the nature of abiding, conditions of departure, or details of punishment.

The role of illā mā shāʾa rabbuka

The verse continues:

إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ رَبُّكَ

illā mā shāʾa rabbuka

Meaning: “Except as your Lord wills.”

Early commentaries explain this phrase in different ways.

Some connect it to excepting a group among the people of the Fire.

Others apply the exception to a period before entry or to the nature and extent of remaining.

Others emphasize that the continuity described depends on divine willing: no external force compels The God’s action.

Al-Ṭabarī reports different views and prefers a reading concerning a group’s departure from the Fire.

This exegetical preference must not become dāma’s own lexical meaning.

Lexically, dāmat expresses only remaining established.

The exception and the final duration of punishment are broader questions requiring all relevant verses.

D W M alone can therefore establish neither a definite end to punishment nor the endlessness of all its forms.

Al-Ṭabarī: the heavens-and-earth idiom and explanations of the exception

Hūd 11:108

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ سُعِدُوا فَفِي الْجَنَّةِ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا مَا دَامَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ رَبُّكَ عَطَاءً غَيْرَ مَجْذُوذٍ

wa-ammā alladhīna suʿidū fa-fī al-jannati khālidīna fīhā mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍu illā mā shāʾa rabbuka ʿaṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdhin

Translation: Those made fortunate are in the Garden, remaining there as long as the heavens and earth endure, except as your Lord wills: a gift not cut off or interrupted.

The same mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍ appears here.

Yet the ending differs from the preceding verse.

It states:

عَطَاءً غَيْرَ مَجْذُوذٍ

ʿaṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdhin

“An uninterrupted gift.”

Majdhūdh belongs to J DH DH and means cut off or severed.

The expression therefore concerns an uninterrupted gift.

This sentence matters for understanding the continuity of the Garden’s blessings.

But ghayra majdhūdh is not part of dāma’s lexical meaning: it is an independent description qualifying the giving.

Study of J DH DH

Does the heavens’ and earth’s endurance yield exactly the same conclusion in both verses?

The verses share a construction but have different endings.

Hūd 11:107 emphasizes the Lord’s will and freedom to act.

Hūd 11:108 adds uninterrupted giving alongside the same exception.

Research must retain the shared mā dāmat construction without overlooking the distinct final sentences.

This distinction matters in an independent study of abiding, divine willing, and the hereafter.

Lexical research on D W M should not replace all Qur’anic evidence with one word made to decide every detail of the hereafter.

6. Dāʾim: continuing blessings and perseverance in action

Al-Raʿd 13:35: ukuluhā dāʾimun wa-ẓilluhā

مَثَلُ الْجَنَّةِ الَّتِي وُعِدَ الْمُتَّقُونَ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ أُكُلُهَا دَائِمٌ وَظِلُّهَا تِلْكَ عُقْبَى الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا وَعُقْبَى الْكَافِرِينَ النَّارُ

mathalu al-jannati allatī wuʿida al-muttaqūna tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru ukuluhā dāʾimun wa-ẓilluhā tilka ʿuqbā alladhīna ittaqaw wa-ʿuqbā al-kāfirīna al-nāru

Translation: The Garden promised to the mindful is described thus: rivers flow beneath it; its fruit and produce remain available, and so does its shade. That is the outcome of those who were mindful, while the disbelievers’ outcome is the Fire.

Dāʾimun is the active participle of dāma.

Ukuluhā concerns the Garden’s fruit or edible produce.

Dāʾim is grammatically the predicate of ukuluhā.

Wa-ẓilluhā shares this predicate: the Garden’s shade also continues.

The verse thus describes two continuing blessings: edible produce and shade.

Does continuing fruit mean the same particular fruit exists forever?

No; the word itself does not provide those details.

Ukuluhā dāʾimun can concern continuing access to the Garden’s fruit and food.

It does not mean that one particular fruit is never eaten or that fruits do not change.

Nor does the verse deny that fresh fruit ever appears.

What is explicit is that the blessing described does not cease.

Al-Ṭabarī also explains ukuluhā dāʾimun as produce that does not run out for the Garden’s people.

Its precise production and consumption are a separate matter.

Dāʾim compared with khālid

Here dāʾim describes food and shade, rather than directly describing the Garden’s people.

Hūd 11:108 uses khālidīn for people remaining in the Garden.

The Qur’an thus uses different words for people’s remaining and the continuity of blessings.

This does not disconnect the two.

The Garden’s people receive continuing blessings, but each sentence’s grammatical subject must be identified separately.

Al-Ṭabarī: the Garden’s continuing food and shade

Al-Maʿārij 70:23: ʿalā ṣalātihim dāʾimūn

الَّذِينَ هُمْ عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ دَائِمُونَ

alladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalātihim dāʾimūna

Translation: Those who persevere and remain constant in their ṣalāh.

Dāʾimūna is the sound masculine plural of the active participle dāʾim.

ʿAlā ṣalātihim specifies the object of perseverance.

The group’s relationship with ṣalāh is therefore not intermittent, slack, or abandoned.

Does constancy in ṣalāh mean praying without interruption?

No; dāʾimūn itself does not necessarily entail that.

Arabic persistence in an action can mean continuing to pursue it without abandoning it, even if it is done at specified times.

Someone faithful to a commitment throughout life need not perform one outward behavior every second.

Maryam 19:31 also presents the continuing injunction of ṣalāh and zakat throughout life, without implying uninterrupted performance of both.

Dāʾimūn in Al-Maʿārij 70:23 accordingly concerns perseverance and continuity in ṣalāh.

Its complete manner, times, and relationship between worship and practical or ethical effects belong to the separate study “Ṣalāh in the Qur’an.”

Dāʾimūn compared with yuḥāfiẓūn

Al-Maʿārij 70:34 states:

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ يُحَافِظُونَ

wa-alladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalātihim yuḥāfiẓūna

This describes those who protect and guard their ṣalāh.

Dāʾimūn belongs to D W M and focuses on continuity and perseverance.

Yuḥāfiẓūn belongs to Ḥ F Ẓ and emphasizes guarding, caring for, and preventing loss.

The qualities are close but not identical.

Someone may perform an action for a long period while neglecting its quality and proper performance.

They may also try to protect a valuable action but lack persistence if they do it only intermittently.

Both continuity and protection concern ṣalāh in these verses.

Rendering both simply as “they pray” would erase the lexical distinction.

Does dāʾimūn also concern stillness and reverence during ṣalāh?

Some exegetical accounts connect continuity in Al-Maʿārij 70:23 with calm attentiveness during ṣalāh.

This is not unrelated to the Arabic stillness branch.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah also identifies stillness as a foundation of the root.

Yet Al-Maʿārij describes a group’s sustained relationship with ṣalāh.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:2 also uses a separate word for reverent humility in ṣalāh.

Calm attention may therefore be fitting for ṣalāh, but dāʾimūn should not be restricted without evidence to standing motionless during it.

Al-Ṭabarī’s reports also prominently concern continuing ṣalāh and not abandoning it.

“They persevere in their ṣalāh” preserves continuity without restricting it to unestablished details of performance.

Al-Ṭabarī: regular ṣalāh and the report of avoiding distraction

7. Important forms and non-Qur’anic derivatives

Dāma, dawm, and dawām

Dāma is a Form I verb.

Dawm and dawām are familiar verbal nouns.

Their temporal use concerns a condition remaining and continuing.

Other uses also concern stillness and remaining in a state.

Daymūmah

Daymūmah concerns continuity and endurance.

Arabic geographical usage also applies it to an extensive desert.

It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Dāʾim and dāʾimūn

Dāʾim is the active participle of dāma.

The Qur’an uses dāʾimun for the Garden’s food and dāʾimūna for a group’s perseverance in ṣalāh.

Their number and grammatical role differ, but both retain continuity as their semantic core.

Adāma and idāmah

Adāma is Form IV.

In ordinary use, it can mean making something continue.

Some early uses also express calming a pot’s boiling.

Form IV therefore should not always mean merely extending time.

Dāwama and mudāwamah

Dāwama ʿalā al-amr means persistently pursuing a task and continuing to perform it.

Mudāwamah is its verbal noun.

Contemporary Persian also uses mudāwamat for continuity in performing a task.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Istadāma and istidāmah

Some uses of istadāma concern seeking continuity and trying to keep something going.

Earlier uses also record waiting, deliberation, and proceeding thoughtfully.

Qays ibn Zuhayr’s witness belongs here.

The sentence’s context must therefore determine the meaning.

Dīmah and diyam

Dīmah is gentle, continuing rain.

Diyam is its plural.

These words do not occur in the Qur’an.

Dawwama and tadwīm

Different uses of dawwama concern circling, a bird’s sustained flight, rotating something, and sometimes calming it.

Tadwīm is this form’s verbal noun.

Lexicographers’ disagreement over movement in the sky and on the ground shows that its exact meaning was not identical in every situation.

Duwwāmah and duwām

Duwwāmah names a spinning top and certain rotational movements.

Some sources also use duwām, with ḍammah, for dizziness and a sense of the head spinning.

Its vowel marking and context differ from dawām meaning continuity.

Every unvocalized occurrence should therefore not be translated as endurance without attending to vowels and the sentence.

Dawm: a tree name

Dawm also names a tree, associated in sources with muql and certain palm-like or large trees.

This use is also familiar in place names such as Dūmat al-Jandal.

The plant name’s existence does not establish historically that endurance or stillness caused the naming.

This lexical branch requires independent botanical and historical evidence and has no direct role in understanding the nine Qur’anic occurrences.

Early lexicons: derivatives, dizziness, and the tree name

8. Related roots

D W M (dāma) and B Q Y (baqiya)

Arabic baqiya concerns remaining rather than disappearing.

Dāma likewise concerns continuing and enduring.

Dawām may emphasize continuity during a period or condition, whereas baqāʾ emphasizes remaining against ending or passing away.

The families overlap but are not complete synonyms in every sentence.

Study of B Q Y

D W M (dāma) and KH L D (khalada)

Qur’anic khulūd concerns prolonged remaining and, in some contexts, endless remaining.

Dawām also expresses continuing but can concern limited periods in different uses.

Mā dumtum ḥuruman in Al-Māʾidah 5:96 concerns the period of consecration.

Mā dumtu ḥayyan in Maryam 19:31 concerns a lifetime.

Hūd 11:107 and Hūd 11:108 combine both families in one expression.

Dāma and khalada should therefore not be treated as one word in two forms.

Study of KH L D

D W M (dāma) and TH B T (thabata)

Thabata concerns being established and firm.

Dāma can likewise express a condition remaining established.

Thabāt may focus on firmness against change or pressure, while dawām concerns continuing over time.

Something may remain stable for a period but disappear when that period ends.

Stability and continuity can therefore accompany one another without meaning the same thing.

Study of TH B T

D W M (dāma) and M K TH (makatha)

Makatha concerns staying and lingering in a place or condition.

Dāma also expresses continuity.

Makth often concerns a person staying in a particular place or period.

Dawām can also concern a condition, blessing, action, or relationship itself.

Al-Raʿd 13:35 concerns food and shade continuing, beyond a person simply staying somewhere.

D W M (dāma) and W Ẓ B (wāẓaba)

Wāẓaba concerns persistently pursuing an action without abandoning it.

Dāwama ʿalā al-amr occurs in the same field.

Dāma, however, can broadly concern inanimate things and conditions with no will to act.

Regular observance usually concerns a person’s conduct and effort.

Dāʾimūn in Al-Maʿārij 70:23 can thus concern regular observance of ṣalāh, without reducing the root’s entire meaning to voluntary effort.

D W M (dāma) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥafiẓa)

Ḥafiẓa concerns guarding and caring for.

Dāma concerns continuity and remaining established.

Al-Maʿārij 70:23 and Al-Maʿārij 70:34 use both families to describe ṣalāh.

This combination shows that continuing an action and protecting it are related but distinct qualities.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

D W M (dāma) and D W R (dāra)

Dāra concerns turning and rotational movement.

Some early D W M uses also describe birds’ circling and spinning tops.

But not every continuity is circling.

Nor does every rotational movement necessarily last forever.

These families can overlap in uses concerning sound and movement, but remain distinct in the main sense of continuity and endurance.

Study of D W R

D W M (dāma) and D H R (dahara)

Arabic dahr concerns extended time and the passage of ages.

Dawām concerns something remaining established over time.

Both concepts may occur in a sentence: time passes and a condition lasts through part of it.

But time itself and a phenomenon’s persistence through time do not mean the same thing.

Dahr should therefore not mean absolute continuity, nor dawām time itself.

Study of D H R

9. Questions of understanding and translation

Does dāʾim always mean eternal?

No.

A condition can endure for a period and then end.

The Qur’an repeatedly uses mā dāma for limited periods.

Eternity or endlessness therefore needs suitable evidence.

Al-Raʿd 13:35 concerns the Garden’s continuing blessing in the hereafter.

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 limits continuity to consecration’s duration.

One root can therefore have different temporal scopes in different contexts.

Does continuity mean that nothing changes?

No.

A condition may remain fundamentally established while its details change.

Gentle rain may continue for a period without every drop or moment having exactly the same intensity.

Someone may likewise persevere in a responsibility while adapting its execution properly to circumstances.

Continuity should therefore not be equated with complete motionlessness or unchanging details.

Does continuity in action mean uninterrupted activity?

Not necessarily.

Maryam 19:31 and Al-Maʿārij 70:23 concern continuity in relation to ṣalāh.

These verses alone do not say that people must engage in one bodily behavior at every moment.

Persistence in a responsibility may consist of regular performance, not abandoning it, and remaining faithful over time.

Its practical form must be understood through verses concerning that responsibility itself.

Do dāʾimūn and yuḥāfiẓūn mean the same thing?

No.

Al-Maʿārij gives two different descriptions.

Dāʾimūn emphasizes continuity.

Yuḥāfiẓūn emphasizes guarding and caring for.

Translating both identically would lose some of the Qur’an’s lexical precision.

Is continuity equivalent to good performance?

No.

Someone may persevere in wrongdoing.

They may also do something right only intermittently.

Continuity itself does not judge whether its subject is right or wrong.

Moral value comes from the act, intention, manner, and effects.

Al-Maʿārij 70:23 praises believers’ continuity, but the praise arises from the description together with ṣalāh, rather than every persistence or repetition being inherently virtuous.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

Depending on context, dāma can be rendered “remained established,” “continued,” or “endured.”

Mā dāma is usually more precisely rendered “as long as” or “while.”

For the active participle dāʾim, “lasting,” “constant,” “continuing,” or “permanent” may fit the context.

Āl ʿImrān 3:75 — إِلَّا مَا دُمْتَ عَلَيْهِ قَائِمًا

“Unless you remain persistently on their case.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:24 — مَا دَامُوا فِيهَا

“As long as they are in it.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 — مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا

“As long as you are in consecration.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:117 — مَا دُمْتُ فِيهِمْ

“While I remained among them.”

Hūd 11:107 and Hūd 11:108 — مَا دَامَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ

“As long as the heavens and earth endure.”

The contextual explanation should also examine this expression’s rhetorical use for very long duration or the permanence intended.

Al-Raʿd 13:35 — أُكُلُهَا دَائِمٌ وَظِلُّهَا

“Its fruit and produce continue, and so does its shade.”

Maryam 19:31 — مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا

“As long as I live.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:23 — عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ دَائِمُونَ

“They persevere and remain constant in their ṣalāh.”

This preserves continuity and perseverance without deriving independent new details of ṣalāh’s manner or schedule from the word itself.

For non-Qur’anic uses, dīmah can mean gentle, continuous rain; mudāwamah, persistent pursuit; istidāmah, ordinarily seeking continuity and in certain early uses deliberation; and tadwīm al-ṭāʾir, a bird’s circling or sustained flight.

11. Conclusion

Arabic D W M concerns remaining, persistence, and continuity.

Ibn Fāris identifies stillness and adherence as its core. Standing water is thus called dāʾim, and a derivative also expresses calming a pot’s boiling.

But the family’s uses are not limited to bodily stillness.

Rain can keep falling, a bird can circle, someone can persevere in a task, and a condition can hold for a specified period.

Early Arabic evidence connects persistence, deliberation, calm, and continuity within this family without making them all present in every use.

D W M occurs nine times in the Qur’an.

In seven verbal occurrences, mā dāma expresses a condition’s duration: pursuing a trust, a group’s presence in the land, consecration, Jesus’s presence among people, the continuity mentioned in hereafter verses, and human life.

In Al-Raʿd 13:35, dāʾim describes the Garden’s continuing blessings.

In Al-Maʿārij 70:23, dāʾimūn describes perseverance in ṣalāh.

Brief root definition: D W M indicates a thing, condition, or action remaining established and continuing over time; certain early uses also concern stillness, adherence, sustained circling, or persistently pursuing a task.

The crucial distinction is that continuity does not determine its own duration.

Something may last only a limited period or be presented as unending in another context.

A responsibility’s or action’s continuity also need not mean uninterrupted performance at every moment.

Verse translation and interpretation should therefore distinguish a condition remaining established, an action continuing, its protection, and endless duration. Each needs its own wording and contextual evidence.
