Grazing amid abundant provision, enjoying plenty freely, ease, and outings; examining يَرْتَعْ in Joseph’s story

The root R T ʿAYN in early Arabic is chiefly associated with grazing and eating amid ample provision, without the pressure of shortage. Its tangible example is an animal moving freely through a rich pasture and enjoying the available grass and fodder.

A broader human use is also known: enjoying food, ease, an outing, pleasure, and the resources available to someone.

Ratʿ is not precisely synonymous with playing. Play may be one leisure activity, but enjoying plenty and the opportunity to benefit from it are more prominent in this root.

One early explanation of the root’s central meaning reads:

الرَّاءُ وَالتَّاءُ وَالْعَيْنُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ؛ وَهِيَ تَدُلُّ عَلَى الِاتِّسَاعِ فِي الْمَأْكَلِ.

al-rāʾu wa-al-tāʾu wa-al-ʿaynu kalimatun wāḥidah; wa-hiya tadullu ʿalā al-ittisāʿi fī al-maʾkal

That is: “R, T, and ʿAYN form a single lexical family indicating ample scope in eating.”

رَتَعَ يَرْتَعُ can describe a person or animal eating what they wish, in a setting of abundance and fertility.

In the Qur’anic text based on the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, root R T ʿAYN occurs only once: يَرْتَعْ in Yūsuf 12:12.

Yūsuf 12:12

أَرْسِلْهُ مَعَنَا غَدًا يَرْتَعْ وَيَلْعَبْ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

arsilhu maʿanā ghadan yartaʿ wa-yalʿab wa-innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūn

Forqan’s published Persian translation, translated here for comparison:

Original Persian verse translation: Yūsuf 12:12

“Send him with us tomorrow so that he may enjoy himself and play; we are his guardians.”

This rendering appropriately expresses enjoyment while retaining play as a separate action. A brief explanation can clarify the word’s background in abundant pasture and ease.

A further issue matters: in other established readings, يَرْتَعِ or نَرْتَعِ is analyzed as a form of اِرْتَعَى, from the different root R ʿAYN Y. The differences therefore involve more than changing the subject from “he” to “we”; some forms also change the root and the verb’s sense.

1. Basic meaning in early Arabic

Grazing amid plenty and fertility

The following explanation describes the verb’s use for livestock:

رَتَعَتِ الْمَاشِيَةُ تَرْتَعُ رُتُوعًا، أَيْ أَكَلَتْ مَا شَاءَتْ.

rataʿati al-māshiyatu tartaʿu rutūʿan, ay akalat mā shāʾat

That is: “The livestock grazed, eating whatever they wished.”

This describes an opportunity to feed freely, rather than feeding under severe constraint.

Imagine a herd in a lush pasture, with ample fodder and room to move, without the pressure of scarce food or intense competition.

رَتَعَتْ suits such a scene of grazing and plentiful provision.

By contrast, merely eating a little amid drought and shortage does not necessarily convey this sense of abundant enjoyment.

Eating and drinking in ease and plenty, and livestock moving freely through pasture, are attested uses of the family.

In the animal example, pasture, food, and relative freedom to enjoy it occur together.

This description does not mean that every use of ratʿ must concern animals or literally eating grass.

From animal grazing to human enjoyment

The following expression also occurs for people:

خَرَجْنَا نَرْتَعُ وَنَلْعَبُ، أَيْ نَنْعَمُ وَنَلْهُو.

kharajnā nartaʿu wa-nalʿabu, ay nanʿamu wa-nalhū

That is: “We went out to enjoy ourselves and play,” or “to enjoy ease and amusement.”

This resembles the pairing of enjoyment and play in Yūsuf 12:12.

When the expression refers to people, it does not mean they literally eat grass like livestock.

One derivational explanation treats animals’ feeding as the basic use and human enjoyment as its extension.

This offers a defensible semantic connection, but it does not independently establish the historical order in which every use arose.

The tangible pastoral meaning and its human extension should therefore be distinguished without claiming a certain history of development.

Does ratʿ mean overeating or excess?

One lexical explanation glosses الرَّتْع as eating greedily or voraciously.

This is an attested explanation and should not be disregarded.

Other uses emphasize abundance and the freedom to enjoy it without necessarily implying greed.

Excess is therefore not an essential component of every use of the root.

Yūsuf 12:12 provides no independent indication that the brothers promised Joseph overeating or gluttony.

They were trying to persuade their father by offering Joseph enjoyment and play.

The translation should consequently not add greed, excess, or blame to the verb without contextual support.

2. Important Arabic forms and derivatives

رَتَعَ يَرْتَعُ: enjoying freely

رَتَعَ يَرْتَعُ is a basic Form I verb.

For livestock, the verb describes grazing in lush ground and feeding freely.

For people, it can describe eating and drinking in ease, enjoying ample provision, and an outing in suitable surroundings.

Attested verbal nouns include رَتْع, رُتُوع, and رِتَاع.

The only form of this root in the Qur’anic text based on Ḥafṣ’s reading is يَرْتَعْ.

رَتْع and رَتْعَة: enjoyment and abundance

رَتْع names the action or condition of freely enjoying plenty.

رَتْعَة is recorded for abundance, ease, and ample provision, with either a still or an open t sound: رَتْعَة and رَتَعَة.

An old proverb illustrates the meaning:

الْقَيْدُ وَالرَّتْعَةُ

al-qaydu wa-al-ratʿah

That is: “Fetters and plentiful provision.”

The expression is related of a captive whose captors provided ample food and comfort. Despite restricted movement, he gained weight.

One account identifies ʿAmr ibn al-Ṣaʿiq as the proverb’s first speaker; another relates that Ghaḍbān al-Shaybānī used it in answering al-Ḥajjāj.

The proverb matters because it connects ratʿah with abundant provision, without requiring freedom from every restriction.

Someone may be confined in one respect yet enjoy ample food and comfort in another.

Ratʿ should therefore not be translated simply as “absolute freedom.” The freedom involved chiefly concerns enjoyment amid plenty.

رَاتِع: enjoying plenty or grazing

رَاتِع is the active participle of رَتَعَ.

For animals, it describes livestock grazing freely in pasture.

For people, it can describe someone enjoying ease and abundance.

Attested plurals for grazing livestock include رِتَاع, رُتَّع, رُتُع, and رُتُوع. رَوَاتِع is also recorded as a plural of the feminine descriptor رَاتِعَة; رَاتِعُون is used for people.

These plural forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

مَرْتَع and مَرَاتِع: a place of pasture and enjoyment

مَرْتَع is a place noun: somewhere livestock graze and find suitable food.

Its plural is مَرَاتِع.

The word is familiar in both Arabic and Persian in the sense of pasture.

By extension, it can denote a place of human ease and enjoyment: an environment offering ample provision.

The extension connects pasture with a setting of human enjoyment, but it does not justify calling every pleasant place a مَرْتَع without regard to actual usage.

In some literary expressions, the word can describe a setting for excessive indulgence. That judgment depends on the situation and behavior described.

أَرْتَعَ: providing the opportunity to graze and enjoy

أَرْتَعَ is a Form IV verb.

In أَرْتَعَ إِبِلَهُ, someone takes camels to pasture or lets them graze freely.

Here, the subject provides the opportunity to graze; in رَتَعَتِ الْإِبِلُ, the camels themselves are the subject.

أَرْتَعَ الْمَطَرُ is also used of rain that produces vegetation and suitable pasture.

The rain does not itself eat grass. Its result—providing food for animals—explains the use of the verb.

أَرْتَعَتِ الْأَرْضُ is used of land with abundant herbage and pasture.

These uses show that the family covers both grazing itself and providing the conditions for it.

مُرْتِع and مُرْتِعُون

مُرْتِع belongs to Form IV.

Applied to land, it describes a place providing ample pasture and fodder.

Applied to people, مُرْتِعُون can describe those enjoying ease and abundance.

مُرْتِع should not be confused with مُرْتَاع.

In its familiar use, مُرْتَاع belongs to اِرْتَاعَ from R W ʿAYN and concerns fear or alarm, rather than enjoyment of pasture.

رَتَّاع: a seeker of lush pasture

رَتَّاع can describe someone who follows or seeks rich pastures for livestock.

Here, the فَعَّال pattern suits repeated or habitual pursuit of pasture; the pattern alone does not require a professional occupation.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be imposed on the brothers’ description in Joseph’s story.

رَتَعَ فِي لَحْمِهِ: figurative backbiting

The following is used figuratively for backbiting:

رَتَعَ فِي لَحْمِهِ

rataʿa fī laḥmihi

It means speaking ill of someone behind their back.

The image treats unrestricted enjoyment of another person’s flesh as an intrusion upon their honor.

This is a particular attested figurative use.

It does not mean that every occurrence of the root denotes backbiting.

There is no reference to backbiting in يَرْتَعْ in Yūsuf 12:12.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

رِتَاع in al-Quṭāmī’s poetry

The Umayyad poet al-Quṭāmī, praising Zufar ibn al-Ḥārith al-Kilābī, says:

أَكُفْرًا بَعْدَ رَدِّ الْمَوْتِ عَنِّي
وَبَعْدَ عَطَائِكَ الْمِائَةَ الرِّتَاعَا

a-kufran baʿda raddi al-mawti ʿannī / wa-baʿda ʿaṭāʾika al-miʾata al-ritāʿā

Approximate meaning:

“Ingratitude after you turned death away from me, and after your gift of a hundred freely grazing camels?”

الرِّتَاعَا describes camels grazing freely and enjoying abundant fodder; it is a plural descriptor here.

The verse provides tangible evidence for grazing amid ample pasture.

Early grammatical explanations likewise gloss الرِّتَاع as camels grazing wherever they wish.

A transmitted variant has الرِّبَاع, with b rather than t, which belongs to another lexical family and has a different meaning.

Al-Quṭāmī belongs to the Umayyad period. This is therefore evidence from the Islamic period, not an independently dated pre-Islamic witness.

لَحْمِي رَتَعْ in Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil’s poetry

A verse attributed to Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil reads:

وَيُحَيِّينِي إِذَا لَاقَيْتُهُ
وَإِذَا يَخْلُو لَهُ لَحْمِي رَتَعْ

wa-yuḥayyīnī idhā lāqaytuhu / wa-idhā yakhlū lahu laḥmī rataʿ

“He greets me when I meet him, but when alone with my flesh, he feeds freely.”

The verse depicts someone who is friendly in person but speaks ill of the poet in his absence.

The image concerns a person’s honor rather than literal eating.

In literary terms, the poet compares backbiting to eating human flesh.

This has a conceptual resemblance to Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12:

أَيُحِبُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ يَأْكُلَ لَحْمَ أَخِيهِ مَيْتًا

a-yuḥibbu aḥadukum an yaʾkula laḥma akhīhi maytan

“Would any of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother?”

However, ratʿ does not occur in that verse. The Qur’anic image of eating a brother’s flesh must not be counted as an occurrence of this root.

Suwayd’s verse is transmitted with different wordings, including the opening وَحَبِيبٌ لِي. One wording should therefore not be declared the sole form of the poem without evidence.

Suwayd was a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The date of this particular verse, however, is not securely known.

What these witnesses establish

The two poetic witnesses illustrate two important semantic fields.

Al-Quṭāmī’s verse concerns actual grazing freely amid plenty.

Suwayd’s verse applies the same field figuratively to human conduct.

The comparison shows that applying ratʿ to people need not entail literal eating.

The evidence does not, however, limit the root in all its uses to blameworthy indulgence, overeating, or backbiting.

For its general meaning, freely enjoying available abundance remains a more appropriate explanation.

No independently established pre-Islamic verse with uncontested attribution and wording emerged from the witnesses examined. This limitation should be retained; Islamic-period evidence must not be assigned to a time before revelation without justification.

4. Qur’anic يَرْتَعْ in Yūsuf 12:12

Yūsuf 12:12

أَرْسِلْهُ مَعَنَا غَدًا يَرْتَعْ وَيَلْعَبْ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

arsilhu maʿanā ghadan yartaʿ wa-yalʿab wa-innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūn

“Send him with us tomorrow so that he may enjoy himself and play; we are his guardians.”

Morphology and syntax

In Ḥafṣ’s reading from ʿĀṣim, يَرْتَعْ is a masculine singular third-person Form I imperfect verb in the jussive.

Its implicit subject refers to Joseph.

It follows the request أَرْسِلْهُ, meaning:

“Send him with us so that he may enjoy himself.”

In the usual syntactic analysis, يَرْتَعْ is jussive as the result of a request. وَيَلْعَبْ is coordinated with it and is also jussive.

Two related but distinct actions are consequently expressed:

يَرْتَعْ: that he may enjoy ease and the plenty available around him.

وَيَلْعَبْ: and play.

Translating يَرْتَعْ precisely as “play” would obscure the distinction between the two verbs.

Together, they can suggest enjoyment, an outing, and play.

Variant readings: يَرْتَعْ, نَرْتَعْ, and يَرْتَعِ

This issue is particularly important for the present root study.

Different forms of this verb are transmitted in the readings of Yūsuf 12:12.

First reading: يَرْتَعْ وَيَلْعَبْ

This is the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Both verbs begin with y and refer to Joseph.

Here, يَرْتَعْ belongs to R T ʿAYN and concerns enjoyment and ease.

Second reading: نَرْتَعْ وَنَلْعَبْ

Both verbs have the first-person plural prefix n.

The speakers include themselves in the enjoyment of the outing and play.

This form occurs in established readings, including those of Abū ʿAmr and Ibn ʿĀmir.

The root remains R T ʿAYN, but the subject changes from Joseph to “we.”

Third reading: يَرْتَعِ وَيَلْعَبْ

Here, the final ʿayn of the first verb carries a kasrah vowel.

This form is analyzed as coming from اِرْتَعَى يَرْتَعِي, from R ʿAYN Y.

On this analysis, the verb concerns tending or pasturing livestock, rather than the Form I رَتَعَ denoting enjoyment amid plenty.

A first-person plural form with kasrah is also transmitted: نَرْتَعِ.

It can mean “that we may tend livestock.” The explanation “that we may care for one another” is also transmitted, so not all explanations of this reading are confined to herding.

The readings therefore introduce two kinds of difference: a change of subject from “he” to “we,” and, in some readings, a change of morphological analysis and root from R T ʿAYN to R ʿAYN Y.

Assessing the readings

This study uses Ḥafṣ’s reading to examine the form present in Forqan’s Qur’anic text.

Other readings should nevertheless remain visible, since their linguistic differences matter.

In particular, يَرْتَعِ with kasrah is not merely another pronunciation of the same يَرْتَعْ verb. In the analysis discussed here, it is formed from another root.

One interpretive preference favors the reading with y and a still ʿayn: the brothers were trying to persuade Jacob to send Joseph by promising Joseph ease and pleasure.

This preference fits the request’s context, but it does not establish that other recognized readings lack linguistic support.

The more precise finding is that Ḥafṣ’s reading refers to Joseph’s enjoyment; some readings describe the brothers’ enjoyment; and forms derived from اِرْتَعَى concern herding or care.

5. The context of Joseph’s story

Promising ease and play to gain the father’s trust

In Yūsuf 12:8, Yūsuf 12:9, and Yūsuf 12:10, the brothers discuss their father’s love for Joseph and how to remove him.

One proposal is to kill him; another is to leave him in a well.

Then, in Yūsuf 12:11, they say to their father:

يَا أَبَانَا مَا لَكَ لَا تَأْمَنَّا عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَنَاصِحُونَ

yā abānā mā laka lā taʾmannā ʿalā yūsufa wa-innā lahu la-nāṣiḥūn

“Our father, why do you not trust us with Joseph? We are his sincere well-wishers.”

In the next verse, they offer him an outing and enjoyment.

The promise is part of an attempt to persuade their father deceptively.

They describe activities pleasant for a child or young person: going out, enjoying the surroundings, and playing.

Their actual intention is different from their stated offer.

Two levels should therefore be distinguished:

First is the meaning of what the brothers say: يَرْتَعْ retains the sense of enjoyment and ease.

Second is the speakers’ intention: they use the promise to gain permission and carry out their own plan.

Deception belongs to the narrative situation; it is not part of the verb’s lexical meaning.

Is play condemned in this verse?

No. وَيَلْعَبْ appears in the brothers’ speech as an activity offered to persuade Jacob.

The wrongdoing condemned in the story is their plan to harm Joseph and deceive their father, rather than mere play or enjoyment of ease.

These matters should be kept distinct.

Conversely, because the speakers are acting deceptively, their words should not be treated as an independent Qur’anic command about the necessity or virtue of play.

The verse reports the characters’ words; the moral assessment of their conduct comes from the narrative as a whole.

Connection with Yūsuf 12:13 and Yūsuf 12:14

Their father speaks of his sadness at Joseph’s departure and his fear that a wolf might eat him while they are inattentive.

The brothers answer by emphasizing the strength of their group.

The promise of enjoyment is therefore accompanied by an assurance of safety.

Protection is expressed by the separate root Ḥ F Ẓ, not by ratʿ itself.

Nor does Ḥafṣ’s reading necessarily state that Joseph would tend livestock.

Connection with Yūsuf 12:17

The brothers later claim:

إِنَّا ذَهَبْنَا نَسْتَبِقُ وَتَرَكْنَا يُوسُفَ عِنْدَ مَتَاعِنَا

innā dhahabnā nastabiqu wa-taraknā yūsufa ʿinda matāʿinā

“We went racing and left Joseph beside our belongings.”

Their account continues the earlier picture of an outing and recreation.

نَسْتَبِقُ belongs to S B Q and concerns racing or competing to get ahead.

That is not the same root or meaning as يَرْتَعْ.

The truth or falsity of their report is also a separate question from the verb’s lexical sense.

The narrative as a whole, including Yūsuf 12:15 and Yūsuf 12:17, makes clear that they put Joseph in the well and brought their father a false account.

This does not change the lexical meaning of the enjoyment originally promised.

6. Distinctions from related roots

R T ʿAYN and R ʿAYN Y: enjoyment and grazing

R ʿAYN Y concerns grazing, tending, and care.

R T ʿAYN also has uses connected with grazing.

The overlap does not make them a single root.

رَعَى can describe both an animal grazing and someone caring for or tending others.

Ratʿ emphasizes enjoying plenty and the freedom to benefit from it.

Not every use of R ʿAYN Y necessarily conveys plentiful provision.

The distinction is especially important when assessing the variant readings of Yūsuf 12:12.

Study of R ʿAYN Y

R T ʿAYN and L ʿAYN B: enjoyment and play

L ʿAYN B concerns play and amusement.

It stands beside يَرْتَعْ in Yūsuf 12:12.

Together the verbs describe related aspects of a pleasant outing.

One expresses enjoyment and ease; the other explicitly expresses play.

Translating both simply as “play” would conceal the distinction.

R T ʿAYN and M T ʿAYN: benefiting and enjoyment

M T ʿAYN has uses involving benefit and enjoyment.

This helps explain why the Persian rendering “enjoy himself” suits يَرْتَعْ.

However, مَتَاع has a wider field, including possessions and temporary benefits.

R T ʿAYN has a more specific pastoral and alimentary background in these uses.

This is a tendency within their semantic fields rather than an absolute division between all occurrences.

Study of M T ʿAYN

R T ʿAYN and N ʿAYN M: enjoyment and blessing

نَعِمَ and نَعِيم concern ease, well-being, and blessing.

The expression نَرْتَعُ وَنَلْعَبُ has been explained as نَنْعَمُ وَنَلْهُو, “enjoy ease and amusement.”

This supports the connection between ratʿ and enjoyment of plenty.

Nevertheless, نِعْمَة belongs to a separate root.

Verses containing that root should not be counted as Qur’anic occurrences of R T ʿAYN.

Study of N ʿAYN M

R T ʿAYN and R W ʿAYN: enjoyment and fear

Attention to spelling and morphology matters here.

رَتَعَ belongs to R T ʿAYN.

اِرْتَاعَ, concerning fear or alarm, belongs to R W ʿAYN.

Their unvocalized forms can look similar.

يَرْتَعْ in Yūsuf 12:12 does not mean “become afraid.”

R T ʿAYN and R B ʿAYN: pasture, season, and place

R B ʿAYN includes uses concerning four, spring, residence, and pasture.

Spring vegetation can bring some uses close to the field of grazing.

The roots are nonetheless distinct.

The الرِّبَاع variant in al-Quṭāmī’s verse replaces t with b and changes the root under discussion.

7. Proposed translation for Forqan

Forqan’s existing Persian rendering of this root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is suitable; translated for comparison:

Original Persian verse translation: Yūsuf 12:12

“Send him with us tomorrow so that he may enjoy himself and play; we are his guardians.”

The Persian “enjoy himself” conveys the relevant benefit and ease.

The Persian rendering does not by itself reveal the tangible pastoral image of grazing freely amid plenty. That background is better explained in a word note or root study than inserted into the direct translation by adding pasture or food.

For the Qur’anic use, renderings such as “overeat,” “indulge in debauchery,” “behave without restraint,” or “backbite” should be avoided: the sentence does not establish those meanings.

For a human subject in Ḥafṣ’s reading, “graze” could misleadingly suggest herding or literally eating grass. “Enjoy himself” is more appropriate.

The morphological note should also retain the variant readings, since يَرْتَعِ, analyzed from R ʿAYN Y, has another sense.

8. Concluding findings

In its tangible uses, R T ʿAYN concerns grazing and eating freely amid plenty.

“Ample scope in eating” is one explanation of the family’s core meaning. Uses involving abundant food, ease, and enjoyment are also attested within it.

The family extends to human enjoyment of food, provision, ease, and an outing.

Figuratively, “feeding freely on another’s flesh” can mean backbiting and speaking ill of them; this particular sense should not be extended to every use of the root.

In the Qur’anic text based on Ḥafṣ’s reading, the root occurs once, in Yūsuf 12:12.

Joseph’s brothers ask their father to send him with them to enjoy an outing, ease, and play. The context shows that this promise helps gain their father’s trust and carry out their plan. Their deceptive intention nevertheless does not turn the lexical meaning of يَرْتَعْ into deception.

The readings also matter. يَرْتَعْ and نَرْتَعْ with a still ʿayn belong to R T ʿAYN; forms with kasrah can, in the early analysis discussed here, derive from اِرْتَعَى and R ʿAYN Y.

This distinction should be retained when presenting the readings.

A concise definition can therefore be proposed:

R T ʿAYN primarily concerns grazing and eating freely where food is abundant. It extends to people’s enjoyment of ease, provision, outings, and leisure. Some figurative uses concern uninhibited use of another’s wealth or honor, but this negative judgment is not an essential part of the root. In Ḥafṣ’s reading of Yūsuf 12:12, يَرْتَعْ refers to Joseph enjoying ease and an outing, alongside the separate verb وَيَلْعَبْ, “and play.”
