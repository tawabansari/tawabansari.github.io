Becoming still, ceasing to move, and remaining in place

Brief root meaning: Becoming still and the subsiding of movement or activity; remaining in a position and ceasing to flow or advance. Depending on its subject, the root can describe water, wind, ships, people, and other things.

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

The three-consonant root R K D concerns stillness, settling, and cessation of movement. Its family can describe water ceasing to flow, wind subsiding, or a ship remaining in place.

In Arabic, رَكَدَ الماءُ means that water became still or stopped flowing. رَكَدَتِ الرِّيحُ describes wind subsiding, and رَكَدَتِ السَّفِينَةُ describes a ship that has stopped moving.

The root is not restricted to natural phenomena. Arabic also records its use for people ceasing movement or activity, a balance settling into equilibrium, and even a heavy, full bowl.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in a clear depiction of ships ceasing to advance on the sea.

The form examined and the Qur’anic derivative

رَوَاكِدَ — Al-Shūrā 42:33

This is the broken plural of رَاكِدَة, on the pattern فَوَاعِل. رَاكِدَة is the feminine active participle corresponding to رَاكِد.

In the verse it describes ships remaining in place, unable to advance.

Arabic also has other derivatives in this family:

  • رَكَدَ: Became still, stopped moving, or remained fixed.
  • يَرْكُدُ: Becomes still or ceases to move.
  • رُكُود: Stillness, settling, or cessation.
  • رَاكِد: Something or someone that has become still or ceased moving.
  • رَاكِدَة: The feminine of رَاكِد.
  • رَوَاكِد: Plural of رَاكِدَة; things remaining still or fixed.
  • رَكْدَة: An instance or brief period of settling or pausing.
  • مَرَاكِد: Places where people or other beings rest or remain.
  • رَكُود: In one lexical use, a description of a large, heavy, full bowl.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but only رَوَاكِدَ occurs in the Qur’an in the form listed here.

The Qur’anic study should therefore focus on that single occurrence; the other derivatives help explain the family’s wider semantic range.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Stillness and remaining fixed

An early lexical explanation identifies one principal meaning for R K D: stillness.

It illustrates this with water and wind settling, a balance becoming level, and a group of people becoming still.

These uses describe a condition in which movement, oscillation, or previous activity has subsided or stopped.

Stillness here should not be turned into the absolute absence of every kind of motion in every part of an object.

Standing water may have small surface waves while lacking a forward flow. A becalmed ship may rock with the water without making progress along its route.

In ordinary usage, rukūd should therefore be understood relative to the movement or activity in question.

What matters is the cessation of a flow, displacement, or activity that existed before or was expected to continue.

Standing and flowing water

One of the clearest Arabic expressions is مَاء رَاكِد, “standing water.”

Standing water contrasts with water flowing from one place to another.

This contrast matters because standing water does not necessarily mean spoiled, contaminated, or unusable water.

Spoilage and contamination are additional properties requiring separate evidence. رَاكِد itself describes the water’s state of flow or stillness.

Today one may likewise speak of standing water in a pond, pool, or hollow. This accords with the older meaning, but none of these locations belongs to the lexical definition of rukūd.

Wind, heat, and the sun

Lexical records use رَكَدَتِ الرِّيحُ for wind becoming still.

They also record رَكَدَ الحَرُّ and رَكَدَتِ الشَّمْسُ concerning the heat and sun around midday.

These uses concern a condition described as settled or apparently stationary.

Around midday, the sun’s changing altitude becomes less noticeable near the highest part of its daily path. This concerns its apparent height, not an assertion that its entire apparent motion slows or stops.

The expression رَكَدَتِ الشَّمْسُ does not establish that the sun physically stops moving in space.

The language describes an observed condition rather than presenting a theory of celestial structure and motion.

Distinguishing observational language from scientific explanation matters when interpreting the root.

A balance settling into equilibrium

رَكَدَ المِيزَانُ describes a balance that has settled and become level.

Here rukūd concerns the end of oscillation, not the balance becoming unusable.

When the pans continue to rise and fall, they have not yet settled. Once the oscillation subsides, the balance’s equilibrium can be read or assessed.

In this use, stillness can be desirable and necessary for the task.

The root therefore carries no inherent negative judgment.

In contemporary Persian, “rukūd” often suggests stalled progress or weak economic activity. In early Arabic, the same family can describe something settling into balance.

People becoming still and activity subsiding

رَكَدَ القَوْمُ is recorded for a group that has become quiet or ceased movement and activity.

Here people are the subject of the stillness.

The verb alone does not determine whether they stopped because of fatigue, fear, rest, expectation, or completion of a task.

The reason must be established from context.

رَكَدَ العَصِيرُ is also recorded for grape juice whose bubbling has subsided.

The core meaning concerns the reduction or cessation of bubbling, not grapes or a particular beverage as such.

These examples show that one root can describe displacement, oscillation, bubbling, and collective activity without making these phenomena identical.

رَوَاكِد: fixed hearthstones

Early Arabic also uses رَوَاكِد for أَثَافِي: stones supporting a cooking pot.

These stones ordinarily remain in place as supports for the vessel.

Here the word names things whose stability in position is prominent.

The referent differs from Al-Shūrā 42:33. In the verse, the word describes ships; in the hearth usage, it refers to stationary stones.

The shared meaning is stability in position, not the material of the stones, the shape of the hearth, or the kind of cooking equipment.

Resting places and a heavy bowl

Lexical records use مَرَاكِد for places where a person or another being stays or rests.

In some uses it refers to recesses and shelters in the terrain.

جَفْنَةٌ رَكُودٌ also describes a large, heavy, full bowl.

Heaviness and fullness are prominent here. A connection with sitting firmly and heavily is conceivable, but should not be presented as the proven historical origin of the name.

رَكُود in this particular sense must be distinguished from رُكُود, “stillness.” Vowels and word formation matter.

3. Early poetic evidence and the range of usage

رَكْدَة: pausing and settling after alarm

A poem by al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm al-Ṭāʾī, an Umayyad poet, contains:

لَهَا كُلَّمَا رِيعَتْ صَدَاةٌ وَرَكْدَةٌ
بِمُصْدَانَ أَعْلَى ابْنَيْ شَمَامِ الْبَوَائِنِ

lahā kullamā rīʿat ṣadātun wa-rakdatun / bi-muṣdāna aʿlā ibnay Shamāmi al-bawāʾini

Approximate translation: “Whenever she is startled, there is an echo and a pause, at a place of echoes high on the twin heights of Shamām.” صَدَاة concerns sound or echo; مُصْدَان is associated with a place where sound reverberates and should not simply be translated as a shelter.

The verse describes a mountain animal reacting to alarm and then becoming still for a time.

An early explanation describes it striking the rock with its forefeet, then stopping to listen to the echo.

رَكْدَة refers to the pause or stillness following that movement or reaction.

The example matters because it shows stillness following movement: the animal reacts and then stops.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ lived after the revelation of the Qur’an. This is evidence from early Islamic Arabic, not a poem to be presented as pre-Islamic.

مَرَاكِد: places of staying and shelter

A poem attributed to a Hudhalī poet reads:

أَرَتْهُ مِنَ الْجَرْبَاءِ فِي كُلِّ مَوْطِنٍ
طِبَابًا فَمَثْوَاهُ النَّهَارَ الْمَرَاكِدُ

arat-hu mina al-jarbāʾi fī kulli mawṭinin / ṭibāban fa-mathwāhu al-nahāra al-marākidu

Approximate translation: “At every place it showed him strips of the sky; the recesses were his dwelling by day.” The strips refer to portions of sky visible between the terrain’s openings.

One transmitted explanation describes a wild ass pursued by horses, taking refuge among mountain ravines; details of the scene and attribution should be kept separate from the word’s lexical meaning.

المَرَاكِد here denotes places where the animal stays or hides.

Lexical transmissions differ in attribution and wording. The evidence examined does not establish a definite pre-Islamic date for this verse.

It is useful evidence for Arabic usage, but should not be described as certainly pre-Islamic without further investigation.

Its value lies in illustrating the family’s locational branch: a place where a being remains and stops moving.

A fixed location and moving parts

Lexical records also contain a use concerning a بَكْرَة, a pulley wheel, which at first appears contrary to the ordinary meaning of stillness.

رَكَدَتِ البَكْرَةُ is explained both as the pulley remaining fixed and as its revolving.

This variation shows that certain restricted or technical uses can differ from the ordinary sense of stillness.

A device’s fixed location may also be distinguished from its rotating parts. Without clear historical evidence, however, that distinction should not be asserted as the established explanation for both usages.

Such cases remind us to test a proposed semantic core against evidence, rather than impose one mechanical definition on every use.

This restricted usage does not turn رَوَاكِد in Al-Shūrā 42:33 into rotation or rapid motion. The verse’s context independently indicates ships ceasing to advance.

4. The Qur’anic occurrence: رَوَاكِدَ in Al-Shūrā 42:33

Al-Shūrā 42:32

Verse text:

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ الْجَوَارِ فِي الْبَحْرِ كَالْأَعْلَامِ

wa-min āyātihi al-jawāri fī al-baḥri ka-al-aʿlāmi

Al-Shūrā 42:32

Translation:

“Among His signs are the ships sailing on the sea like mountains.”

Al-Shūrā 42:33

Verse text:

إِنْ يَشَأْ يُسْكِنِ الرِّيحَ فَيَظْلَلْنَ رَوَاكِدَ عَلَىٰ ظَهْرِهِ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ

in yashaʾ yuskini al-rīḥa fa-yaẓlalna rawākida ʿalā ẓahrihi inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūrin

Al-Shūrā 42:33

Translation:

“If He wills, He stills the wind, and they remain motionless upon its surface. In that are signs for everyone steadfast and grateful.”

These two verses form one continuous picture.

The first introduces ships moving on the sea, appearing like mountains.

The second describes the possibility of the wind subsiding and those same ships ceasing to advance.

رَوَاكِدَ: ships no longer advancing

رَوَاكِدَ is the plural of رَاكِدَة and here describes ships whose movement has stopped.

Grammatically it is the accusative predicate of the incomplete verb فَيَظْلَلْنَ.

The feminine plural pronoun in فَيَظْلَلْنَ refers to الجَوَار in the preceding verse.

الجَوَار is the plural of جَارِيَة, here denoting ships sailing on the sea.

The subject is therefore the ships themselves, not the people aboard them.

This matters in translation. The verse describes ships remaining stationary on the sea rather than directly describing their passengers ceasing movement.

A ship’s stopping also affects its passengers, but this consequence comes from the seafaring context, not the direct meaning of رَوَاكِد.

Distinguishing يُسْكِنِ الرِّيحَ from رَوَاكِدَ

Two related word families occur in a clear relationship:

يُسْكِنِ الرِّيحَ concerns stilling the wind.

فَيَظْلَلْنَ رَوَاكِدَ concerns ships ceasing to move.

The expressions do not merely repeat one thing; they describe two connected phenomena.

The wind becomes still, and ships dependent on it cease making progress.

رَوَاكِد thus emphasizes the resulting condition of the ships.

“Remain motionless” or “cease to advance” suits this usage.

رَاكِد here does not mean destroyed, broken, or sunk.

A ship may remain afloat while no longer advancing.

عَلَىٰ ظَهْرِهِ: remaining on the sea’s surface

The pronoun in عَلَىٰ ظَهْرِهِ refers to البَحْر, “the sea,” in the preceding verse.

The phrase therefore concerns the ships remaining on the sea’s surface.

ظَهْر here refers to the upper or outward surface of something.

It should not be restricted to the physical back of a human or animal, nor used to infer some new physical structure of the sea.

The image is clear: the ships are still on the sea but are not progressing along their course.

This distinguishes becoming still from sinking.

Does rukūd mean the cessation of all motion?

In ordinary language, saying that a ship has stopped usually means it has stopped progressing on its route.

That does not necessarily exclude every vibration, rocking movement, or small displacement.

A floating ship may move slightly with waves or currents while remaining stalled in its voyage.

Conversely, this explanation should not be imposed on the verse as though it discusses such small movements or the precise mechanisms of ocean currents.

In language accessible to its audience, the verse describes ships ceasing to move when the wind becomes still.

“Remain motionless” is therefore appropriate in context, while lexical analysis should clarify that the stillness concerns the relevant movement, not necessarily the absolute absence of all physical motion.

فَيَظْلَلْنَ: continuation of a condition

فَيَظْلَلْنَ comes from ظَلَّ, which in such constructions expresses continuation or remaining in a condition.

Here the ships remain becalmed after the wind subsides.

Continuation is expressed by فَيَظْلَلْنَ, while رَوَاكِد specifies the condition.

This distinction matters because رَوَاكِد alone does not determine how long the pause lasts.

The word does not establish whether the ships stop for hours, days, or forever.

Nor does ظَلَّ here necessarily restrict their remaining to daylight hours.

The appropriate meaning is the continuation of the condition caused by the wind becoming still.

Does the verse concern every ship in every era?

Sailing ships use wind for propulsion. In that situation, the wind subsiding can stop their progress.

That is the relationship the verse depicts.

It does not establish that every ship, in every period and with every technology, moves solely through wind power.

Vessels with engines or other propulsion can move without wind.

This does not alter the verse’s meaning: its depiction concerns ships whose movement depends on wind.

Nor should modern technology make the Qur’anic image seem merely historical and insignificant.

The dependence of motion on natural conditions and means remains understandable. That broader observation must not replace the particular meaning of رَوَاكِد.

A ship’s stillness: benefit and difficulty

After describing the becalmed ships, Al-Shūrā 42:33 says:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ

inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūrin

Al-Shūrā 42:33

There are signs in this for a steadfast and grateful person.

The pairing of steadfastness and gratitude is noteworthy.

A moving ship enables travel, trade, reaching a destination, and benefiting from the sea. Stopping may bring waiting, difficulty, and lost opportunities.

Yet these consequences do not constitute the lexical meaning of رَوَاكِد.

It describes a state of movement; benefit or harm depends on actual circumstances and consequences.

Stillness is not always undesirable. In some situations a subsiding wind or halted movement reduces danger, while in a sailing voyage it may prevent further progress.

The word rukūd therefore does not establish that every stop is punishment or every movement a blessing.

Connection with Luqmān 31:31

Luqmān also combines the image of a moving ship with صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ.

Verse text:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ الْفُلْكَ تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ بِنِعْمَتِ اللَّهِ لِيُرِيَكُمْ مِنْ آيَاتِهِ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ

a-lam tara anna al-fulka tajrī fī al-baḥri bi-niʿmati Allāhi li-yuriyakum min āyātihi inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūrin

Luqmān 31:31

Translation:

“Have you not seen that ships sail on the sea by the favor of The God, so that He may show you some of His signs? In that are signs for everyone steadfast and grateful.”

Luqmān 31:31

This verse highlights ships moving, while Al-Shūrā 42:33 also presents their ceasing to advance.

Both share the phrase لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ.

The connection suggests that signs are not seen only in movement and reaching a destination. Changing conditions and human dependence on the means of travel can also invite reflection.

صَبَّار does not here mean motionlessness or passive surrender. Qur’anic patience has a broader field than silently enduring difficulty, including steadfastness and considered conduct in different circumstances.

Gratitude likewise is not confined to reaching a destination; recognizing and appreciating blessings matters too.

These reflections arise from reading related verses, not from the direct meaning of R K D.

Distinguishing stillness from a ship’s destruction

The next verse says:

Al-Shūrā 42:34

أَوْ يُوبِقْهُنَّ بِمَا كَسَبُوا وَيَعْفُ عَنْ كَثِيرٍ

aw yūbiqhunna bi-mā kasabū wa-yaʿfu ʿan kathīrin

Al-Shūrā 42:34

“Or He destroys them for what they have earned, and pardons much.”

يُوبِقْهُنَّ concerns destruction.

It belongs to the different family W B Q and does not mean the same as رَوَاكِد.

In Al-Shūrā 42:33, the ships remain on the sea but cease moving.

Al-Shūrā 42:34 presents their destruction as another possibility.

This contrast confirms that rukūd in the preceding verse does not mean destruction.

The connection between human deeds and harmful consequences in verse 34 should not become a blanket judgment that every maritime accident or injured person is necessarily suffering for personal sin.

Such a judgment does not follow from رَوَاكِد. Responsibility and consequences require examination of the related verses as a whole.

5. Comparison with related roots

R K D (rukūd) and S K N (sukūn)

These roots have closely related semantic fields.

Sukūn concerns becoming still and the subsiding of movement or disturbance.

Rukūd likewise concerns settling, ceasing to flow, or remaining in a condition.

Al-Shūrā 42:33 brings both fields into one sentence:

يُسْكِنِ الرِّيحَ concerns the wind becoming still.

رَوَاكِدَ concerns ships consequently ceasing movement.

This makes their respective functions in this verse clear, but does not prove that sukūn is always the cause and rukūd always the result throughout Arabic.

Wind and water can themselves be described directly with رَكَدَ.

The distinction belongs to this sentence’s actual relationship rather than an absolute rule governing the two roots.

R K D (rukūd) and J R Y (jary)

Jary concerns movement and flowing.

In Al-Shūrā 42:32, الجَوَار denotes sailing ships; in the next verse those same ships are described as رَوَاكِد.

The words form a clear contrast in this context.

الجَوَار emphasizes the ships’ motion, and رَوَاكِد their ceasing that motion.

“No longer sailing onward” can therefore help explain رَوَاكِد.

Jary and rukūd are not absolute opposites in every use. The kind of flow or stillness depends on the verb’s subject.

R K D (rukūd) and W Q F (waqf)

Waqf concerns standing or stopping movement.

In some uses, waqf and rukūd can refer to similar conditions.

Waqf may also describe stopping a person or thing, while the clear Qur’anic use of R K D describes a condition in which ships find themselves.

In Al-Shūrā 42:33, the wind becomes still and the ships remain becalmed.

The verse does not use waqf to command someone directly to halt the ships.

“Stopping” is therefore a suitable rendering of the result, but the two Arabic roots should not be treated as identical in every formation and use.

R K D (rukūd) and R S W (rusūw)

R S W concerns settling and becoming firmly established.

In Hūd 11:41, مُرْسَاهَا accompanies مَجْرَاهَا, concerning the ship’s coming to rest or mooring.

Rusūw can emphasize a ship settling in its place, while rukūd in Al-Shūrā 42:33 emphasizes its ceasing to advance.

The conditions can coincide but are not identical.

A ship can stop progressing for lack of wind without anchoring in a harbor.

An anchored ship may be secured intentionally in a particular place, rather than merely stopped by the wind becoming still.

رَوَاكِد should therefore not be translated as “anchored ships” without evidence.

R K D (rukūd) and R K B (rukūb)

The roots share their first two consonants but differ in meaning.

R K B primarily concerns riding a mount or boarding a means of transport.

R K D concerns settling and ceasing movement.

Someone can be aboard a ship whether it is moving or becalmed.

Being aboard and being stationary are independent descriptions.

Shared letters do not themselves prove a derivational relationship.

R K D (rukūd) and R Q D (ruqād)

R Q D primarily concerns sleeping.

R K D concerns stillness and cessation of movement.

A sleeping person may seem still, but sleeping and remaining stationary are not lexically identical.

In Al-Kahf 18:18, رُقُود describes sleeping people; in Al-Shūrā 42:33, رَوَاكِد describes ships no longer moving.

Their visual similarity does not mean that the ships in Al-Shūrā are “asleep” or that the people of the cave have merely “stopped moving.”

Each root has its own meaning.

6. Interpretive challenges and translation proposals for Forqan

The main translation challenge is distinguishing stillness, stopping, and destruction.

رَوَاكِد in Al-Shūrā 42:33 describes ships still on the sea’s surface but no longer advancing.

“Motionless,” “stationary,” “no longer advancing,” and “remaining in place” are therefore reasonable equivalents.

“Sunk,” “destroyed,” “permanently disabled,” and “anchored” add features not supplied by the word itself.

The modern Persian social and economic sense of “rukūd” should not be imported into the verse either.

“Economic stagnation” concerns reduced or halted economic activity and can be an extension of the notion of activity stopping. The Qur’anic subject here, however, is ships and their movement at sea.

رَوَاكِد should not be translated as “ships in economic recession” or “unprosperous ships.”

Proposed rendering of the Qur’anic word

رَوَاكِدَ — Al-Shūrā 42:33

Defensible equivalents include:

  • “Motionless”
  • “Stationary”
  • “No longer advancing”
  • “Remaining in place”

In the full sentence, “and they remain motionless upon its surface” is natural and appropriate.

An alternative making cessation of progress more explicit is:

“Then they cease to advance on its surface.”

The supplied Persian wording, corresponding to “they remain motionless upon its surface,” is defensible in meaning and construction and does not require compulsory replacement.

Proposed Forqan word-box explanation

Word: رَوَاكِدَ

Root: R K D

Brief root meaning: Becoming still, remaining fixed, and ceasing movement or flow.

Meaning in this verse: Ships that cease advancing on the sea’s surface when the wind becomes still.

This explanation preserves the root meaning while specifying the derivative’s function in its context.

7. Conclusion and final definition

R K D forms an Arabic family concerning stillness, settling, and remaining fixed.

In early uses, water stops flowing, wind becomes still, a ship stops moving, a balance settles, and people cease movement or activity.

Other branches call fixed hearthstones رَوَاكِد and use مَرَاكِد for places where people or animals stay and rest.

This range shows that rukūd is not limited to stopping spatial movement. Yet weakness, corruption, uselessness, or destruction should not be added to every use.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: رَوَاكِدَ in Al-Shūrā 42:33.

It describes ships ceasing to advance on the sea when the wind becomes still.

The preceding verse introduces those ships as الجَوَار, sailing vessels. The contrast between movement and its cessation is therefore clear within the context itself.

This condition differs from the ships’ destruction in the next verse and does not lexically mean sinking or perishing.

Final definition of R K D:

In its principal field, R K D denotes becoming still, ceasing movement, flow, or activity, and remaining in a condition or position.

The verb’s subject determines what ceases: flowing water, blowing wind, a ship’s progress, a balance’s oscillation, or human movement and activity.

The Qur’anic رَوَاكِد describes ships no longer advancing, not destroyed ships or necessarily vessels devoid of every minor physical movement.

The root carries no inherent positive or negative moral judgment. Stillness may provide welcome calm and stability in one situation and prevent further progress in another.

Understanding it therefore requires distinguishing the state of stillness itself, what causes it, and its consequences. The lexical meaning is stillness or cessation; cause and result are established by the sentence and context.
