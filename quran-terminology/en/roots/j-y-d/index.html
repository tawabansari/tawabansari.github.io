1. Introducing the root

Arabic J Y D concerns the neck. Its principal word, jīd, names the bodily part joining head and trunk, where necklaces and other ornaments may be worn. Some Arabic usages particularly describe women’s necks, associating them with length, proportion, and beauty.

Persian “gardan,” neck, is a clear, natural equivalent. Early Arabic evidence reveals differences in how jīd, ʿunuq, and raqabah are used. These mostly concern context, the body part’s role in an image, and descriptive or figurative usage, rather than wholly separate and incompatible meanings.

Ibn Fāris identifies the neck as the semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah. Lisān al-ʿArab and other authoritative lexicons likewise define jīd as neck, while recording different scopes: the whole neck, its front, or where a necklace sits.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, as jīdihā in Al-Masad 111:5. This limited occurrence, together with early Arabic evidence, offers a clear account of the word’s meaning.

2. Forms and derivatives

The main recorded Arabic forms are:

Jīd: neck. A noun retaining that meaning in its Qur’anic occurrence.

Ajyād and juyūd: two recorded plurals of jīd, meaning necks.

Jayad: length or shapeliness of the neck. Lexicons record this verbal noun for an elongated neck, sometimes including proportion and beauty.

Jayida: a verb meaning to become long-necked or acquire a shapely neck. It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ajyad: long-necked or well-proportioned in the neck. Some lexical accounts apply it both to a person and to the neck itself, as in ʿunuqun ajyadu.

Jaydāʾ: the feminine adjective, describing a woman with a long, elongated, or shapely neck.

Jaydānah: an adjective close to jaydāʾ, describing a woman with a beautiful, proportioned neck.

Some lexicons also record jūd as a plural of the adjective jaydāʾ. Its written resemblance to forms in J W D does not remove the need to examine its morphology and meaning separately.

Only jīd with the attached pronoun -hā occurs in the Qur’an. Jīd names the body part, whereas jayad and adjectives such as jaydāʾ describe its features. An adjective’s meaning must not be imposed on the basic noun.

3. Semantic core and physical image

The neck as a body part

The core of J Y D is clear and physical: the neck. Brief root meaning: The neck; in descriptive forms, its length, elongation, and proportions.

Ibn Fāris writes:

الْجِيمُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالدَّالُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْعُنُقُ.

al-jīmu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-dālu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-ʿunuq

That is: “Jīm, yāʾ, and dāl have one semantic principle: the neck.”

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: the neck

This links the family to a particular bodily part rather than abstract beauty, pride, or obedience.

The neck supports the head and participates in turning, raising, and lowering it. It also provides a location for a necklace, collar, or, sometimes, a rope or binding.

These physical features matter in Arabic literary imagery, but a body part’s characteristics are distinct from its name. A long neck may be considered beautiful, while jīd itself simply denotes the neck.

The whole neck or its front?

Lexical sources record three explanations: the neck generally, its front, and the place of a necklace.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs first define jīd as neck, then report the necklace-site and front-of-neck definitions. The variation does not establish one precise anatomical boundary.

Lisān and Tāj: the scope of the neck definition

The word should not be restricted to a small portion of the neck without evidence. Early poetry often concerns its overall form, length, and movement.

Neck is therefore the principal, broader equivalent. Front of the neck or necklace-site may help explain a particular occurrence, but does not replace the general definition.

Length and beauty

A proportionately long, elongated neck was a feature of beauty in Arabic descriptions. Ajyad and jaydāʾ belong to that descriptive field.

Some dictionaries define jayad as length accompanied by beauty, or slenderness with length. This describes the neck’s quality rather than naming the body part.

Jīd and jaydāʾ can be compared with eye and beautiful-eyed: naming the organ does not establish a favorable quality. Beauty must come from an adjective, comparison, or context.

4. Usage in pre-Islamic Arabic

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets shows jīd as an established word before Qur’anic revelation. It appears in descriptions of beauty, movement, and literary imagery.

The proportioned neck in Imruʾ al-Qays

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

وَجِيدٍ كَجِيدِ الرِّئْمِ لَيْسَ بِفَاحِشٍ
إِذَا هِيَ نَصَّتْهُ وَلَا بِمُعَطَّلِ

wa-jīdin ka-jīdi al-riʾmi laysa bi-fāḥishin / idhā hiya naṣṣat-hu wa-lā bi-muʿaṭṭali

Translation: “And a neck like the neck of a white gazelle, not excessively long when she raises it, nor without adornment.”

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: raising, proportion, and adornment

The poet compares a woman’s neck to that of a white gazelle. Al-riʾm denotes the white gazelle, while naṣṣat-hu expresses raising the neck.

Laysa bi-fāḥishin excludes excessive, disproportionate length. The poet praises proportion as well as length.

Wa-lā bi-muʿaṭṭali indicates that the neck is not bare of jewelry.

The witness matters in two ways: jīd names the woman’s neck, while the rest of the verse supplies beauty, proportion, and adornment.

Even a word associated with beauty in literary tradition can receive meaningful repeated description and emphasis. The reliable point is that the poet names the neck, then independently describes its proportions, raising, and jewelry. These associations do not make beauty necessary to every occurrence of jīd.

Movement in ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād includes:

وَكَأَنَّمَا الْتَفَتَتْ بِجِيدِ جَدَايَةٍ
رَشَإٍ مِنَ الْغِزْلَانِ حُرٍّ أَرْثَمِ

wa-ka-annamā iltafatat bi-jīdi jadāyatin / rashaʾin mina al-ghizlāni ḥurrin arthami

Translation: “As though, turning to look, she turned with the neck of a noble young gazelle, white-marked on its upper lip and nose.”

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: the young gazelle and artham

The beloved’s turning neck is compared with a young gazelle’s movement.

Jadāyah and rashaʾ concern a young gazelle; the other descriptions complete the animal image.

Unlike the Imruʾ al-Qays verse, the image concerns movement as well as shape and proportion.

The neck’s movement and flexibility can become a literary comparison. Jīd still means neck; the comparison and context supply the beauty of its movement.

The witness shows an active word in physical description within the transmitted pre-Islamic poetic tradition, depicting different conditions of the neck.

Male usage and the neck’s condition in Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur reads:

وَلَقَدْ أَرُوحُ إِلَى التُّجَّارِ مُرَجَّلًا
مَذِلًا بِمَالِي لَيِّنًا أَجْيَادِي

wa-la-qad arūḥu ilā al-tujjāri murajjalan / madhilan bi-mālī layyinan ajyādī

Translation: “I go to the wine sellers, my hair dressed, freely spending my wealth, with my neck relaxed and drooping.” Al-tujjār here denotes wine sellers, rather than being left unexplained as merchants of any goods.

Al-Zamakhsharī, Al-Fāʾiq: the ilā al-tujjār wording

Ajyādī is the plural of jīd with a first-person suffix. It does not give the speaker several necks: an explanation transmitted from Al-Liḥyānī treats each part or side of the neck as a jīd and pluralizes accordingly.

Al-Liḥyānī in Lisān: pluralizing the neck’s parts

Al-Jawharī explains the same verse in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ through a neck inclining from drunkenness. Layyinan ajyādī should therefore not become ethical leniency, flexibility in trading, or obedience without evidence. Here relaxation and drooping describe a physical condition in a drinking context.

Al-Jawharī, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: wine sellers and the neck drooping from intoxication

The witness establishes two points.

First, jīd was not confined to women’s necks. Male usage is also attested, even though descriptions of women’s beauty were more prevalent.

Second, the neck’s image can accompany the speaker’s physical condition and situation. Intoxication and drooping come from context and lexical explanation; jīd itself means neither intoxication nor moral pliancy or obedience.

What the pre-Islamic witnesses show

The three witnesses clarify the range: Imruʾ al-Qays associates the neck with proportion and jewelry; ʿAntarah depicts its movement; and Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur describes relaxation and drooping in a drinking setting.

Neck remains the core in all three. The accompanying adjectives, verbs, and poetic purpose vary.

The transmitted evidence shows physical usage and capacity for imagery before the Qur’an. Jīdihā in Al-Masad therefore requires no newly invented root meaning.

5. Lexical questions and differences of usage

Is jīd inherently complimentary?

The relation of jīd to praise and blame is an important disputed issue.

Tāj al-ʿArūs quotes Al-Suhaylī’s view that jīd is used in praise and ʿunuq in blame. On this reading, Al-Masad’s jīd can produce irony or a contrasting image: a rope occupies the place associated with a necklace. Tāj also notes Al-Shihāb al-Khafājī’s criticism in his commentary on Al-Shifāʾ, so the proposal remains a debated rhetorical account rather than an undisputed rule.

Tāj: Al-Suhaylī’s account and Al-Khafājī’s criticism

The literary proposal merits attention but becomes problematic as an absolute lexical law.

ʿUnuq is itself a general, not inherently disparaging, name for the neck. The positive force of poetic jīd often comes from descriptions and comparisons. Al-Masad also places jīd in a condemnatory context while preserving its bodily meaning.

More precisely, jīd developed a strong literary association with neck beauty, particularly women’s necks; the word does not always mean beautiful neck.

This preserves the literary background without imposing praise on every occurrence.

The noun’s formation and vocalization

Lexical sources discuss jīd’s morphological pattern. Sībawayh is reported to allow two original patterns, fiʿl and fuʿl, whereas Al-Akhfash allows only fiʿl. In the second Sībawayh possibility, changing jīm to kasrah avoids yāʾ after ḍammah.

Lisān and Tāj: Sībawayh’s two proposed patterns

The dispute concerns sound structure and morphology rather than decisively altering the established neck meaning.

Jīd, neck, must also remain distinct from jayad, the neck’s length or shapeliness. Their different vowels are real even where unvocalized writing conceals them.

Does jayyid, good, belong to this root?

Common jayyid, meaning good, commendable, or of good quality, resembles jīd in writing but belongs to J W D.

A visible yāʾ does not establish J Y D as its root. Wāw and yāʾ can change in weak-root inflections, so a finished word’s appearance need not reproduce its root structure.

The resemblance does not make the neck originally mean goodness or superiority, or derive its beauty associations from moral goodness.

Each family requires independent linguistic evidence.

Ajyād as a place name

Ajyād is both a plural of jīd and a recorded place name in Mecca.

Accounts of the name’s origin differ, some invoking the neck and others historical traditions. Matching the name to the plural does not alone prove its historical derivation.

Ajyād must not be conflated with jiyād, fine horses: their vocalization and lexical roles differ. The geographical name is also transmitted as Jiyād without hamzah, so context must distinguish that place-name variant from jiyād meaning horses.

Lisān: Ajyād and the geographical Jiyād variant

6. The Qur’anic occurrence

The sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Masad 111:5.

Al-Masad 111:5

فِي جِيدِهَا حَبْلٌ مِّن مَّسَدٍ

fī jīdihā ḥablun min masad

Translation: “around her neck is a rope of twisted fibers.”

Jīdihā consists of jīd, neck, and -hā, her, referring to a woman.

The preposition fī precedes it. Fī ordinarily concerns location within a container or area, but natural Persian here can say “on her neck” for the neck and rope construction.

Fī jīdihā is a fronted predicate and ḥablun the postponed subject. Min masadin specifies the rope’s character or material.

The sentence describes a rope around the woman’s neck. Jīd itself does not mean rope, binding, collar, captivity, or punishment; any such connections come from other words and the surah’s context.

Relation to the preceding verse

Al-Masad 111:4

وَامْرَأَتُهُ حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ

wa-imraʾatuhu ḥammālata al-ḥaṭab

Translation: “and his wife, the carrier of firewood,”

The next verse continues describing the same woman. The suffix -hā in jīdihā refers to Abū Lahab’s wife.

Ḥammālata al-ḥaṭabi concerns carrying firewood, followed by the rope around her neck. A rope can bind or carry a load, making the physical association understandable.

The surah also concerns Abū Lahab’s and his wife’s outcome, so the rope may form part of their condemned end rather than simply an everyday firewood-carrying scene.

Al-Ṭabarī: the surah’s context and accounts of masad

Jīd alone cannot determine whether the image describes an earthly action, only a figure of speech, or specific otherworldly punishment. That belongs to interpreting the complete surah and its verses, rather than defining neck.

Jewelry and rope

Jīd’s literary background permits attention to a contrasting image.

Imruʾ al-Qays describes a woman’s neck with jewelry and proportion. Al-Masad describes the same body part with a rope.

For an audience familiar with the literary usage, jīd might evoke the necklace-site as well as directly naming the neck. The rope occupies jewelry’s place in that reading, strengthening the condemning image.

The reading fits the linguistic background without proving that this contrast necessarily explains the Qur’an’s word choice. Neck is the established meaning; the literary contrast remains a defensible contextual interpretation.

Masad and the rope’s material

Study of Ḥ B L; Study of M S D

Two further words contribute to ḥablun min masadin.

Ḥabl names the rope. Masad concerns twisting and assembling strands into rope; lexicons record usages involving palm fiber and other materials.

The Persian equivalent “a rope of twisted strands” thus conveys the image without fixing one material beyond doubt.

These explanations belong to Ḥ B L and M S D, rather than J Y D’s core.

Lexical meaning and contextual conclusion

Three levels should remain distinct.

Jīdihā directly means her neck.

A rope around it creates a physical image through the whole construction.

The image’s connection to the condemnation and outcome of Abū Lahab’s wife comes from the surah’s context.

The distinction prevents artificially redefining jīd as “a neck undergoing punishment” or “the place of humiliation.”

7. Related roots

J Y D (jīd) and ʿ N Q (ʿunuq)

Study of ʿ N Q

ʿUnuq is a general Arabic name for the neck, used for people and animals in direct or figurative expressions.

The Qur’an uses ʿunuq and its plural in various verses. Al-Isrāʾ 17:13 connects human conduct with the neck, while Sabaʾ 34:33 places necks in an image of fetters.

Jīd occurs only in Al-Masad, while pre-Islamic poetry often describes necks through it, especially in images of beauty.

This does not make ʿunuq always disparaging and jīd always complimentary. Both name the body part; positive or negative force comes from construction and context.

Jīd acquired particular descriptive associations in Arabic literary tradition, whereas ʿunuq has broader usage.

J Y D (jīd) and R Q B (raqabah)

Study of R Q B

Raqabah also concerns the neck but can figuratively denote a person, particularly in expressions about release from bondage or dependence.

Fakku raqabatin in Al-Balad 90:13 belongs to that setting: the construction concerns freeing a person in bondage rather than merely naming a body part.

Body-part terms can extend into social and legal relationships, but the same extension cannot be transferred to jīd without evidence.

Al-Masad’s jīdihā directly names the woman’s neck, where a rope is placed. Slavery or ownership would go beyond the word’s own meaning.

J Y D (jīd) and J W D (jawd and jayyid)

Study of J W D

Their written forms resemble one another, but Qur’anic root study must distinguish J Y D and J W D.

Jawd, jūd, and jayyid belong to J W D, concerning goodness, quality, and generosity.

Al-jiyād in Ṣād 38:31 describes fine or choice horses. It belongs to J W D rather than jīd, neck.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: al-jiyād belongs to J W D

Although jiyād and jīd both show jīm, yāʾ, and dāl, root assignment must consider linguistic history, morphology, and actual contextual meaning.

Al-jiyād must therefore not be added to J Y D’s Qur’anic derivatives.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Neck is the principal equivalent for the noun jīd.

Her neck is the precise choice for jīdihā in Al-Masad, fitting the lexical meaning while leaving the literary image available to readers.

The installed Forqan translation of the complete verse reads:

“around her neck is a rope of twisted fibers.”

In a different Arabic text, jaydāʾ might mean a woman with a long or elongated, proportioned neck. Such descriptions are not direct equivalents of jīd.

Adorned neck, beautiful neck, bound neck, necklace-site, or humiliated neck require supporting evidence in the particular text; none should become the root’s fixed equivalent.

The base translation need not expand into extended interpretation to preserve the image. It should convey the sentence, leaving historical, rhetorical, and contextual analysis in the explanation.

9. Conclusion

Arabic J Y D concerns the neck. Jīd names the body part, while adjectives such as ajyad and jaydāʾ concern length, elongation, or proportion.

The transmitted pre-Islamic poetic evidence shows an established Arabic word. Imruʾ al-Qays describes proportion and jewelry, ʿAntarah the neck’s movement, and Al-Aswad ibn Yaʿfur a relaxed, drooping neck in a drinking setting.

The core remains despite the different images. Beauty, movement, jewelry, or relaxation comes from the construction and context.

Al-Masad uses jīd for a rope around Abū Lahab’s wife’s neck. Its literary background permits a jewelry–rope contrast, without making that interpretation the word’s lexical meaning.

The stable basic definition is neck. Length, beauty, and proportion belong to descriptive forms, while literary or figurative usages require accompanying evidence. Her neck in Al-Masad 111:5 preserves a clear, natural, faithful rendering for Forqan.
