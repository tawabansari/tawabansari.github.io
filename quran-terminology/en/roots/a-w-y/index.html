The Qur’anic Root A W Y

Taking shelter, giving shelter, gathering, and lodging: from physical refuge to human support and a final abode

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَوَى / سَآوِي
(awā / sa-āwī)
Qur’anic form I verb; five occurrences

فَأْوُوا
(faʾwū)

أَوَيْنَا
(awaynā)

(āwā / āwākum)

The Arabic root A W Y concerns joining, gathering, and taking a place with someone or somewhere. أَوَى (awā) describes turning toward a place and lodging there; آوَى (āwā) can mean receiving someone, sheltering them, or providing a place for them; مَأْوَى (maʾwā) is where a person or creature goes and stays.

The Qur’an uses this family in different situations: Noah’s son intends to take refuge on a mountain; the young companions of the cave lodge in a cave; Joseph receives his brother and parents; The God provides shelter for Muhammad, who was an orphan; and descriptions of people’s outcomes speak of the abodes of those in Paradise and Hell.

The root therefore means more than either taking refuge from danger or simply inhabiting a place. Some contexts emphasize danger and the need for protection; others emphasize bringing close, receiving, gathering, or lodging.

Arabic also has another branch: أَوَى لَهُ (awā lahu), feeling compassion or pity for someone. This matters to the family’s history, although gathering, taking shelter, and sheltering are more prominent in the Qur’anic occurrences.

1. Qur’anic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The Qur’anic family falls into three groups: form I awā, with sa-āwī, āwī, awā, faʾwū, and awaynā; form IV āwā, with āwākum, āwaw, āwā, āwaynāhumā, tuʾwī, and fa-āwā; and the noun maʾwā, with definite, indefinite, and pronominal forms. The root occurs 36 times in 36 verses across 23 surahs: five form I verbs, nine form IV verbs, and 22 maʾwā nouns.

Arabic also records īwāʾ, uwiyy, taʾawwā, maʾwāh, and istaʾwā. These must not be listed as derivatives actually occurring in the Qur’an.

Gathering and joining

Earlier Arabic أَوَى الرَّجُلُ إِلَى مَنْزِلِهِ (awā al-rajulu ilā manzilihi) means returning to one’s home and lodging there. آوَيْتُهُ (āwaytuhu) describes receiving someone or providing a place for them.

These uses concern moving from dispersion toward gathering and settling. Someone wandering in the desert returns to a home or tent; a traveler goes to a resting place; someone without shelter may find a place with a person or group.

The family also describes animals. أَوَتِ الْإِبِلُ إِلَى أَهْلِهَا (awati al-ibilu ilā ahlihā) concerns camels returning to their owners, and maʾwā al-ibil is their gathering and resting place.

In تَأَوَّتِ الطَّيْرُ (taʾawwati al-ṭayru), birds join together and gather. The root is therefore not confined to buildings or human shelter: gathering and joining themselves may be its subject.

A line attributed to Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah reads:

فَتَأَوَّتْ لَهُ قَرَاضِبَةٌ مِنْ
كُلِّ حَيٍّ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَلْقَاءُ

fa-taʾawwat lahu qarāḍibatun min / kulli ḥayyin ka-annahum alqāʾu

The line means: “Then riffraff from every tribe gathered around him, as though they were people cast aside.” Taʾawwat concerns groups gathering from different communities. Qarāḍibah and alqāʾ disparage those assembled; the gathering itself is expressed by taʾawwat. The example shows joining and assembling without necessarily hiding in a refuge or fleeing a particular danger. This wording is one transmission of the poem attributed to Al-Ḥārith; attribution to the pre-Islamic period does not alone establish each line’s precise date.

Compassion and pity

In another branch, أَوَيْتُ لِفُلَانٍ (awaytu li-fulānin) means feeling compassion or tenderness toward someone.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā writes:

بَانَ الْخَلِيطُ وَلَمْ يَأْوُوا لِمَنْ تَرَكُوا
وَزَوَّدُوكَ اشْتِيَاقًا أَيَّةً سَلَكُوا

bāna al-khalīṭu wa-lam yaʾwū li-man tarakū / wa-zawwadūka ishtiyāqan ayyatan salakū

The line means: “The companions departed, showing no pity for those they left; whichever way they went, they left you supplied with longing.” It describes companions leaving without pity for the person left behind. Ayyatan here, with a word such as jihah understood, means whichever direction they took, rather than asking their destination.

Lam yaʾwū li-man tarakū concerns their lack of compassion and disregard for the person left behind.

The preposition matters. Awā ilā more often concerns joining and taking a place with someone or somewhere, whereas awā lahu in this usage concerns compassion for someone.

Lexicons, including Maqāyīs al-Lughah, distinguish gathering from compassion. A human connection is conceivable: compassion may move someone to receive another. That explanation alone does not establish either meaning as the historical source of the other.

Nor should compassion replace sheltering or receiving in every Qur’anic āwā without evidence. Compassion may motivate the conduct, but the verb’s meaning within its sentence requires separate examination.

2. Awā: taking shelter and lodging

Noah’s son: a refuge that does not save

Surah Hūd states:

قَالَ سَآوِي إِلَىٰ جَبَلٍ يَعْصِمُنِي مِنَ الْمَاءِ قَالَ لَا عَاصِمَ الْيَوْمَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا مَنْ رَحِمَ وَحَالَ بَيْنَهُمَا الْمَوْجُ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُغْرَقِينَ

qāla sa-āwī ilā jabalin yaʿṣimunī mina al-māʾi qāla lā ʿāṣima al-yawma min amri allāhi illā man raḥima wa-ḥāla baynahumā al-mawju fa-kāna mina al-mughraqīna

“He said: I shall take refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water. He said: Today there is no protector against the command of The God, except for whom He shows mercy. The wave came between them, and he was among the drowned.” (Hūd 11:43)

Sa-āwī is form I awā. Initial sa- indicates the future: “I shall take refuge on a mountain.”

Ilā jabalin identifies the destination. Protection from the water, however, comes from yaʿṣimunī mina al-māʾ, rather than sa-āwī itself.

Noah’s son intends to approach a place he thinks will shelter him, but the continuation shows that it cannot save him.

Awā therefore does not itself guarantee that the destination is safe. The verb describes seeking a place or refuge; the attempt’s success or failure is a separate matter.

Lot: seeking a strong source of support

The same surah states:

قَالَ لَوْ أَنَّ لِي بِكُمْ قُوَّةً أَوْ آوِي إِلَىٰ رُكْنٍ شَدِيدٍ

qāla law anna lī bikum quwwatan aw āwī ilā ruknin shadīdin

“He said: If only I had strength against you, or could take refuge in a strong support.” (Hūd 11:80)

Āwī is a first-person form I imperfect, accompanied by ilā.

Ruknin shadīdin denotes strong support. In Lot’s confrontation with people intending harm to his guests, it may describe a social or protective force able to defend him.

Unlike the mountain in Noah’s son’s account, the destination is not identified as a particular physical location. It is a power whose support could be relied upon.

Awā can thus relate to shelter and support beyond a physical place. Identifying ruknin shadīdin with a particular historical or theological referent nevertheless requires separate contextual examination.

The root still concerns turning toward and seeking support from something the person regards as protective.

The cave companions: choosing a place of refuge

Surah Al-Kahf states:

إِذْ أَوَى الْفِتْيَةُ إِلَى الْكَهْفِ فَقَالُوا رَبَّنَا آتِنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً وَهَيِّئْ لَنَا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا رَشَدًا

idh awā al-fityatu ilā al-kahfi fa-qālū rabbanā ātinā min ladunka raḥmatan wa-hayyiʾ lanā min amrinā rashadan

“When the young men took refuge in the cave and said: Our Lord, grant us mercy from Yourself and prepare right guidance for us in our affair.” (Al-Kahf 18:10)

Awā here refers to the young men taking their place in the cave.

Al-Kahf 18:16 also commands faʾwū ilā al-kahf, after mentioning their withdrawal from their people and what they worship besides The God.

The constructions are close: a person or people turn toward a place and lodge there.

The verses also show the young men both taking shelter in the cave and asking The God for mercy and a right course.

Physical shelter and seeking divine aid are therefore not opposed. The verse neither denies the cave’s real role nor makes it an independent source of every means of rescue.

Awā itself describes going to and lodging in the cave. Their reason for withdrawal, request for mercy, and outcome emerge from the wider account.

Awaynā ilā al-ṣakhrah: stopping and resting

In Al-Kahf, Moses’s companion says:

قَالَ أَرَأَيْتَ إِذْ أَوَيْنَا إِلَى الصَّخْرَةِ فَإِنِّي نَسِيتُ الْحُوتَ وَمَا أَنْسَانِيهُ إِلَّا الشَّيْطَانُ أَنْ أَذْكُرَهُ وَاتَّخَذَ سَبِيلَهُ فِي الْبَحْرِ عَجَبًا

qāla a-raʾayta idh awaynā ilā al-ṣakhrati fa-innī nasītu al-ḥūta wa-mā ansānīhu illā al-shayṭānu an adhkurahu wa-ittakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri ʿajaban

“He said: Do you remember when we stopped beside the rock? I forgot the fish, and none but Satan made me forget to mention it; it took its way into the sea in a wondrous manner.” (Al-Kahf 18:63)

This is particularly useful for establishing the root’s range. Awaynā describes stopping beside the rock.

The verse does not describe Moses and his companion fleeing a particular danger or threat. They stopped at the rock during their journey, and the companion recalls the incident involving the fish.

Restricting awā to escaping danger and seeking rescue would therefore be inaccurate.

It may mean approaching a place and lodging or stopping there, without its lexical meaning necessarily including danger, flight, or rescue.

Together, the five form I occurrences share turning toward and taking a place with someone or somewhere. Seeking refuge, stopping, and relying on a protective force are contextual realizations of that meaning.

3. Āwā: receiving another and providing shelter or a place

Form I usually describes someone going to a place and lodging there. Form IV describes a person or agent receiving someone else, bringing them close, or providing a place for them.

This should not become an exceptionless rule for all Arabic. Earlier Arabic also records transitive form I usage. The distinction is clear and useful in the Qur’anic occurrences, but verbal form alone does not determine meaning.

Joseph receiving his brother and parents

Surah Yūsuf states:

وَلَمَّا دَخَلُوا عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ آوَىٰ إِلَيْهِ أَخَاهُ قَالَ إِنِّي أَنَا أَخُوكَ فَلَا تَبْتَئِسْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-lammā dakhalū ʿalā yūsufa āwā ilayhi akhāhu qāla innī anā akhūka fa-lā tabtaʾis bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūna

“When they entered Joseph’s presence, he received his brother with him and said: I am your brother; do not grieve over what they used to do.” (Yūsuf 12:69)

Āwā ilayhi akhāhu concerns bringing his brother close and receiving him with himself.

It does not merely mean providing a building to live in. Joseph receives his brother and then speaks to him.

Yūsuf 12:99 uses the same verb of his parents: Joseph receives them with him, then speaks of entering Egypt in safety.

Āwā may therefore mean receiving people and bringing them close. Family affection, reassurance, and support may accompany this without becoming the verb’s fixed meaning everywhere.

Wa-āwaynāhumā: lodging two people in a suitable land

Surah Al-Muʾminūn states:

وَجَعَلْنَا ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ وَأُمَّهُ آيَةً وَآوَيْنَاهُمَا إِلَىٰ رَبْوَةٍ ذَاتِ قَرَارٍ وَمَعِينٍ

wa-jaʿalnā ibna maryama wa-ummahu āyatan wa-āwaynāhumā ilā rabwatin dhāti qarārin wa-maʿīnin

“We made Mary’s son and his mother a sign, and lodged them on elevated ground with a settled place and flowing water.” (Al-Muʾminūn 23:50)

Āwaynāhumā is a first-person plural verb with a dual object suffix, describing the lodging of Jesus and Mary.

Ilā rabwatin identifies the destination; dhāti qarārin wa-maʿīnin describes its features.

Sheltering here concerns a suitable place to settle and live. Āwā itself does not mean having a spring, elevated terrain, or flowing water: the other words supply those features.

Nor does the verse alone identify the land geographically. A particular historical identification requires separate evidence.

Tuʾwī ilayka: receiving within a family relationship

Al-Aḥzāb 33:51 addresses Muhammad with turjī contrasted with tuʾwī ilayka, in the context of his relationships with the women concerned.

Tuʾwī ilayka means receiving or lodging with oneself; turjī concerns postponing or temporarily setting aside.

Āwā therefore need not concern someone homeless or a refugee in danger. It may describe receiving someone closely within a family relationship.

The ruling’s full scope and the women’s legal status require the verse and related context. Tuʾwī’s lexical meaning alone cannot establish a general marital ruling.

Faṣīlatihi allatī tuʾwīh: kinship support

Surah Al-Maʿārij states:

وَفَصِيلَتِهِ الَّتِي تُؤْوِيهِ

wa-faṣīlatihi allatī tuʾwīhi

“And his close kin who shelter him.” (Al-Maʿārij 70:13)

Tuʾwīhi describes what someone’s close kin do for him.

Sheltering may thus be social. A family or kinship group may provide belonging and support, beyond a building or physical space.

The surrounding verses concern a day when a guilty person wishes to ransom himself from punishment with loved ones. Describing his kin as sheltering him does not make their support effective in every situation or against every judgment.

The verse draws on a familiar protective kinship relationship while showing its limits in the hereafter.

4. Sheltering the vulnerable: divine support and social responsibility

Fa-āwākum: from vulnerability to shelter

Surah Al-Anfāl states:

وَاذْكُرُوا إِذْ أَنْتُمْ قَلِيلٌ مُسْتَضْعَفُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ تَخَافُونَ أَنْ يَتَخَطَّفَكُمُ النَّاسُ فَآوَاكُمْ وَأَيَّدَكُمْ بِنَصْرِهِ وَرَزَقَكُمْ مِنَ الطَّيِّبَاتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-udhkurū idh antum qalīlun mustaḍʿafūna fī al-arḍi takhāfūna an yatakhaṭṭafakumu al-nāsu fa-āwākum wa-ayyadakum bi-naṣrihi wa-razaqakum mina al-ṭayyibāti laʿallakum tashkurūna

“Remember when you were few and oppressed in the land, fearing that people would snatch you away. He sheltered you, strengthened you with His aid, and provided you with good things, so that you might give thanks.” (Al-Anfāl 8:26)

Fa-āwākum occurs alongside ayyadakum and razaqakum.

These verbs are related without meaning the same thing. Āwākum describes providing shelter and a secure place; ayyadakum, strengthening and support; razaqakum, provision.

Rendering āwā simply “helped” would blur its difference from ayyadakum bi-naṣrihi. Rendering it only “gave you houses” would inadequately convey support and release from vulnerability.

“He sheltered you” preserves the verb’s principal sense here.

Alladhīna āwaw wa-naṣarū: sheltering and helping

Al-Anfāl 8:72 and Al-Anfāl 8:74 describe people who sheltered and helped others.

Āwaw alongside naṣarū matters. Sheltering concerns receiving people and providing a place they can occupy; naṣr concerns help and support against difficulty or an enemy.

The actions may accompany one another historically without being identical. Someone may provide shelter without joining all the recipient’s activities, or defend someone from danger without lodging them at home.

The Al-Anfāl context concerns migration, receiving migrants, and supporting them. Āwā thus includes practically receiving people and making a place for them in social relations.

Sheltering here is more than expressing affection or saying something kind. It is concrete social conduct accompanying help and support.

A-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā: sheltering an orphan

Surah Al-Ḍuḥā states:

أَلَمْ يَجِدْكَ يَتِيمًا فَآوَىٰ

a-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā

“Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you?” (Al-Ḍuḥā 93:6)

The God reminds Muhammad of his orphaned condition and the shelter provided for him.

Fa-āwā is form IV. It describes sheltering, with its object understood from context.

This relates to orphanhood, but A W Y does not itself mean being an orphan. Orphanhood is a family condition; sheltering is a supportive response to someone’s need in that condition.

Nor can fa-āwā reconstruct every historical detail of Muhammad’s guardianship. The verse states that he found shelter; particular people and stages require independent historical evidence.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 also commands that the orphan not be oppressed or humiliated. Reading the verses together connects the reminder of Muhammad’s shelter with his conduct toward orphans.

That connection should not restrict āwā to raising an orphan. Sheltering may be part of care and guardianship, but the word is broader.

Divine shelter may thus take effect in human life through actual support being provided. The particular historical means remain separate from the verb’s meaning.

Compassion and protection: close but distinct

Earlier awā lahu, feeling compassion for someone, is humanly close to āwā, sheltering.

Compassion may move someone to receive a person without shelter. Yet sheltering need not always arise from that feeling: responsibility, kinship, social obligation, or other motives may act.

Conversely, someone may feel compassion while lacking the practical ability to shelter another.

Lexical study should therefore recognize both branches without translating every āwā as feeling pity or every awā lahu as providing shelter.

5. Maʾwā: a place to stay, without inherent safety or comfort

Maʾwā belongs to A W Y and denotes where a person or creature goes and takes a place.

Lexicons discuss it as a place noun and, in some constructions, with verbal-noun force. Each sentence therefore requires distinguishing the location from sheltering or lodging as a concept.

Qur’anic maʾwā occurs chiefly in descriptions of people’s outcomes: some concern Paradise, many others Hell.

This contrast is decisive for defining the root.

Jannātu al-maʾwā: the believers’ good abode

Surah Al-Sajdah states:

أَمَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ فَلَهُمْ جَنَّاتُ الْمَأْوَىٰ نُزُلًا بِمَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

ammā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fa-lahum jannātu al-maʾwā nuzulan bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūna

“Those who believe and do righteous deeds will have gardens of dwelling, as hospitality for what they used to do.” (Al-Sajdah 32:19)

Jannātu al-maʾwā is the good abode of those who believe and act righteously.

Maʾwā itself concerns lodging and dwelling. The abode’s goodness emerges from jannāt, nuzulan, and the context of faith and righteous action.

Al-Najm 53:15 also has jannatu al-maʾwā.

The phrase can be understood as “the garden of dwelling” or “the garden of the abode.” Without other evidence, it does not determine every detail of that place’s mode of existence or the identity of particular groups residing there.

Fa-maʾwāhumu al-nār: an undesirable abode

The next verse of Al-Sajdah states:

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ فَسَقُوا فَمَأْوَاهُمُ النَّارُ كُلَّمَا أَرَادُوا أَنْ يَخْرُجُوا مِنْهَا أُعِيدُوا فِيهَا وَقِيلَ لَهُمْ ذُوقُوا عَذَابَ النَّارِ الَّذِي كُنْتُمْ بِهِ تُكَذِّبُونَ

wa-ammā alladhīna fasaqū fa-maʾwāhumu al-nāru kullamā arādū an yakhrujū minhā uʿīdū fīhā wa-qīla lahum dhūqū ʿadhāba al-nāri alladhī kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūna

“As for those who disobeyed, their abode is the Fire. Whenever they wish to leave, they are returned to it and told: Taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny.” (Al-Sajdah 32:20)

The same maʾwā family here describes the place of those undergoing punishment.

Maʾwā therefore need not be safe, pleasant, or desired. It may be where someone remains despite wanting to leave.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:39 states:

فَإِنَّ الْجَحِيمَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَىٰ

fa-inna al-jaḥīma hiya al-maʾwā

“Then Hell is indeed his abode.” (Al-Nāziʿāt 79:39)

And Al-Nāziʿāt 79:41:

فَإِنَّ الْجَنَّةَ هِيَ الْمَأْوَىٰ

fa-inna al-jannata hiya al-maʾwā

“Then Paradise is indeed his abode.” (Al-Nāziʿāt 79:41)

The constructions are almost identical, yet one calls Hell the maʾwā, the other Paradise.

Maʾwā itself thus means neither Paradise nor Hell. It identifies the final abode; its nature comes from the other words.

“Refuge” is therefore not always the best equivalent. In English, it commonly suggests a place sought for protection from danger, whereas Hell here is a place of punishment, rather than relief from it.

“Abode,” “dwelling place,” or “final abode” are consequently clearer in many hereafter passages.

Maʾwā alongside mathwā and maṣīr

Āl ʿImrān 3:151 states maʾwāhumu al-nār and, in the same verse, mathwā al-ẓālimīn.

Both concern one otherworldly referent but derive from different roots.

Maʾwā concerns lodging and a place of shelter or dwelling. Mathwā derives from TH W Y and concerns remaining or residing.

Maʾwā also occurs alongside maṣīr, as in Al-Nisāʾ 4:97, Al-Tawbah 9:73, and Al-Nūr 24:57. Maṣīr concerns the course or outcome reached, while maʾwā emphasizes one’s abode.

These distinctions matter in translation. Equivalents may overlap around abode or outcome, but root and sentence differences should remain visible.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:151, natural but distinct expressions can render them: “their abode is the Fire” and “what an evil dwelling place for the wrongdoers.”

Maʾwā and human choice

Form I may describe someone independently approaching a place of shelter or rest. Maʾwā in Hell passages, however, need not imply freely choosing to enter.

Grammatically, maʾwā names a place without itself specifying the cause of entry, personal choice, or the manner of remaining there.

Those punished in Al-Sajdah even wish to leave. Maʾwā should therefore not be defined as a place freely chosen for comfort.

Lodging, finding shelter, being received, and settling can occur in different circumstances. Context determines whether the condition is desirable.

6. Distinguishing related roots

A W Y (awā) compared with A W B (āba)

Study of A W B

Both may concern coming back to a place, but their principal fields differ.

Awā emphasizes joining, lodging, and finding shelter with someone or somewhere. Āba more directly concerns returning; maʾāb may denote the destination or outcome of return.

Someone returning home from travel and lodging there realizes both meanings. Yet sa-āwī ilā jabalin concerns seeking refuge on a mountain, rather than returning to a former place.

Wa-ilayhi maʾāb in Al-Raʿd 13:36 instead concerns returning to The God, without necessarily lodging in physical shelter.

The resemblance of maʾwā and maʾāb should not merge their roots.

A W Y (maʾwā) compared with TH W Y (mathwā)

Study of TH W Y

Thawā concerns staying or residing; mathwā denotes the place of residence.

Maʾwā may likewise be a dwelling place, but belongs to a family emphasizing joining, gathering, and sheltering.

In some hereafter verses they refer to the same place. Shared reference does not make their entire lexical ranges identical.

A W Y (awā) compared with L J A (lajaʾa)

Study of L J A

Lajaʾa concerns seeking refuge with someone or somewhere against danger or pressure.

Awā may also mean taking refuge, but its range is broader. Al-Kahf 18:63 uses it for stopping by a rock without mentioning a particular threat.

Qur’anic maljaʾ instead relates to finding escape and refuge, as in Al-Tawbah 9:118, which speaks of no refuge from The God except toward Him.

“Taking refuge” can therefore serve both families in some passages, but not every awā occurrence.

A W Y (āwā) compared with N Ṣ R (naṣara)

Study of N Ṣ R

Naṣr concerns help and support. Al-Anfāl 8:72 and Al-Anfāl 8:74 place sheltering and helping together.

Their association shows a relationship, rather than complete synonymy.

Āwā means receiving, lodging, and providing shelter; naṣr means helping and supporting. They may combine in a single social action.

A W Y (awā) compared with A W H (awwāh)

Study of A W H

Awwāh belongs to A W H and concerns sighing and emotional response.

Earlier awā lahu may also mean compassion, but that semantic overlap does not establish identical roots.

Awwāh describes pronounced sighing and tenderness of heart; Qur’anic āwā concerns receiving and sheltering.

Compassion may motivate sheltering, but the words differ in structure and direct meaning.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Form I awā can mean “took refuge,” “turned toward,” “lodged,” or “stopped somewhere to rest,” according to context.

Sa-āwī ilā jabalin in Hūd 11:43 naturally becomes “I shall take refuge on a mountain”; āwī ilā ruknin shadīdin in Hūd 11:80, “take refuge in a strong support.”

“Taking refuge in the cave” fits Al-Kahf 18:10 and Al-Kahf 18:16. “We stopped” or “lodged beside the rock” is more precise and natural in Al-Kahf 18:63.

Form IV āwā can mean “sheltered,” “received with oneself,” “lodged,” or “provided shelter for.”

“Received with himself” or “lodged with himself” conveys Yūsuf 12:69 and Yūsuf 12:99 better than “sheltered.” “We lodged them on elevated ground” suits Al-Muʾminūn 23:50.

“Sheltered” clearly conveys support in Al-Anfāl 8:26 and Al-Ḍuḥā 93:6.

Preserve āwaw versus naṣarū in Al-Anfāl 8:72 and Al-Anfāl 8:74: “they sheltered and helped.”

“Receiving with oneself” fits tuʾwī ilayka in Al-Aḥzāb 33:51. In Al-Maʿārij 70:13, “his kin who shelter him” conveys the word’s social dimension.

Maʾwā should not have one fixed equivalent everywhere. “Abode” or “dwelling place” is often natural for an otherworldly destination. Jannatu al-maʾwā may be “the garden of dwelling”; maʾwāhumu al-nār becomes “their abode is the Fire.”

Choose “refuge” where sheltering is truly prominent. Its usual English suggestion of safety and relief from danger could mislead in descriptions of Hell.

Conclusion

The Arabic root A W Y concerns joining, gathering, and taking a place with someone or somewhere. Its range includes taking shelter, sheltering another, receiving someone, and naming a dwelling or abode.

Form I describes people turning toward a place and lodging there. They may seek escape from danger, like the cave companions, or merely stop to rest, like Moses and his companion beside the rock.

Form IV emphasizes receiving and lodging another. The God shelters, Joseph receives his relatives, and some believers shelter and help others. Shelter may thus be physical or social.

Maʾwā names an abode or place to stay, without itself meaning safety, comfort, or rescue. The Qur’an applies it to both the believers’ good abode and the place of punishment. The root cannot therefore be confined to a safe refuge.

Another branch is compassion, attested in earlier awā lahu. It is humanly close to sheltering but should not be imposed on every Qur’anic derivative without evidence.

This family’s semantic core is joining and taking a place with someone or somewhere. From this come taking shelter, sheltering, and a place to dwell; an independent branch of earlier usage also records compassion and pity.
