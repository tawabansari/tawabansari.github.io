Loud crying, calling for help, and answering a cry: from an appeal for aid to rescuing the caller

Arabic Ṣ R KH concerns raising the voice, crying out, and seeking help. Its family includes ṣurākh for a cry, ṣārikh and mustaṣrikh for a person calling for help, and muṣrikh for one answering another’s cry.

The family expresses both the cry of someone facing fear, distress, or need and responding to that cry with aid. Some derivatives therefore identify the person seeking help, while others identify the helper.

The root occurs five times in four Qur’anic verses: بِمُصْرِخِكُمْ and بِمُصْرِخِيَّ in Ibrāhīm 14:22, يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18, يَصْطَرِخُونَ in Fāṭir 35:37, and صَرِيخَ in Yā Sīn 36:43.

Seeking and providing help are prominent in these uses. Crying out alone, however, does not always mean requesting aid: someone may cry through fear, pain, surprise, or another event without addressing a particular helper.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences in four derived forms

1. The semantic core in Arabic

Sound and a loud cry

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root as:

أُصَيْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى صَوْتٍ رَفِيعٍ

uṣaylun yadullu ʿalā ṣawtin rafīʿin

That is, a principle indicating a raised or loud sound.

He connects ṣurākh with it and explains صَرَخَ يَصْرُخُ as raising the voice.

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الصَّرْخَةُ: الصَّيْحَةُ الشَّدِيدَةُ عِنْدَ الْفَزَعِ أَوِ الْمُصِيبَةِ

al-ṣarkhatu al-ṣayḥatu al-shadīdatu ʿinda al-fazaʿi awi al-muṣībati

Ṣarkhah thus denotes an intense cry uttered in fear or calamity.

Another explanation applies ṣurākh to intense sound generally.

The basic meaning is therefore raising the voice and crying out. Fear, pain, and distress are familiar settings, but not necessary components of every use.

Someone may cry in fear, raise the voice to announce danger, or cry to request aid.

All involve a raised voice, but the speaker’s intention and the cry’s result differ.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: raised sound and ṣarkhah

Calling for help

Arabic ṣarakha can describe someone loudly seeking help in distress.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الصَّارِخُ: الْمُسْتَغِيثُ

al-ṣārikhu al-mustaghīthu

Ṣārikh thus denotes someone seeking aid.

Mustaṣrikh likewise describes a person asking another for help.

The cry informs others of distress and calls them to assist.

Someone in danger may loudly summon another. The cry then requests intervention as well as expressing fear or pain.

This does not make every cry a request for help.

A cry of surprise or anger may seek no aid. Help-seeking is established where context or word form indicates it.

Answering the cry

Another branch concerns someone who answers a distressed caller and helps.

Arabic records:

اسْتَصْرَخَنِي فَأَصْرَخْتُهُ

istaṣrakhanī fa-aṣrakhtuhu

That is, “He called on me for help, and I answered his cry.”

The expression clearly distinguishes two roles.

Istaṣrakhanī describes the person asking for aid.

Aṣrakhtuhu describes the one responding with help.

In Tahdhīb al-Lughah, Al-Azharī explains that familiar Arabic usage takes ṣārikh as the caller for help, muṣrikh as the helper, and mustaṣrikh likewise as someone seeking aid.

Aṣrakha here therefore need not mean making another person’s voice louder; it can mean helping that person.

This is essential for Ibrāhīm 14:22: muṣrikh there means a helper or rescuer, rather than someone crying out personally.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Azharī on caller and rescuer

2. Early Arabic uses

Calling for aid in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

A verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, the familiar pre-Islamic poet, reads:

إِذَا مَا سَمِعْنَا صَارِخًا مَعَجَتْ بِنَا
إِلَى صَوْتِهِ وُرْقُ الْمَرَاكِلِ ضُمَّرُ

idhā mā samiʿnā ṣārikhan maʿajat binā / ilā ṣawtihi wurqu al-marākili ḍummaru

An approximate rendering: “Whenever we hear someone crying for help, our lean horses with gray flanks swiftly carry us toward his voice.”

Ṣārikhan identifies someone summoning others through a cry.

The second hemistich shows the hearers moving toward his voice.

The poet describes a response to someone needing help, rather than merely hearing a loud sound.

The witness attests the link between crying and requesting aid in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Help-seeking comes from ṣārikh’s use and the situation in the verse; the root does not necessarily express a request for aid in every occurrence.

Asās al-Balāghah and lexicons: Zuhayr’s witness and its horses

A rescuer in an Arabic proverb

Lexicons record the proverb:

عَبْدٌ صَرِيخُهُ أَمَةٌ

ʿabdun ṣarīkhuhu amatun

That is, “A male slave whose rescuer is a female slave.”

The traditional explanation describes someone whose helper is also too weak in social position and power to provide effective aid.

Ṣarīkh here means a rescuer or helper.

The word can thus identify the intended responder, unlike the usual help-seeking use of ṣārikh.

The proverb reports the language and social relations of its period; it does not endorse evaluating human worth through enslavement or status.

Its exact origin is unknown. Inclusion in dictionaries alone does not make it definite pre-Islamic evidence.

Ṣarīkh: caller or rescuer?

Lisān al-ʿArab records both meanings for ṣarīkh: one seeking aid and one providing it.

Some lexicographers therefore include it among words used in contrasting directions.

This does not mean it expresses two opposing meanings simultaneously in every sentence.

If someone cries and summons others, ṣarīkh may identify the caller.

If someone hastens to help another, it may identify the rescuer.

In some uses, ṣarīkh also denotes the cry for help itself.

These distinctions follow the word’s role and context.

A cry and a sudden event

Arabic also records this expression for something unexpectedly taking a person by surprise:

كَصَرْخَةِ الْحُبْلَى

ka-ṣarkhati al-ḥublā

That is, “Like the cry of a pregnant woman.”

Its explanation concerns a cry uttered with pain or a sudden event.

Intensity and the sudden response are prominent.

It does not restrict Ṣ R KH inherently to pregnancy, childbirth, or surprise.

Those are situations in which a cry may occur.

Loud animal calls

Lexicons record ṣarrākh for a peacock.

Some uses also call a rooster ṣārikh because of its loud voice.

The root’s sound sense is clear, without a request for help.

Loud cries or calls therefore extend beyond appeals for aid and can describe animal sounds.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: proverbs, ṣarīkh, and animal calls

3. Qur’anic uses

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18: يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ, calling on Moses for help

فَأَصْبَحَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ خَائِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي اسْتَنْصَرَهُ بِالْأَمْسِ يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ قَالَ لَهُ مُوسَىٰ إِنَّكَ لَغَوِيٌّ مُبِينٌ

fa-aṣbaḥa fī al-madīnati khāʾifan yataraqqabu fa-idhā alladhī istanṣarahu bi-al-amsi yastaṣrikhuhu qāla lahu mūsā innaka la-ghawiyyun mubīnun

Translation: He was in the city, fearful and watchful. Suddenly, the very man who had sought his help the day before was again calling on him for aid. Moses said to him: “You are plainly astray.”

Yastaṣrikhuhu is a Form X imperfect.

Its subject is the person asking Moses for aid, and its pronoun hu refers to Moses.

The caller thus seeks help from Moses in this construction.

Distinguishing istanṣarahu from yastaṣrikhuhu

Two verbs describe the man’s conduct:

اسْتَنْصَرَهُ بِالْأَمْسِ

istanṣarahu bi-al-amsi

And:

يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ

yastaṣrikhuhu

Istanṣarahu belongs to N Ṣ R and expresses requesting aid.

Yastaṣrikhuhu belongs to Ṣ R KH and here concerns calling for help.

The verbs are close in this situation but have different semantic ranges.

Istinṣār focuses on requesting support; istiṣrākh relates to a cry summoning a helper.

Yastaṣrikhuhu does not specify volume, the distance between the people, or the exact manner of requesting aid.

Nor does every istiṣrākh necessarily involve a loud voice audible from far away. Usage may have become established for seeking aid even when acoustic details are unstated.

Al-Ṭabarī explains istiṣrākh here through istighāthah, showing its proximity to requesting help.

Does asking for help prove the requester’s conduct is right?

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15 describes Moses helping someone in a confrontation during which another person is killed.

The same man seeks aid again in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18.

Moses, however, tells him:

إِنَّكَ لَغَوِيٌّ مُبِينٌ

innaka la-ghawiyyun mubīnun

This sequence shows that asking for aid does not by itself prove every action of the caller right.

Someone may genuinely face danger or need help while also bearing responsibility for the confrontation.

Conversely, istaṣrakha alone does not establish that the person lies or is undeserving of aid.

Assessing his conduct requires examining the account.

Yastaṣrikhuhu directly expresses asking for help; it does not determine the request’s rightness, the caller’s intention, or precisely how the hearer must respond.

Al-Ṭabarī: the renewed appeal for Moses’s aid

Ibrāhīm 14:22: مَا أَنَا بِمُصْرِخِكُمْ

وَقَالَ الشَّيْطَانُ لَمَّا قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَعَدَكُمْ وَعْدَ الْحَقِّ وَوَعَدْتُكُمْ فَأَخْلَفْتُكُمْ وَمَا كَانَ لِيَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ سُلْطَانٍ إِلَّا أَنْ دَعَوْتُكُمْ فَاسْتَجَبْتُمْ لِي فَلَا تَلُومُونِي وَلُومُوا أَنْفُسَكُمْ مَا أَنَا بِمُصْرِخِكُمْ وَمَا أَنْتُمْ بِمُصْرِخِيَّ إِنِّي كَفَرْتُ بِمَا أَشْرَكْتُمُونِ مِنْ قَبْلُ إِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

wa-qāla al-shayṭānu lammā quḍiya al-amru inna allāha waʿadakum waʿda al-ḥaqqi wa-waʿadtukum fa-akhlaftukum wa-mā kāna lī ʿalaykum min sulṭānin illā an daʿawtukum fa-istajabtum lī fa-lā talūmūnī wa-lūmū anfusakum mā anā bi-muṣrikhikum wa-mā antum bi-muṣrikhiyya innī kafartu bi-mā ashraktumūni min qablu inna al-ẓālimīna lahum ʿadhābun alīmun

Translation: When the matter has been settled, Satan says: “The God indeed promised you a true promise. I also promised you, but broke my promise. I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I am no rescuer of yours, nor are you rescuers of mine. I disavow your earlier associating me as a partner. The wrongdoers indeed have painful punishment.”

Muṣrikh here means a helper or rescuer.

It derives from aṣrakha, which in aṣrakhahu can mean responding to a cry and providing aid.

The two phrases are:

مَا أَنَا بِمُصْرِخِكُمْ

mā anā bi-muṣrikhikum

وَمَا أَنْتُمْ بِمُصْرِخِيَّ

wa-mā antum bi-muṣrikhiyya

In the first, Satan denies being his hearers’ rescuer.

In the second, he says they are not his rescuers either.

Why does muṣrikh mean rescuer?

Arabic أَصْرَخَ فُلَانًا means helping someone in a use concerning appeals for aid.

Muṣrikh derives from that verb and identifies someone answering a caller for help.

Al-Ṭabarī explains muṣrikh in Ibrāhīm 14:22 through mughīth: a helper.

“I do not make your cry louder” or “you do not make my cry louder” would therefore miss the intended meaning.

The issue is inability to help and rescue one another, rather than amplifying another person’s voice.

Promises and the ability to rescue

At the beginning, Satan contrasts the promise of The God with his own.

He says he made a promise and failed to keep it.

He then discusses his authority over the hearers and recalls their responsibility for responding to his invitation.

Next he denies mutual rescue.

“I am no rescuer of yours” thus belongs within a network of promise, following, responsibility, and inability to save.

Inviting people to a path, promising its outcome, and being able to save its followers are three different matters.

Ṣ R KH itself does not mean promising, obedience, or moral responsibility. Those concepts come from other parts of the verse.

Nor should Satan’s words become an unlimited claim that no person can help another in any situation. The verse concerns a particular state after the matter is settled, in which the parties cannot rescue one another.

Al-Ṭabarī: muṣrikh as mughīth and the denial of rescue

The morphological difference between the forms

Bimuṣrikhikum contains singular muṣrikh with a second-person plural pronoun.

Bimuṣrikhiyya, however, contains the sound masculine plural of muṣrikh with the first-person possessive yāʾ, since the subject antum is plural.

The difference therefore involves more than the final pronoun: the second form also encodes plural helpers.

Recognized readings differ in the final vowel of bimuṣrikhiyya. Ḥamzah reads the doubled final yāʾ with kasrah; the familiar Ḥafṣ reading has fatḥah.

The difference concerns pronunciation and possessive construction without changing the sentence’s basic meaning.

Fāṭir 35:37: يَصْطَرِخُونَ فِيهَا

وَهُمْ يَصْطَرِخُونَ فِيهَا رَبَّنَا أَخْرِجْنَا نَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا غَيْرَ الَّذِي كُنَّا نَعْمَلُ أَوَلَمْ نُعَمِّرْكُمْ مَا يَتَذَكَّرُ فِيهِ مَنْ تَذَكَّرَ وَجَاءَكُمُ النَّذِيرُ فَذُوقُوا فَمَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ مِنْ نَصِيرٍ

wa-hum yaṣṭarikhūna fīhā rabbanā akhrijnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan ghayra alladhī kunnā naʿmalu a-wa-lam nuʿammirkum mā yatadhakkaru fīhi man tadhakkara wa-jāʾakumu al-nadhīru fa-dhūqū fa-mā li-al-ẓālimīna min naṣīrin

Translation: They cry out in it: “Our Lord, bring us out, that we may act righteously, differently from what we used to do.” Did We not give you enough life for anyone willing to take heed to do so? The warner also came to you. So taste; the wrongdoers have no helper.

Yaṣṭarikhūna is a Form VIII imperfect.

It comes from iṣṭarakha: the Form VIII tāʾ becomes ṭāʾ after ṣād.

Arabic iṣṭarakha al-qawmu describes people crying out loudly and seeking help in distress.

Here the cry accompanies a particular request:

رَبَّنَا أَخْرِجْنَا

rabbanā akhrijnā

That is, “Our Lord, bring us out.”

Yaṣṭarikhūna thus involves both a raised voice and an appeal for release in this context.

Does Form VIII always indicate greater intensity?

Some morphological explanations connect iṣṭirākh with repeated or intense cries.

The callers’ hardship and appeal fit a loud cry here.

Form VIII alone does not establish several people crying simultaneously, successive cries, or greater intensity than every use of ṣarakha.

Form VIII has varied Arabic uses; its precise meaning follows the verb and context.

Yaṣṭarikhūna can therefore be rendered “they cry out,” or here “they cry for help,” without specifying the cries’ number or acoustic qualities.

A cry and its answer

The verse ends:

فَمَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ مِنْ نَصِيرٍ

fa-mā li-al-ẓālimīna min naṣīrin

That is, “The wrongdoers have no helper.”

This directly relates to the opening appeal.

They ask to be brought out, but the ending states the absence of a helper.

Naṣīr nevertheless belongs to N Ṣ R, rather than Ṣ R KH.

The families are close in aid-related meaning without being lexically identical.

Iṣṭirākh expresses the appeal here, while naṣīr concerns the helper.

The absence of a helper in this situation comes from the full construction; it is not a general component of iṣṭarakha.

Al-Ṭabarī: iṣṭirākh and the change from tāʾ to ṭāʾ

Yā Sīn 36:43: فَلَا صَرِيخَ لَهُمْ

وَإِنْ نَشَأْ نُغْرِقْهُمْ فَلَا صَرِيخَ لَهُمْ وَلَا هُمْ يُنْقَذُونَ

wa-in nashaʾ nughriqhum fa-lā ṣarīkha lahum wa-lā hum yunqadhūna

Translation: If We will, We drown them; then they have no rescuer, nor are they delivered.

Ṣarīkh here means a rescuer or helper.

Fa-lā ṣarīkha lahum uses lā of categorical negation to state the absence of a rescuer.

The continuation says:

وَلَا هُمْ يُنْقَذُونَ

wa-lā hum yunqadhūna

That is, “Nor are they rescued.”

The subject is thus the lack of aid and deliverance from drowning.

Why does ṣarīkh mean rescuer here?

Arabic records ṣarīkh for both a caller for help and a rescuer.

Here, however, the people face drowning.

“There is no one crying out for them” would not clearly convey the relationship with rescue.

“They have no rescuer” fits the following sentence.

Al-Ṭabarī also explains ṣarīkh here through mughīth: someone who comes to save them.

Rescuer is therefore preferable on both lexical and contextual grounds.

Does the phrase deny every kind of aid in the world?

The verse concerns the particular danger of drowning.

Yā Sīn 36:41 mentions carrying human offspring in a laden ship; Yā Sīn 36:42 mentions other means of transport.

Verse 43 then raises the possibility of drowning.

Yā Sīn 36:44 speaks of divine mercy and enjoyment for an appointed time.

The negation thus concerns a situation in which, if drowning occurs by the will of The God, no one can save them from it.

It does not deny people’s ordinary ability to help one another in everyday dangers.

Ṣarīkh itself does not mean drowning or death; those concepts come from nughriqhum and the context.

Al-Ṭabarī: the absence of a rescuer from drowning

4. Distinguishing important forms

Ṣarakha and ṣurākh

Ṣarakha is Form I and means raising the voice or crying out.

Ṣurākh is its verbal noun.

Neither form occurs in the Qur’an, but both help establish the family’s basic sense.

Actual cries can arise from fear, pain, anger, an appeal for help, or another motive.

Ṣarkhah and ṣārikh

Ṣarkhah denotes a cry or intense shout.

Ṣārikh is the Form I active participle, describing someone crying out.

In familiar aid-seeking usage, it may identify the caller for help.

Some lexical accounts also transmit “rescuer,” but Al-Azharī treats that use as less widespread than the familiar caller sense.

Ṣārikh should therefore not be translated as rescuer in every sentence.

Aṣrakha and muṣrikh

Aṣrakha is Form IV.

In aid-related uses, it means providing help and responding to a caller.

Muṣrikh is its active participle and means rescuer.

Ibrāhīm 14:22 uses this participle twice with attached pronouns.

Morphology and established verbal meaning therefore matter for distinguishing caller and rescuer.

Form IV does not always mean providing help across Arabic verbs. This meaning belongs to Ṣ R KH’s particular usage.

Istaṣrakha and mustaṣrikh

Istaṣrakha is Form X; its familiar use expresses requesting rescue.

Mustaṣrikh is its active participle: someone seeking aid.

The verbal form yastaṣrikhuhu occurs in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18.

Seeking aid fits both its construction and the narrative context.

Iṣṭarakha and iṣṭirākh

Iṣṭarakha is Form VIII and concerns crying out or appealing for help.

Iṣṭirākh is its verbal noun.

Yaṣṭarikhūna in Fāṭir 35:37 describes a group crying for help under punishment.

Lexicons also record taṣārakha for people crying to one another or crying out collectively.

These forms should be distinguished from istaṣrakha, whose Qur’anic use requests help from another person.

Ṣarīkh

Ṣarīkh has the pattern faʿīl.

Arabic records it for a caller for aid, a rescuer, and sometimes the cry for help itself.

Rescuer is clearer in Yā Sīn 36:43.

The faʿīl pattern alone does not make it invariably active or invariably passive with one fixed meaning. Lexical usage and construction help determine its sense.

5. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (صَاحَ)

Arabic Ṣ Y Ḥ concerns crying, calling, and shouting.

Ṣayḥah occurs repeatedly in the Qur’an, sometimes denoting an intense, destructive cry.

Ṣ R KH also concerns loud cries, but appeals for aid and responses to them are prominent in its Qur’anic uses.

This does not mean every ṣurākh necessarily asks for help or every ṣayḥah is a sound without an addressee.

The families overlap in sound-related uses while retaining their own forms and applications.

Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ) and GH W TH (غَوْث)

Arabic ghawth concerns helping someone in distress.

Istighāthah requests such aid.

Istiṣrākh can also request help, but comes from a family whose basic sense concerns crying and raising the voice.

Yastaṣrikhuhu in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 can be explained as “asks him for help”; “cries out to him for help” better preserves its relationship with ṣurākh.

Muṣrikh likewise approaches mughīth: lexicographers and commentators explain one through the other.

The families are therefore close in seeking and providing aid, without sharing an identical root or semantic range.

Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ) and N Ṣ R (نَصَرَ)

Arabic N Ṣ R concerns aid and support.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 uses istanṣarahu and yastaṣrikhuhu for the same person.

Both verbs can thus express a request for help.

Naṣr can occur without crying or a loud sound.

Ṣ R KH’s main branch concerns sound, with some derivatives extending to seeking or providing help.

Providing aid and calling for it can therefore be related without being the same concept.

Study of N Ṣ R

Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ) and N D W (نِدَاء)

Arabic nidāʾ means calling or addressing another.

Qur’anic calling can involve a loud voice, quiet speech, or a summons for a particular purpose.

Ṣurākh usually emphasizes intensity of sound; nidāʾ emphasizes the act of calling and addressing.

A cry for help may therefore be a call, but not every call is an intense cry.

The cry in Fāṭir 35:37 accompanies a request to be brought out. That does not make every prayer or call to The God require a loud cry.

Study of N D W / N D Y

Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ) and J ʾ R (جَأَرَ)

Arabic jaʾara concerns loud cries and supplication.

Yajʾarūna in Al-Muʾminūn 23:64 concerns a group seized by punishment.

Iṣṭirākh in Fāṭir 35:37 likewise occurs under punishment with a plea for release.

The uses are close but belong to different roots.

J ʾ R emphasizes crying and lamenting; Ṣ R KH also has a clear family of help-seeking and rescuing expressions.

That distinction should not become an absolute rule that one is always more intense than the other.

Study of J ʾ R

Ṣ R KH (صَرَخَ) and Ṣ R Ḥ (صَرَّحَ)

Despite similar letters, the roots are separate.

Speech-related Ṣ R Ḥ concerns explicit, open expression; Qur’anic ṣarḥ concerns a building or architectural space.

Ṣ R KH, however, concerns crying and seeking aid.

Someone may speak both loudly and explicitly, but volume and clarity are different properties.

Taṣrīḥ need not be shouting, nor does ṣurākh itself mean clearly expressing truth.

Study of Ṣ R Ḥ

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

The main difficulty is that some forms identify a person needing help while others identify the helper.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 describes someone calling on Moses for aid.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan denies being his followers’ rescuer.

Yā Sīn 36:43 states the absence of a rescuer in danger of drowning.

Fāṭir 35:37 describes a group crying for release.

Translating all the forms simply as crying out loses requesting and providing help.

Defining the entire root solely as helping, conversely, neglects its basic loud-cry sense.

Must every appeal receive an affirmative response?

Istaṣrakha itself means seeking help.

The request alone does not settle its rightness, the caller’s responsibility, or the appropriate response.

Moses’s account makes this clear: the person seeking help encounters his rebuke.

The lexical act of requesting help should therefore remain distinct from moral judgment about all the requester’s actions.

Does failure to answer a cry always mean indifference?

No.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan says he cannot rescue his hearers.

Yā Sīn 36:43 likewise concerns a situation without a rescuer to deliver them.

Inability, indifference, deliberate refusal, and unwillingness to help are different concepts.

Each verse requires examining whether it concerns inability, refusal, or absence of a possibility of rescue.

Muṣrikh denotes the helper, but the word alone does not explain that helper’s absence.

Does crying always indicate weakness?

Cries often occur amid danger, fear, and distress.

Crying itself, however, need not mean complete helplessness, moral cowardice, or defeat.

Someone may cry to warn others of danger, request another person’s rescue, or announce an important event.

Ṣurākh’s basic sense identifies a kind of sound; the situation determines its cause and value.

Fāṭir 35:37 concerns punishment and a plea for release. That context should not determine the meaning of every human cry.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

For Form I ṣarakha, “cried out” or “raised a cry” is suitable.

For istaṣrakha, where requesting aid is clear, “called for rescue” or “asked for help” is preferable.

For aid-providing muṣrikh and ṣarīkh, “rescuer” or “one who answers a cry for help” is natural and precise.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 — يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ

Preferred rendering:

“He was calling on him for help.”

A renewed appeal to Moses is clear here.

Ibrāhīm 14:22 — مَا أَنَا بِمُصْرِخِكُمْ وَمَا أَنْتُمْ بِمُصْرِخِيَّ

Preferred rendering:

“I am no rescuer of yours, nor are you rescuers of mine.”

This preserves both providing aid and the reciprocal relationship between the clauses.

Fāṭir 35:37 — وَهُمْ يَصْطَرِخُونَ فِيهَا

Preferred rendering:

“They cry out in it.”

The continuation establishes these cries as appeals for release; the verb’s own translation need not incorporate all that contextual explanation.

Yā Sīn 36:43 — فَلَا صَرِيخَ لَهُمْ

Preferred rendering:

“Then they have no rescuer.”

Rescuer fits the danger of drowning and absence of deliverance.

For non-Qur’anic forms, ṣurākh can be rendered “cry,” ṣarkhah “a cry or intense shout,” usual aid-seeking ṣārikh “caller for help,” and mustaṣrikh “one seeking help.”

8. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ R KH denotes loud crying or intense sound that can arise through fear, pain, distress, or a request for help.

A family of established help-seeking and rescuing uses accompanies this sound-related meaning.

Ṣārikh and mustaṣrikh usually identify the person seeking aid, while muṣrikh identifies the helper. Depending on context, ṣarīkh can denote a caller, a rescuer, or the caller’s cry.

The root has five occurrences in four Qur’anic verses.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 concerns requesting aid from Moses. Ibrāhīm 14:22 denies Satan and his followers’ ability to rescue one another. Fāṭir 35:37 describes cries for release under punishment. Yā Sīn 36:43 states the absence of a rescuer in danger of drowning.

Short definition: Ṣ R KH basically denotes a loud cry; in distress and need, its uses extend to calling for help or answering such a call. Brief root meaning: Loud crying; calling for help; answering a cry and providing aid, according to form and context.

The family therefore requires distinguishing crying out, calling for rescue, and answering someone’s cry.

These are related but distinct situations. Qur’anic translation identifies caller and rescuer through morphology, pronouns, and context, rather than one fixed equivalent for every derivative.
