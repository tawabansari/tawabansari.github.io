The Arabic root و ج ل denotes fear and apprehension felt inwardly. This state may arise before an unknown danger, the possibility of an unwelcome event, or awareness of someone’s greatness and power.

The Quran uses the root both for Abraham’s fear of unfamiliar guests and for believers’ hearts when God is remembered and they consider their return to their Lord.

This range shows that وَجَل is not inherently a religious virtue or exclusively fear of punishment. Its central meaning is apprehension; context determines its source, direction, and value.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, وَجَل was recognized as fear and apprehension. وَجِلَ described someone who became afraid of a person, event, or possibility.

An important witness occurs in an elegy attributed to the pre-Islamic period. It describes a warrior feared even by tribes he did not intend to attack:

وَكَمْ مِنْ قَبِيلٍ وَإِنْ لَمْ تَكُنْ

wa-kam min qabīlin wa-in lam takun

أَرَدْتَهُمْ مِنْكَ بَاتُوا وِجَالَا

aradtahum minka bātū wijālā

“How many tribes, though you had not intended to attack them, spent the night in fear of you.”

The poem mourns ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb al-Hudhalī; its attribution differs between Janūb al-Hudhaliyyah and ʿAmrah bint al-ʿAjlān. It has also been reported that Janūb and ʿAmrah were names for the same woman; different names need not establish two distinct poets.

وِجَال is a plural description of frightened people. The tribes were not necessarily at war with the man: even the possibility of confronting him was enough to arouse fear.

Here وَجَل concerns fear arising from imagining danger or knowing another person’s capabilities. An actual attack is not necessary for the feeling to occur.

Despite differences in attribution and some transmitted wording, وِجَال in this elegy witnesses the meaning “apprehensive” in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Another witness occurs in poetry by Maʿn ibn Aws al-Muzanī, who lived across the transition to Islam:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا أَدْرِي وَإِنِّي لَأَوْجَلُ

la-ʿamruka mā adrī wa-innī la-awjalu

عَلَى أَيِّنَا تَغْدُو الْمَنِيَّةُ أَوَّلُ

ʿalā ayyinā taghdū al-maniyyatu awwalu

“By your life, I do not know—and I am apprehensive—which of us death will visit first.”

The analysis of أَوْجَلُ here is disputed: it has been read as an adjective meaning apprehensive and as a present-tense verb, “I fear.” Both readings convey concern about an unknown future. The poet knows death is certain but does not know whom it will reach first.

The verse shows that وَجَل can accompany concern about a future event. Maʿn lived across the transition to Islam, and this verse’s date is not independently established; it illustrates continuity of an old meaning, not a certain pre-Quranic date for this particular line.

Together, these witnesses indicate that a recognized meaning of the family in early Arabic was fear before danger, power, or a possible event.

The word alone does not judge whether the fear is justified, specify its intensity, or describe the person’s subsequent behavior.

2. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms include:

وَجِلَ: Feared, became apprehensive, or experienced inward alarm.

وَجَل: Fear and inward apprehension.

وَجِل: A person who is apprehensive.

وَجِلَة: The feminine form of وَجِل: apprehensive or fearful.

وِجَال: A plural description of apprehensive people.

أَوْجَل: An adjective for an apprehensive person, which can also mean “more apprehensive” in comparative constructions; the same written form may be a first-person present-tense verb, depending on its construction.

أَوْجَلَهُ: Frightened him or caused him apprehension.

مَوْجَل: One recorded verbal noun for fear and apprehension.

Arabic sources also record وَجُلَ for growing old, and مَوْجِل or وَجِيل for a hollow collecting water. These limited uses do not occur in the Quran, and their historical connection with fear has not been clearly established. A single unproven image should not be constructed to unite every form.

Semantic Core

The principal and Quranic branch has this central meaning:

Feeling fear before something experienced as concerning, frightening, or significant and powerful.

Two points should remain distinct in this definition.

First, the feeling of apprehension itself, which the word denotes.

Second, its cause, which may be a threat to life, an unfamiliar situation, God’s greatness, or awareness of accountability and return.

The cause is not an invariant part of the root’s meaning; it must be established from each context.

Although Persian can express some uses through “the heart trembling,” bodily shaking or physical heart-throbbing is not a necessary, fixed component of this root.

3. Quranic Forms and Their Uses

و ج ل occurs five times in the Quran, in four written forms:

وَجِلَتْ, وَجِلُونَ, تَوْجَلْ, and وَجِلَةٌ.

The Quranic verbs belong to Form I, and the descriptive forms belong to the same family.

A. Al-Anfāl 8:2 — Believers’ Hearts When God Is Remembered

إِنَّمَا الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَإِذَا تُلِيَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُهُ زَادَتْهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ

innamā al-muʾminūna alladhīna idhā dhukira allāhu wajilat qulūbuhum wa-idhā tuliyat ʿalayhim āyātuhu zādathum īmānan wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn

Al-Anfāl 8:2

“The believers are only those whose hearts become apprehensive when The God is remembered; when His signs are recited to them, they increase them in faith, and they place their trust in their Lord.”

وَجِلَتْ is a past-tense verb and قُلُوبُهُمْ its subject.

Although قُلُوب is plural, the verb is feminine singular, a recognized Arabic construction with nonhuman plurals.

The Quran does not say that believers constantly live in terror. It states that when God is remembered, their hearts respond with apprehension.

This state accompanies two further qualities: increased faith on hearing the signs, and trust in the Lord.

The apprehension here is therefore not presented as driving people away from God or keeping them in despair. It belongs to a description combining faith and trust in God.

In this context, it can be understood as fear accompanied by awareness of God’s greatness and power and human responsibility before Him.

Nevertheless, وَجَل should not be translated simply as respect, recognition of greatness, or humility. These may accompany or result from the feeling, but do not replace its direct meaning of fear.

Nor does the verse say that a believer fears only God’s punishment. وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ itself does not impose that limitation.

B. Al-Ḥijr 15:52 — Abraham’s Fear of the Guests

إِذْ دَخَلُوا عَلَيْهِ فَقَالُوا سَلَامًا قَالَ إِنَّا مِنْكُمْ وَجِلُونَ

idh dakhalū ʿalayhi fa-qālū salāman qāla innā minkum wajilūn

Al-Ḥijr 15:52

“When they entered upon him and said: peace. He said: we are afraid of you.”

وَجِلُونَ is a masculine plural description meaning apprehensive or afraid.

مِنْكُمْ identifies the source or object of that apprehension: the guests who had come to Abraham.

Here وَجَل concerns a human response to an unfamiliar situation.

Hūd 11:70 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28 help explain its cause.

In Hūd 11:70, Abraham sees that the guests do not reach for the food and feels fear of them inwardly.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28 expresses the same feeling through فَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً.

These verses show that و ج ل, و ج س, and خ و ف can describe one shared experience.

أَوْجَسَ expresses experiencing the feeling, خِيفَةً identifies it as fear, and وَجِلُونَ describes those who experience that fear.

This overlap does not make every use of the roots identical, but cautions against artificial, absolute boundaries between them.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:52, قَالَ is singular, yet Abraham uses the plural إِنَّا مِنْكُمْ وَجِلُونَ. That plural must be preserved, although the phrase alone does not identify who besides Abraham experienced the feeling.

C. Al-Ḥijr 15:53 — Reassurance Against Fear

قَالُوا لَا تَوْجَلْ إِنَّا نُبَشِّرُكَ بِغُلَامٍ عَلِيمٍ

qālū lā tawjal innā nubashshiruka bi-ghulāmin ʿalīm

Al-Ḥijr 15:53

“They said: do not fear; we give you good news of a knowledgeable boy.”

تَوْجَلْ is a second-person singular present-tense verb, made jussive after prohibitive لَا.

لَا تَوْجَلْ means “do not fear” or “do not be apprehensive.”

This responds to Abraham’s words in the preceding verse.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:52, Abraham fears them; in Al-Ḥijr 15:53, they reassure him and bring good news.

Together, the verses clarify the root: وَجَل can be apprehension before an unfamiliar situation even when that situation presents no actual danger.

The presence of fear does not therefore establish the presence of real danger.

Abraham’s fear here is not inherently evidence of weak faith or a moral fault. The Quran describes his feeling and subsequent reassurance.

This occurrence prevents limiting every Quranic use of وَجَل to an exclusively devotional or praiseworthy sense.

D. Al-Ḥajj 22:35 — Apprehension Alongside Patience, Salat, and Giving

الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَالصَّابِرِينَ عَلَىٰ مَا أَصَابَهُمْ وَالْمُقِيمِي الصَّلَاةِ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ

alladhīna idhā dhukira allāhu wajilat qulūbuhum wa-al-ṣābirīna ʿalā mā aṣābahum wa-al-muqīmī al-ṣalāti wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn

Al-Ḥajj 22:35

“Those whose hearts become apprehensive when The God is remembered, who endure what befalls them, establish Salat, and spend from what We have provided them.”

وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ is the same construction found in Al-Anfāl 8:2.

The two verses nevertheless have different contexts.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:34, the addressee is instructed to give good news to the مُخْبِتِينَ, those humble and yielding before their Lord. Identifying the addressee as the Messenger comes from the discourse; that verse does not explicitly contain the title رَسُول.

Al-Ḥajj 22:35 describes their qualities: apprehensive hearts when God is remembered, patience under what befalls them, establishing Salat, and spending from their provision.

Here وَجَل is one quality of humble people who act.

In this context, fear of God is not presented as withdrawal from life, constant terror, or abandonment of responsibility. It accompanies perseverance and good action.

These implications arise from the verse’s combined qualities, not from the lexical meaning of وَجَل alone.

The semantic core remains the same in Al-Anfāl and Al-Ḥajj: apprehension of the heart when God is remembered. What differs is the set of accompanying qualities.

E. Al-Muʾminūn 23:60 — Apprehension While Giving and Remembering the Return

وَالَّذِينَ يُؤْتُونَ مَا آتَوْا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ وَجِلَةٌ أَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ رَاجِعُونَ

wa-alladhīna yuʾtūna mā ātaw wa-qulūbuhum wajilatun annahum ilā rabbihim rājiʿūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:60

“And those who give what they give while their hearts are apprehensive, because they are returning to their Lord.”

وَجِلَةٌ is a feminine singular description predicated of قُلُوبُهُمْ.

Unlike Al-Anfāl 8:2 and Al-Ḥajj 22:35, the verse describes the hearts’ condition with an adjective rather than the verb وَجِلَتْ.

This morphological difference matters, but does not establish a permanent, unchanging condition. The actual form here is وَجِلَةٌ, not وَاجِلَةٌ; context must determine the feeling’s duration.

وَقُلُوبُهُمْ وَجِلَةٌ describes the condition of their hearts while they give.

أَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ رَاجِعُونَ then recalls their return to their Lord.

Here apprehension is connected with awareness of return and accountability.

Those who give do not merely consider the good deed accomplished; they also keep their return to their Lord in view. The wording does not restrict what is given to money alone.

The verse does not explicitly say that they are certain their deeds have been rejected or that God is displeased with them. Such a conclusion should not be added.

Nor does it say that giving is motivated by terror. The act of giving and apprehensive hearts are two parts of the sentence presented together.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:61 describes these people as hastening toward good deeds and taking the lead in them.

This connection presents the apprehension of Al-Muʾminūn 23:60 alongside doing good, rather than as an obstacle to it.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:62 also states that God burdens no soul beyond its capacity and that they are not wronged.

Read together, the verses portray goodness, responsibility before God, and His justice, rather than requiring endless terror regardless of how much good a person does.

4. Quranic Constructions and Translation Challenges

وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ and قُلُوبُهُمْ وَجِلَةٌ

Al-Anfāl 8:2 and Al-Ḥajj 22:35 express apprehension through a verb:

وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ

wajilat qulūbuhum

Al-Anfāl 8:2; Al-Ḥajj 22:35

That is, their hearts became apprehensive or afraid.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:60 uses the same semantic family in a nominal sentence:

وَقُلُوبُهُمْ وَجِلَةٌ

wa-qulūbuhum wajilah

Al-Muʾminūn 23:60

That is, their hearts are apprehensive.

The first emphasizes the occurrence of the state under the conditions described; the second describes the hearts’ condition itself.

This distinction helps translation but does not independently establish that the first state is brief and the second permanent.

Does Wajal Necessarily Mean Physical Heart Trembling?

Three of the root’s five Quranic occurrences attribute apprehension to hearts.

In Persian, “his heart trembled” naturally expresses fear. It does not make وَجَل the name of a specific medical or physiological reaction.

Quranic language also connects the heart with faith, perception, tranquility, apprehension, hardness, and softness.

“The heart trembling” may therefore be a natural image of fear in translation, without restricting the word’s entire meaning to the physical organ’s beating.

Is Wajal Always Praiseworthy?

The Quranic occurrences clarify the answer.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:52, Abraham fears unfamiliar guests. Knowledge of their identity and message removes this fear.

Al-Anfāl 8:2 and Al-Ḥajj 22:35 include apprehensive hearts among believers’ and humble people’s qualities.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:60, the same feeling accompanies giving and awareness of return to the Lord.

وَجَل itself names neither virtue nor vice. Its object, cause, and the person’s response determine its moral significance.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

W J L Compared with Kh W F

خ و ف covers a broad field of fear and apprehension, used in the Quran for worldly dangers, enemies, the future, punishment, and other situations.

وَجَل belongs to this field, with inward feeling and an apprehensive person or heart prominent in its Quranic uses.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:53, Abraham’s apprehension is met with لَا تَوْجَلْ.

The same story uses لَا تَخَفْ in Hūd 11:70 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28.

This overlap shows how close the two roots can be in particular contexts.

It does not justify calling every خَوْف external and every وَجَل exclusively inward: خَوْف can also be wholly inward.

The difference is largely one of emphasis: خَوْف broadly names fear, while وَجَل in these passages highlights apprehension and inward alarm.

W J L Compared with W J S

Lexical uses of و ج س concern perceiving or sensing something, especially an unobtrusive sign.

The Quran uses أَوْجَسَ خِيفَةً for inwardly experiencing apprehension.

وَجِلَ describes becoming apprehensive itself.

Abraham’s story allows comparison: Hūd 11:70 expresses experiencing fear through أَوْجَسَ, while in Al-Ḥijr 15:52 Abraham describes his condition through وَجِلُونَ.

This does not establish و ج س as an invariably initial perceptual stage followed by و ج ل as fear. No fixed sequence follows from all their uses.

W J L Compared with W J F

و ج ف concerns swift movement and throbbing or agitation.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8, قُلُوبٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاجِفَةٌ describes hearts agitated and throbbing that Day.

وَجَل directly concerns the feeling of fear and apprehension.

Fear may cause heart-throbbing, and the experiences may accompany each other, but this association does not make the roots identical.

Al-Ḥashr 59:6 also uses و ج ف for driving horses and camels swiftly, showing that its meaning cannot be restricted to fear.

W J L Compared with Kh Sh Y

خ ش ي also concerns fear; many Quranic contexts associate it with awareness of greatness, power, or an outcome.

Fāṭir 35:28, for example, connects fear of God with knowledge.

وَجَل likewise arises in believers’ hearts when God is remembered, without making the two roots wholly identical.

In several contexts, khashyah has a prominent connection with knowledge, while wajal emphasizes felt apprehension.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute rule making every khashyah unemotional or every wajal devoid of understanding.

6. Apprehension and Tranquility in Quranic Meaning

Al-Anfāl 8:2 describes believers’ hearts becoming apprehensive when God is remembered.

Al-Raʿd 13:28, however, says that hearts find tranquility in remembering God.

At first, these may seem inconsistent: how can remembering God bring both apprehension and tranquility?

The distinction between the two states helps explain this.

وَجَل concerns apprehension, while اطمئنان concerns the heart’s tranquility and settledness.

The states need not be mutually exclusive.

A person may feel apprehension through awareness of God’s greatness and personal responsibility, while finding tranquility in trust in their Lord.

This is consistent with Al-Anfāl 8:2 itself, where trust in the Lord accompanies وَجَل.

Al-Zumar 39:23 likewise brings different bodily and inward responses together: the skins of those who fear their Lord first shiver, then their skins and hearts soften toward God’s remembrance.

The believer’s response is thus not restricted to a single feeling.

Reconciling the verses does not require replacing fear in وَجَل with mere respect, or emptying اطمئنان of tranquility.

The Quran can speak of apprehension, humility, trust, and tranquility in the human relationship with the Lord, since these concern different aspects of that relationship.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Translation should preserve fear and apprehension without adding an intensity or cause not contained in the word. These are editorial proposals, not replacements for existing verse word boxes.

Al-Anfāl 8:2

“When The God is remembered, their hearts become apprehensive.”

“Their hearts tremble” is also natural, but “become apprehensive” conveys fear more explicitly.

Al-Ḥijr 15:52

“He said: we are afraid of you.”

This preserves both the fearful state and the plural wording.

Al-Ḥijr 15:53

“They said: do not fear; we give you good news of a knowledgeable boy.”

“Do not be afraid” is another direct, natural equivalent.

Al-Ḥajj 22:35

“Those whose hearts become apprehensive when The God is remembered…”

Keeping this consistent with Al-Anfāl 8:2 is preferable, since the Arabic wording is identical.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:60

“And those who give what they give while their hearts are apprehensive, because they are returning to their Lord.”

Unlike the verbal occurrences, “their hearts are apprehensive” preserves the descriptive form وَجِلَةٌ.

Overall, “become apprehensive,” “be apprehensive,” “fear,” and, where appropriate, “be inwardly afraid” suit this family.

Humility, respect, wonder, or obedience alone should not replace its lexical meaning, although some may accompany wajal.

8. Conclusion

و ج ل concerns fear and apprehension in Arabic. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrates fear of human power and danger, while poetry near the beginning of Islam supports the same semantic field.

The root occurs five times in the Quran, in four written forms.

Two occurrences describe Abraham’s fear before unfamiliar guests. The other three concern believers’ hearts when God is remembered or while giving and remaining aware of return to their Lord.

This contextual range shows that وَجَل is not inherently confined to fear of punishment, devotional fear, or a particular moral state. Apprehension is its core throughout the Quranic uses; the verses establish its source and value.

The root can be defined as becoming apprehensive or feeling fear before a person, situation, or matter experienced as frightening or concerning.

In uses concerning God, this feeling may accompany awareness of His greatness, human responsibility, humility, and return. These features arise from context rather than the root’s lexical meaning alone.

For Forqan, “become apprehensive” and “be apprehensive” are therefore defensible principal equivalents. They convey both Abraham’s fear of the guests and believers’ hearts without imposing a specifically religious meaning on every occurrence.
