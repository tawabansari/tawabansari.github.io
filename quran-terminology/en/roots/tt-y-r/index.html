Flight, birds, omens, and destiny: from movement in the sky to human responsibility for conduct and its outcome

The Arabic root ṭ y r principally concerns flying and birds moving through the air. طَارَ means “flew,” يَطِيرُ means “flies,” and طَيْر denotes birds. Its uses extend beyond bodily flight, however. Scattering in different directions, shares falling to people, taking omens from birds, and attributing good or bad fortune to external signs also belong to this family.

The Quran displays several important branches of this semantic range. طير sometimes denotes actual birds: creatures that fly, form communities, eat, and have their place in creation. Birds also appear in the accounts of Abraham, Jesus, David, and Solomon. Elsewhere, تطيّر concerns taking bad omens and regarding people as ill-omened. In some verses, طائر concerns a person’s lot, destiny, or what belongs to them through conduct and its consequences. Finally, مستطير describes something spreading and becoming widespread.

These branches are linguistically related but should not be reduced to a single meaning. A bird, flight, an omen, a share, conduct, and dispersal are distinct concepts, each requiring contextual examination.

The root occurs 29 times in 24 Quranic verses, in five morphological groups: once as the simple verb يَطِيرُ, three times in forms of تَطَيَّرَ, nineteen times as طَيْر, five times as طَائِر, and once as مُسْتَطِير.

A central distinction is that birds’ actual movement is not the same as the significance people assign to it as a sign of good or bad fortune. The Quran presents birds as signs of creation, while also recounting and criticizing the attribution of people’s misfortune to Messengers and those calling them to truth.

1. Historical meanings in early Arabic

Flight and the movement of birds

In early Arabic, طَارَ الطَّائِرُ means that a bird flew. طَيْر is a general designation for birds; طَائِر is the active participle of طار and also functions as a noun for a bird.

طَيَرَان and طَيْر are recorded for flying and moving through the air. Such uses highlight movement by wings through the space above the ground.

طَيْر can denote birds collectively or as a kind, without identifying a particular species.

Alongside its participial structure, طائر is an established Arabic name for a bird. It should therefore not invariably be translated merely “one that flies”: sometimes the bird itself is intended.

A poetic witness from Imruʾ al-Qays

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the renowned pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

وَقَدْ أَغْتَدِي وَالطَّيْرُ فِي وُكُنَاتِهَا
بِمُنْجَرِدٍ قَيْدِ الْأَوَابِدِ هَيْكَلِ

wa-qad aghtadī wa-l-ṭayru fī wukunātihā / bi-munjaridin qaydi al-awābidi haykal

“At dawn, while the birds were still in their roosts, I would set out on a swift, mighty horse, as though it fettered the fleeing wild beasts.”

The poet describes setting out early, before the birds have left their roosting places.

الطير denotes actual birds, and وكناتها their resting places and roosts.

The witness establishes two points. طير was a familiar name for birds in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period, and it described their natural life without necessarily treating them as omens or signs of destiny.

This is a well-known witness from pre-Islamic poetry, transmitted in Imruʾ al-Qays’s collected verse and commentaries on the Muʿallaqāt. Nevertheless, the poet’s historical attribution and the authenticity of each transmitted reading remain distinct matters.

Birds and omens among early Arabs

Among some pre-Islamic Arab groups, observing birds and other animals became associated with taking omens.

Before travelling or undertaking something important, a person might observe a bird and infer something about the future from its direction, sound, or appearance.

Arabic dictionaries record السَّانِح and الْبَارِح for animals passing in different directions relative to an observer.

Common accounts use سانح for movement from one direction and بارح for its opposite. However, reports and groups differ over precise directional definitions and which passage was considered favorable or unfavorable.

A single uniform system of divination should therefore not be attributed to all pre-Islamic Arabs.

The defensible historical point is that observing birds and certain animals could serve as a means of taking omens. Against this background, طِيَرَة and تَطَيُّر became familiar terms for omens, especially unfavorable ones.

A poetic witness attributed to al-Aʿshā

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā is transmitted in the following reading:

أَجَارَهُمَا بِشْرٌ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ بَعْدَمَا
جَرَى لَهُمَا طَيْرُ السَّنِيحِ بِأَشْأَمِ

ajārahumā bishrun mina al-mawti baʿdamā / jarā lahumā ṭayru al-sanīḥi bi-ashʾam

“Bishr saved the two from death after the passing birds had brought them an ill omen.”

Here طير السنيح is not simply a name for flying birds. Their passage, or the sign attributed to it, is associated with an unfavorable outcome for people.

This use shows that, beyond their natural meaning, birds featured in early expressions of omens and imagined human outcomes.

The poetic image does not establish a real connection between birds’ movements and future events. It attests to a belief, not a bird’s ability to determine human destiny.

Al-Aʿshā is a recognized poet of the late pre-Islamic period. The transmitted verse supports the historical use of unfavorable omens, while its exact readings and attribution remain matters for textual study.

From flight to a person’s allotted share

In early Arabic, طائر was not restricted to birds or omens. The family also occurs in expressions describing a share that falls to someone.

Expressions such as طَارَ لَهُ سَهْمُهُ are recorded for a person receiving a share in a division.

Nothing literally flies here. A share from among those distributed has fallen to a particular person.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

تَطِيرُ عَدَائِدُ الْأَشْرَاكِ شَفْعًا
وَوِتْرًا وَالزَّعَامَةُ لِلْغُلَامِ

taṭīru ʿadāʾidu al-ashrāki shafʿan / wa-witran wa-l-zaʿāmatu li-l-ghulām

“The partners’ portions are allotted in pairs and singly, while leadership falls to the boy.”

This verse is transmitted as part of Labīd’s lament for his brother Arbad. Since Arbad’s death is reported after the emergence of Islam, the poem should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic. الزعامة has been explained as leadership, a chief’s special share, or a piece of armor; “leadership” above follows one reading.

Its linguistic importance remains: تطير here concerns distributing and receiving portions, rather than actual birds flying or taking bad omens.

The example helps explain the semantic connection between flight, receiving an allotted portion, and the idea of fortune or one’s lot.

It does not prove that every meaning concerning destiny and omens necessarily developed from dividing shares. The evidence supports overlapping semantic fields, not a single proven historical route.

Semantic core

The evidence identifies several related branches.

The family’s direct, clear meaning is flying and moving through the air.

Extended uses employ that image for rapid movement or scattering in different directions.

Social and cultural uses include receiving a share, taking omens, and attributing good or bad fortune to signs.

These branches should not be merged into an invented definition. When طائر names a bird, it means a bird; when it concerns a person’s lot and outcome, context and established usage determine its sense.

2. Actual birds in the Quran: flight, life, and their place in creation

A large portion of the Quranic uses of ṭ y r concerns actual birds.

These verses describe creatures within creation that move, eat, form groups, and sometimes have a special relationship with Prophets.

طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ: a bird flying with its two wings

Al-Anʿām 6:38 states:

وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ إِلَّا أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِي الْكِتَابِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يُحْشَرُونَ

wa-mā min dābbatin fī al-arḍi wa-lā ṭāʾirin yaṭīru bi-janāḥayhi illā umamun amthālukum mā farraṭnā fī al-kitābi min shayʾin thumma ilā rabbihim yuḥsharūn

Al-Anʿām 6:38

“There is no creature on earth, nor a bird flying with its two wings, but that they are communities like you. We have neglected nothing in the Book; then to their Lord they will be gathered.”

Al-Anʿām 6:38

Two forms of ṭ y r appear together: طَائِرٍ and يَطِيرُ.

طائر names the bird; يطير is the verb for flying.

بِجَنَاحَيْهِ refers to the bird’s two wings.

The construction is linguistically clear: the Quran describes a creature flying with its wings.

Yet the verse does not stop at movement. It calls birds and other creatures أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ: communities like you.

This matters because birds are not presented merely as moving objects or human tools. They have their own lives and order within creation.

أمم أمثالكم does not make all biological, social, intellectual, or responsible capacities of humans and birds identical. The expression presents them as communities; the nature and extent of further similarities require separate consideration.

The verse also bears on recognizing animals’ place in creation. Greater human power should not lead people to consider other beings devoid of value or place in the created order.

Flight and being held in the air

Al-Naḥl 16:79 states:

أَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ مُسَخَّرَاتٍ فِي جَوِّ السَّمَاءِ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا اللَّهُ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

a-lam yaraw ilā al-ṭayri musakhkharātin fī jawwi al-samāʾi mā yumsikuhunna illā allāhu inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūn

Al-Naḥl 16:79

“Have they not looked at the birds made subject in the space of the sky? None holds them but The God. Surely in that are signs for people who believe.”

Al-Naḥl 16:79

Al-Mulk 67:19 also states:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ فَوْقَهُمْ صَافَّاتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا الرَّحْمَٰنُ إِنَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ بَصِيرٌ

a-wa-lam yaraw ilā al-ṭayri fawqahum ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna mā yumsikuhunna illā al-raḥmānu innahu bi-kulli shayʾin baṣīr

Al-Mulk 67:19

“Have they not looked at the birds above them, spreading and folding their wings? None holds them but the Entirely Merciful. Indeed, He sees all things.”

Al-Mulk 67:19

Both verses present birds as observable signs of creation.

Al-Naḥl highlights their presence and movement in the sky’s space; Al-Mulk draws attention to spreading and folding wings.

صَافَّاتٍ belongs to ṣ f f and describes the wings’ spread configuration. يَقْبِضْنَ belongs to q b ḍ and describes drawing them in.

Spreading and folding wings should therefore not be built into the meaning of طير itself. The bird is the subject; the other expressions describe its movement.

These verses relate to actual observation of nature. Birds move through the air by their wings and bodily structure, within the order that makes such movement possible.

Understanding mechanisms of flight, including wing shape, airflow, and forces acting on a body, need not negate the Creator’s role in bringing about that order. Conversely, the verse should not be turned into a claim that birds move without physical mechanisms or that natural laws are suspended.

The verses invite reflection on the order and power of creation through this observable phenomenon.

Birds in feeding, ordinary life, and imagery

In Yūsuf 12:36, one prisoner dreams of birds eating the bread on his head. In Yūsuf 12:41, Joseph speaks of birds eating from one prisoner’s head.

طير in these passages denotes birds feeding.

The dream image and the event described in its interpretation must nevertheless be distinguished. Birds appearing in a dream do not give طير a fixed meaning of death or punishment.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:31, birds also appear in an image of falling and being snatched away.

The verse pictures birds snatching someone who has fallen from the sky, as an image of the condition of one who associates partners with The God.

طير itself does not mean associating partners or falling. Those concepts come from the whole comparison.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:21 uses لَحْمِ طَيْرٍ for food in Paradise. طير names a bird there, without a sense of omen or destiny.

Birds in the accounts of Abraham and Jesus

In Al-Baqarah 2:260, The God responds to Abraham’s request to see how the dead are brought to life by telling him to take four birds.

الطير denotes actual birds; أَرْبَعَةً supplies the number four.

For ṭ y r, the essential point is that the word names birds. Abraham’s precise action, the meaning of فَصُرْهُنَّ, placing portions on hills, and calling them are separate questions concerning the verbs and construction of the verse.

طير alone cannot establish particular species named in external narratives.

Āl ʿImrān 3:49 and Al-Māʾidah 5:110 describe something fashioned from clay in a bird’s shape and becoming a bird by The God’s permission.

هَيْئَةِ الطَّيْرِ and طَيْرًا differ: first the bird’s form is mentioned, then becoming a bird by divine permission.

طير is thus not restricted to outward shape here. Becoming a bird is expressed by the complete construction.

Al-Fīl 105:3 states:

وَأَرْسَلَ عَلَيْهِمْ طَيْرًا أَبَابِيلَ

wa-arsala ʿalayhim ṭayran abābīl

Al-Fīl 105:3

“And He sent against them birds in groups.”

Al-Fīl 105:3

طَيْرًا names the birds; أَبَابِيلَ describes their being in groups.

أبابيل should not be made the name of a particular species without evidence. Nor does طير specify these birds’ exact biological characteristics.

3. Birds in the accounts of David and Solomon

The Quran gives birds a special place in the accounts of David and Solomon.

Lexically, the words still name birds, while accompanying verbs and relationships clarify other aspects of the accounts.

Birds glorifying alongside David

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79, Sabaʾ 34:10, and Ṣād 38:19 mention birds in connection with David.

Ṣād 38:19 states:

وَالطَّيْرَ مَحْشُورَةً كُلٌّ لَهُ أَوَّابٌ

wa-l-ṭayra maḥshūratan kullun lahu awwāb

Ṣād 38:19

“And the birds, gathered together; each was ever returning to him.”

Ṣād 38:19

الطير names the birds, and مَحْشُورَةً describes their being gathered.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79 speaks of mountains and birds glorifying alongside David. In Sabaʾ 34:10, أَوِّبِي مَعَهُ occurs in the context of mountains and birds.

These uses describe a relationship between David and birds beyond ordinary observation.

But طير does not thereby acquire a new meaning such as angels, spirits, or flying equipment. Such substitutions require independent evidence.

Nor does their glorification alone establish that their perception, language, or manner of glorifying is exactly like that of humans.

The Quran states their glorification and special relationship with David. Their manner should be examined within the evidence the verses supply. In Ṣād 38:19, the reference of لَهُ has also been discussed; the translation preserves the pronoun rather than inserting an exclusive identification into it.

Solomon’s knowledge of birds’ speech

Al-Naml 27:16 states:

وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَانُ دَاوُودَ وَقَالَ يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنْطِقَ الطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَهُوَ الْفَضْلُ الْمُبِينُ

wa-waritha sulaymānu dāwūda wa-qāla yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ʿullimnā manṭiqa al-ṭayri wa-ūtīnā min kulli shayʾin inna hādhā la-huwa al-faḍlu al-mubīn

Al-Naml 27:16

“Solomon inherited from David and said: O people, we have been taught the speech of birds and given of everything. Indeed, this is manifest favor.”

Al-Naml 27:16

مَنْطِقَ الطَّيْرِ concerns knowledge taught to Solomon.

منطق belongs to a different family and concerns speech and expression. طير names the beings to whom that speech is attributed.

This does not make ṭ y r itself mean speaking or intelligent communication.

In Al-Naml 27:17, birds are mentioned alongside jinn and humans among the forces gathered for Solomon.

In Al-Naml 27:20, Solomon inspects the birds and asks about the hoopoe’s absence.

The subsequent hoopoe account shows that this communication is not restricted to meaningless natural sounds. The hoopoe brings a report about another society, its ruler, religious conduct, and circumstances.

The account should not be turned without evidence into a general theory about all birds’ abilities or a claim that the hoopoe was nonbiological.

The text describes a particular bird and a special communication in Solomon’s story. Conclusions should remain proportionate to that evidence.

Birds’ prayer and glorification

Al-Nūr 24:41 states:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُسَبِّحُ لَهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالطَّيْرُ صَافَّاتٍ كُلٌّ قَدْ عَلِمَ صَلَاتَهُ وَتَسْبِيحَهُ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِمَا يَفْعَلُونَ

a-lam tara anna allāha yusabbiḥu lahu man fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-l-ṭayru ṣāffātin kullun qad ʿalima ṣalātahu wa-tasbīḥahu wa-allāhu ʿalīmun bi-mā yafʿalūn

Al-Nūr 24:41

“Have you not seen that The God is glorified by whoever is in the heavens and earth, and by the birds with wings spread? Each knows its prayer and glorification, and The God knows what they do.”

Al-Nūr 24:41

صَافَّاتٍ describes birds with their wings spread.

The verse then mentions prayer and glorification. “Each knows” follows the reading in which each creature is the subject of عَلِمَ; another interpretation understands The God as knowing each creature’s prayer. This difference does not change the meaning of طير.

The verse describes birds beyond bodily movement, placing them among a wider range of beings connected with divine glorification.

Using صلات for both does not establish that birds perform human worship with the same movements and words.

Conversely, not knowing the manner of their prayer and glorification does not make the Quranic statement meaningless or merely decorative.

Ṭ y r still names birds here; prayer and glorification belong to separate studies of those concepts.

4. تَطَيَّرَ: taking bad omens and blaming others for misfortune

تطيّر is one of this family’s important semantic extensions.

تَطَيَّرَ بِالشَّيْءِ means taking something as a bad omen or regarding it as ill-omened.

This has a historical connection with observing birds and interpreting their movement as a sign of the future. In established usage, however, no bird needs to be present.

A person can take an unfavorable omen from another person, a time, a place, or an event.

All three Quranic verbal occurrences in this branch concern regarding Messengers or callers to truth as ill-omened.

Pharaoh’s people: blaming Moses for adversity

Al-Aʿrāf 7:131 states:

فَإِذَا جَاءَتْهُمُ الْحَسَنَةُ قَالُوا لَنَا هَٰذِهِ وَإِنْ تُصِبْهُمْ سَيِّئَةٌ يَطَّيَّرُوا بِمُوسَىٰ وَمَنْ مَعَهُ أَلَا إِنَّمَا طَائِرُهُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

fa-idhā jāʾat-humu al-ḥasanatu qālū lanā hādhihi wa-in tuṣibhum sayyiʾatun yaṭṭayyarū bi-mūsā wa-man maʿahu alā innamā ṭāʾiruhum ʿinda allāhi wa-lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:131

“When good came to them, they said: This is ours. But if adversity struck them, they regarded Moses and those with him as ill-omened. Indeed, their lot is only with The God, but most of them do not know.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:131

يَطَّيَّرُوا responds here to adversity, سَيِّئَةٌ.

They claimed favorable circumstances as their own, but when an unwelcome event occurred, they treated Moses and his companions as its cause.

تطيّر is not merely a feeling of worry here: it attributes ill fortune and a bad outcome to people.

The Quran reports their statement. Blaming Moses for adversity is their allegation, not the Quran’s endorsement.

The verse ends by saying their طائر is with The God.

The response distinguishes the true reference for destiny and judgment from people’s superstitious attributions.

It does not establish that every social calamity happens without natural causes or human conduct playing any part. The context itself discusses Pharaoh’s people’s behavior and response to signs.

Thamūd: regarding Ṣāliḥ as ill-omened

Al-Naml 27:47 states:

قَالُوا اطَّيَّرْنَا بِكَ وَبِمَنْ مَعَكَ قَالَ طَائِرُكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ بَلْ أَنْتُمْ قَوْمٌ تُفْتَنُونَ

qālū iṭṭayyarnā bika wa-bi-man maʿaka qāla ṭāʾirukum ʿinda allāhi bal antum qawmun tuftanūn

Al-Naml 27:47

“They said: We take you and those with you as an ill omen. He said: Your lot is with The God; rather, you are a people being tested.”

Al-Naml 27:47

Ṣāliḥ’s people regard his presence and that of his companions as ill-omened.

Ṣāliḥ replies طائركم عند الله, then directs attention to the test they are undergoing.

Rather than looking for the cause of misfortune in Ṣāliḥ’s person, they are directed to examine their own situation and conduct toward truth.

The continuing account describes a group spreading corruption and plotting a night attack on Ṣāliḥ and his family.

The story itself thus provides a clear example of human conduct and responsibility.

Difficulty or an unwelcome event does not prove that someone inviting people to reform is ill-omened.

Threatening Messengers through an allegation of ill fortune

Yā Sīn 36:18 states:

قَالُوا إِنَّا تَطَيَّرْنَا بِكُمْ لَئِنْ لَمْ تَنْتَهُوا لَنَرْجُمَنَّكُمْ وَلَيَمَسَّنَّكُمْ مِنَّا عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

qālū innā taṭayyarnā bikum la-in lam tantahū la-narjumannakum wa-la-yamassannakum minnā ʿadhābun alīm

Yā Sīn 36:18

“They said: We regard you as ill-omened. If you do not desist, we will surely stone you, and a painful punishment from us will touch you.”

Yā Sīn 36:18

Taking bad omens has led here to threats and violence.

People not only blame the Messengers for misfortune but use the allegation to threaten them with stoning and abuse.

The next verse gives the reply:

قَالُوا طَائِرُكُمْ مَعَكُمْ أَئِنْ ذُكِّرْتُمْ بَلْ أَنْتُمْ قَوْمٌ مُسْرِفُونَ

qālū ṭāʾirukum maʿakum a-in dhukkirtum bal antum qawmun musrifūn

Yā Sīn 36:19

“They said: Your lot is with you. Is it because you have been reminded? Rather, you are a people exceeding bounds.”

Yā Sīn 36:19

طائركم معكم separates responsibility for their predicament from the Messengers’ presence and directs attention back to the people themselves.

بَلْ أَنْتُمْ قَوْمٌ مُسْرِفُونَ further characterizes their conduct as exceeding limits.

The two verses illustrate how a superstitious notion can become a means of blaming and oppressing others.

The criticism goes beyond superstition: it exposes people who shift responsibility for their problems onto others instead of listening to truth and correcting their conduct.

5. طَائِر: a person’s lot, outcome, and responsibility for conduct

طائر occurs five times in the Quran.

In Al-Anʿām 6:38 it plainly means a bird. The other four uses concern omens, a person’s lot, or their outcome.

The noun’s meaning thus extends beyond actual birds.

طَائِرُهُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ and طَائِرُكُمْ مَعَكُمْ

Al-Aʿrāf 7:131 and Al-Naml 27:47 place their طائر “with The God,” directing the matter of their lot and its judgment to Him.

Yā Sīn 36:19, however, says طائركم معكم: their lot, or the ill fortune they allege, is connected with themselves.

These statements need not conflict.

One identifies the ultimate reference for knowledge, sovereignty, and judgment; the other points people toward their conduct and responsibility.

A person’s destiny being with The God does not establish that their own conduct plays no role in their outcome.

Nor does human responsibility place actions and consequences outside divine knowledge and sovereignty.

Both constructions reject blaming the Messengers’ presence for ill fortune and redirect attention from superstition to truth and responsibility.

وَكُلَّ إِنْسَانٍ أَلْزَمْنَاهُ طَائِرَهُ فِي عُنُقِهِ

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13 states:

وَكُلَّ إِنْسَانٍ أَلْزَمْنَاهُ طَائِرَهُ فِي عُنُقِهِ وَنُخْرِجُ لَهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ كِتَابًا يَلْقَاهُ مَنْشُورًا

wa-kulla insānin alzamnāhu ṭāʾirahu fī ʿunuqihi wa-nukhriju lahu yawma al-qiyāmati kitāban yalqāhu manshūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13

“We have fastened every person’s record to their neck; and on the Day of Resurrection We will bring out for them a book they will find spread open.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13

This is an important figurative use of طائر.

طَائِرَهُ does not mean an actual bird tied around a person’s neck here.

The verse describes a book brought out for a person on the Day of Resurrection. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:14, the person is told to read it and is sufficient to account for themselves.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:15 likewise places responsibility for guidance and straying with the individual; no bearer carries another’s burden.

This sequence connects طائر in Al-Isrāʾ 17:13 with what belongs to a person and becomes evident in their reckoning.

Early Arabic could use طائر for a person’s allotted portion. Here أَلْزَمْنَاهُ, فِي عُنُقِهِ, and the book of reckoning establish an association with inseparability and personal responsibility.

فِي عُنُقِهِ emphasizes its remaining attached to the person, as something hung around the neck or bound to them.

The image does not establish that a person is condemned from birth to sin or an imposed destiny that renders choice ineffective.

The following verses themselves make people responsible for their own guidance and straying.

“Record” is therefore a natural contextual rendering, but it comes from the passage as a whole, not from طائر independently always meaning a book of deeds.

Does طائر mean an unchangeable decree?

Lexical and exegetical discussions have explained a person’s طائر as fortune, lot, deeds, or destiny.

These senses are related but distinct.

A share is what falls to a person.

An action is what a person does.

A consequence is a result of conduct or other factors.

Destiny may refer to a person’s outcome and what happens to them.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:13, the book of reckoning and the verses concerning individual responsibility strengthen the connection with conduct and its record.

Not every Quranic طائر should therefore be translated “deeds.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:131 and Al-Naml 27:47 it answers an allegation of bad omens; “destiny,” “lot,” or “what befalls them” better suits that context.

In Yā Sīn 36:19, the response points people toward their responsibility in the face of reminder and their exceeding of limits.

Thus this branch of طائر concerns the lot and outcome attaching to a person; whether that denotes conduct, its consequences, or alleged fortune depends on context.

6. مُسْتَطِيرًا: spreading and becoming widespread

Al-Insān 76:7 states:

يُوفُونَ بِالنَّذْرِ وَيَخَافُونَ يَوْمًا كَانَ شَرُّهُ مُسْتَطِيرًا

yūfūna bi-l-nadhri wa-yakhāfūna yawman kāna sharruhu mustaṭīrā

Al-Insān 76:7

“They fulfill their vows and fear a day whose evil is widespread.”

Al-Insān 76:7

مُسْتَطِيرًا belongs to ṭ y r and is the active participle of اسْتَطَارَ.

استطار describes something dispersing, spreading, or becoming widespread.

Lexical usages include غُبَارٌ مُسْتَطِير for dust scattered through the air and فَجْرٌ مُسْتَطِير for dawn light spreading across the horizon.

This branch highlights movement and diffusion in different directions.

مستطير does not mean a bird. Dust becoming مستطير has not turned into a bird: it has dispersed through space.

In Al-Insān, مُسْتَطِيرًا describes شَرُّهُ, the day’s evil or hardship.

It therefore conveys that evil being widespread and far-reaching.

The root itself does not mean otherworldly punishment, fear, or destruction here. Those notions come from the verses’ context.

Nor can مستطير alone establish the day’s duration or the physical manner in which punishment spreads.

This use completes the root’s range: alongside birds and omens, the family has a branch concerning dispersal and diffusion.

7. Related roots and translation proposals

Ṭ Y R and J N Ḥ: flight and wings

Ṭ y r concerns flying and the name of birds.

One use of j n ḥ concerns a bird’s wing.

In Al-Anʿām 6:38, طائر and يطير belong to the first family, while بجناحيه belongs to the second.

Their combination identifies the wing as the bodily means of flight, but طائر is not itself a lexical compound meaning “having two wings.”

Wing shape and movement are described by separate Quranic words.

Ṭ Y R and Ṣ F F: flight and spread wings

Al-Nūr 24:41 and Al-Mulk 67:19 describe birds as صافات.

Ṣ f f concerns alignment and ordering, and in this use, spreading wings.

صافات is therefore not another derivative of طير.

One root names the creature or its flight; the other describes its wings’ configuration.

Ṭ Y R and F ʾ L: birds and omens

In familiar Arabic usage, فَأْل commonly concerns taking a favorable indication, for example from a good word heard; it is also recorded more broadly for an unfavorable omen.

تطيّر, in its established use, primarily concerns taking a bad omen.

Historically, observing birds helped shape this use, but not every omen is taken from a bird’s movement.

The Quran uses تطيّر for regarding Messengers as ill-omened.

Mention of this family therefore does not mean that the Quran endorses predicting the future from birds’ movements.

Ṭ Y R and Q S M: receiving a share and dividing

In older Arabic, طار له سهمه can describe a share falling to a person in a distribution.

Q s m concerns the act of dividing into portions.

Here طائر or the family’s verbs can present the share from the perspective of its falling to someone, while قسم emphasizes allocating portions.

This connection does not make every Quranic طائر a financial share.

Ṭ Y R and ʿ M L: a person’s lot and conduct

عمل denotes work or an action someone performs.

طائر in Al-Isrāʾ 17:13 connects with the person’s record and responsibility.

The words are nevertheless lexically distinct.

The book of reckoning permits طائره to be rendered “their record” in that verse, but this one use does not make طائر synonymous with عمل throughout Arabic.

Translation proposals for Forqan

The appropriate equivalent depends on the derivative and its context.

طَارَ / يَطِيرُ: “flew” / “flies.”

طَيْر: “birds”; in some constructions, “a bird” or birds as a kind.

طَائِر in Al-Anʿām 6:38: “a bird.”

تَطَيَّرَ: “took a bad omen” or “regarded as ill-omened.”

اطَّيَّرْنَا بِكَ in Al-Naml 27:47: “we have taken you as an ill omen.”

تَطَيَّرْنَا بِكُمْ in Yā Sīn 36:18: “we regard you as ill-omened.”

طَائِرُهُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:131: “their lot is with The God.”

طَائِرُكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ in Al-Naml 27:47: “your lot is with The God.”

طَائِرُكُمْ مَعَكُمْ in Yā Sīn 36:19: “your lot is with you.”

طَائِرَهُ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:13: “their record,” with its relation to conduct, responsibility, and reckoning explained.

مُسْتَطِير: “spread,” “dispersed,” or “widespread.”

كَانَ شَرُّهُ مُسْتَطِيرًا in Al-Insān 76:7: “whose evil is widespread.” In this description of a future day, كَانَ need not be rendered as past time.

طير should not invariably become “destiny,” nor طائر invariably “a bird.”

“Scientific prediction” is also an unsuitable rendering of تطيّر: the expression concerns assigning favorable or unfavorable significance to signs for which that relationship has not been established.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:13, retaining “We tied their bird to their neck” without explanation can be obscure. “Their record” fits the book and reckoning more naturally, while the original image should remain part of the lexical discussion.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic family ṭ y r centers on flight and birds, extending to rapid movement, dispersal, distribution and receipt of shares, omens, and outcomes attributed to people.

The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates طير for actual birds in pre-Islamic language. Al-Aʿshā’s verse reflects the association between birds’ movements and ill omens. Labīd’s تطير concerning the allocation of shares shows the family’s extension to a person’s portion, although that poem is associated with the period after Islam’s emergence.

The Quran uses this root 29 times in five morphological groups.

Passages concerning actual birds discuss flight, wings, communities, food, glorification, and their place in creation. Birds also occur in special situations in the accounts of Abraham, Jesus, David, and Solomon.

In the تطيّر branch, people regard Messengers and callers to truth as ill-omened. Taking bad omens is not merely a private belief in these verses: it can lead to blame, evasion of responsibility, and even threats and violence against others.

In uses of طائر concerning destiny, the Quran distinguishes allegations of ill fortune from actual responsibility. طائرهم عند الله identifies the ultimate reference for destiny and judgment; طائركم معكم directs people back to their conduct; and ألزمناه طائره في عنقه, alongside the book of reckoning, concerns the attachment of conduct and its consequences to the person.

مستطير in Al-Insān 76:7 shows another branch: something becoming widespread, without actual flight by a living creature.

A final definition of ṭ y r is: principally flying, movement through the air, and the name of birds, with linguistic extensions to dispersal, receiving a share, omens, and an outcome attributed to a person.

These branches do not merge into one meaning. An actual bird, a bad omen, a person’s lot, a record of conduct, and something spreading are different uses of the family. Each form’s meaning must be understood through usage and context.

A central Quranic conclusion is the distinction between observing nature and constructing unsupported judgments from it. A bird and its flight can invite understanding of creation, but they do not without evidence predict human good or bad fortune.

Likewise, a person speaking truth is not a reason to call a society ill-omened or doomed. Against such accusations, the Quran calls people toward truth, examination of their conduct, and acceptance of responsibility.

Birds have their place in creation; humans are responsible for what they think, choose, and do. The connection between those domains within ṭ y r does not mean that human destiny is written in birds’ flight.
