Principal meanings: becoming lean or scant of flesh, particularly through loss of bodily fat and flesh; in another branch, restraining oneself from food or patiently enduring hardship.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The three-consonant root ع ج ف concerns leanness and reduced bodily flesh and fat, including a condition in which a person's or animal's bones become more prominent.

عَجِفَ means becoming lean; أَعْجَف describes a lean being, with feminine عَجْفَاء. عِجَاف is a plural that can apply to males and females.

Its bodily sense concerns scant flesh or fat and a lean condition, rather than narrow proportions alone. The adjective does not, however, always establish a prior history of weight loss.

A camel made lean through inadequate food, with prominent bones, can be أَعْجَف. An animal with naturally slender proportions need not be in the same condition.

Attested Arabic uses describe people, camels, horses, cattle, sheep, and other animals.

Extended uses describe poorly productive land, slender blades, and things lacking usual fullness, thickness, or abundance.

Bodily leanness, an object's slenderness, and unproductive land are related applications, not identical conditions.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

ثَوَى عِنْدَ الْوَدِيَّةِ جَوْفَ بُصْرَى

thawā ʿinda al-wadiyyati jawfa buṣrā

أَبُو الْأَيْتَامِ وَالْكَلِّ الْعِجَافِ

abū al-aytāmi wa-l-kalli al-ʿijāfi

Translation: He lay beside al-Wadiyyah, in Buṣrā—the protector of orphans and lean dependents. وَدِيَّة can denote a young palm, but treating it here as an ordinary noun or a particular place-name requires the poem's commentary and textual transmission.

According to the transmitted account accompanying the poem, it mourns al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥabīb al-Sulamī, who died while traveling with the poet toward Syria.

ثَوَى here concerns remaining or lying at the place of death.

الْكَلّ can describe someone dependent on another's support because they cannot provide for themselves.

الْعِجَاف describes lean, scant-fleshed people living in need and hardship.

The deceased is portrayed as supporting orphans and dependents in material and bodily distress.

The witness matters because عجاف was not confined to animals: it also described lean people.

This single verse, consisting of two hemistichs, is transmitted in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays. There are variants in the place-name's spelling; the attribution is familiar, although the passage's exact date has not been independently established here.

It provides a witness for human leanness within the family's early usage.

Another witness: slender objects

A verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī says:

تَرَاحُ يَدَاهُ لِمَحْشُورَةٍ

tarāḥu yadāhu li-maḥshūratin

خَوَاظِي الْقِدَاحِ عِجَافِ النِّصَالِ

khawāẓī al-qidāḥi ʿijāfi al-niṣāli

The verse describes hands moving readily toward arrows with substantial shafts and slender arrowheads. The relevant point is the contrast between full shafts and thin points.

النِّصَال is the plural of نَصْل and here means arrowheads.

عِجَاف describes their slenderness.

Unlike an animal, an arrowhead does not lose flesh through hunger. Its narrowness or thinness motivates the descriptive extension.

Umayyah has been classed as a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; much of his life and poetry belongs to the Islamic and Umayyad periods. Without dating this verse, it cannot be treated as securely pre-Islamic.

The witness nevertheless illustrates classical Arabic's extension from scant bodily flesh to an object's thinness.

Another branch: self-restraint and endurance

Early dictionaries record عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ عَنِ الطَّعَامِ for withholding oneself from desired food, particularly to allow someone else to have it.

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ عَلَى الْمَرِيضِ concerns patiently nursing a sick person and enduring the demands of care.

A semantic connection with leanness is understandable: giving up food can lead to bodily loss, while enduring a difficult task involves limiting one's wishes or comfort.

Yet enduring hardship and becoming lean are not the same meaning.

Someone may patiently nurse a patient without losing weight. An animal may become lean through illness without any voluntary self-restraint.

Self-restraint and endurance should therefore remain distinct from bodily leanness.

This branch is recorded in Arabic sources, but the evidence examined here does not independently date all its uses to the pre-Islamic period.

2. The lexical family and extensions of meaning

Recorded forms include عَجِفَ, عَجُفَ, عَجَف, عَجْف, عُجُوف, أَعْجَف, عَجْفَاء, عَجِف, عِجَاف, عَجِيف, أَعْجَفَ, تَعْجِيف, and تَعَجُّف.

These forms occur in several semantic branches.

عَجِفَ and عَجُفَ: becoming lean

عَجِفَ is a principal verb meaning to become lean or scant of flesh.

عَجُفَ is also recorded.

عَجِفَتِ الدَّابَّةُ describes an animal becoming lean, with reduced flesh or fat.

Such a state may arise through insufficient food, illness, poor nourishment, or other difficulties.

The verb itself does not identify the cause.

One animal may become lean because grazing is inadequate; another may have enough food yet lose weight through illness.

The bodily condition can be similar while its cause differs.

عجف describes bodily condition, not necessarily its medical or economic cause.

عَجَف: leanness and loss of flesh

عَجَف, with fatḥah on ʿayn and jīm, names leanness or becoming scant of flesh.

Dictionaries explain it with ذَهَابُ السِّمَنِ: loss of fatness or bodily fullness.

A prominent example is reduced flesh and fat around the bones, making them more visible.

This is not a numerical definition of weight or fat. The lexical description prescribes no particular percentage of weight loss.

Leanness and physical weakness also differ.

A lean being may retain sufficient ability for many tasks, while a fat one may be weak through illness or other circumstances.

عَجَف should therefore not be reduced to general incapacity.

أَعْجَف and عَجْفَاء: lean or scant of flesh

أَعْجَف is masculine; عَجْفَاء is its feminine form.

جَمَلٌ أَعْجَفُ means a lean camel.

بَقَرَةٌ عَجْفَاءُ means a lean bovine.

The description applies to people and animals and, by extension, slender objects or unproductive land.

Dictionaries also record عَجِف and عَجِيف for a lean being.

These singular forms do not themselves occur in the Quran.

عِجَاف: the plural of lean

عِجَاف is the familiar plural associated with أَعْجَف and عَجْفَاء.

It can describe males or females.

Morphological discussions regard the فِعَال plural for this أَفْعَل / فَعْلَاء adjective as unusual.

Some lexicographers explain its form by opposition to سِمَان: the plural for fat beings provided an analogy for the contrasting lean ones.

Others cite comparable formations and do not treat it as wholly without parallels.

Accounts of its regularity differ, but عِجَاف is an established Arabic form and occurs in the Quran.

For translation, the important point is that Yūsuf directly contrasts سِمَان and عِجَاف.

أَعْجَفَ: making lean

أَعْجَفَ is Form IV and, in the bodily use, means making lean or causing loss of flesh.

In أَعْجَفَ الدَّابَّةَ, the subject causes the animal to become lean.

The difference from عَجِفَ is clear.

عَجِفَتِ الدَّابَّةُ means that the animal became lean.

أَعْجَفَ الدَّابَّةَ means that someone made the animal lean.

The first concerns the animal's condition or change of state; the second introduces its cause as the subject.

أَعْجَفَ الْقَوْمُ is also recorded for people whose livestock have become lean. It does not by itself say that the owners deliberately restricted their animals' food.

Livestock condition may reflect people's material circumstances, but the appearance of a lean animal alone does not establish its cause or the owner's conduct.

عَجَّفَ and تَعْجِيف: insufficient eating or nourishment

عَجَّفَ is Form II; تَعْجِيف is its verbal noun.

Recorded uses of تعجيف include eating short of satiety, insufficient nourishment, or passing one's food to someone else before being satisfied.

During scarcity, someone might reduce their portion so another person in need can eat.

This can be voluntary; in another situation, inadequate food may be involuntary and result from poverty.

These situations should not be treated as ethically identical.

Dictionaries also use تعجيف for poor nourishment and associated leanness.

Limited food and its effects are therefore prominent in this branch.

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ: restraining oneself

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ عَنِ الطَّعَامِ describes restraining oneself from food.

نَفْسَهُ is the verb's object.

The person holds back from something desired.

A different preposition changes the construction's setting.

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ عَلَى الْمَرِيضِ concerns patient perseverance in nursing a sick person.

The family thus describes not only bodies but also limiting one's wishes and enduring difficulty.

Quranic عِجَاف does not belong to this branch and should not mean patient people or those giving up their food.

Unproductive land and slender objects

أَرْضٌ عَجْفَاءُ is recorded for poorly productive land lacking sufficient growth.

Land has no bodily flesh or fat to lose; deficient fertility is likened to a living being's leanness.

نَصْلٌ أَعْجَفُ can describe a slender blade.

Here thinness or narrowness is prominent.

These uses illustrate extension from bodily fullness to an object's volume or thickness, or a place's productivity.

عجاف should not universally become “dry” or “unproductive.” Such equivalents require a suitable subject and context.

3. Quranic occurrences

The root occurs twice, in two verses, only as عِجَافٌ.

Both occur in Yūsuf: Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46.

Both describe lean cattle in the king's dream.

No other verb or derivative of this family occurs in the Quran.

A. عِجَاف: lean cattle in the king's dream

وَقَالَ الْمَلِكُ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ ۖ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي رُؤْيَايَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لِلرُّؤْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ

wa-qāla al-maliku innī arā sabʿa baqarātin simānin yaʾkuluhunna sabʿun ʿijāfun wa-sabʿa sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu aftūnī fī ruʾyāya in kuntum li-l-ruʾyā taʿburūn

Yūsuf 12:43

The king said, “I see seven fat cattle being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry. Chiefs, give me an answer about my dream, if you interpret dreams.”

Yūsuf 12:43

عِجَافٌ describes the seven lean cattle.

سِمَانٍ describes the seven fat ones.

The two words contrast directly.

سِمَان belongs to س م ن and concerns bodily fatness and fullness.

عِجَاف belongs to ع ج ف and concerns leanness or scant flesh and fat.

The dream's contrast is seven fat cattle and seven lean ones.

Why عِجَاف rather than simply weak?

عِجَاف directly describes bodily leanness.

ضَعِيف, from ض ع ف, concerns weakness or reduced ability.

A lean animal may also have less strength, but the concepts are not identical.

A fat animal may have reduced mobility or activity because of illness.

“Seven weak cattle” therefore fails to preserve the specific sense of سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ.

“Seven lean cattle” retains the bodily feature contrasted with the others' fatness.

Does عِجَاف mean hungry?

Leanness can result from inadequate food, but عجاف does not inherently say that an animal is hungry at that moment.

An animal made lean by a prolonged shortage might be eating when observed.

A fat animal might also be hungry at that moment.

Hunger and leanness are distinct states.

In the dream, the lean cattle eat the fat ones.

Eating is expressed by يَأْكُلُهُنَّ, not the adjective عِجَاف.

The role of يَأْكُلُهُنَّ

The verse uses يَأْكُلُهُنَّ.

Its object pronoun هُنَّ refers to the seven fat cattle.

سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ identifies the seven lean ones doing the eating.

The sentence thus says that seven lean cattle eat seven fat ones.

This is an unusual image compared with ordinary cattle feeding.

The verse, however, describes a dream.

The dream's behavior should not be turned into a rule about cattle in nature.

Nor should عجاف be changed to “predatory cattle.” The adjective describes leanness; eating belongs to another part of the dream image.

Cattle and ears of grain

The same dream shows seven green ears of grain alongside dry ones.

خُضْرٍ describes greenness; يَابِسَاتٍ describes the others' dryness.

The images correspond to the contrast between سِمَان and عِجَاف.

One side presents fat cattle and green ears.

The other presents lean cattle and dry ears.

Animal and plant descriptions should nevertheless remain distinct.

عجاف concerns the animal's bodily leanness.

يابسات concerns the ears' dryness.

They are not lexical synonyms, although the dream connects both with shortage and hardship.

B. عِجَاف: the dream retold to Joseph

يُوسُفُ أَيُّهَا الصِّدِّيقُ أَفْتِنَا فِي سَبْعِ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعِ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ لَعَلِّي أَرْجِعُ إِلَى النَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

yūsufu ayyuhā al-ṣiddīqu aftinā fī sabʿi baqarātin simānin yaʾkuluhunna sabʿun ʿijāfun wa-sabʿi sunbulātin khuḍrin wa-ukhara yābisātin laʿallī arjiʿu ilā al-nāsi laʿallahum yaʿlamūn

Yūsuf 12:46

“Joseph, truthful one, give us an answer about seven fat cattle being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry, so that I may return to the people, that they may know.”

Yūsuf 12:46

The king's dream is now recounted to Joseph.

عِجَافٌ has the same form and meaning in both verses.

The two Quranic occurrences therefore do not differ in lexical meaning.

Their narrative positions differ.

In Yūsuf 12:43, the king himself tells his dream to the chiefs and those around him.

In Yūsuf 12:46, the man previously released from prison recounts it to Joseph and requests an answer.

Both set seven lean cattle against seven fat ones.

Leanness remains a stable and significant element of the dream rather than a detail changed during retelling.

The syntax of سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ

Both verses use سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ.

سَبْعٌ is the grammatical subject of يَأْكُلُهُنَّ and is nominative.

عِجَافٌ is also nominative, describing the seven lean cattle.

بَقَرَاتٍ is explicit earlier; the second group does not repeat the noun, which the context supplies.

The full sense is therefore “seven lean cattle.”

Omitting the repeated noun makes the sentence more compact and fluent.

Grammatically عِجَافٌ modifies سَبْعٌ; semantically it describes the cattle meant by the number, not an abstract number becoming lean or fat.

4. عِجَاف and the interpretation of the dream

Joseph answers after the dream is recounted.

Yūsuf 12:47 describes seven years of steady cultivation and instructs that the harvested grain remain in its ears, apart from a small amount for consumption.

Yūsuf 12:48 describes seven hard years following the first period, consuming what was stored, except a little kept in reserve.

Yūsuf 12:49 then describes a year of relief.

The dream's fat and lean cattle thus correspond to different periods of production and food supply.

Seven fat cattle and years of provision

Fat cattle stand opposite lean cattle in the dream.

Joseph first describes seven years of cultivation and production.

Their fatness corresponds to a period allowing crops to be grown, harvested, and stored.

سِمَان does not lexically mean productive years.

That significance comes from the interpretation and Joseph's answer.

Seven lean cattle and seven hard years

The seven lean cattle eat the fat ones.

In Yūsuf 12:48, seven hard years consume the earlier stores, apart from a little preserved.

The relation between the images becomes clear.

Just as lean cattle consume fat ones in the dream, the hard years will involve consuming reserves from years of provision.

This is central to the interpretation.

The lexical meaning of عجاف must nevertheless be distinguished from its significance in this dream.

عِجَاف means lean beings in Arabic.

Here the lean cattle correspond to a period of hardship and shortage.

“Years of famine” is therefore not a direct translation of عجاف; the years are known through interpretation, not the adjective alone.

Does عِجَاف mean drought?

أَرْضٌ عَجْفَاءُ is also recorded for unproductive land.

That use concerns poor yield or inadequate growth.

The dream's lean cattle likewise correspond to hard years that consume earlier stores.

These connections do not make “drought” a universal equivalent of عجاف.

Drought concerns rainfall shortage and its effects; animal leanness can have different causes.

Yūsuf 12:48 uses سَبْعٌ شِدَادٌ: seven hard years.

The phrase does not specify rainfall amounts or all the years' natural conditions.

Hardship and food shortage are clear in the interpretation, but عجاف is not inherently a meteorological term.

From knowledge of the future to preparation

Joseph does more than announce that hard years will follow years of provision.

He also sets out a practical response.

Yūsuf 12:47 calls for leaving the harvest in its ears, except a little to be eaten.

Yūsuf 12:48 describes consumption of earlier reserves during the hard years, except a little retained.

Knowledge of future circumstances is therefore joined to preparation and management of resources.

The interpretation is not passive prediction: it enables decisions and action.

For example, a community consuming all its resources immediately in productive years may face greater difficulty when yields decline.

Preserving some resources for future needs can improve its ability to endure shortages.

That is a social and economic application of the story, not the lexical meaning of ع ج ف.

The bodily sense remains leanness or scant flesh and fat; its place in the dream connects it with provision, shortage, and preparation for the future.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ع ج ف and س م ن: leanness and fatness

سِمَن concerns fatness and bodily fullness of flesh and fat.

عَجَف contrasts with it through scant flesh and fat and a lean condition.

Yūsuf 12:43 and Yūsuf 12:46 bring the two families into the same image.

سِمَان describes the fat cattle.

عِجَاف describes the lean ones.

Their bodily contrast is direct.

Fatness alone does not establish complete health, nor does leanness identify its exact cause or the animal's entire condition.

The words primarily describe bodily appearance or condition.

ع ج ف and ه ز ل: leanness and wasting

هُزَال concerns leanness, wasting, or reduced bodily flesh.

Dictionaries sometimes explain عَجَف directly with هزال.

The two are consequently close in bodily uses.

ع ج ف also includes other branches, such as restraining oneself from food and patiently enduring hardship.

This prevents treating every use of the two families as synonymous.

Another Arabic use of هَزْل means joking or unserious speech; that differs from bodily leanness.

Al-Ṭāriq 86:14 uses الْهَزْلِ in contrast with serious discourse, not for bodily thinness.

Similarity between forms and some meanings should not merge distinct semantic fields.

ع ج ف and ض ع ف: leanness and weakness

ضَعْف concerns diminished strength or ability.

عجف concerns leanness or scant bodily flesh and fat.

They may coexist but are not identical.

Inadequate nourishment can leave someone both lean and weak, making both descriptions appropriate.

Another person may be lean yet sufficiently strong.

A fat person may be weak in movement or in a particular task.

“The incapable” therefore loses the specific bodily meaning of عجاف.

“Lean” fits Yūsuf better because it contrasts with “fat.”

ع ج ف and ج و ع: leanness and hunger

جُوع means hunger or need for food.

عَجَف describes bodily leanness.

Hunger can contribute to leanness, but the relation is not necessary in every instance.

An animal briefly hungry may remain fat.

A lean animal may not be hungry at that moment.

Hunger concerns food need; عجف concerns bodily condition.

This distinction rules out “seven hungry cattle” as an adequate translation of سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ.

ع ج ف and خ م ص: scant flesh and an empty belly

خ م ص concerns abdominal hollowness and hunger.

مَخْمَصَة occurs in the Quran, including Al-Tawbah 9:120, for hunger or food shortage.

عجف is not confined to the belly: it concerns broader bodily leanness.

Someone may have an empty stomach from hunger without yet becoming lean.

Someone very lean may have a full stomach after eating.

The two conditions are related but distinct.

ع ج ف and ج د ب: leanness and unproductive land

جَدْب concerns dryness, reduced fertility, or land lacking sufficient growth and yield.

أَرْضٌ عَجْفَاءُ likewise describes poorly productive land.

Here the image of a lean living being extends to the land's lack of yield.

The earth does not literally lose flesh or fat.

عجفاء is an extended description of land, whereas جدب directly concerns land and productivity.

Likewise, the king's عجاف are lean cattle. Their interpretation as hard years does not make animal leanness and unproductive land lexically identical.

ع ج ف and ي ب س: leanness and dryness

ي ب س concerns drying or loss of moisture.

Yūsuf 12:43 uses يَابِسَاتٍ for dry ears of grain.

The same verse uses عِجَاف for lean cattle.

The dream brings the descriptions together without making them synonyms.

Dry grain concerns the plant's moisture and condition.

Lean cattle concern scant bodily flesh and fat.

عجاف should therefore not become “dry,” nor يابسات “lean.”

Both can signify shortage or hardship within the dream, but that connection comes from imagery and interpretation, not identical roots.

6. Morphological and semantic observations

The form عِجَاف

عِجَاف is a broken plural, appearing in the Quran as nominative عِجَافٌ.

Its familiar masculine singular is أَعْجَف, and its feminine singular عَجْفَاء.

The plural can describe males and females.

The Yūsuf verses use grammatically feminine بقرات. In classical Arabic, بقرة can designate one member of the cattle kind, male or female; grammatical gender alone does not establish the animals' biological sex.

Morphological sources discuss this unusual plural and its relationship to سِمَان.

Its unusual formation does not change the basic meaning.

In both verses it refers to lean cattle.

Bodily description and the cause of leanness

The dream's cattle are described as lean.

The adjective alone does not say why.

In ordinary life, inadequate food, illness, or other conditions can cause an animal to lose weight.

Joseph's interpretation links the image with hard years.

The cause or significance inferred from interpretation should not become part of the adjective's fixed lexical meaning.

عجاف describes leanness; years of shortage are what the cattle correspond to in this particular dream.

Description and behavior

عِجَاف describes the seven cattle; يَأْكُلُهُنَّ expresses eating.

These differ grammatically and semantically.

The lean cattle do the eating, but leanness does not itself mean eating, attacking, or destroying.

In the interpretation, consuming stored resources comes from the eating image and Joseph's answer, not عجاف alone.

Moral evaluation

The bodily sense of ع ج ف carries no inherent positive or negative moral judgment.

Leanness may result from poverty, illness, food shortage, or other circumstances.

It does not by itself determine a person's or animal's moral worth, will, or conduct.

In عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ, however, restraint from food may accompany sacrifice or prioritizing another's need.

That use does not make all leanness a virtue.

Involuntary deprivation also differs from freely chosen self-restraint.

Quranic عِجَاف describes the cattle's bodies in a dream without morally judging them.

7. Proposed renderings for Forqan

The principal Quranic equivalent is “lean.”

It naturally conveys scant flesh and contrasts with “fat” for سِمَان.

عَجِفَ: became lean; lost bodily flesh or fat.

عَجَف: leanness, scant flesh, or reduced fatness.

أَعْجَف: lean, scant of flesh.

عَجْفَاء: the feminine form of lean; in other uses, poorly productive land.

عِجَاف: lean beings; in the Quran, lean cattle.

أَعْجَفَ: made lean; caused loss of flesh or fat.

عَجَّفَ / تَعْجِيف: eating short of satiety or passing food to another before being satisfied; poor nourishment and associated leanness in other recorded uses. The exact sense depends on construction and context.

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ عَنِ الطَّعَامِ: withheld oneself from desired food.

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ عَلَى الْمَرِيضِ: patiently continued nursing a sick person and endured its difficulty.

Yūsuf 12:43

8. Conclusion

ع ج ف has two principal Arabic branches: leanness or scant flesh and fat, and self-restraint or endurance.

عَجِفَ concerns becoming lean; أَعْجَف and عَجْفَاء describe a lean being; عِجَاف is the plural.

The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates lean, needy people. Dictionaries also record animals, unproductive land, and slender objects.

عَجَفَ نَفْسَهُ can describe restraint from food or patient endurance. This has an intelligible connection with bodily leanness, but not every lean condition results from voluntary restraint.

The Quran uses the family only twice, both as عِجَافٌ in Yūsuf.

In Yūsuf 12:43, the king describes seven fat cattle eaten by seven lean ones.

Yūsuf 12:46 recounts the same dream to Joseph.

Both contrast عجاف, lean, with سمان, fat.

Joseph then describes seven years of cultivation and storage in Yūsuf 12:47, seven hard years consuming reserves in Yūsuf 12:48, and a year of relief in Yūsuf 12:49.

The lean cattle correspond to hardship and shortage; eating the fat ones corresponds to consumption of earlier reserves.

That is the interpretation of the dream, not a replacement for the lexical meaning of عجاف.

Differences from هزال, ضعف, جوع, خمص, and يبس should remain clear. Leanness may accompany weakness, hunger, or deprivation without making these states synonymous.

In the Quran, ع ج ف is a bodily description within a dream image, connected through Joseph's interpretation with a community's material and economic circumstances.

Final definition: the principal bodily branch of ع ج ف concerns leanness or scant flesh and fat, including becoming lean. It describes people and animals and extends to thin objects or unproductive land. Another branch concerns withholding oneself from food and patiently enduring difficulty. The Quran uses only عِجَافٌ, for seven lean cattle in the king's dream; Joseph's interpretation connects them with seven hard years consuming reserves from preceding years.
