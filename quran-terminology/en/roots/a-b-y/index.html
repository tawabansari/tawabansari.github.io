The Qur’anic Root A B Y

Ibāʾ: refusing and declining; from disobedience and rejection to resolve and declining a trust

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَبَىٰ
(abā)
Qur’anic past verb: he refused; also occurs with wa- and fa-

يَأْبَىٰ
(yaʾbā)
Qur’anic imperfect; The God is its subject in Al-Tawbah 9:32

تَأْبَىٰ
(taʾbā)
Qur’anic feminine singular imperfect; its subject is hearts

لَا يَأْبَ
(lā yaʾba)
Jussive imperfect after prohibitive lā; twice in Al-Baqarah 2:282

أَبَوْا
(abaw)
Qur’anic masculine plural past verb; Al-Kahf 18:77

أَبَيْنَ
(abayna)
Qur’anic feminine plural past verb; Al-Aḥzāb 33:72

إِبَاء / إِبَاءَة
(ibāʾ / ibāʾah)
Lexical verbal nouns; absent from the Qur’anic text

آبٍ
(ābin)

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root A B Y concerns refusing, declining, and withholding acceptance. أَبَىٰ (abā) is used when someone will not accept something, declines to perform an action, or refuses to comply with a request, proposal, or command.

Such refusal may arise from arrogance, hostility, fear, dissatisfaction, or firm resolve. None of these motives, however, is an invariable part of the root’s meaning.

The Qur’an uses this verb for Iblīs refusing to prostrate, Pharaoh refusing to accept the signs, people declining a social responsibility, villagers refusing hospitality, and the heavens, earth, and mountains declining the trust. It also uses the verb of The God, saying that He will accept nothing other than completing His light.

This range shows that ibāʾ, in its lexical meaning, is neither invariably blameworthy nor invariably a mark of dignity or nobility. Its value depends on what is accepted or rejected, who the subject is, and the sentence’s context.

Qur’anic forms

A B Y occurs 13 times in 12 verses across eight surahs. All its occurrences belong to the Form I verb abā; no independent nominal form from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

The attested inflected forms are:

  • أَبَىٰ (abā): he refused or declined; Al-Baqarah 2:34, Al-Ḥijr 15:31, and Ṭā Hā 20:116.
  • فَأَبَىٰ (fa-abā): so he refused; Al-Isrāʾ 17:89, Al-Isrāʾ 17:99, and Al-Furqān 25:50.
  • وَأَبَىٰ (wa-abā): and he refused; Ṭā Hā 20:56.
  • وَتَأْبَىٰ (wa-taʾbā): and they refuse; Al-Tawbah 9:8, concerning the hearts of a group of covenant breakers.
  • وَيَأْبَى (wa-yaʾbā): and He refuses; Al-Tawbah 9:32, concerning The God.
  • لَا يَأْبَ (lā yaʾba): let him not refuse; twice in Al-Baqarah 2:282, concerning the scribe and witnesses.
  • فَأَبَوْا (fa-abaw): so they refused; Al-Kahf 18:77.
  • فَأَبَيْنَ (fa-abayna): so they declined; Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, concerning the heavens, earth, and mountains.

In this list, forms prefixed with fa- or wa- have the same underlying verb. These particles connect them syntactically to other clauses; they do not create a new root.

Other familiar Arabic forms in this family include إِبَاء (ibāʾ) and إِبَاءَة (ibāʾah), “refusal”; آبٍ (ābin), someone who refuses; أَبِيّ (abiyy), someone who will not submit to something; أُبَاة (ubāh), people who refuse; and تَأَبَّى (taʾabbā), to refuse or resist something.

These nouns and derivatives help define the family’s semantic range, but they should not be presented as words occurring in the Qur’an.

2. The root in early Arabic

Ibāʾ: refusing something presented to a person

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies refusal as the root’s principal meaning.

He illustrates ibāʾ through a situation in which something is offered to someone, who declines to accept it.

This usage distinguishes encountering a proposal from accepting it. Something may be offered to a person without that person wishing to accept it.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records أَبَى الشَّيْءَ (abā al-shayʾa) for disliking or being unwilling to accept something, and refusing it.

These usages suggest three related elements: encountering something, not accepting it, and declining to participate or act.

They should not become a rigid formula requiring every sentence to describe a formal offer or explicit command before the refusal. Arabic can describe someone disliking and refusing something without reporting every stage of an offer or request.

Is ibāʾ stronger than imtināʿ?

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains ibāʾ as “intense refusal.” In this account, every ibāʾ is a refusal, but not every refusal is as firm or intense as ibāʾ.

This distinction fits many Qur’anic uses. Iblīs, for example, refuses to prostrate in a context of arrogance. Pharaoh also calls the signs false and refuses after seeing them.

Al-Rāghib’s explanation should not imply that every occurrence of abā necessarily expresses the strongest possible degree of refusal.

A refusal’s intensity can depend on its circumstances, motive, and consequences.

In Al-Kahf 18:77, a village’s inhabitants refuse hospitality. In Al-Baqarah 2:282, the scribe and witnesses are instructed not to refuse their tasks.

Here the verb itself means refusing or declining; the root does not fully determine the intensity, duration, or motives involved.

“Refusing and declining” is therefore a suitable basic meaning, with firmness or intensity treated as a feature prominent in many uses.

Abiyy: a person who will not accept humiliation

Arabic uses رَجُلٌ أَبِيٌّ (rajulun abiyyun) for someone unwilling to accept humiliation or oppression.

The related expression أَبِيُّ الضَّيْمِ (abiyyu al-ḍaym) means someone who will not submit to oppression and humiliation.

A verse by Al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm says:

نَمَانِي كُلُّ أَصْيَدَ مِنْ أَمَانٍ
أَبِيِّ الضَّيْمِ مِنْ نَفَرٍ أُبَاةِ

namānī kullu aṣyada min amānin / abiyyi al-ḍaymi min nafarin ubāti

“Proud men of Amān raised me: men who reject oppression, from a people who will not submit to humiliation.”

Here abiyy and ubāh concern refusing humiliation.

Ibāʾ can therefore be a praiseworthy human quality.

That positive value should not be transferred to every occurrence of the root.

The same language that calls someone abiyy for rejecting oppression can use abā of someone who refuses to accept the truth.

Thus the moral value depends on what the person refuses, rather than on the act of refusing itself.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ was an Islamic-period poet. This is evidence from early Arabic, but it is not pre-Islamic poetry.

A pre-Islamic witness for abayān

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes a verse attributed to Abū al-Mujashshir al-Ḍabbī:

وَقَبْلَكَ مَا هَابَ الرِّجَالُ ظُلَامَتِي
وَفَقَّأْتُ عَيْنَ الْأَشْوَسِ الْأَبَيَانِ

wa-qablaka mā hāba al-rijālu ẓulāmatī / wa-faqqaʾtu ʿayna al-ashwasi al-abayāni

“Before you, too, men feared to wrong me; and I put out the eye of the haughty, defiant man.”

Here الأَبَيَان (al-abayān), with a vowel on the bāʾ, describes someone very obstinate who does not readily submit to another.

This witness shows the root’s use in Arabic poetry for human defiance as well.

Defiance is not necessarily a virtue. Someone may resist oppression or stand against the truth out of arrogance.

Sources classify Abū al-Mujashshir as a pre-Islamic poet and attribute this verse to him. Its transmission in a later dictionary should nevertheless be distinguished from independent confirmation of the poem’s date and exact wording.

Ibāʾ in animal behavior

Arabic also uses آبِيَة (ābiyah) for an animal that refuses food or water.

مَاءٌ مَأْبَاة (māʾun maʾbāh) denotes water that camels refuse to drink.

In camel husbandry, ābiyah and its plural أَوَابِي (awābī) are also recorded for she-camels that have been covered by a male but have not conceived. In Abū ʿAmr’s account, this term applies until their first conception. This usage should not be turned into a conscious decision by an animal to reject pregnancy.

These usages matter because they show that ibāʾ was not confined to human moral or doctrinal decisions.

An animal may turn away from water or food without its behavior constituting arrogance or denial of truth.

The lexical core is thus refusal and nonacceptance. Attributing intention, arrogance, or moral responsibility depends on the context and the nature of the subject.

A morphological point

The Arabic verb أَبَىٰ يَأْبَىٰ (abā yaʾbā) has a distinctive vowel pattern.

Early grammatical discussions, including Sībawayh’s work and reports transmitted by Ibn Manẓūr, note the unusual fatḥah in its imperfect.

Some verbs of comparable structure would normally have a different imperfect vowel, but yaʾbā is established in familiar Arabic usage.

The Qur’an has both yaʾbā and the jussive form يَأْبَ (yaʾba).

These differences arise from inflection. They do not create separate meanings such as greater refusal or a special kind of arrogance.

3. Iblīs and Pharaoh: refusing a command or signs

Iblīs: refusal accompanied by arrogance

Al-Baqarah, 2:34

وَإِذْ قُلْنَا لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ اسْجُدُوا لِآدَمَ فَسَجَدُوا إِلَّا إِبْلِيسَ أَبَىٰ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ وَكَانَ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-idh qulnā li-al-malāʾikati usjudū li-ādama fa-sajadū illā iblīsa abā wa-istakbara wa-kāna mina al-kāfirīn

“And when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate yourselves to Adam,’ they prostrated, except Iblīs. He refused and acted arrogantly, and was among the disbelievers.”

This is one of the clearest occurrences of the root.

A command to prostrate is given. The angels prostrate, while Iblīs refuses to do so.

The Qur’an does not stop at abā. It immediately adds وَاسْتَكْبَرَ (wa-istakbara), then states that he was among the disbelievers.

Three concepts should therefore be distinguished.

Abā expresses his refusal to perform the command.

Istakbara concerns self-exaltation and arrogance.

كَانَ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ (kāna mina al-kāfirīn) locates Iblīs within the field of disbelief.

Lexically, abā expresses refusal, while the other two expressions identify Iblīs’s arrogance and disbelief in this context. Their juxtaposition alone does not make them synonyms.

The verse describes a refusal accompanied by arrogance and disbelief. That association does not erase each root’s independent meaning.

Ibāʾ in other accounts of Iblīs

Al-Ḥijr, 15:31

إِلَّا إِبْلِيسَ أَبَىٰ أَنْ يَكُونَ مَعَ السَّاجِدِينَ

illā iblīsa abā an yakūna maʿa al-sājidīn

“Except Iblīs, who refused to be among those prostrating.”

Here abā is followed by أَنْ يَكُونَ (an yakūna), “to be.”

The object of refusal is being among those prostrating.

One may therefore say that Iblīs refused to prostrate with the others. Abā itself, however, does not simply mean separating from a group.

In this context, remaining apart from those prostrating results from his refusal to carry out the command.

Ṭā Hā, 20:116

وَإِذْ قُلْنَا لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ اسْجُدُوا لِآدَمَ فَسَجَدُوا إِلَّا إِبْلِيسَ أَبَىٰ

wa-idh qulnā li-al-malāʾikati usjudū li-ādama fa-sajadū illā iblīsa abā

“And when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate yourselves to Adam,’ they prostrated, except Iblīs. He refused.”

This verse recounts the same event more briefly.

Al-Baqarah also mentions arrogance and disbelief; Ṭā Hā highlights Iblīs’s refusal.

Comparison shows that the Qur’an can recount one event with different details. A fuller understanding requires reading the relevant passages together.

Every detail of one verse should not, however, be made part of a word’s lexical meaning in another verse.

Pharaoh: calling false and refusing

Ṭā Hā, 20:56

وَلَقَدْ أَرَيْنَاهُ آيَاتِنَا كُلَّهَا فَكَذَّبَ وَأَبَىٰ

wa-la-qad araynāhu āyātinā kullahā fa-kadhdhaba wa-abā

“We certainly showed him all Our signs, but he called them false and refused.”

Pharaoh is the subject of the verb here.

He encounters the signs, calls them false, and refuses acceptance.

Again there are two distinct verbs: كَذَّبَ (kadhdhaba) and أَبَىٰ (abā).

Kadhdhaba expresses calling something false or untrue.

Ibāʾ expresses refusing and withholding acceptance.

The behaviors can accompany one another without being identical.

A person may decline to accept a claim without yet explicitly calling it a lie. Someone may also regard something as false and consequently refuse to accept it.

Here the Qur’an states both concerning Pharaoh’s behavior.

“He called them false and refused” is therefore preferable to reducing both verbs to “he denied.”

4. Refusal in human relationships: responsibility, hospitality, and covenants

The scribe and witnesses

A B Y occurs twice in Al-Baqarah 2:282.

The verse concerns recording debts, writing financial obligations, obtaining witnesses, and protecting the parties’ rights.

First the scribe is instructed not to refuse to write; the witnesses are then instructed not to refuse when summoned to bear witness.

Both occurrences have لَا يَأْبَ (lā yaʾba).

Syntactically, this is an imperfect in the jussive after prohibitive lā. Its subject differs between the two occurrences: first the scribe, then the witnesses.

Ibāʾ here does not mean arrogance or disbelief.

A scribe or witness may refuse a requested task. In regulating financial rights, the Qur’an restricts such refusal under the circumstances it describes.

The root itself does not determine when writing or testimony becomes obligatory, what excuses are acceptable, or how witnesses should be protected from harm and pressure.

These issues depend on the verse as a whole and other principles concerning justice and testimony.

Early explanations also differ on whether the prohibition concerning witnesses primarily concerns agreeing to become witnesses or giving testimony already undertaken.

The lexical meaning of yaʾba therefore cannot establish an unconditional legal ruling for every request to testify.

The central point for the root study is clear: in a particular setting, refusal may concern a social responsibility without the word inherently meaning disbelief or hostility.

The villagers’ refusal of hospitality

Al-Kahf, 18:77

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَيَا أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ اسْتَطْعَمَا أَهْلَهَا فَأَبَوْا أَنْ يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا فَوَجَدَا فِيهَا جِدَارًا يُرِيدُ أَنْ يَنْقَضَّ فَأَقَامَهُ ۖ قَالَ لَوْ شِئْتَ لَاتَّخَذْتَ عَلَيْهِ أَجْرًا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā atayā ahla qaryatin istaṭʿamā ahlahā fa-abaw an yuḍayyifūhumā fa-wajadā fīhā jidāran yurīdu an yanqaḍḍa fa-aqāmahu; qāla law shiʾta la-ittakhadhta ʿalayhi ajrā

“So the two proceeded until they reached a village’s inhabitants. They asked its people for food, but the people refused to offer them hospitality. They then found a wall there on the point of collapse, and he set it upright. Moses said, ‘Had you wished, you could have taken payment for it.’”

Here فَأَبَوْا (fa-abaw) is a masculine plural past verb.

Its subject is the village’s inhabitants, and the refusal is specified by أَنْ يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا (an yuḍayyifūhumā), “to offer the two hospitality.”

What they refused was hospitality to the two travelers.

Abaw alone does not establish that they were doctrinally hostile to Moses or acted out of religious arrogance.

The Qur’an does not state their precise motive in this verse.

The context highlights their response to a request for food; after the wall is repaired, Moses suggests taking payment.

Their refusal of hospitality is therefore important to understanding Moses’s objection.

The root itself nevertheless cannot establish a moral or legal ruling covering every form of hospitality.

Ibāʾ here simply expresses rejection of a particular request.

Reassuring mouths and unaccepting hearts

Al-Tawbah, 9:8

كَيْفَ وَإِنْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ لَا يَرْقُبُوا فِيكُمْ إِلًّا وَلَا ذِمَّةً ۚ يُرْضُونَكُمْ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَتَأْبَىٰ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ فَاسِقُونَ

kayfa wa-in yaẓharū ʿalaykum lā yarqubū fīkum illan wa-lā dhimmah; yurḍūnakum bi-afwāhihim wa-taʾbā qulūbuhum wa-aktharuhum fāsiqūn

“How could their covenant be trusted, when, if they prevailed over you, they would observe neither bond nor covenant concerning you? They please you with their mouths, while their hearts refuse; and most of them are defiantly disobedient.”

The hearts of the group under discussion are the subject of taʾbā here.

The Qur’an distinguishes the words from their mouths from their inward stance.

Outwardly they speak to please their audience, but genuine inward acceptance and commitment are absent.

This shows that ibāʾ need not involve an overt declaration of opposition.

Someone may agree outwardly without accepting something inwardly.

Ibāʾ cannot therefore be confined to saying “no.”

Nor does ibāʾ itself mean hypocrisy. The discrepancy between outward and inward behavior comes from the contrast between mouths and hearts and the covenant context.

This verse concerns a group with specified characteristics in a relationship involving covenants and hostility. It should not be generalized without grounds to everyone with a similar religious or social label.

5. Rejecting truth, ingratitude, and declining the trust

“Most people refused anything except disbelief”

Al-Isrāʾ, 17:89

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَا لِلنَّاسِ فِي هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ مِنْ كُلِّ مَثَلٍ فَأَبَىٰ أَكْثَرُ النَّاسِ إِلَّا كُفُورًا

wa-la-qad ṣarrafnā li-al-nāsi fī hādhā al-qurʾāni min kulli mathalin fa-abā aktharu al-nāsi illā kufūrā

“We have certainly presented every kind of example to people in this Qur’an in varied ways, but most people refused anything except disbelief and denial.”

Here the verb of refusal is followed by إِلَّا كُفُورًا (illā kufūran).

The construction does more than express refusal: it confines their position to disbelief and denial.

In natural wording, they persist in denial in response to the Qur’an’s varied examples and explanations.

Kufūran, however, belongs to K F R, not A B Y.

Ibāʾ should therefore not itself be defined as disbelief.

Disbelief and denial are the stance or outcome connected with refusal in this context.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:99 likewise describes the wrongdoers’ refusal and persistence in disbelief after speaking of The God’s power to recreate human beings and His setting an appointed term.

There, too, ibāʾ concerns the audience’s judgment and response to a truth presented to them.

These verses do not make everyone who declines to accept a statement a disbeliever or wrongdoer. Their subject is refusal of what the Qur’an presents with evidence and explanation in those contexts.

Ibāʾ and ingratitude in the rain context

Al-Furqān, 25:50

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَاهُ بَيْنَهُمْ لِيَذَّكَّرُوا فَأَبَىٰ أَكْثَرُ النَّاسِ إِلَّا كُفُورًا

wa-la-qad ṣarrafnāhu baynahum li-yadhdhakkarū fa-abā aktharu al-nāsi illā kufūrā

“We have certainly distributed it among them in varied ways so that they might take heed, but most people refused anything except ingratitude and denial.”

Its construction resembles Al-Isrāʾ 17:89, but the context differs.

Al-Isrāʾ discusses varied examples presented in the Qur’an.

In Al-Furqān, the preceding verses discuss sending winds, bringing down pure water from the sky, reviving the land, and providing drink for people and livestock.

The stronger reading therefore takes the pronoun in صَرَّفْنَاهُ (ṣarrafnāhu) as referring to water or rain and its distribution or circulation among people.

Early explanations also report the possibility that the pronoun refers to the Qur’an, but the close connection with the water and rain verses favors the first reading.

This distinction matters for understanding kufūran.

In a context of rain and life’s blessings, kufr can also concern ingratitude for a blessing.

The shared wording فَأَبَىٰ أَكْثَرُ النَّاسِ إِلَّا كُفُورًا (fa-abā aktharu al-nāsi illā kufūran) should therefore not be explained identically in both surahs without regard to their subjects.

Abā itself nevertheless retains the sense of refusal in both.

The difference comes from the subject, the context of kufūr, and the preceding sentences.

“The God refuses anything except completing His light”

Al-Tawbah, 9:32

يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَيَأْبَى اللَّهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يُتِمَّ نُورَهُ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ الْكَافِرُونَ

yurīdūna an yuṭfiʾū nūra allāhi bi-afwāhihim wa-yaʾbā allāhu illā an yutimma nūrahu wa-law kariha al-kāfirūn

“They wish to extinguish the light of The God with their mouths, but The God refuses anything except completing His light, even if the disbelievers dislike it.”

This is one of the most important witnesses to the root’s semantic value.

Here the subject of yaʾbā is The God Himself.

Ibāʾ therefore cannot inherently mean arrogance, sin, disobedience, or denial of truth.

The opponents’ wish to extinguish the light of The God stands against what He determines concerning its completion.

The Arabic construction يَأْبَى اللَّهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يُتِمَّ نُورَهُ (yaʾbā allāhu illā an yutimma nūrahu) excludes other possibilities and affirms what follows illā, “except.”

“The God refuses anything except completing His light” preserves this construction in English.

A smoother rendering is also possible: “But The God will complete His light.”

The second conveys the sentence’s outcome but preserves less of its original image of refusal.

Attributing ibāʾ to The God here does not mean refusal arising from fear, human anger, or an inability to choose.

The verb appears in the context of divine will set against the opponents’ efforts.

The basic sense of refusal remains, but human psychological characteristics should not automatically be transferred to The God.

“They declined to bear it”: the heavens, earth, and mountains

Al-Aḥzāb, 33:72

إِنَّا عَرَضْنَا الْأَمَانَةَ عَلَى السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالْجِبَالِ فَأَبَيْنَ أَنْ يَحْمِلْنَهَا وَأَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا وَحَمَلَهَا الْإِنْسَانُ ۖ إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا

innā ʿaraḍnā al-amānata ʿalā al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-al-jibāli fa-abayna an yaḥmilnahā wa-ashfaqna minhā wa-ḥamalahā al-insān; innahu kāna ẓalūman jahūlā

“We offered the trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, but they declined to bear it and were fearful of it, while the human being undertook it. Indeed, he has been greatly unjust and ignorant.”

Fa-abayna is a feminine plural past verb referring to the heavens, earth, and mountains.

Their refusal is followed by أَنْ يَحْمِلْنَهَا (an yaḥmilnahā): declining to bear the trust.

The verse immediately adds وَأَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا (wa-ashfaqna minhā), “and they were fearful of it.”

This matters because their refusal is accompanied by fear and apprehension concerning the trust, rather than arrogance.

In Al-Baqarah 2:34, Iblīs refuses and acts arrogantly.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, the heavens, earth, and mountains decline the trust and are fearful of it.

Not every Qur’anic ibāʾ can therefore be called an arrogant refusal.

Early explanations differ over how the trust was offered and whether attributing refusal to the heavens, earth, and mountains is direct or figurative language.

A B Y itself does not resolve that disagreement.

Its lexical meaning is declining. The nature of the trust, how it was offered, and its relation to human responsibility require a separate study of the verse.

Nor does the verse establish that the heavens, earth, and mountains committed sin or disbelief like Iblīs. It states no such judgment.

6. Ibāʾ and neighboring roots

A B Y and M N ʿ: refusing and preventing

Study of M N ʿ

Imtināʿ and ibāʾ are very close in Arabic.

Manʿ, however, can also concern preventing someone else.

Someone may prevent another person from accessing something, thereby obstructing that person.

Abā, by contrast, generally concerns the subject’s own refusal to accept or do something.

This distinction is not absolute, but it helps in translation.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, the scribe must not refuse to write. The verse is not directly instructing him not to prevent others from writing.

A B Y and K R H: refusing and disliking

Study of K R H

كَرِهَ (kariha) concerns disliking or being unwilling to have something.

Someone may dislike something yet still do it.

Ibāʾ more directly concerns nonacceptance or refusal.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 uses both families: The God accepts nothing other than completing His light, even if the disbelievers dislike it.

Their occurrence together illustrates that disliking and refusing are related but distinct concepts.

A B Y and ʿ Ṣ Y: refusing and disobeying

Study of ʿ Ṣ Y

ʿIṣyān concerns opposing or disobeying a command.

Ibāʾ can also describe refusing a command, as in Iblīs’s behavior.

Not every ibāʾ, however, is disobedience.

Someone may decline a proposal they were under no obligation to accept.

Nor does Al-Aḥzāb 33:72 describe the heavens, earth, and mountains declining the trust as arrogant disobedience.

Judging whether an act constitutes disobedience requires establishing whether there was a binding command and how the subject stood in relation to it.

A B Y and K B R: refusing and arrogance

Study of K B R

Istikbār concerns self-exaltation and seeing oneself as greater than one’s true standing.

In Al-Baqarah 2:34, Iblīs both refuses and acts arrogantly.

Their association does not make the verbs synonymous.

Refusing oppression can be right, while refusing a rightful command out of self-exaltation can be blameworthy.

Ibāʾ may describe either, but istikbār applies where self-exaltation is actually present.

A B Y and K F R: refusal and disbelief

Study of K F R

Several verses associate ibāʾ with kufr or kufūr.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:89, Al-Isrāʾ 17:99, and Al-Furqān 25:50 use the refusal construction with illā kufūran.

Refusing something does not in itself constitute disbelief.

Al-Furqān 25:50 concerns rain and divine blessing, while Al-Isrāʾ 17:89 concerns examples presented in the Qur’an.

Kufr and kufūr should therefore be examined in their particular contexts.

The attribution of ibāʾ to The God in Al-Tawbah 9:32 is also especially clear evidence against making disbelief an invariable element of this root’s meaning.

A B Y and J Ḥ D: refusal and knowing denial

Study of J Ḥ D

Jaḥd can concern denying something a person recognizes or knows about.

Ibāʾ expresses refusal without itself determining how much the subject knows.

In Ṭā Hā 20:56, Pharaoh calls the signs false and refuses after encountering them. His knowledge, the nature of his denial, and his responsibility emerge from the relevant account as a whole.

“He knowingly denied the truth” should therefore not be inserted into every translation of abā.

A B Y and R F Ḍ: refusing and rejecting

Rafḍ concerns setting aside and rejecting something, overlapping with the ordinary English sense of rejection or nonacceptance.

This field overlaps with ibāʾ.

In Qur’anic uses of abā, however, the subject’s refusal to accept something presented to them is prominent.

“Rejected” suits some verses, while “refused” or “declined” conveys the Qur’anic verb more clearly in many others.

A B Y and A B W: refusal and fatherhood

Study of A B W

A B Y is not the same root as A B W.

A B W concerns fathers, forefathers, and parents in the Qur’an.

A B Y concerns refusal and nonacceptance.

Similar spellings, such as أَبِي (abī) in expressions concerning a father and the verb أَبَىٰ (abā), do not establish a shared root.

It must also be distinguished from A B B, concerning pasture vegetation; A B Q, fleeing; and A B L, concerning camels.

7. Translation choices for Forqan

The most suitable basic equivalents are “refuse” and “decline to accept.”

“Refuse to comply” is also natural in many contexts, especially where someone declines a command or responsibility.

The translation must fit the subject and the object of refusal.

In Al-Baqarah 2:34, “Iblīs refused and acted arrogantly” preserves the distinction between ibāʾ and istikbār.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:31, “refused to be among those prostrating” conveys the full construction.

In Ṭā Hā 20:56, “called them false and refused” keeps calling false distinct from refusing.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, “Let the scribe not refuse to write” and “Let the witnesses not refuse when summoned” are natural. The legal conditions of both instructions require interpretation of the whole verse.

In Al-Tawbah 9:8, “They please you with their mouths, while their hearts refuse” preserves the contrast between outward and inward behavior.

In Al-Tawbah 9:32, “But The God refuses anything except completing His light” stays close to the construction of refusal and exception.

In Al-Kahf 18:77, “The villagers refused to offer the two hospitality” is suitable.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:89 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:99, refusal is associated with disbelief and denial. Translation should preserve the relationship without turning abā itself into “disbelieved.”

In Al-Furqān 25:50, the rain and water context supports “They refused anything except ingratitude and denial.”

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, “They declined to accept and bear it” is appropriate. Adding “out of arrogance” would be wrong, since the verse itself speaks of fear and apprehension.

Brief meanings for word-by-word boxes

أَبَىٰ (abā): he refused, declined, or withheld acceptance.

يَأْبَىٰ (yaʾbā): he refuses or will not accept.

لَا يَأْبَ (lā yaʾba): let him not refuse or decline.

أَبَوْا (abaw): they refused or declined.

أَبَيْنَ (abayna): they declined; in Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, concerning the heavens, earth, and mountains.

None of these forms should have arrogance, disbelief, fear, disobedience, or self-respect written into it as an invariable and necessary root meaning.

Such features may accompany ibāʾ in particular verses, but do not follow from the verb alone.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root A B Y concerns not accepting, declining, and refusing something.

In human use, refusal may arise from firm resolve. Accordingly, abiyy can describe someone who will not submit to humiliation or oppression.

The same family can describe someone who refuses to accept truth or perform a clear responsibility.

In animal contexts, the family also concerns refusing food or water, and includes a term for she-camels that have not yet conceived. These uses carry no specified moral value.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs 13 times in 12 verses, all in verbal forms.

Iblīs refuses to prostrate, and his refusal accompanies arrogance. Pharaoh calls the signs false and refuses. The scribe and witnesses are instructed not to refuse their responsibilities. A village’s people refuse hospitality, while another group offers reassuring words outwardly without accepting inwardly.

Several verses associate ibāʾ with disbelief and ingratitude. Kufr nevertheless belongs to another root and should not erase the independent meaning of ibāʾ.

In Al-Tawbah 9:32, The God Himself is the subject of ibāʾ. This clearly shows that refusal is not inherently blameworthy in its lexical meaning.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, the heavens, earth, and mountains decline the trust and are fearful of it. Not every ibāʾ can therefore be regarded as arrogant defiance or disobedience.

For Forqan, the basic definition is: Ibāʾ is nonacceptance or refusal of something presented to the subject or requested of them. The refusal may be firm and serious, but the root itself does not determine its moral value or motive. Those depend on what is refused, the subject’s situation, and the sentence’s context.

This definition accommodates every Qur’anic use without equating ibāʾ with arrogance, disbelief, disobedience, fear, or praiseworthy resolve.

In reading the Qur’an, the question is not only “Who refused?” We must also ask: What did they refuse, why did they refuse, and in what context does the Qur’an present that refusal?

These questions distinguish Iblīs’s refusal, Pharaoh’s rejection, the villagers’ refusal, the heavens and earth declining the trust, and the divine will to complete His light.
