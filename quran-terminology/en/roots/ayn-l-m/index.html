Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root ʿ L M

Knowing, recognition, the disclosure of truth, teaching, signs, and worlds known through distinction and recognition

Forms Covered from This Root

In this page, the root ع ل م is studied semantically, then the most important Qur’anic forms of this root are explained separately:

  • عَلِمَ / يَعْلَمُ / اعْلَمُ — simple verb; he knew, he knows, know
  • عُلِمَ / يُعْلَمُ — passive verb; it was known, it is known
  • عَلَّمَ / يُعَلِّمُ / عَلِّمْ — Form II; he taught, he teaches, teach
  • عُلِّمَ / يُعَلَّمُ — passive Form II; it was taught, it is taught
  • تَعَلَّمَ / يَتَعَلَّمُ — Form V; learning, acquiring knowledge
  • عِلْم — noun / verbal noun; knowledge, recognition, awareness
  • عَلِيم — adjective; knowing, aware, possessing knowledge
  • عُلَمَاء — plural; the knowledgeable, those who possess knowledge
  • عَلَّام — intensive form; All-Knowing, greatly knowing, intensely aware
  • أَعْلَم — comparative/superlative; more knowing, most knowing, more aware
  • مَعْلُوم / مَعْلُومَات — passive participle; known, made known, known things
  • مُعَلَّم — passive participle of Form II; taught, trained, instructed
  • عَالَم / عَالَمِين — noun; world, worlds, realms, or created categories known through distinction
  • عَلَامَات — signs
  • أَعْلَام — high signs, visible markers, or things that show the way

Note: Some Arabic forms from this root, such as أَعْلَمَ / يُعْلِمُ, اسْتَعْلَمَ, عَالِم in the sense of “knower / scholar,” the singular عَلَامَة, and مَعَالِم, are known in Arabic. But they are not listed as direct Qur’anic forms of this root. This page focuses mainly on the forms that occur in the Qur’an, or that are needed to understand the Qur’anic usage of this root.

General Meaning of the Root ʿ L M

The root ع ل م in the Qur’an points to the semantic field of knowing, recognition, awareness, becoming clear, teaching, sign, and distinction. This root is not limited to storing information in the mind. In the Qur’an, knowledge is usually connected with recognizing truth, seeing signs, understanding responsibility, and separating oneself from ignorance and assumption.

Knowledge means that something becomes clear to the human being; something comes out of concealment; or there is a sign that leads the human being to recognition. For this reason, a semantic connection can be seen between ʿilm and ʿalāmah. A sign shows the way, and knowledge makes truth clearer for the intellect and the heart.

In the Qur’an, knowledge is always a great blessing, but it also carries danger. If knowledge brings the human being nearer to The God, truth, justice, reverent awareness, humility, and righteous action, then knowledge is light and guidance. But if knowledge becomes a tool of arrogance, domination, argument, distortion, or concealing truth, that same knowledge can become a witness against the human being.

So Qur’anic knowledge is not merely knowing words, reports, information, or technical terms. Knowledge is a recognition that makes the human being more responsible before truth. The more a person knows, the more complete the proof becomes upon him.

ʿIlm: Beyond Information

In our time, many people treat knowledge and information as the same thing. A person who has more data, more books, or more terminology is often considered more knowledgeable. But the Qur’an has a deeper view.

Knowledge in the Qur’an must be near to the recognition of truth. If a person knows something but takes no lesson from it, or recognizes the truth but conceals it, or sees the signs but passes over them, that knowing has not become guidance.

From the Qur’anic view, knowledge must have an ethical effect. It should distance the human being from arrogance, injustice, blind following, imitation of ancestors, group bias, and baseless obedience to powers. Knowledge that does not make the human being more humble, more just, more truthful, and more awake has not yet fully borne fruit in the Qur’anic sense.

For this reason, the Qur’an repeatedly calls the human being to think, to know, to see signs, to hear truth, and to come out of ignorance. Knowledge is a path out of darkness; but only when the human being places it in the service of truth.

Knowledge Against Assumption, Ignorance, and Baseless Following

One of the important roles of the root ع ل م in the Qur’an is to distinguish knowledge from assumption, ignorance, and baseless following.

Ẓann means assumption; something that may be true or may not be. Jahl means not knowing, or refusing to accept a truth that should be recognized. Baseless following means that the human being follows fathers, elders, tribe, majority, tradition, or people of power without knowledge.

The Qur’an repeatedly warns that the human being must not follow something without knowledge. This is a very important principle, because religion, ethics, judgment, and human life should not be built on guesswork, rumor, imitation, or bias.

Knowledge, in this sense, makes the human being responsible. If he does not know, he must not claim knowledge. If he knows, he must not conceal it. If a sign becomes clear, he must not pass over it with arrogance.

So Qur’anic knowledge takes the human being out of ignorance, restrains his tongue, reforms his judgment, and separates his path of servanthood from blind imitation.

ʿAlima / Yaʿlamu: Knowing and Being Aware

The forms عَلِمَ and يَعْلَمُ are among the foundations of this root. These forms point to knowing, being aware, understanding, and recognizing.

In the Qur’an, this knowing is sometimes attributed to the human being and sometimes to The God. But there is a foundational difference between the knowledge of The God and the knowledge of the human being. Human knowledge is limited, gradual, dependent, and liable to error. A human being learns something today, and tomorrow he may forget it or understand it more accurately. But the knowledge of The God is complete, all-encompassing, without need, and surrounding the visible and the hidden.

When the Qur’an says that The God knows, it means nothing is hidden from Him: not the outward form of actions, not intentions, not what is in the breasts, not the future, not the past, and not what the human being has even hidden from himself.

But when the human being knows, that knowing must make him more humble. Because whatever the human being knows, his knowledge is still very little before the fullness of truth. Human knowledge should bring him to sincere questioning, right action, and caution in making claims.

ʿUlima / Yuʿlamu: Being Known and Becoming Clear

The passive forms عُلِمَ and يُعْلَمُ point to something being known or becoming clear. In this state, attention is on the clarification of the truth itself, not only on the subject who knows.

This usage is important because in the Qur’an some things remain hidden until The God makes them clear, or the signs reveal them, or reality becomes clear through experience, trial, and time.

The becoming-known of a truth can be mercy for the human being or proof against him. It is mercy if, after the truth becomes clear, the human being reforms his path. It is proof if the human being recognizes the truth and opposes it.

So knowledge is not only inside the mind. Sometimes external reality also becomes clear in a way that removes excuse from the human being. When something becomes known, the human being’s responsibility toward it becomes greater.

ʿAllama / Yuʿallimu: Teaching and Conveying Knowledge

Form II of this root, such as عَلَّمَ and يُعَلِّمُ, points to teaching and giving instruction. Teaching means knowledge is carried from one place to another, but in the Qur’anic view teaching is not merely the transfer of words.

True teaching means that the human being understands something, takes direction through it, and gains the ability to distinguish. If someone merely memorizes words but does not understand their meaning, responsibility, and guidance, teaching has not yet become complete.

In the Qur’an, The God teaches. The prophets also teach. The Book, wisdom, signs, and the signs of The God are all fields of teaching. This shows that education in the Qur’an is not only the work of school or formal study; the whole existence can be a place of teaching if the human being opens his eyes and heart.

But teaching is also a trust. Whoever teaches must not turn knowledge into a tool of domination, religious trade, humiliating others, or constructing a sacred class. Lord-oriented teaching should bring the human being nearer to The God, truth, justice, and reform.

Taʿallama / Yataʿallamu: Learning and Acquiring

The form يَتَعَلَّمُ points to learning and acquiring knowledge. This usage shows that the human being is not merely a passive receiver; he moves along the path of learning, chooses, and learns something.

But the Qur’an shows that not every learning is good. A human being can learn something that harms him and brings no benefit. Therefore the criterion of learning is not simply “knowing more”; what matters is what a person learns, from what source, with what intention, and for what purpose.

Knowledge that brings the human being nearer to reform, justice, mercy, and tawḥīd is a blessing. But knowledge that pulls the human being toward corruption, deception, separation, domination, or arrogance can become fitnah.

So learning in the Qur’an carries responsibility. The human being must ask: does this knowledge I am learning bring me nearer to truth, or does it take me farther from it?

ʿIlm: Knowledge, Recognition, and the Measure of Responsibility

عِلْم is one of the most important forms of this root. Knowledge in the Qur’an is a blessing, a trust, and a measure of responsibility.

When the human being has no knowledge, he must not make a certain claim. When he has knowledge, he must not conceal it or act against it. Knowledge makes the human tongue heavier, not freer to make every claim.

The Qur’an places knowledge against assumption, desire, blind imitation, and baseless claims. Many deviations begin where the human being attributes something to The God without knowledge, turns a ruling into religion without proof, or follows something for which he has no knowledge.

So knowledge in the Qur’an is not only knowing; it is also a measure of truthfulness. The one who does not know and says “I do not know” is closer to knowledge than the one who does not know but speaks with certainty.

ʿAlīm, ʿAllām, and Aʿlam: Degrees of Awareness and Divine Knowledge

عَلِيم is one of the important divine descriptions in the Qur’an. This word shows that The God is knowing; but the knowledge of The God is not like human knowledge. The God does not learn through experience, instruction, guesswork, or time. His knowledge is complete, all-encompassing, and surrounding.

عَلَّام is an intensive form and appears in expressions connected to knowing the unseen and hidden things. This word emphasizes the intensity and all-encompassing nature of knowledge. No hidden thing lies outside the knowledge of The God.

أَعْلَم also shows that The God knows better: better concerning intentions, paths, worthiness, misguidance, and what people claim about themselves or others.

This part is very important for the human being. When The God is ʿAlīm and Aʿlam, the human being must not sit in the station of absolute judgment. We see outward appearances, but The God knows inner realities. We see a part of time, but The God knows the whole path.

So faith in the knowledge of The God brings both tranquility and humility. Tranquility, because truth is not lost with The God. Humility, because the human being understands that his knowledge is limited.

ʿUlamāʾ: Those Whose Knowledge Is Affected by Signs

عُلَمَاء is a plural that points to those who know. But in the Qur’anic view, knowledge is not recognized merely by title, clothing, social position, or memorized information.

The true knowledgeable person is one who sees signs and is affected by them. If the human being sees the signs of The God in creation, nature, history, the self, the Book, and life, but his heart does not soften, his knowledge has not yet reached reverent awareness and humility.

Qur’anic knowledge makes the human being humble, not arrogant. Whoever truly knows understands that the world is greater than his understanding, The God knows more than he does, and truth is not owned by a particular group or class.

So ʿulamāʾ in the Qur’an are not merely “owners of religious information.” True knowledge must bring the human being nearer to recognizing The God, aware fear, justice, and responsibility.

Maʿlūm and Muʿallam: What Is Known or Taught

مَعْلُوم means something that has been known, recognized, or made clear. مَعْلُومَات also points to known things, or to known times and recognized signs.

When something is known, it has come out of ambiguity. This can relate to worship, time, ruling, or other realities. Being known means the human being is not facing something completely unknown and obscure; there is a boundary, sign, or recognition.

مُعَلَّم means taught or trained. This word shows that a being or a human being can, through teaching, gain an ability or knowledge it did not possess before.

In all of these uses, one principle is clear: knowledge and teaching bring the world out of pure disorder. Things receive names, boundaries, signs, meanings, and positions.

ʿAlāmāt and Aʿlām: Signs That Illuminate the Way

عَلَامَات and أَعْلَام point to signs and visible markers. This part of the root ع ل م shows that knowledge is not only in the mind; the world too is full of signs.

A sign is something that leads the human being to recognition. It shows a way, a direction, a danger, a place, a destination, or a truth. If a sign is seen correctly, it brings the human being out of confusion.

In the Qur’an, signs are not only for outward knowing. Signs call the human being to reflection, gratitude, guidance, and recognition of the Lord. Whoever sees a sign but passes over it is like someone who has a lamp on the road but does not use its light.

So ʿalāmāt and aʿlām connect the root of knowledge to the external world: The God does not speak only in the Book; He has also placed signs in the horizons, the selves, history, nature, and the order of the world.

ʿĀlam and ʿĀlamīn: Worlds, Created Categories, and Fields of Recognition

عَالَم and عَالَمِين are among the most important forms of this root for understanding Surah Al-Fatihah. This word is usually translated as “world” or “worlds,” but its meaning is wider than one simple physical world.

ʿĀlam can point to a field of beings, a category of creatures, a known order, or a realm among the realms of existence. ʿĀlamīn is plural and carries the human gaze beyond one group, tribe, species, or limited domain.

The connection of this word with the root ع ل م is important. A world is something that is known in some way, has signs, has order, and can lead the human being to recognition. The world is not blind and meaningless; the world is sign-bearing.

In the expression رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ, ʿālamīn is not merely a literary plural. It shows that the rubūbiyyah of The God extends over all fields of existence, all categories of created beings, all levels of being, and all known and unknown worlds.

So ʿālamīn should not be limited to “people” or “human beings.” The human being is part of the ʿālamīn, not all of it. The God is the Rabb of all worlds: the world of human beings, the world of angels, the world of living creatures, the world of nature, the unseen world, and every world we know or do not know.

Rabb al-ʿĀlamīn in Surah Al-Fatihah

In Surah Al-Fatihah, after الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, the expression رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ appears:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

This composition places two important roots beside one another: ر ب ب and ع ل م. The God is worthy of ḥamd because He is Rabb; and His rubūbiyyah is not limited to one tribe, one community, one earth, one heaven, or one known world. He is Rabb al-ʿālamīn.

This expression takes the human being out of narrow vision. If The God is Rabb al-ʿālamīn, no tribe, madhhab, race, language, or group can claim The God as its exclusive possession. All worlds are under His rubūbiyyah.

Rabb al-ʿālamīn also shows that the worlds are not Lordless. Every world has its order, measure, guidance, and path in relation to the Rabb. The human being is also among the ʿālamīn and must seek his way from the Lord of all worlds.

So Al-Fatihah, from its beginning, makes the human gaze universal and beyond the self. Before the human being says إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ and اهْدِنَا, he learns that he is speaking with the Lord of all worlds, not with the god of one tribe or one group.

Divine Knowledge and Human Knowledge

In the Qur’an, divine knowledge and human knowledge are both mentioned, but they are never on the same level.

The knowledge of The God is complete, all-encompassing, without need, and surrounding everything. The God knows the visible and the hidden, past and future, outward and inward, intention and action, what is spoken and what is unspoken.

Human knowledge is limited, gradual, dependent, and liable to error. The human being must learn, ask, experience, think, and sometimes correct his own mistake. This limitation should keep the human being humble.

The problem begins when the human being makes his limited knowledge absolute. When a person calls his assumption knowledge, or presents his own understanding as the final judgment of The God, or uses incomplete knowledge as a standard for humiliating others, he has left the proper discipline of knowledge.

Human knowledge is sound when it stands beneath the knowledge of The God. That means the human being knows, but does not claim complete surrounding; understands, but remains humble; teaches, but does not claim ownership of truth.

Knowledge, Humility, and the Danger of Arrogance in Knowing

Knowledge can save the human being, but it can also make him arrogant. This is one of the great dangers of knowing.

A person who knows a little may imagine that he has understood everything. A person who learns religious or scientific terminology may see himself above others. A person who reads books may move closer to argument and self-display instead of moving closer to truth.

The Qur’an calls the human being to knowledge joined with humility. Real knowledge must restrain the human being from baseless claims, rushed judgment, humiliating others, and attributing speech to The God without knowledge.

On the other hand, ignorance must not be sanctified. Intellectual humility does not mean hostility toward knowledge. The human being must learn, ask, investigate, and see the signs; but he must know that knowledge is a trust, not an instrument of arrogance.

So the right path is this: seeking knowledge, joined with reverent awareness, truthfulness, caution, and humility before Al-ʿAlīm.

Summary

The root ع ل م is one of the foundational roots of the Qur’an. This root covers a wide field of knowing, recognition, teaching, learning, sign, becoming known, and known worlds.

Knowledge in the Qur’an is not merely information. Knowledge should bring the human being nearer to truth, responsibility, humility, justice, and recognition of The God. Knowledge that is not connected to truth can become arrogance, distortion, domination, or fitnah.

عَلِمَ and يَعْلَمُ speak of knowing. عَلَّمَ and يُعَلِّمُ speak of teaching. تَعَلَّمَ and يَتَعَلَّمُ speak of learning. عِلْم speaks of knowledge and recognition itself. عَلِيم, عَلَّام, and أَعْلَم speak of the all-encompassing and higher knowledge of The God. عُلَمَاء speaks of those who should, through seeing signs, arrive at reverent awareness and responsibility. عَلَامَات and أَعْلَام speak of signs that illuminate the way. And عَالَمِين speaks of worlds and created fields that stand under the rubūbiyyah of The God.

In Al-Fatihah, the expression رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ shows that ḥamd belongs to The God because He is the Lord of all worlds; not only the Rabb of one tribe, one community, one language, or one limited world.

So if the root ع ل م is understood correctly, it reforms the human being’s view of knowledge, his view of the world, his view of The God, and his discipline before truth. The human being learns to know without becoming arrogant; to see signs without passing over them; and to recognize the worlds without forgetting Rabb al-ʿālamīn:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ
