Ashes, gray coloration, eye inflammation, and destruction

Brief root meaning: Ashes and fine residues of burning; in other branches, gray coloration and darkness, inflammation and pain of the eye, destruction and deterioration, and, in a separate usage, very rapid movement. This family has several semantic branches that should not all be derived from a single image.

1. Introducing the root and its Quranic form

The three-consonant Arabic root R M D produces a family of words associated with ashes, gray coloration, eye inflammation, destruction, and certain other conditions.

Its best-known word is ramād, ashes: the material remaining after wood and other substances burn. It commonly consists of fine particles that wind may scatter.

Arabic ramad also denotes inflammation, pain, or injury of the eye. Armad can describe someone suffering from this condition; in the color branch, the same form describes something grayish and dark.

In another usage, ramd concerns destruction and perishing. Lexical records also attest irmidād for very rapid movement or running.

These senses differ. Ashes, an eye disorder, and rapid movement are distinct phenomena; shared consonants alone cannot establish a single historical origin for them.

The root occurs just once in the Quran: in Ibrāhīm, where the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord are compared to ashes scattered by a violent wind.

The form examined and the Quranic derivative

كَرَمَادٍ — Ibrāhīm 14:18

This form consists of the comparative particle ka- and the noun ramād.

Here, ramādin is an indefinite noun in the genitive case.

It means “like ashes.”

Other forms of this family are also known in Arabic:

  • رَمَاد — ramād: Ashes, fine residues of burning.
  • رَمَادَة — ramādah: A portion of ashes; in another usage, destruction and famine.
  • أَرْمِدَة، أَرْمِدَاء — armidah, armidāʾ: Attested forms associated with ashes; accounts differ over the number and classification of the latter.
  • رِمْدِد — rimdid: Very fine ashes, thoroughly reduced by burning or scattering.
  • رَمَّدَ — rammada: Placed something in ashes or covered it with ashes.
  • مُرَمَّد — murammad: Something placed in ashes, including food cooked among ashes or embers.
  • رَمَد — ramad: Eye inflammation, pain, or injury.
  • رَمِدَ يَرْمَدُ — ramida yarmadu: The eye developed ramad.
  • أَرْمَد، رَمِد — armad, ramid: Someone affected by ramad; armad also describes gray coloration in another branch.
  • رَمْد — ramd: Destruction or perishing.
  • رَمَدَ القوم — ramada al-qawm: The people perished.
  • ارْمِدَاد — irmidād: Rapid movement or running.

These forms are recorded in lexicons, but only ka-ramādin occurs in the Quran in the forms listed here.

The Quranic study therefore centers on ramād and the image of scattered ashes. The other branches help establish the family's historical and linguistic range.

2. Semantic branches and early Arabic usage

Three branches in an early lexical analysis

One early lexical analysis identifies three separate semantic foundations for R M D:

First, disease or injury of the eye.

Second, coloration and the appearance associated with ashes.

Third, a kind of rapid movement or running.

This division matters: even early lexical analysis did not reduce every usage to one simple core.

Darkness or inflammation of the eye might be associated with coloration or dust, but this possibility alone does not prove that the eye condition was originally named after ashes.

Nor does an ostrich's rapid movement have a sufficiently clear connection with ashes or eye inflammation to establish a common origin without evidence.

The sounder method is to identify each branch separately and accept only relationships supported by linguistic evidence.

Ramād: ashes remaining after burning

An early lexical definition explains ramād as follows:

دُقَاقُ الْفَحْمِ مِنْ حُرَاقَةِ النَّارِ وَمَا هَبَا مِنَ الْجَمْرِ فَطَارَ دُقَاقًا

duqāqu al-faḥmi min ḥurāqati al-nāri wa-mā habā mina al-jamri fa-ṭāra duqāqan

Translation: “Fine particles of charcoal produced by burning, and what rises from embers and flies away in fine particles.”

This definition connects ashes with fire and burnt material.

Ashes are not fire itself. Fire brings about combustion; ashes are among its residues.

Ashes also differ from large pieces of unburnt or partly burnt charcoal, although charcoal and ashes may occur together in a hearth.

Ramādah can denote a portion of ashes, while armidah is an attested plural. Accounts differ over armidāʾ: it has been treated as a collective form and as a singular alternative to ramād.

In the Quran, ramād functions as a material noun. It need not identify one piece of ash; it names a substance consisting of many particles.

Rimdid: very fine ashes

Lexical sources use rimdid for very fine, thoroughly reduced ashes.

Their explanations describe ashes reduced to very small particles through burning and scattering.

This feature helps explain the Quranic image.

The finer and lighter the particles, the more readily wind may scatter them.

Nevertheless, rimdid should not replace ramād in the verse: the Quran uses the general word for ashes.

Nor does Ibrāhīm 14:18 specify particle size numerically or scientifically.

The image centers on a violent wind and the scattering of ashes.

Rammada: placing something in ashes

Arabic rammada al-shawāʾ concerns placing food in ashes or among embers.

Murammad can describe meat cooked in this way.

This usage shows that everyday life did not treat ashes solely as useless fire residue.

Older cooking methods also used the heat of embers and the surrounding ashes.

Arabic records a proverb, شَوَى أَخُوكَ حَتَّى إِذَا أَنْضَجَ رَمَّدَ: someone cooked food but, once it was ready, put it in ashes or spoiled it.

Figuratively, the proverb describes someone who spoils a good deed through subsequent misconduct, such as reminding its recipient of the favor.

The verb rammada does not itself mean to remind someone of a favor; that sense comes from the proverb's figurative use and setting.

Gray coloration and darkness

Arabic armad can describe something grayish, dark, or dust-colored.

Lexical descriptions use غُبْرَةٌ فِيهَا كُدْرَةٌ, a dusty gray coloration containing darkness or murkiness.

This family also describes the coloration of certain animals.

For example, naʿāmatun ramdāʾ denotes an ostrich characterized by a dark gray coloration.

Ẓalīmun armad likewise describes a male ostrich.

Related forms also describe certain fabrics and waters with a grayish or dark appearance.

These usages concern color and appearance, rather than actual burning.

Something can be gray without having burned in a fire.

Not every use of armad therefore implies actual ashes or a fire.

Ramad: inflammation or injury of the eye

Another branch concerns an eye disorder.

Arabic records the expression:

رَمِدَتِ الْعَيْنُ تَرْمَدُ رَمَدًا

ramidati al-ʿaynu tarmadu ramadan

Meaning: “The eye developed ramad.”

In early usage, ramad denotes pain, inflammation, swelling, or injury of the eye.

Armad can also describe someone whose eye has this condition.

This usage should be distinguished from gray coloration.

The same Arabic form can refer to color in one branch and a condition of illness in another.

The term ramad alone does not identify a particular modern diagnosis, microbial agent, allergy, or medical cause.

In early language it was a general designation for a painful, inflamed eye.

The eye sense is an attested lexical usage, but it should not be transferred to Quranic ramād.

Ramd: destruction and perishing

Arabic also records ramd, with a vowelless middle consonant, for destruction or perishing.

Ramada al-qawmu is attested of people who perished.

The same verb was used of sheep dying from cold or frost.

One can imagine a connection between destruction and ashes, since fire may consume something until only ashes remain.

That explanation alone, however, does not establish that every destruction sense derives from the noun for ashes.

The early lexical analysis likewise distinguishes the disease, color, and rapid-movement branches.

Ramādah is also recorded for destruction and hardship.

The historical name ʿĀm al-Ramādah refers to a year of famine and hardship during the rule of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb.

Two explanations are transmitted for this name: extensive losses of people and property, or the ashen appearance of land and vegetation during drought.

This naming belongs to the period after Quranic revelation and cannot by itself determine the historical origin of ramād.

Irmidād: movement and speed

In another branch, irmidād denotes rapid movement or running.

Irmadda al-ẓalīmu is recorded of a male ostrich accelerating.

Related forms also describe a camel or another animal moving rapidly.

Here the subject is movement and speed, rather than ashes, eye inflammation, or destruction.

Shared consonants do not prove that an animal's rapid movement was named after ashes scattered by wind.

Such an image may be attractive poetically, but it lacks sufficient lexical evidence as a historical explanation.

Rapid movement should therefore remain a separate attested branch rather than being imposed on the Quranic meaning of ramād.

3. Poetic evidence and the social use of ashes

Abundant ashes as a sign of hospitality

The following verse is transmitted as part of a poem by the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah:

عَظِيمُ رَمَادِ القِدْرِ لَا مُتَعَبِّسٌ
وَلَا مُؤْيِسٌ مِنْهَا إِذَا هُوَ أَوْقَدَا

ʿaẓīmu ramādi al-qidri lā mutaʿabbisun / wa-lā muʾyisun minhā idhā huwa awqadā

Translation: “His cooking pot leaves abundant ashes; he is neither grim-faced nor one who disappoints those seeking its food when he lights the fire.”

In this verse, abundant ashes signify hospitality and feeding others.

Where cooking used wood or similar fuels, repeatedly lighting fires and preparing food could leave plentiful ashes.

“Great in the ashes of the cooking pot” therefore serves as a figurative expression for generous hospitality.

This evidence matters because ashes did not always represent ruin or worthlessness in Arabic.

The substance that can suggest something consumed and finished in one setting can suggest activity, cooking, and hospitality in another.

The image's positive or negative value comes from its setting and sentence.

Ramād does not itself mean generosity here. The plentiful ashes of a pot, in the context of lighting fires and preparing food, suggest generosity.

This distinction is essential to understanding the Quranic usage.

Are ashes inherently worthless?

In ordinary language, ashes may be the residue of something consumed and lost.

They cannot, however, always be defined lexically as useless or worthless.

Ashes have had various practical uses. They can also be a positive sign of hospitality in early Arabic poetry.

When the Quran compares a group's deeds to ashes, the remainder of the verse must be considered before attributing worthlessness to the word itself.

In Ibrāhīm 14:18, the ashes are in a specific situation: a violent wind blows upon them on a stormy day.

This situation, rather than the mere existence of ashes, provides the comparison.

Scattered ashes in early Islamic poetry

The following expression is quoted from al-Kumayt ibn Zayd, a poet of the Umayyad period:

رَمَادًا أَطَارَتْهُ السَّوَاهِكُ رِمْدِدَا

ramādan aṭārathu al-sawāhiku rimdidā

Translation: “Very fine ashes scattered by fierce winds.”

The phrase depicts fine ashes dispersed by wind.

Al-sawāhik refers to fierce winds, while rimdid describes the extreme fineness of the ashes here.

This evidence postdates Quranic revelation and should not be labeled pre-Islamic poetry.

It nevertheless helps establish the family's linguistic usage.

Its image resembles Ibrāhīm 14:18: fine ashes cannot remain in their former state and place under a violent wind.

This later poem cannot establish a pre-Quranic source for the image; similarity alone does not determine the direction of influence between the texts.

Ramd as destruction: a poetic witness requiring caution

A lexical record attributes the following verse to Abū Wajzah al-Saʿdī:

صَبَبْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ حَاصِبِي فَتَرَكْتُكُمْ
كَأَصْرَامِ عَادٍ حِينَ جَلَّلَهَا الرَّمْدُ

ṣababtu ʿalaykum ḥāṣibī fa-taraktukum / ka-aṣrāmi ʿādin ḥīna jallalahā al-ramdu

Translation: “I poured my pebble-driving storm upon you and left you like the groups of ʿĀd when destruction enveloped them.”

Here, al-ramd means destruction.

This attribution should be transmitted cautiously. The verse is not used here to establish a securely dated pre-Quranic occurrence; it illustrates the attested destruction sense of ramd.

The verse serves as a recorded lexical witness, rather than definitive evidence of a particular poet's pre-Quranic usage.

Al-ramd here is not the name of ashes; it belongs to the destruction branch.

Each derivative must therefore be understood through its vocalization, formation, and context.

4. Quranic usage: ka-ramādin in Ibrāhīm 14:18

The verse

Ibrāhīm 14:18

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ ۖ أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَرَمَادٍ اشْتَدَّتْ بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ ۖ لَا يَقْدِرُونَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الضَّلَالُ الْبَعِيدُ

mathalu alladhīna kafarū bi-rabbihim aʿmāluhum ka-ramādin ishtaddat bihi al-rīḥu fī yawmin ʿāṣifin lā yaqdirūna mimmā kasabū ʿalā shayʾin dhālika huwa al-ḍalālu al-baʿīdu

Ibrāhīm 14:18

Translation:

“The likeness of those who disbelieve in their Lord is this: their deeds are like ashes upon which the wind blows fiercely on a stormy day. They have no power over anything of what they have earned. That is far-reaching error.”

The meaning of ka-ramādin

Ka-ramādin combines the comparative particle ka- with the noun ramād.

Ka- expresses likeness; ramād names ashes.

Its direct meaning is therefore “like ashes.”

The verse uses ashes as a comparison for the deeds of the group described.

The comparison does not end with this word.

The rest of the sentence specifies the condition of the ashes:

اشْتَدَّتْ بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ

ishtaddat bihi al-rīḥu fī yawmin ʿāṣifin

Ibrāhīm 14:18

The wind has blown fiercely upon them on a stormy day.

The comparison thus concerns ashes exposed to a violent wind and scattered.

This distinction matters.

Saying only “their deeds are like ashes” omits part of the verse's central image.

The violent wind and stormy day play a decisive role in the comparison.

Why ashes?

Ashes commonly consist of fine particles.

While those particles remain in place, they may form a heap.

A violent wind can separate them and scatter them in different directions.

Someone trying to keep those ashes in the same place or gather them back into their earlier state would face difficulty.

The verse uses this observable feature to explain the condition of deeds.

Ramād itself does not mean “a destroyed deed.”

Ashes are a real substance; their scattering by wind is the event described in the sentence.

The outcome of the comparison should not become the noun's lexical definition.

Ishtaddat bihi al-rīḥ: the role of wind

Ishtaddat, from Sh D D, expresses intensification.

Al-rīḥ means the wind.

The pronoun -hi in bihi refers to the ashes. The preposition bi- connects the wind's violent action with them.

Wind is therefore the force acting upon the ashes in this image.

The ashes do not scatter by themselves: the violent wind plays a role.

This does not mean that people's deeds are physically destroyed by an actual wind.

Wind belongs to the simile; the Quran is not thereby describing a physical mechanism for destroying deeds.

Fī yawmin ʿāṣif: on a stormy day

ʿĀṣif, from ʿ Ṣ F, concerns a violently blowing wind.

Yawmin ʿāṣif describes the day as stormy.

This is an intelligible ordinary expression, comparable to “a rainy day,” “a hot day,” or “a stormy day.”

The day is not itself a creature producing wind; a violent wind blows during it.

“On a stormy day” is therefore an appropriate translation.

The wind's intensity and the day's character come from ishtaddat and ʿāṣif, rather than R M D.

The properties of the whole image should not be transferred into the definition of ashes.

Lā yaqdirūna mimmā kasabū ʿalā shayʾ

The verse continues:

لَا يَقْدِرُونَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ

lā yaqdirūna mimmā kasabū ʿalā shayʾin

Ibrāhīm 14:18

This sentence makes the comparison's outcome clear.

Those described have no effective power over, or beneficial access to, what they earned.

The ashes image suggests someone whose ashes have been scattered by a violent wind and who can no longer recover the same collection.

The deeds image concerns a person who needs the result of their actions but finds no beneficial support in it.

The comparison is therefore about losing the ability to benefit, rather than merely having few deeds.

Someone may have done many things in worldly life, even taking pride and hope in them, yet find at the reckoning that what seemed a source of support is of no use.

This conclusion comes from the complete verse, rather than ramād alone.

Does the verse erase every worldly effect of deeds?

The deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord are compared to scattered ashes.

The occurrence of an act in the world should be distinguished from its agent's benefit in the hereafter.

If someone saves another person from death, that rescue is a real event.

If someone feeds a hungry person, the recipient actually benefits from the food.

The agent's beliefs do not turn those real effects into events that never occurred.

The Quran can nevertheless make a different judgment about deeds' value and ultimate outcome before The God.

In Ibrāhīm 14:18, the statement that they have no power over what they earned follows the scattered-ashes image and emphasizes their inability to benefit from their deeds.

The verse should not be converted into a claim that all worldly effects of deeds physically disappear.

Nor can ramād itself establish a final judgment about every good or bad action or every particular person.

The subject concerns disbelief in one's Lord and the ultimate outcome of deeds within the wider Quranic discourse, rather than defining ashes as inherently worthless.

The significance of alladhīna kafarū bi-rabbihim

The opening identifies the group as alladhīna kafarū bi-rabbihim, those who disbelieve in their Lord.

It does not pronounce an identical judgment on everyone who makes a mistake or disagrees over an issue.

Kufr is a separate Quranic concept requiring study of its own root and usages.

Knowledge, intention, and responsibility also matter when judging people's conduct.

This article studies R M D and does not replace a separate study of kufr.

For the meaning of ashes, the essential point is that the Quran compares a specified group's deeds to scattered ashes.

That comparison concerns their deeds' outcome; it does not turn ramād into a doctrinal or moral label.

Dhālika huwa al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd

The verse finally calls this condition al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd.

Ḍalāl, from Ḍ L L, concerns going astray and departing from the right way.

Baʿīd emphasizes the extent of that distance.

This conclusion establishes the comparison's moral and spiritual direction.

Ḍalāl and ramād are nevertheless different words.

Ashes provide the image; error is the judgment expressed at its conclusion.

Ramād should therefore not be translated directly as “error,” “disbelief,” or “a false deed.”

Those implications come from context and the verse's conclusion, rather than the name of ashes.

5. Connections with other Quranic images

Ashes and scattered dust

Al-Furqān 25:23 states:

وَقَدِمْنَا إِلَىٰ مَا عَمِلُوا مِنْ عَمَلٍ فَجَعَلْنَاهُ هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًا

wa-qadimnā ilā mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin fa-jaʿalnāhu habāʾan manthūran

Al-Furqān 25:23

Translation: “We turn to whatever deeds they have done and make them scattered dust.”

Habāʾan manthūran evokes scattered particles.

The image resembles the scattered ashes in Ibrāhīm 14:18.

Both verses concern the loss of benefit from deeds in divine judgment.

Habāʾ and ramād are nevertheless different words.

Ramād names the residue of burning, while habāʾ concerns very fine dispersed particles.

The substances or images may share lightness and dispersibility without being lexically identical.

Ramād in Ibrāhīm 14:18 should therefore be rendered “ashes,” rather than “dust,” even though the comparisons approach a similar outcome.

Ashes and soil upon a smooth rock

Al-Baqarah 2:264 describes someone spending wealth for display.

Its example is a smooth rock covered with soil: heavy rain falls on it and washes the soil away.

The outcome resembles scattered ashes.

In both, something that may seem a possession or support is put beyond reach by an external force.

There is also an important difference.

In Al-Baqarah 2:264, soil rests on a rock and rain washes it off.

In Ibrāhīm 14:18, violent wind scatters ashes.

Water acts in one image; wind acts in the other.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 also concerns spending accompanied by reminders of favors, injury, and display, whereas Ibrāhīm 14:18 addresses the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord.

The examples should not be equated in every detail.

They share the loss of benefit, but each verse supplies its own moral circumstances and imagery.

An uprooted tree and scattered ashes

Ibrāhīm 14:24 compares a good word to a good tree with a firm root.

Ibrāhīm 14:26 compares a bad word to a bad tree uprooted from the ground, without stability.

These verses help explain the interconnected images in Ibrāhīm.

Ibrāhīm 14:18 compares deeds to ashes scattered by wind.

Ibrāhīm 14:26 depicts a tree displaced from its site and lacking stability.

Both images concern the loss of firmness and support.

Ashes and a tree are not lexically identical.

A tree has roots and a living structure; ashes are the residue of burnt material.

This difference gives each verse its particular image.

The ashes verse highlights scattering and inability to benefit.

The tree verse highlights the absence of a firm root and settled position in the ground.

These related images should therefore illuminate one another without erasing their differences.

6. Comparison with related roots

R M D (ramād) and R M Ḍ (ramaḍ)

These roots differ only in their final consonant, but their primary meanings are not identical.

Quranic R M D concerns ashes.

Arabic R M Ḍ concerns intense heat, hot ground, and the effects of heat on things.

Ashes may remain hot after burning or result from heat, but ashes are not heat itself.

Similar consonants also do not prove that one root derives from the other.

The residue of burning must be distinguished from heat itself.

R M D (ramād) and Ḥ R Q (ḥarq)

Ḥarq concerns burning and setting something alight.

Ramād names one possible residue of burning.

The relationship is clear, but the meanings differ.

In Ṭā Hā 20:97, la-nuḥarriqannahu is commonly interpreted as burning; grinding or filing is also discussed in lexical and exegetical treatments. This interpretive question is separate from the meaning of ramād.

Ibrāhīm 14:18 does not describe combustion itself; ashes are already present as the material in the comparison.

Ka-ramādin therefore does not directly state that deeds are burned in fire.

Burning can explain how ashes arise, but this comparison emphasizes their scattering by wind.

R M D (ramād) and H B W (habāʾ)

Habāʾ concerns fine dispersed particles.

Ramād names ashes.

Al-Furqān 25:23 uses habāʾan manthūran, while Ibrāhīm 14:18 uses ka-ramādin, qualified by the action of a violent wind.

The images overlap in scattering and the inability to gather or benefit from what is dispersed.

They do not name the same material or have identical lexical meanings.

Translating both simply as “dust” erases the distinction between ashes and dispersed particles.

“Ashes” is consequently more precise for ramād.

R M D (ramād) and T R B (turāb)

Turāb denotes soil or earth.

Soil and ashes may resemble each other, but their origins and uses differ.

Ashes arise from burning materials; turāb generally refers to the earth's soil.

The Quran uses turāb in passages about human creation, bodies returning to earth, and other subjects.

Ramād occurs only in the comparison involving deeds and wind-scattered ashes.

It should therefore not be reduced to “soil” or “earth dust.”

The basic translation should preserve the material's name.

R M D (ramd) and H L K (halāk)

Some lexical sources attest ramd in the sense of destruction.

Halāk has broad Quranic uses concerning the destruction of people or communities.

The roots are nevertheless distinct.

In Ibrāhīm 14:18, ramād names ashes; the verse does not describe people's destruction with the verb halaka.

In the lexical expression ramada al-qawm, by contrast, a group of people perishes.

A meaning attested for one branch should not be transferred to every derivative.

Nor does the comparison with scattered ashes by itself provide the lexical definition of halāk.

R M D (ramād) and R B D (rubdah)

Some Arabic usages of R B D concern dark, gray, or blackish coloration.

Lexical sources discuss similarities between armad and certain forms of arbad, sometimes with differing transmissions or analyses of their consonants.

These similarities matter for historical word study.

They do not establish that R M D and R B D are one root.

Nor does gray coloration in both families alter the Quranic meaning of ramād.

Ramād in Ibrāhīm 14:18 names ashes, rather than merely a color.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan and conclusions

Translating the Quranic form

Word: كَرَمَادٍ — Ibrāhīm 14:18

Direct equivalent:

“Like ashes.”

Within the full construction:

“Their deeds are like ashes upon which the wind has blown fiercely on a stormy day.”

This preserves both the word's meaning and its role in the image.

“Ashes” is preferable to “dust,” “soil,” or “scattered particles” because the Quran names a specific substance.

Ka-ramādin should also not be translated as “like worthless deeds.”

The deeds' loss of benefit emerges from the following sentence and the verse's conclusion, rather than from the lexical meaning of ashes.

These explanations should remain separate. These proposals do not replace existing authored explanations in verse word boxes.

Conclusions and final definition

Arabic R M D is a family with several branches.

The familiar ramād branch concerns ashes and fine residues of burning.

In the color branch, armad and related forms describe gray coloration and darkness.

Another branch uses ramad for eye inflammation and pain.

Some usages also give ramd the sense of destruction, while irmidād denotes rapid movement or running.

Early lexical analyses do not always trace these branches to one historical origin; one analysis distinguishes three separate semantic foundations.

A single definition such as “burning,” “perishing,” or “scattering” therefore cannot explain the entire family.

Poetry transmitted from the pre-Islamic period shows that ramād was familiar in Arabic life and culture.

In the poem attributed to ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah, plentiful cooking-pot ashes signify hospitality. Elsewhere, ashes may suggest something burnt or material scattered by wind.

The word itself consequently carries no fixed moral value.

R M D occurs only once in the Quran: ka-ramādin in Ibrāhīm 14:18.

The verse compares the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord to ashes upon which a violent wind blows on a stormy day.

The image principally conveys the loss of benefit from what a person regarded as an achievement.

That outcome arises from the whole comparison. Ashes alone do not mean error or worthlessness.

Final definition of R M D:

This linguistic family has several distinct branches:

1. Ramād: Ashes, the fine residue of burning materials.

2. Ramad: Eye inflammation or injury.

3. Armad and related forms: Gray coloration and darkness; in another usage, someone affected by ramad of the eye.

4. Ramd and ramādah: Destruction or perishing in certain usages.

5. Irmidād: Very rapid movement or running, recorded as a separate lexical branch.

These meanings should not be derived from one another without historical evidence.

Only the first sense appears in the Quranic usage.

Ka-ramādin means “like ashes.” Its role is to depict deeds from which benefit is unavailable when needed.

Ashes supply the material of the comparison, violent wind causes dispersal, and inability to benefit is the outcome stated by the verse.

Forqan's study and translation should distinguish the material's name, its condition within the simile, and the verse's moral and eschatological conclusion.

This preserves the clear meaning of ramād and prevents error, disbelief, the loss of deeds, or other judgments from becoming unsupported lexical definitions of ashes.
