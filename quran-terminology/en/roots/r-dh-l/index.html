Lowliness, inferior quality, and a most diminished condition

In Arabic, R DH L denotes inferiority, poor quality, or low rank. It applies to objects, human characteristics, and people’s standing in others’ eyes. رَذْل (radhl) and رَذِيل (radhīl) can describe something low, inferior, or undesirable; أَرْذَل (ardhal) expresses a lower or less desirable degree.

This inferiority is not always of one kind. Fabric may be called radhl because it is worn and of poor quality; conduct may be deemed radhīl because it is morally objectionable; and a person may be numbered among the أَرَاذِل (arādhil) by those who consider themselves superior.

These uses differ. Poor quality in an object, objectionable conduct, and low social standing attributed to a person are distinct judgments.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an. Two occurrences are in Noah’s narrative, where his opponents call his followers أَرَاذِلُنَا (arādhilunā) and الْأَرْذَلُونَ (al-ardhalūn). The other two appear in أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ (ardhali al-ʿumur), a period when a person may lose some previously acquired abilities and knowledge through advanced age.

This distinction matters: demeaning people on the basis of social standing differs from describing decline and incapacity at a stage of life. The Qur’an’s quotation of Noah’s opponents does not endorse their judgment of the believers.

Qur’anic forms: أَرَاذِلُنَا in Hūd 11:27; أَرْذَلِ in Al-Naḥl 16:70 and Al-Ḥajj 22:5; and الْأَرْذَلُونَ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:111.

Arabic also records رَذْل (radhl), رَذِيل (radhīl), رَذُلَ (radhula), رَذَالَة (radhālah), رُذَالَة (rudhālah), رُذُولَة (rudhūlah), رُذَال (rudhāl), رُذَلَاء (rudhalāʾ), أَرْذَال (ardhāl), رَذِيلَة (radhīlah), رَذَّلَ (radhdhala), أَرْذَلَ (ardhala), and اِسْتَرْذَلَ (istardhala). These do not occur in precisely these forms in the Qur’an and should be distinguished from the derivatives actually used there.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Inferiority and undesirable quality

A lexical explanation of radhl states:

الرَّذْلُ: الدُّونُ مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ

al-radhlu al-dūnu min kulli shayʾin

Meaning: “Radhl is the lower or inferior part of anything.”

The root is therefore not confined to human beings.

Lexical usage describes radhl as low or inferior, while rudhālah names the less desirable part of something.

دُون (dūn) here concerns a lower level of rank, quality, or valuation.

A lower rank does not always mean moral corruption.

A product may have poorer workmanship than another without any connection to moral virtue or vice.

When this family describes human conduct, however, it can concern moral undesirability.

A root definition should preserve the shared field of inferiority and undesirability, while identifying the kind of inferiority from what is described and its context.

Thawbun radhlun: inferior, worn clothing

Arabic records ثَوْبٌ رَذْلٌ (thawbun radhlun) and ثَوْبٌ رَذِيلٌ (thawbun radhīlun) for old, dirty, or inferior clothing.

Radhl here describes the garment’s condition.

The garment may be considered undesirable because it is worn, made of poor material, or unsatisfactory in appearance.

The description alone says nothing about its wearer’s character or morality.

Someone may wear simple or worn clothing because of poverty, living conditions, daily work, or personal choice.

An inferior garment does not establish that its owner is morally base or of inferior human worth.

This distinction matters in Noah’s opponents’ speech: they use a demeaning designation for his followers, although social standing and appearance alone do not measure the soundness of belief and conduct.

Dirhamun radhlun: inferior money or goods

Lexical usage records دِرْهَمٌ رَذْلٌ (dirhamun radhlun) for an inferior or undesirable dirham.

أَرْذَلَ فُلَانٌ دَرَاهِمِي (ardhala fulānun darāhimī) describes someone judging another person’s dirhams inferior or rejecting their quality.

The judgment concerns the quality of money or a medium of exchange.

رُذَالَة (rudhālah) also names the inferior remainder left after the better parts have been selected.

The meaning is clear: some items in a collection are selected for quality, while others occupy a lower level.

This application to goods should not immediately be transferred to human relationships.

A person is not a commodity whose worth is measured by material quality, property, clothing, or economic usefulness.

This is especially relevant when arādhil is used to demean a group of people.

Radhula and radhdhala: a condition and judging something inferior

رَذُلَ يَرْذُلُ رَذَالَةً (radhula yardhulu radhālatan) means becoming inferior, low, or undesirable.

Radhīl is an adjective from the same family.

The transitive رَذَلَهُ (radhalahu) and أَرْذَلَهُ (ardhalahu) concern making something inferior or low. رَذَّلَهُ (radhdhalahu) is also proposed in explanations of Form II for that meaning. Judging something and causing a condition should not be conflated without context.

اِسْتَرْذَلَهُ (istardhalahu) is recorded for considering someone or something inferior or low.

These forms require distinguishing a characteristic’s existence from someone’s judgment about it.

When something is actually worn or inferior, the adjective may describe its condition.

When someone calls another person radhīl or ardhal, however, it may express only the speaker’s outlook and judgment.

The word itself does not establish that the judgment is correct.

This is clear in the Qur’an: the two occurrences demeaning people are spoken by Noah’s opponents, rather than directly defining the believers’ true worth on The God’s authority.

Radhīlah and the development of moral usage

Arabic uses رَذِيلَة (radhīlah) opposite فَضِيلَة (faḍīlah) for an objectionable moral characteristic or behavior.

This use is established in ethical disciplines and later writings.

General undesirability should nevertheless be distinguished from the technical ethical term vice.

In that technical use, vice names a moral trait assessed against standards of virtue.

Radhl, by contrast, can describe cloth, dirhams, goods, bodily condition, or social standing.

Every Qur’anic use of the root cannot therefore be translated as moral corruption.

Ardhali al-ʿumur in Al-Naḥl and Al-Ḥajj concerns a period of life and diminished abilities, rather than an older person becoming corrupt or wicked.

Nor does the opponents’ demeaning description of Noah’s followers establish moral vice in those followers.

Early evidence and the limits of etymology

Early Arabic dictionaries record inferiority and poor quality as the core of this family.

Thawbun radhlun, thawbun radhīlun, رُذَالَةُ الشَّيْءِ (rudhālatu al-shayʾi), and uses concerning dirhams and people show its linguistic range.

A phrase’s presence in an early dictionary alone does not establish when it first arose.

This article presents no independent poetic witness whose wording and attribution to the pre-Qur’anic period have been examined. Its evidence in this section consists of recorded lexical expressions.

Citing early poetry requires a particular verse and examination of its wording and attribution; a dictionary meaning alone cannot replace a poetic witness.

Lexical explanations also discuss the neighboring family R DH A, concerning weakness and emaciation, alongside this family.

That proximity and semantic resemblance may interest historical research, but two shared initial letters and similarity between inferiority and weakness do not alone prove a historical relationship.

R DH L must therefore rest on its own independent evidence.

2. Arādhilunā: the chiefs demean Noah’s followers

The root’s first Qur’anic occurrence is in Hūd 11:27:

فَقَالَ الْمَلَأُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَوْمِهِ مَا نَرَاكَ إِلَّا بَشَرًا مِثْلَنَا وَمَا نَرَاكَ اتَّبَعَكَ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ هُمْ أَرَاذِلُنَا بَادِيَ الرَّأْيِ وَمَا نَرَىٰ لَكُمْ عَلَيْنَا مِنْ فَضْلٍ بَلْ نَظُنُّكُمْ كَاذِبِينَ

fa-qāla al-malaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qawmihi mā narāka illā basharan mithlanā wa-mā narāka ittabaʿaka illā alladhīna hum arādhilunā bādiya al-raʾyi wa-mā narā lakum ʿalaynā min faḍlin bal naẓunnukum kādhibīna

“Then the chiefs of his people who disbelieved said: We see you only as a human like ourselves; and we see none following you except our lowly people, at first sight. We see no superiority in you over us; rather, we think you are liars.”

Who uses this expression?

In Hūd 11:27, the speakers are identified as الْمَلَأُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَوْمِهِ (al-malaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qawmihi).

Al-malaʾ here concerns chiefs and people of social standing among the community.

They assess Noah and his followers and claim that only those they regard as lowly have followed him.

Arādhilunā is thus part of Noah’s opponents’ speech.

The distinction is decisive: the Qur’an quotes their speech; it does not endorse their valuation of Noah’s followers through this designation.

Overlooking that distinction could suggest that the Qur’an itself calls Noah’s believing companions base or unworthy.

That is not how the verse is constructed.

The speakers are those who deny Noah and invoke his followers’ social standing to reject his call.

The morphology of arādhilunā

A common analysis takes arādhil as the broken plural of ardhal, joined to the pronoun nā as arādhilunā. Other lexical analyses include a plural of a plural. Their differences do not change the contempt expressed in the speech.

In الَّذِينَ هُمْ أَرَاذِلُنَا (alladhīna hum arādhilunā), hum is the subject of the nominal clause and arādhilunā its predicate.

The final nā refers to the speakers: they rank these people as their community’s lowly members.

The possessive construction identifies ranking from that group’s perspective.

The pronoun nā alone does not establish that these people were slaves, servants, legal dependents, or members of particular families.

The verse supplies no such information.

What is clear is a group’s contemptuous judgment of Noah’s followers’ standing.

Were Noah’s followers actually base people?

The verse itself offers no evidence of moral corruption or wrongful conduct in these individuals.

The opponents merely assign them a low rank.

In Hūd 11:27, they also say; an excerpt:

وَمَا نَرَىٰ لَكُمْ عَلَيْنَا مِنْ فَضْلٍ

wa-mā narā lakum ʿalaynā min faḍlin

“And we see no superiority in you over us.”

They assess the matter in terms of superiority and inferiority.

In their view, Noah and his followers must possess some recognizable distinction to make their call worth accepting.

This argument measures a message’s truth by its followers’ social rank.

Those are different standards.

A wealthy, influential, or socially prominent person may speak falsely; someone with few possessions or little social standing may accept a true statement.

Deeming Noah’s followers lowly therefore supplies no argument against his message by itself.

Are poor people and manual workers intended?

Exegetical and lexical explanations discuss the followers’ social position.

One lexical report treats attributing occupations such as weaving and cupping to them as part of their opponents’ outlook. The same explanation emphasizes that occupations do not themselves diminish religious worth.

The report can illustrate an attitude of class prejudice toward certain occupations.

Hūd 11:27 itself, however, names no particular occupation.

Arādhilunā should therefore not be replaced in translation by weavers, laborers, cuppers, or a specific occupational group.

Poverty can be a reason people are treated as lowly in a society, but ardhal is not lexically synonymous with poor.

Poverty concerns lack of property; radhl concerns inferiority or being considered undesirable.

A discriminatory outlook may connect them, but they should not be equated.

Bādiya al-raʾyi: judgment at first sight

Arādhilunā is followed by بَادِيَ الرَّأْيِ (bādiya al-raʾyi).

That phrase does not belong to R DH L and requires separate examination.

Its grammatical connection with the sentence has different explanations.

One interpretation has the opponents claiming that Noah’s followers believed hastily, without sufficient reflection.

Another has them claiming that, at first sight or in outward appearance, only lowly people have followed him.

Readings and lexical analyses also discuss forms connected with appearing and beginning.

None of these possibilities makes arādhilunā mean hasty people.

Arādhilunā demeans people’s rank; bādiya al-raʾyi is another part of the opponents’ judgment about the followers.

The two phrases should therefore not be merged into a single lexical definition.

3. Noah’s response: human worth is not set by those who demean others

Noah’s response is particularly important for understanding arādhilunā within the narrative.

He says in Hūd 11:29; an excerpt:

وَمَا أَنَا بِطَارِدِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّهُمْ مُلَاقُو رَبِّهِمْ وَلَٰكِنِّي أَرَاكُمْ قَوْمًا تَجْهَلُونَ

wa-mā anā bi-ṭāridi alladhīna āmanū innahum mulāqū rabbihim wa-lākinnī arākum qawman tajhalūna

“And I will not drive away those who believe. They will meet their Lord, but I see you as a people acting ignorantly.”

Noah refuses to discard his followers.

The chiefs’ contempt is therefore no basis for excluding them from Noah’s company.

He also says in Hūd 11:30:

وَيَا قَوْمِ مَنْ يَنْصُرُنِي مِنَ اللَّهِ إِنْ طَرَدْتُهُمْ أَفَلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ

wa-yā qawmi man yanṣurunī mina allāhi in ṭaradtuhum a-fa-lā tadhakkarūna

“My people, who would help me against The God if I drove them away? Will you not take heed?”

The question shows that Noah considers expelling believers a serious wrong.

That conclusion is not part of the lexical meaning of R DH L.

The root concerns inferiority and unfavorable valuation; the rejection of social discrimination and defense of believers arise from Noah’s conduct and speech in context.

Tazdarī aʿyunukum: contempt in others’ eyes

Hūd 11:31 states; an excerpt:

وَلَا أَقُولُ لِلَّذِينَ تَزْدَرِي أَعْيُنُكُمْ لَنْ يُؤْتِيَهُمُ اللَّهُ خَيْرًا اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ

wa-lā aqūlu li-alladhīna tazdarī aʿyunukum lan yuʾtiyahumu allāhu khayran allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā fī anfusihim

“Nor do I say of those your eyes despise that The God will never give them any good. The God knows best what is within them.”

This helps explain the preceding speech.

تَزْدَرِي (tazdarī) belongs to another root and concerns despising or regarding with contempt.

Noah makes the issue more explicit: those his opponents despise should not be considered excluded from divine good on the basis of that outlook.

He does not say that social lowliness necessarily makes them purer or better than everyone else.

Nor does he claim knowledge of all their inward qualities and actions.

Instead, he says The God knows best what is within them.

He thus rejects his opponents’ contemptuous judgment while refraining from claiming certain knowledge of people’s inner states.

The chiefs’ social standard cannot replace the reality of faith, conduct, and divine judgment.

4. Wa-ittabaʿaka al-ardhalūn: the same objection in Al-Shuʿarāʾ

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:111 presents another occurrence in Noah’s narrative:

قَالُوا أَنُؤْمِنُ لَكَ وَاتَّبَعَكَ الْأَرْذَلُونَ

qālū a-nuʾminu laka wa-ittabaʿaka al-ardhalūna

“They said: Shall we believe you when the lowly have followed you?”

This is close in meaning to the opponents’ words in Hūd 11:27.

In both verses, they make his followers’ social standing a reason to reject his call.

The word formation nevertheless differs.

Al-ardhalūn and arādhilunā

Al-ardhalūn is the sound masculine plural of ardhal and bears the definite article.

Arādhilunā in Hūd 11:27 is, in the common analysis, the broken plural of the same form, made definite by attachment to nā.

Both are plural. They differ in plural formation and definiteness. Both refer in context to people Noah’s opponents deem lowly, but neither establishes the number of individuals by itself.

The broken and sound plurals alone do not prove that Noah had necessarily fewer or more followers in one situation than the other.

Nor can one form be shown to express necessarily greater contempt than the other.

The whole sentence and conversational context convey the intensity of objection and contempt. Plural formation alone establishes no definite scale.

A-nuʾminu laka: using followers’ standing to reject a message

The opponents ask in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:111; an excerpt:

أَنُؤْمِنُ لَكَ

a-nuʾminu laka

“Shall we believe you?”

In the same verse, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:111, they then state their objection; an excerpt:

وَاتَّبَعَكَ الْأَرْذَلُونَ

wa-ittabaʿaka al-ardhalūna

“When the lowly have followed you.”

They connect accepting Noah’s call with his followers’ social position.

The company of those they deem lowly seems to prevent their accepting the same call themselves.

A message’s validity, however, is not determined by the wealth, occupation, lineage, or social standing of those accepting it.

This follows from the opponents’ reasoning and Noah’s response later in the narrative.

Wa-mā ʿilmī bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūn

Noah answers in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:112; an excerpt:

وَمَا عِلْمِي بِمَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-mā ʿilmī bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūna

“And what knowledge have I of what they used to do?”

He continues in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:113:

إِنْ حِسَابُهُمْ إِلَّا عَلَىٰ رَبِّي لَوْ تَشْعُرُونَ

in ḥisābuhum illā ʿalā rabbī law tashʿurūna

“Their account rests only with my Lord, if you could perceive.”

Noah entrusts final judgment of people’s deeds to The God.

He does not accept the opponents’ allegation as established truth, but neither claims complete awareness of all his followers’ actions.

He states explicitly in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:114:

وَمَا أَنَا بِطَارِدِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-mā anā bi-ṭāridi al-muʾminīna

“And I will not drive away the believers.”

His response in Al-Shuʿarāʾ is consistent with his response in Hūd.

In neither case does social contempt become a pretext for expelling the followers.

The lexical boundary remains: al-ardhalūn itself does not mean believers who must not be expelled. The opponents call these people by that name; Noah subsequently clarifies their position against the contemptuous judgment.

Does the Qur’an consider low-status people inherently better than the wealthy?

These two accounts establish no such absolute rule.

They portray people who demean a message’s followers by social rank instead of examining the message.

That does not make every poor or low-status person morally virtuous simply because of their economic circumstances.

Wealth and social standing alone likewise prove neither corruption nor denial of truth.

The narrative criticizes making social rank the measure of a divine call’s truth and a pretext for demeaning or excluding others.

5. Ardhali al-ʿumur: life’s most diminished stage of ability

The root’s two other Qur’anic occurrences concern a stage of human life.

Ardhal here does not describe people’s social standing or moral corruption. It occurs with الْعُمُرِ (al-ʿumur), life or lifespan.

Ardhali al-ʿumur in Al-Naḥl

Al-Naḥl 16:70 states:

وَاللَّهُ خَلَقَكُمْ ثُمَّ يَتَوَفَّاكُمْ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْ لَا يَعْلَمَ بَعْدَ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ قَدِيرٌ

wa-allāhu khalaqakum thumma yatawaffākum wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kay lā yaʿlama baʿda ʿilmin shayʾan inna allāha ʿalīmun qadīrun

“The God created you, then takes you in death. Some of you are returned to life’s most diminished stage, so that after knowledge they know nothing. The God is knowing and powerful.”

Ardhali is in a construct phrase with al-ʿumur.

Morphologically, ardhal follows the afʿal pattern. Here it denotes a very undesirable condition within a period of life.

What, however, makes that condition undesirable?

Al-Naḥl 16:70 continues; an excerpt:

لِكَيْ لَا يَعْلَمَ بَعْدَ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا

li-kay lā yaʿlama baʿda ʿilmin shayʾan

“So that after knowledge he knows nothing.”

The verse concerns someone whose capacity to know and remember has severely diminished after possessing knowledge and understanding.

Being most diminished concerns a person’s abilities at a stage of life, rather than their moral worth or human dignity.

Yuraddu belongs to another root

Al-Naḥl 16:70 states; an excerpt:

يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ

yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri

يُرَدُّ (yuraddu) belongs to R D D, rather than R DH L.

It is a passive verb expressing being returned.

Returning should therefore not be made part of ardhal itself.

Yuraddu expresses returning or reaching another condition; ardhali al-ʿumur identifies that condition.

Together they portray someone who, after acquiring strength and knowledge, may return to a stage of incapacity during life.

This does not mean time literally reverses or the older person becomes a child.

The person remains in later life but loses some abilities previously acquired.

Study of R D D

Does ardhali al-ʿumur identify a particular age?

No.

The verse gives no numerical age at which this condition begins.

The phrase cannot be fixed at seventy, eighty, ninety, or any other age.

Exegetical reports propose different ages, but the Qur’an itself sets no numerical criterion.

People also do not all experience incapacity to the same degree at a particular age.

Some retain substantial cognitive abilities into advanced age, while others encounter severe decline earlier.

Al-Naḥl 16:70 also says; an excerpt:

وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ

wa-minkum man yuraddu

“And among you is one who is returned.”

The construction does not place everyone in the same condition.

The verse therefore does not establish that every older person enters ardhali al-ʿumur simply by passing a specified age.

Li-kay lā yaʿlama baʿda ʿilmin shayʾan

This phrase is particularly significant for the meaning.

عِلْم (ʿilm) belongs to ʿ L M and concerns knowing.

بَعْدَ عِلْمٍ (baʿda ʿilmin) indicates that the person previously possessed knowledge and understanding.

The verse then describes severe diminishment of the ability to know.

It is therefore not simply about limited knowledge in someone who has never learned anything.

The subject is loss of abilities and knowledge previously possessed.

The phrase should not, however, be restricted to a particular medical diagnosis.

The Qur’an names no specific disease here and does not explain the biological causes of declining memory and cognition.

Lā yaʿlama ... shayʾan occurs in a context of severe decline in knowing. It should not be converted into a mathematical assertion that the person has absolutely no perception, recognition, or experience.

The verse describes the severity of cognitive decline, rather than inventorying every retained or lost human ability.

6. Ardhali al-ʿumur in Al-Ḥajj: changing human abilities

The phrase also occurs in Al-Ḥajj 22:5; an excerpt:

وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُتَوَفَّىٰ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْلَا يَعْلَمَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا

wa-minkum man yutawaffā wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kaylā yaʿlama min baʿdi ʿilmin shayʾan

“Some of you are taken in death, and some are returned to life’s most diminished stage, so that after knowledge they know nothing.”

This is close in meaning to Al-Naḥl 16:70.

The wider context in Al-Ḥajj nevertheless differs.

From human formation to diminished abilities

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 begins by addressing people; an excerpt:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ فِي رَيْبٍ مِنَ الْبَعْثِ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu in kuntum fī raybin mina al-baʿthi

“People, if you are in doubt about resurrection...”

It recalls human formation: from dust, a drop, a clinging substance, and a lump of flesh through development in the womb, birth, and reaching maturity of ability.

It then mentions some people dying and others reaching decline and incapacity.

Ardhali al-ʿumur is thus part of a description of changes across human life.

Someone initially without ability gradually develops, reaches strength, and may lose some abilities again late in life.

This relates to the verse’s central reminder of The God’s power to create and raise the dead.

That message comes from the complete verse, rather than from R DH L independently.

Ashuddakum and ardhali al-ʿumur

An earlier part of Al-Ḥajj 22:5 states; an excerpt:

ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ

thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum

“Then that you may reach your full strength.”

أَشُدَّكُمْ (ashuddakum) refers to a stage of human strength and ability.

Death or reaching ardhali al-ʿumur is mentioned afterward.

This sequence connects the development of ability with its later decline at a stage of life.

It does not mean everyone reaches peak strength at one fixed age or subsequently loses ability at an identical rate.

The Qur’an describes an overall course and the possibility of change, rather than a medical chart of precise ages.

Does incapacity in old age indicate personal baseness?

No.

Ardhal here is joined to al-ʿumur, rather than to a person’s moral character.

The subject is an undesirable period in terms of diminished abilities.

This verse should therefore not be used to call an older person experiencing memory loss, physical incapacity, or dependence on others base, worthless, or without dignity.

Diminished mental ability also does not establish worthlessness in every human or moral dimension.

The Qur’an discusses the condition of abilities here, rather than judging inherent human worth.

This distinction is both lexically and ethically necessary.

Changing abilities in other verses

Al-Rūm 30:54 states; an excerpt:

اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ ضَعْفٍ ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ ضَعْفٍ قُوَّةً ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ ضَعْفًا وَشَيْبَةً

allāhu alladhī khalaqakum min ḍaʿfin thumma jaʿala min baʿdi ḍaʿfin quwwatan thumma jaʿala min baʿdi quwwatin ḍaʿfan wa-shaybatan

“The God is the One who created you from weakness, then placed strength after weakness, then placed weakness and gray hair after strength.”

The verse describes the general course of weakness, strength, and returning weakness.

Yā Sīn 36:68 also states:

وَمَنْ نُعَمِّرْهُ نُنَكِّسْهُ فِي الْخَلْقِ أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-man nuʿammirhu nunakkis-hu fī al-khalqi a-fa-lā yaʿqilūna

“Whomever We grant long life, We reverse in creation. Will they not reason?”

These verses help establish the conceptual connections concerning changes in human ability.

However, ضَعْف (ḍaʿf), شَيْبَة (shaybah), and تَنْكِيس (tankīs) belong to other roots.

They should not all be turned into meanings of radhl.

Diminished physical ability, gray hair, and loss of some knowledge or recognition are related phenomena, but they differ lexically and in what they describe.

Ardhali al-ʿumur in the two verses under discussion is particularly associated with severe loss of cognitive ability, and should not be generalized to every ordinary sign of advancing age.

7. R DH L and related roots

R DH L (radhl) and R D ʾ (radīʾ)

Radīʾ belongs to R D ʾ, one branch of which concerns poor quality and inferiority.

Radhl can also describe inferior goods.

ثَوْبٌ رَذْلٌ (thawbun radhlun) and ثَوْبٌ رَدِيءٌ (thawbun radīʾun), for example, are close in describing undesirable clothing quality.

The roots are nevertheless distinct.

Qur’anic R DH L also covers social inferiority in the speakers’ eyes and a most diminished condition at a stage of life.

R D ʾ requires its own independent uses. Shared reference to poor quality does not justify merging the roots.

Nor should the similarity between radīʾ and رَدِيَ (radiya), to perish, from R D Y cause confusion.

Study of R D ʾ

R DH L (radhl) and DH L L (dhull)

Arabic ذُلّ (dhull) concerns humiliation, humility, or tameness, depending on form and context.

Radhl concerns inferiority and undesirability.

They may be close in some situations: someone treated as contemptible may also be called base by another person.

That proximity does not make the meanings identical.

The family of dhull can also concern tameness or humility and is not negative in every use.

Radhl as a description, however, usually carries an unfavorable or contemptuous valuation.

Arādhilunā should therefore not simply be translated as our humble people; that would remove the contempt in Noah’s opponents’ speech.

Conversely, humility or lack of social power should not automatically be called baseness.

Study of DH L L

R DH L (radhl) and S F L (sufl)

Sufl concerns being low or occupying a lower level or position.

In physical usage, the lower position can be spatial.

Elsewhere it may concern a person’s rank or condition.

Radhl also concerns lowliness, but in clear uses carries undesirability or an inferior valuation of quality or rank.

Sufl and radhl thus overlap without being identical.

Al-Tīn 95:5 states:

ثُمَّ رَدَدْنَاهُ أَسْفَلَ سَافِلِينَ

thumma radadnāhu asfala sāfilīna

“Then We returned him to the lowest of the low.”

This expression belongs to S F L, rather than R DH L.

Interpretations differ about its referent, including a human being’s ultimate outcome or a condition of incapacity.

The resemblance between أَسْفَلَ (asfala) and أَرْذَلِ (ardhali) does not establish that they certainly express precisely the same thing.

Particular attention is needed to Al-Tīn 95:6 and its exception.

Each expression must therefore first be examined in its own context.

R DH L (radhl) and Ḍ ʿ F (ḍaʿf)

Ḍaʿf concerns incapacity or a lack of strength or ability.

Diminished ability can be an important feature of the condition described by ardhali al-ʿumur.

Weakness itself, however, is not baseness.

A child may initially be weak, but that does not make the child a base human being.

Illness, physical incapacity, and dependence on others similarly do not establish moral corruption.

Ardhali al-ʿumur describes an undesirable period in terms of lost abilities; its continuation specifies a severe decline in knowing.

Weakness may therefore form part of the condition described, without constituting the whole meaning of R DH L.

Study of Ḍ ʿ F

R DH L (radhl) and K R M (karam)

In the Qur’an, karam relates to nobility, honor, worth, and standing.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 states; an excerpt:

إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ

inna akramakum ʿinda allāhi atqākum

“The most honored of you before The God is the most mindful of you.”

This helps assess the social judgment in Noah’s narrative.

His opponents despise his followers on the basis of a rank they themselves assign them.

The Al-Ḥujurāt verse, however, relates honor before The God to taqwā.

Wealth, lineage, occupation, or social position cannot therefore automatically measure honor before The God.

That conclusion follows from the related verses concerning human worth and taqwā, rather than from R DH L independently.

Nor should radhl and karam be treated as complete opposites in all lexical uses: the former can concern product quality and the latter also has other semantic fields.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent is unsuitable for every form of this family.

رَذْل (radhl): low; inferior; base or undesirable, depending on what is described.

رَذِيل (radhīl): inferior; undesirable; in moral usage, having an objectionable trait or conduct.

رَذَالَة / رُذَالَة (radhālah / rudhālah): radhālah, with fatḥah on rāʾ, denotes inferiority or baseness; rudhālah, with ḍammah, denotes the undesirable remainder after the better parts are selected. The vocalizations should be distinguished.

رُذَال (rudhāl): inferior or low; recorded both as a description and as a rare plural. In a construction with the name of something, it can also denote its inferior part.

رُذَلَاء / أَرْذَال (rudhalāʾ / ardhāl): recorded plurals for people deemed low or undesirable.

رَذُلَ (radhula): became inferior or low; an intransitive verb. Considering something inferior expresses another person’s judgment and should not be equated with this verb without regard to construction.

رَذَّلَ (radhdhala): in Form II, made low or inferior. Contempt and unfavorable valuation must be assessed in each context.

اِسْتَرْذَلَ (istardhala): deemed inferior; regarded as contemptible or low.

رَذِيلَة (radhīlah): an objectionable trait; moral vice. This technical use should not be imposed on every Qur’anic form.

أَرْذَل (ardhal): lower; worse; baser. With al-ʿumur, life’s most diminished or declining condition.

أَرَاذِلُنَا (arādhilunā): our lowly people; those the speakers assign a low social rank.

الْأَرْذَلُونَ (al-ardhalūn): the lowly; people deemed base or contemptible in the speakers’ eyes.

أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ (ardhali al-ʿumur): life’s most diminished stage; its most declining and debilitating period.

Hūd 11:27 — excerpt

وَمَا نَرَاكَ اتَّبَعَكَ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ هُمْ أَرَاذِلُنَا بَادِيَ الرَّأْيِ

wa-mā narāka ittabaʿaka illā alladhīna hum arādhilunā bādiya al-raʾyi

“And we see none following you except our lowly people, at first sight.”

Our lowly people is lexically suitable here, provided the explanation identifies this as the opponents’ description, rather than the Qur’an’s final judgment on the followers’ worth.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:111

قَالُوا أَنُؤْمِنُ لَكَ وَاتَّبَعَكَ الْأَرْذَلُونَ

qālū a-nuʾminu laka wa-ittabaʿaka al-ardhalūna

“They said: Shall we believe you when the lowly have followed you?”

The lowly similarly conveys the speech’s social contempt.

Poor people or workers should not replace it without explanation, since the Arabic word is not confined to those groups.

Al-Naḥl 16:70 — excerpt

وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْ لَا يَعْلَمَ بَعْدَ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا

wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kay lā yaʿlama baʿda ʿilmin shayʾan

“Some of you are returned to life’s most diminished stage, so that after knowledge they know nothing.”

Life’s most diminished stage can preserve the Arabic construction, provided it is clear that diminished abilities are intended, rather than baseness of character.

The Persian equivalent of life’s most declining stage is also natural and defensible, although it foregrounds a more explanatory, contextual aspect of the meaning.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 — excerpt

وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرَدُّ إِلَىٰ أَرْذَلِ الْعُمُرِ لِكَيْلَا يَعْلَمَ مِنْ بَعْدِ عِلْمٍ شَيْئًا

wa-minkum man yuraddu ilā ardhali al-ʿumuri li-kaylā yaʿlama min baʿdi ʿilmin shayʾan

“Some of you are returned to life’s most diminished stage, so that after knowledge they know nothing.”

For consistency in Forqan, ardhali al-ʿumur should preferably have one principal rendering in both verses, unless a genuine difference in construction and context requires another equivalent.

It should not become moral corruption in one verse and bodily decline in the other.

9. Conclusion

Arabic R DH L denotes lowliness, poor quality, and an inferior rank or condition.

This is clear in uses concerning objects, such as inferior cloth and dirhams.

In human usage, the family can describe objectionable behavior or demeaning judgments about people.

An object’s actual quality, a behavior’s moral worth, and social judgments about people must be distinguished.

The Qur’an uses the root four times, in two main fields.

First, Noah’s opponents call his followers arādhilunā and al-ardhalūn. These expressions convey the speakers’ contempt.

Quoting their words does not endorse actual baseness in the believers. Noah’s response also shows his refusal to expel his followers because of his opponents’ social judgment.

Nor does Noah claim certain knowledge of every person’s inward state and actions. He entrusts final judgment to The God.

Second, ardhali al-ʿumur in Al-Naḥl and Al-Ḥajj concerns a period when someone may experience severe cognitive decline after acquiring strength and knowledge.

This describes an undesirable condition at a stage of life, rather than a deterioration of older people’s moral character.

The Qur’an assigns no numerical age and does not generalize the condition to everyone at one fixed time.

The two fields fundamentally differ: in Noah’s narrative, people are demeaned by others; in the lifespan verses, a debilitating condition is described.

The root should therefore not always be restricted to moral corruption, poverty, low social rank, physical incapacity, or old age.

Nor should an invented material image, such as falling from a position or separating a collection’s inferior part, be asserted as the definite origin of all its meanings.

Final definition: R DH L denotes inferiority and an undesirable rank or quality. It can concern objects, behavior, social standing in others’ eyes, or a condition at a stage of life. In the Qur’an, arādhilunā and al-ardhalūn convey Noah’s opponents’ contemptuous judgment of his followers, whereas ardhali al-ʿumur concerns life’s most diminished, declining condition in terms of lost abilities. The kind of inferiority, the judgment’s validity, and a person’s moral worth must be learned from context, rather than from the root alone.
