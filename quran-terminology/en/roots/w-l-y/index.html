Qur'anic Root

Qur'anic Root W L Y

Nearness and continuity; a relation that may lead to direction, support, undertaking, belonging, or priority.

By name of The God The Merciful The Compassionate

Short Introduction to the Root

The root W L Y moves in the field of nearness, continuity, following one after another, and the establishment of a relation in which no clear gap or break remains between two sides. From this core arise meanings such as being near, turning toward a direction, standing beside someone, establishing a supportive bond, taking up someone’s matter, gaining priority, and, in some structures, turning away.

This root cannot be translated by one fixed word such as “friend,” “guardian,” “ruler,” or “helper.” Each of these meanings may be correct in a Qur’anic context, but none of them alone contains the whole field of the root. Sometimes the discussion is about spatial nearness, sometimes about bodily direction, sometimes about a social and supportive bond, sometimes about undertaking responsibility, and sometimes about someone who is nearer and more entitled in relation to another.

The root by itself has no positive or negative ethical direction. The God is the wali of the believers, but taghut is also introduced as the awliya of those who cover truth. Believers are awliya of one another, but devils also become awliya of those who choose them. The goodness or evil of this bond is understood from the parties of the relation, its direction, and the context of the verse, not from the letters of the root alone.

Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

The important Qur’anic forms of this root are the following:

وَلِيَ، يَلِي

This is the base verbal form and indicates being near or standing beside something. In the Qur’an, the form “يَلُونَكُمْ” is used about those who are nearer to the addressees.

وَلَّى، يُوَلِّي

In Form II it is used for turning toward a direction, directing, or turning one’s back. Forms such as “فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ,” “وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا,” and “يُوَلُّونَ الْأَدْبَارَ” belong to this pattern.

تَوَلَّى، يَتَوَلَّى

This form has two prominent uses. When it comes with a direct object, it may indicate accepting a bond, undertaking, or aligning with someone, as in “يَتَوَلَّهُمْ.” When it comes with “عن” or in a context of turning the back, it takes the meaning of turning away, distancing oneself, or leaving, as in “تَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ.” Therefore the morphological form alone is not enough to determine the meaning.

وَلِيّ and أَوْلِيَاء

This is said of one who stands in a near relation and, according to context, may be an undertaker, supporter, ally, holder of authority, responsible kin, or the near party in the relation.

مَوْلَىٰ and مَوَالِي

“Mawla” is a relational word whose exact meaning becomes clear from the two sides of the relation and from the context. In Arabic it has been used for kin, ally, supporter, holder of a matter, dependent, and party to a bond. In the Qur’an too it cannot be translated everywhere by one fixed equivalent.

وَلَايَة and وِلَايَة

This noun appears in the Qur’an in Al-Anfal 8:72 and Al-Kahf 18:44. In the Hafs reading both places are read with fathah on the waw, that is “walayah”; but in other valid readings the form “wilayah” with kasrah on the waw also appears in these places.

In lexical sources there are several views about these two forms. Some see “walayah” with fathah mostly as the verbal noun of bond, support, and nearness, and “wilayah” with kasrah more as command, authority, and an office someone undertakes. Others consider the two forms close or overlapping. The existence of both readings in one Qur’anic place also prevents an absolute wall from being built between them.

Therefore the difference between fathah and kasrah may create a semantic leaning in some uses, but it is not a decisive rule. In the Hafs reading, “walayah” in Al-Anfal highlights mutual bond and responsibility, and in Al-Kahf it indicates the true backing and undertaking of The God. The later meaning of wilayah as a formal office or political rule should not be imposed on these two verses without evidence.

أَوْلَىٰ

This indicates being nearer and, through that, being more entitled, more worthy, or having prior right. However, in phrases such as “أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰ” it has a threatening structure and should not be translated like an ordinary comparative noun.

الْأَوْلَيَانِ

“Al-awlayan” is the dual of “al-awla” and the dual form of a comparative noun. Its base meaning is “the two nearer and more entitled,” not “the first two people” in the sense of chronological order.

This form appears in Al-Maidah 5:107 in the context of testimony over a will. According to the structure of the verse, “al-awlayan” points to two people who, by kinship, right, or relation to the deceased and the matter of the will, are nearer and more entitled. Yet because of different readings and several grammatical possibilities in the verse, its exact syntactic role should be explained with caution. What is clear from the word itself is the nearness and prior right of the two, not their being first in time or number.

وَالٍ

This is the active participle from the base form and in the Qur’an is used about one who would undertake protection or care. This form should not be automatically equated with the later administrative meaning “governor of a province.”

مُوَلِّي

This is the active participle of Form II and points to one who turns toward a direction or makes another turn toward a direction. Its subject and pronoun reference must be examined from the structure of that verse itself.

Words such as “muwalah,” “wala,” “istila,” and “mustawli” are known in Arabic, but they are not Qur’anic forms of this root. Likewise, “wilayat” in modern Persian may mean an administrative region, government, or political theory; these meanings should not be imposed directly on the Qur’anic use of “wilayah.”

The Semantic Core of the Root

The core of the root W L Y is “nearness with continuity.” This nearness may be spatial, temporal, relational, social, supportive, legal, or directional. When something comes immediately after something else, it may be said to “yali” it; that is, it stands after it and near it. When someone stands beside another and takes up his matter, he becomes his wali. When a person attaches himself to a group, power, or direction, he does “tawalla.” When someone has a closer relation and a prior right, he is “awla.”

But these extensions should not be turned into an artificial definition. “Nearness” by itself is not equal to love, support, rule, obedience, or guardianship. These effects arise when the type of relation and the context confirm them. A wali may help, but “wali” and “nasir” are not one word, just as the Qur’an brings them side by side. A wali may be a holder of authority, but not every use of “wali” means ruler. A wali may be a friend, but not every friendship creates “wilayah” in the Qur’anic sense.

A cautious definition of the root is this:

W L Y indicates becoming near or being placed near within a bond; a bond that, according to context, may lead to succession, direction, companionship, support, undertaking, belonging, or priority.

The Material Image of the Root

The material image of this root may be seen in two things placed so near to one another that no foreign thing comes between them: someone sitting beside another, something that comes immediately after something else, or a movement continuing toward one direction.

In old Arabic, there is even mention of rain that comes after another rain and is named from this root. The importance of this witness is not the rain itself, but the image of “coming after” and “no gap falling between.”

The same image can explain the relational extension of the root. One who stands beside another may become his supporter; a close relative may undertake his responsibility; groups connected to each other may support one another; and one who turns toward a direction places that direction before himself.

Nevertheless, the material image is only a way to understand the root, not its complete definition. The divine, social, or legal uses of the root should not all be reduced to bodily nearness.

Use of the Root in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, this family of words was used for nearness, neighboring, kinship, joining, undertaking, and support. A wali could be one who stands beside a person or has taken up his matter. Mawla was used for several kinds of relation: kin, ally, neighbor, supporter, liberator, and liberated person.

This breadth does not mean ruleless dispersion. The common face of these uses is the existence of a near and recognized relation. The liberator and the liberated could both be called mawla, not because the word has two opposite meanings, but because both parties stand within a reciprocal relation and the exact position of each is specified by context.

From here it becomes clear that translating mawla without knowing the two sides of the relation is dangerous. In one verse the meaning of supporter may dominate, in another kin or holder of a matter, and in another a bond that no longer benefits either party.

Challenges of Understanding and Rendering

Restricting wali and awliya to friend

One of the most common restrictions is translating wali and awliya always as “friend.” Friendship can be one effect of nearness, but many verses speak of a stronger bond: support, alignment, acceptance of authority, mutual responsibility, or belonging to a camp.

This issue is especially important in verses such as Al-Maidah 5:51. If awliya is translated only as “friends,” the verse may be turned into a prohibition of every kind of friendship or good conduct with Jews and Christians, while Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 does not forbid kindness and justice toward those who did not fight and did not expel people from their homes. In the next verse, “tawallawhum” concerns those who fought, expelled, or supported expulsion. Therefore the meaning of bond and alignment must be understood from the real situation of each verse, not from one fixed rendering.

Turning “awliya of The God” into a religious class

In some later uses, “awliya of The God” became a special class with hierarchies, extraordinary miracles, or hidden powers. But after mentioning “awliya of The God,” the Qur’an identifies them by two qualities: having faith and having taqwa. The text of the verses does not speak of an inherited spiritual class or hidden organization.

Imposing later political meaning on wilayah

The word wilayah later entered legal, theological, and political systems. That semantic history may be studied separately, but it should not cause every Qur’anic use of W L Y to be automatically translated as political government, religious sovereignty, or formal office.

The reported difference between walayah with fathah and wilayah with kasrah should also not be made too absolute. Some sources distinguish between bond and support on one side and command or formal undertaking on the other, but some sources consider the two forms overlapping, and Qur’anic readings record both pronunciations in the same places. The vowel of the waw may therefore create a semantic leaning, but by itself it does not build a definite political or legal system.

Translating mawla uniformly

Mawla is a relational word. Translating it always as “master,” “lord,” “friend,” or “guardian” damages the meaning in some verses. Even in the Qur’an, mawla is used about The God, human beings, and the Fire. These uses show that the parties of the relation and its result must be examined separately in each context.

Treating all uses of tawalla as the same

Tawalla sometimes means accepting a bond and sometimes means turning away. This difference should not be solved by theorizing about the morphological form. The structure of the sentence is the guide: “tawalla fulanan” is not the same as “tawalla anhu,” and a use without an object must also be understood from surrounding sentences.

Methodological Note

In studying this root, four layers must be distinguished:

First, lexical nearness or continuity, which is the general core of the root.

Second, the type of relation, which may be spatial, relational, supportive, legal, social, or directional.

Third, the effect of the relation, such as help, protection, authority, responsibility, or companionship, which is obtained from context.

Fourth, the ethical judgment of the relation, which depends on the party of the bond and its direction.

If these layers are mixed together, the result will be that support, rule, friendship, or enmity is placed inside the definition of the root itself. The Qur’an does not confirm such uniformity. Wali can be positive or negative, and tawalla too can mean joining or turning away.

In divine uses, nearness must not be understood bodily. When The God is called al-wali or mawla, the discussion is not about place and bodily distance; the context speaks of undertaking, support, guidance, mercy, and lack of need for false awliya.

Qur’anic Uses of the Root

1. Direct Nearness

The clearest use of the root core is seen in “yalunakum”:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُمْ مِنَ الْكُفَّارِ وَلْيَجِدُوا فِيكُمْ غِلْظَةً وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُتَّقِينَ

O you who have faith, fight those of the coverers of truth who are nearest to you, and let them find firmness in you; and know that The God is with the people of taqwa.

At-Tawbah 9:123

In this verse, “yalunakum” points to being near in the surroundings of the addressees. Fighting, covering truth, and firmness come from other parts of the verse; none of them is inside the meaning of the root itself.

2. Turning Toward a Direction

Form II in verses about the qiblah expresses turning the face toward a direction:

قَدْ نَرَىٰ تَقَلُّبَ وَجْهِكَ فِي السَّمَاءِ فَلَنُوَلِّيَنَّكَ قِبْلَةً تَرْضَاهَا فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

We see the turning of your face in the sky; so We shall surely turn you to a qiblah that you are pleased with. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram.

Al-Baqarah 2:144

“Fawalli wajhaka” means turn your face toward that side. The sanctity of the mosque, the Messenger’s pleasure, and the ruling of qiblah come from the context; “walla” itself indicates turning and directing.

In contrast, the same form can express turning the back and retreat:

ثُمَّ وَلَّيْتُمْ مُدْبِرِينَ

Then you turned away with your backs.

At-Tawbah 9:25

“Midbirin” clarifies that the direction of movement was backward and with the back turned. Therefore the negative meaning of flight is not obtained from the root alone; the complete structure of the phrase specifies it.

3. Tawalla an: Turning Away and Distancing

وَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ

And he turned away from them and said: O my grief over Yusuf.

Yusuf 12:84

Here “tawalla anhum” means he turned away from them, but this act did not arise from covering truth or denial; the grief of Yaqub shapes its context. This verse prevents every “tawalla” from automatically being taken as rebellion or kufr.

In many other verses, turning away from a message or command appears in a blameworthy context:

وَلَٰكِنْ كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ

But he denied and turned away.

Al-Qiyamah 75:32

In this verse, “kadhdhaba” and “tawalla” are two separate acts: one is denial and the other is turning away. Tawalla by itself does not mean denial.

4. Tawalla With a Direct Object: Accepting a Bond

وَمَنْ يَتَوَلَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا فَإِنَّ حِزْبَ اللَّهِ هُمُ الْغَالِبُونَ

And whoever takes The God, His Messenger, and those who have faith as his wali, then the party of The God, they are the victorious.

Al-Maidah 5:56

Here “yatawalla” comes with an object and does not mean turning away. The discussion is about accepting a bond, direction, and companionship. Victory too is a result expressed by the continuation of the verse and is not inside the lexical meaning of tawalla.

5. Wali as Undertaker in a Human Relation

In the debt verse, the wali is the one who undertakes just dictation in place of a person who is unable:

فَإِنْ كَانَ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ الْحَقُّ سَفِيهًا أَوْ ضَعِيفًا أَوْ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُ أَنْ يُمِلَّ هُوَ فَلْيُمْلِلْ وَلِيُّهُ بِالْعَدْلِ

So if the one upon whom the right rests is foolish, weak, or unable to dictate himself, then his wali should dictate with justice.

Al-Baqarah 2:282

In this verse, wali is not simply a friend. His nearness is joined with responsibility and just representation. Yet justice comes from “bil-adl”; the word wali itself does not guarantee that every wali will necessarily act justly.

In the verse of qisas, the wali of the slain person stands in the position of holder and pursuer of right:

وَمَنْ قُتِلَ مَظْلُومًا فَقَدْ جَعَلْنَا لِوَلِيِّهِ سُلْطَانًا فَلَا يُسْرِفْ فِي الْقَتْلِ

And whoever is killed unjustly, We have given authority to his wali; so he must not exceed the limit in killing.

Al-Isra 17:33

The legal authority in this verse is understood from “We have given him sultan.” Wali is the near and responsible party in the relation; the limit of authority is set by the continuation of the verse.

6. Mutual Social Wilayah

وَالْمُؤْمِنُونَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتُ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَاءُ بَعْضٍ يَأْمُرُونَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَيَنْهَوْنَ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ

Believing men and believing women are awliya of one another; they command what is recognized and restrain from what is denied.

At-Tawbah 9:71

The structure “some of them are awliya of others” does not show a one-way or hierarchical relation. Believing men and women stand in a reciprocal bond. The verse then states some effects of this bond, but all those effects should not be packed into a one-word translation of awliya.

In Al-Anfal, this bond is connected with hijrah, shelter, support, and covenant:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَهَاجَرُوا وَجَاهَدُوا بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنْفُسِهِمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَالَّذِينَ آوَوْا وَنَصَرُوا أُولَٰئِكَ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَاءُ بَعْضٍ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَلَمْ يُهَاجِرُوا مَا لَكُمْ مِنْ وَلَايَتِهِمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ حَتَّىٰ يُهَاجِرُوا

Those who have faith, migrated, and strove with their wealth and selves in the path of The God, and those who sheltered and supported, those are awliya of one another. And those who had faith but did not migrate, you have nothing of their wilayah until they migrate.

Al-Anfal 8:72

In the Hafs reading, “walayatihim” is read with fathah on the waw. This wilayah in the context of the verse is joined to social structure, shelter, support, migration, and mutual responsibility. The verse even limits the obligation of support in view of an existing covenant. Therefore wilayah here is neither an inner feeling of friendship nor a title of formal government; it is a practical and responsibility-bearing relation.

In the reading where the waw is read with kasrah, the aspect of undertaking and responsibility that the community has toward its members can be seen more strongly. Yet since both readings occur in the same context, no absolute wall should be erected between supportive bond and undertaking.

7. The Boundary Between Kindness and Wilayah in a State of Hostility

لَا يَنْهَاكُمُ اللَّهُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَلَمْ يُخْرِجُوكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ أَنْ تَبَرُّوهُمْ وَتُقْسِطُوا إِلَيْهِمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

The God does not forbid you from kindness and justice toward those who did not fight you in religion and did not expel you from your homes; indeed, The God loves those who act with justice.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:8

إِنَّمَا يَنْهَاكُمُ اللَّهُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ قَاتَلُوكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَأَخْرَجُوكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ وَظَاهَرُوا عَلَىٰ إِخْرَاجِكُمْ أَنْ تَوَلَّوْهُمْ وَمَنْ يَتَوَلَّهُمْ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

The God only forbids you from taking as wali those who fought you in religion, expelled you from your homes, and supported your expulsion; and whoever takes them as wali, those are the wrongdoers.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:9

The contrast between these two verses shows that “tawallawhum” is not equal to every human relation, kindness, or justice. The prohibition concerns a bond with a party that is in a state of fighting, expulsion, and support for expulsion. This context does not permit “taking as wali” to be reduced without qualification to “becoming friends.”

8. Bond With Taghut and Satan

اللَّهُ وَلِيُّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا يُخْرِجُهُمْ مِنَ الظُّلُمَاتِ إِلَى النُّورِ وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَوْلِيَاؤُهُمُ الطَّاغُوتُ يُخْرِجُونَهُمْ مِنَ النُّورِ إِلَى الظُّلُمَاتِ

The God is the wali of those who have faith; He brings them out from darknesses into light. And those who cover truth, their awliya are taghut; they bring them out from light into darknesses.

Al-Baqarah 2:257

The verse clarifies the ethical direction of wilayah from the two sides of the relation. The root itself is neither light nor darkness. The difference lies in the wali that is chosen and the path to which this bond leads.

9. Awliya of The God

أَلَا إِنَّ أَوْلِيَاءَ اللَّهِ لَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ ۝ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَكَانُوا يَتَّقُونَ

Be aware: upon the awliya of The God there is no fear, nor do they grieve; those who had faith and had taqwa.

Yunus 10:62–63

The next verse explains “awliya of The God” from within the Qur’an itself. This title belongs to those who had faith and taqwa. The verse does not speak of lineage, office, clothing, a clerical class, or extraordinary power.

10. Awla and Al-Awlayan: Nearness and Prior Right

إِنَّ أَوْلَى النَّاسِ بِإِبْرَاهِيمَ لَلَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ وَهَٰذَا النَّبِيُّ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا

The nearest and most entitled of people to Ibrahim are those who followed him, and this Prophet and those who have faith.

Al Imran 3:68

In this verse, nearness to Ibrahim is not by lineage or slogan; following is introduced as the criterion of being awla.

النَّبِيُّ أَوْلَىٰ بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ مِنْ أَنْفُسِهِمْ

The Prophet is nearer and has prior right in relation to the believers than their own selves.

Al-Ahzab 33:6

Here “awla bi” conveys a nearer relation and prior right. Determining all limits of this right is not possible from the word awla itself and must be studied in the whole structure of the Qur’an.

The dual form of the same comparative noun appears in the verse of the will:

فَإِنْ عُثِرَ عَلَىٰ أَنَّهُمَا اسْتَحَقَّا إِثْمًا فَآخَرَانِ يَقُومَانِ مَقَامَهُمَا مِنَ الَّذِينَ اسْتَحَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ الْأَوْلَيَانِ فَيُقْسِمَانِ بِاللَّهِ لَشَهَادَتُنَا أَحَقُّ مِنْ شَهَادَتِهِمَا وَمَا اعْتَدَيْنَا إِنَّا إِذًا لَمِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

Then if it is discovered that those two deserved sin, two others shall stand in their place from among those whose right was violated, namely the two nearer and more entitled; then they shall swear by The God: our testimony is more rightful than the testimony of those two, and we have not transgressed; indeed, then we would be among the wrongdoers.

Al-Maidah 5:107

“Al-awlayan” does not mean “the first two people” in chronological order. It indicates two who, by kinship, right, or relation to the deceased and the will, are nearer and have prior right. Because of differences in readings and grammatical analyses of the verse, the precise syntactic position of this word has been disputed; therefore beyond this clear core, one grammatical analysis should not be made absolute without reason.

But in the following phrase, “awla” appears in a fixed threatening structure:

أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰ ۝ ثُمَّ أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰ

Woe to you, then woe to you; again woe to you, then woe to you.

Al-Qiyamah 75:34–35

In this phrase, the nearness of threat and an unpleasant consequence remains in the background of the word, but the natural English translation is “woe to you.” It should not be translated like the ordinary use “nearer” or “more entitled.”

11. Mawla and the Breadth of Relation

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّ اللَّهَ مَوْلَى الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَأَنَّ الْكَافِرِينَ لَا مَوْلَىٰ لَهُمْ

That is because The God is the mawla of those who have faith, and the coverers of truth have no mawla.

Muhammad 47:11

In this verse, the context speaks of support and the outcome of the two groups. Mawla can be explained as “supporter and undertaker,” but the base translation should not restrict the whole field of the word to “helper.”

يَوْمَ لَا يُغْنِي مَوْلًى عَنْ مَوْلًى شَيْئًا وَلَا هُمْ يُنْصَرُونَ

A day when no mawla will avail another mawla in anything, and they will not be helped.

Ad-Dukhan 44:41

The contrast between mawla and “they will be helped” shows that mawla and nasir are not identical. Mawla here is the near party in a relation, but on that day this nearness will not benefit the other.

مَأْوَاكُمُ النَّارُ هِيَ مَوْلَاكُمْ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

Your refuge is the Fire; it is your mawla, and miserable is the destination.

Al-Hadid 57:15

Translating mawla as “friend,” “helper,” or “guardian” in this verse is inadequate. The context speaks of a fire that has become their attached abode and bound outcome. It is better in the base translation to preserve the word mawla and explain the relation separately.

12. Mawali: Kin and Bonded Dependents

وَلِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مَوَالِيَ مِمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ

For everyone We have made mawali regarding what parents and near relatives left behind.

An-Nisa 4:33

The context of inheritance brings mawali in this verse close to those who, through kinship or a recognized relation, are connected to what has been left behind. The word alone does not determine all details of the inheritance system.

وَإِنِّي خِفْتُ الْمَوَالِيَ مِنْ وَرَائِي

And I fear the mawali after me.

Maryam 19:5

In the speech of Zakariyya, mawali points to close relatives or successors who remain after him. His concern is understood from the context of the prayer, not from the word itself.

فَإِنْ لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا آبَاءَهُمْ فَإِخْوَانُكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَمَوَالِيكُمْ

If you do not know their fathers, then they are your brothers in religion and your mawali.

Al-Ahzab 33:5

Here mawali comes beside religious brotherhood and points to a recognized social bond that does not replace real lineage. Translation must preserve the real relation intended by the verse, not create a new lineage.

13. Walayah and Wilayah: The Relation and the Undertaking

هُنَالِكَ الْوَلَايَةُ لِلَّهِ الْحَقِّ هُوَ خَيْرٌ ثَوَابًا وَخَيْرٌ عُقْبًا

There, the wilayah belongs to The God, The Truth; He is best in reward and best in outcome.

Al-Kahf 18:44

In the Hafs reading, “al-walayah” is read with fathah on the waw. In the story of the two gardens, reliance on wealth, people, and visible power collapses, and the true backing remains only with The God.

In some other readings, the word is read with kasrah on the waw. This reading can highlight the aspect of authority, ownership, and undertaking more strongly. Yet both readings stand in one context and do not negate one another: true bond and support belong to the One to whom true management and authority also belong.

Better reward and outcome come from the continuation of the verse and should not be inserted into the translation of wilayah itself.

14. Wal: A Protective Undertaker

وَإِذَا أَرَادَ اللَّهُ بِقَوْمٍ سُوءًا فَلَا مَرَدَّ لَهُ وَمَا لَهُمْ مِنْ دُونِهِ مِنْ وَالٍ

And when The God intends hardship for a people, there is no repelling it, and apart from Him they have no protective undertaker.

Ar-Rad 13:11

Wal in this verse is someone who could take up their matter and protect them against what has arrived. The meaning “governor” or “administrative wali” is not obtained from this verse.

Examination of Important Qur’anic Derivatives

Wali and Awliya

Wali is the singular form and awliya is its plural. These two forms in the Qur’an cover a range from individual responsibility to group bond. The wali of the unable person in the debt verse, the wali of the slain person in the qisas verse, the awliya of al-Masjid al-Haram, the mutual awliya of believers, and the awliya of taghut all arise from one relational core, but the type of relation differs in each.

Therefore it is not recommended to render wali in every verse with one fixed word. Sometimes “undertaker,” sometimes “supporter,” sometimes “near holder of right,” sometimes “ally,” and sometimes the term “wali” itself is more suitable.

Mawla

Mawla is even more sensitive than wali to the two-sidedness of the relation. Without knowing the parties, one cannot know whether the discussion is about holder of a matter, dependent, kin, supporter, or attached companion. For this reason, preserving mawla in the base translation and explaining it in the next sentence is more precise in some verses than choosing one narrow equivalent.

Tawalla

Two main paths of this verb must remain separate:

  • “tawalla anhu”: he turned away from him or distanced himself.
  • “tawallahu”: he took him as the party of his bond and wilayah, or undertook his matter.

One should not conclude from Form V itself that this verb always conveys voluntary acceptance, intensity, repetition, or a specific inner state. Such features must be confirmed by context.

Awla and Al-Awlayan

“Awla bi” usually moves from nearness to priority and entitlement. Yet the type of priority may be relational, legal, religious, or connected to destiny. “Awla laka” is also a special threatening structure and must be understood separately.

“Al-awlayan” is the dual of this comparative noun and means “the two nearer and more entitled.” This form should not be confused with “al-awwalan,” meaning “the first two.” In Al-Maidah 5:107, the nearness and entitlement of the two are connected to right, kinship, and the matter of the will, although the exact grammatical role of the word requires caution because of differences in readings and analyses.

Walayah and Wilayah

In some lexical sources, the form with fathah is explained more as the verbal noun of bond, support, and muwalah, while the form with kasrah is connected more with command, authority, or something one undertakes. In other sources, the two forms are considered two close pronunciations or semantically overlapping.

Qur’anic readings too do not allow this distinction to be made an absolute law. Therefore, in every verse the context should be examined first, and then, if needed, the difference of readings may be explained as a semantic capacity.

Al-Wali and Mawla in Divine Descriptions

The Qur’an calls The God both al-wali and mawla:

أَمِ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِهِ أَوْلِيَاءَ فَاللَّهُ هُوَ الْوَلِيُّ وَهُوَ يُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Or have they taken awliya apart from Him? Then The God, He is the wali; and He gives life to the dead, and He has power over all things.

Ash-Shuraa 42:9

وَهُوَ الَّذِي يُنَزِّلُ الْغَيْثَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا قَنَطُوا وَيَنْشُرُ رَحْمَتَهُ وَهُوَ الْوَلِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ

And He is the One who sends down the rain after they have despaired and spreads His mercy; and He is the wali, the praised.

Ash-Shuraa 42:28

Al-wali in these verses is a definite description. The structure “fa-The God, He is the wali” emphasizes the true and decisive wilayah of The God in contrast to the awliya taken besides Him. This wilayah is joined in context to giving life to the dead, power, sending rain, and spreading mercy.

The core of nearness in this use is not bodily. The God does not stand in a place beside creation and does not use human tools for support. His wilayah is complete undertaking, support, guidance, and bringing matters to the outcome that lies within His knowledge and power.

The use of the same root for human beings and for The God does not mean that the level of relation is the same. A human being becomes another’s wali only within the limits of his ability, knowledge, and responsibility, while the wilayah of The God is not dependent, limited, or in need of another supporter.

Mawla is also used about Him:

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِاللَّهِ هُوَ مَوْلَاكُمْ فَنِعْمَ الْمَوْلَىٰ وَنِعْمَ النَّصِيرُ

Hold fast to The God; He is your mawla. Excellent is the mawla, and excellent is the helper.

Al-Hajj 22:78

The coming of al-mawla and an-nasir beside one another shows that these two are not synonyms. Mawla expresses the relation of undertaking and bond, while nasir emphasizes help. Help is one of the effects of divine wilayah, not its entire definition.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

The Role of “an” in Tawalla an

The preposition “an” makes distancing from a starting point visible. “Tawalla anhum” means he turned away from them or moved away. The “an” should not be removed and the verb understood like “tawallahu.”

Direct Object in Tawallahu

When the verb directly takes someone as object, as in “wa man yatawallahum,” the meaning of accepting a bond, aligning, or taking as wali becomes prominent. The exact type of this bond is determined by context.

Awla bi

Awla often comes with “bi” and connects priority or entitlement to a party: “awla bi-Ibrahim” or “awla bil-muminin.” This ba specifies the field in which priority is measured, but by itself it does not determine the type and limits of that priority.

The Structure of Al-Awlayan

Al-awlayan is the dual of al-awla. The final alif of al-awla returns to ya when dualized, and the form al-awlayan is made. Morphologically, this word is a dual comparative noun and means “the two nearer and more entitled.”

In Al-Maidah 5:107, several possibilities have been raised for its syntactic position, including subject, apposition, or placement in a subject-predicate structure. Difference in the reading of the verb “istahaqqa” also affects the analysis of the sentence. Therefore the base meaning of the word is clear, but one grammatical analysis should not be presented without reason as the only possible one.

Vowel Difference Between Walayah and Wilayah

Fathah or kasrah on the waw has been explained in some sources by a difference between the verbal noun of relation and support, and the noun of office or command. Yet lexical sources and Qur’anic readings are not uniform in this distinction. The vowel of the waw may be one clue for understanding, but it cannot by itself make the meaning of bond, rule, authority, or love definite.

Min Dun

In phrases such as “مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ مِن وَلِيّ,” the structure “min dun” shows the position of awliya taken in contrast to The God. Dun here is not merely spatial lower-ness; the context speaks of reliance and seeking refuge independently of or in rivalry with The God.

Wali Beside Nasir

The repeated structure “la wali wa la nasir” shows that wali and nasir are not synonyms. Wali is the near party and undertaker of the relation; nasir is one who helps. In some contexts one person can be both, but the two words highlight two different angles.

The Weak-Root Structure

Because the final letter of the root is ya, this letter appears in different forms in conjugation. In “walla,” “tawalla,” “mawla,” and “awla” it appears as alif maqsurah, but in forms such as “waliyyuh,” “yatawalla,” and plurals, its relation to ya becomes clear again. This orthographic change should not cause these words to be attributed to separate roots.

Comparison With Nearby Roots

W L Y Compared With Q R B: Wilayah and Nearness

Q R B expresses the general field of being near and coming near. W L Y, in addition to nearness, often highlights continuity, following after, or the formation of a close relation. Yet this difference is a tendency, not an absolute rule: one should not make every qurb without relation and every wilayah a legal responsibility.

W L Y Compared With N S R: Wilayah and Support

N S R focuses on helping and giving support against an obstacle or enemy. W L Y focuses on the near relation and the place of one party in relation to another. A wali may give nusrah, but every nasir is not necessarily a wali, and every use of wali does not point to a specific act of help.

W L Y Compared With S H B: Wilayah and Companionship

S H B indicates being with and companionship. Companionship may be short or long, positive or negative, and does not necessarily create supportive commitment or undertaking. W L Y in many contexts shows a denser and more directed relation, but not every deep companionship should automatically be called wilayah.

W L Y Compared With H F Z: Wilayah and Protection

H F Z focuses on preserving, guarding, and preventing loss. Protection may be one effect of wilayah, but wali does not lexically mean hafiz. In divine descriptions too, protection is part of the field of divine wilayah, not all of it.

W L Y Compared With A D W: Wali and Enemy

In Arabic, wali sometimes stands opposite aduww because wali is the near and connected party, while aduww is the transgressing party or the one distanced from this bond. Yet W L Y does not in all uses mean “friend,” and A D W does not in all uses mean merely a personal enemy. The exact contrast must be taken from the context.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For يَلِي:

  • being near
  • being in the closest surrounding area
  • being immediately after something, if the context is temporal

For وَلَّى وجهه:

  • he turned his face toward something
  • he faced that direction

For وَلَّى مدبرا:

  • he turned away with his back
  • he retreated
  • he turned his back, depending on context

For تَوَلَّى عنه:

  • he turned away from him
  • he distanced himself from him
  • he left him, only if the context confirms leaving

For تَوَلَّاه:

  • he took him as his wali
  • he established a supportive bond with him
  • he joined him or aligned with him
  • he took up his matter, if the context is undertaking

For وَلِيّ:

  • wali, with brief explanation
  • undertaker
  • near supporter
  • near holder of right
  • ally, in a context of covenant and alignment
  • guardian, only where responsibility and authority are clear

For أَوْلِيَاء:

  • awliya, with explanation
  • parties of a supportive bond
  • allies
  • near supporters
  • undertakers, in a context of responsibility

“Friends” should be used only in a context where friendship is truly the dominant meaning.

For مَوْلَىٰ:

  • mawla, with contextual explanation
  • supporter and undertaker
  • holder of the matter
  • kin or near party of relation
  • attached companion and outcome, in a verse such as Al-Hadid 57:15

For مَوَالِي:

  • mawali, with explanation
  • near kin
  • bonded dependents
  • heirs or successors, if the context clarifies it

For وَلَايَة and وِلَايَة:

  • wilayah, with explanation
  • supportive bond
  • mutual responsibility
  • undertaking
  • backing and authority, depending on context
  • command or rule, only in a use where context, reading, and sentence structure confirm it

For أَوْلَىٰ:

  • nearer
  • more entitled
  • having prior right
  • more fitting, if the context confirms fittingness

In “awla laka fa-awla,” the natural translation “woe to you, then woe to you” is more suitable, and the background of the nearness of threat may come in separate explanation.

For الْأَوْلَيَانِ:

  • the two nearer and more entitled
  • the two holders of prior right
  • the two nearer kin, only when the context confirms kinship

This form should not be translated as “the first two people.”

For وَالٍ:

  • protective undertaker
  • protector
  • one who takes up another’s matter

The translation “governor” is suitable only in a clear administrative use, not in Ar-Rad 13:11.

Conclusion

The root W L Y indicates a nearness and continuity that can be established between persons, groups, directions, responsibilities, and levels of right. Its base image is the placement of two things beside or after one another; but this image is only an entrance into understanding and should not make all social, legal, or divine uses of the root bodily.

This root does not inherently mean friendship, rule, support, or guardianship. These meanings arise in some contexts from the type of relation. Wali may be a supporter, holder of right, or undertaker; awliya may be parties of bond and alignment; mawla may be one of the two sides of a near relation; and wilayah may indicate mutual responsibility, backing, or undertaking.

Tawalla is a clear example of the need to attend to sentence structure: with a direct object it may convey accepting a bond, and with “an” it may mean turning away. Awla too moves from nearness to priority and entitlement, while al-awlayan is its dual and means two nearer people with prior right, not two first people in chronological order.

The difference between walayah with fathah and wilayah with kasrah may in some contexts show a leaning between bond and support on one side and command or undertaking on the other; but lexical sources and Qur’anic readings do not allow this difference to become an absolute boundary. The context of the verse remains the main criterion for understanding.

The use of the root for The God is not bodily. His being al-wali and mawla indicates undertaking, support, guidance, and backing that do not have the limitation and need of created beings. Human use of the same root stands on a limited level dependent on human ability, knowledge, justice, and responsibility.

Accurate translation of this word family is possible only when the core of nearness is preserved, while the type of bond, its effect, and its ethical judgment are obtained separately from the context of each verse.
