Tilting the neck and face: from a bodily condition to turning away with arrogance and contempt for others

The Arabic root Ṣ ʿ R concerns crookedness, inclination, and turning the face or neck to one side. This may result from illness or a natural bodily characteristic; alternatively, someone may deliberately turn away from another person to express disregard, conceit, or contempt.

In this family, ṣaʿar denotes crookedness of the neck or face, aṣʿar describes a person or animal with that condition, and taṣʿīr denotes turning the face to one side.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as tuṣaʿʿir in Luqmān 31:18.

Here Luqmān advises his son not to tilt his face before people out of conceit. This behavior appears alongside an arrogant manner of walking and condemnation of vanity and boasting.

The crucial distinction is that the lexical meaning of tilting the face is not identical to the moral meaning of arrogance. A crooked neck may be involuntary and physical; the Qur’anic use, however, concerns behavior expressing contempt and superiority toward others.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one Form II occurrence

1. The semantic core: inclination and crookedness

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces this family’s basic meaning as follows:

أَصْلٌ مُطَّرِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مَيَلٍ فِي الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun muṭṭaridun yadullu ʿalā mayalin fī al-shayʾi

That is, the root denotes crookedness or inclination in something.

He then explains ṣaʿar as crookedness of the neck and taṣʿīr as turning the cheek aside out of conceit.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise gives several related definitions for al-ṣaʿar: crookedness of the face, inclination of the cheek to one side, and twisting the neck so that the face turns to either side.

These definitions show that the root’s main branch concerns a change in the position or direction of the head, neck, and face.

The cause of this change is not the same in every use.

Someone may have a crooked neck because of a bodily condition or may deliberately turn the head and face aside.

In the first case, crookedness is a bodily characteristic or condition. In the second, the movement may express a stance or social response.

This makes the root’s behavioral use understandable.

A tilted neck or face in people and animals

Arabic sources use ṣaʿira for a person or animal whose neck or face is inclined to one side.

Aṣʿar is the masculine adjective and ṣaʿrāʾ its feminine form.

The condition may be natural in some animals and caused by illness in others.

Ibn Fāris explicitly states that a human being and a male ostrich, called ẓalīm by the Arabs, may naturally be aṣʿar.

Crookedness of the face or neck is therefore not independently a sign of arrogance.

The distinction matters because the word’s physical meaning is broader than a moral judgment about human behavior.

Ṣaʿar: a condition that twists a camel’s neck

Lexical sources also record ṣaʿar as the name of a condition causing a camel’s neck to bend to one side.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes an illness that twists the camel’s neck and tilts its head aside.

Arabic uses aṣʿar for a camel affected by this condition.

This use denotes an actual bodily condition.

Early lexicographers and commentators also refer to it when explaining the verse in Luqmān: someone arrogant may turn away in a manner resembling the animal’s twisted neck.

The resemblance should not be equated with the illness itself.

The Qur’an is not condemning sick people or those with naturally crooked necks; it prohibits deliberate behavior toward other people.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: neck inclination, bodily condition, and behavior

2. From bodily crookedness to arrogant behavior

Ṣaʿʿara khaddahu: turning the face aside arrogantly

Arabic records the expression:

صَعَّرَ خَدَّهُ

ṣaʿʿara khaddahu

It means inclining one’s cheek to one side.

In its behavioral use, it describes someone who turns away from others with arrogance and contempt.

Khadd denotes the cheek or side of the face.

Ṣaʿʿara khaddahu thus pictures someone who turns his face away instead of facing the person addressing him.

The bodily movement can carry a social message: the other person is considered unworthy of attention or conversation.

This message follows from the expression’s established use in human relationships.

The neck’s movement alone, without knowledge of the situation and intention, does not establish arrogance.

Study of KH D D

The difference between ṣaʿʿara and ṣāʿara

Arabic records both ṣaʿʿara khaddahu and ṣāʿara khaddahu.

The first is Form II, the second Form III.

In their use for arrogant behavior, both concern inclining the face and turning away from another person.

Form III alone does not mean that two people simultaneously turn away from one another. This construction can describe one person’s behavior toward someone else.

Both forms are also known in Qur’anic readings.

The morphological difference should therefore not be turned into two wholly distinct moral meanings.

Why does tilting the face become a sign of arrogance?

In human communication, the face is among the most visible ways of showing attention, disregard, and response.

Someone speaking with another person may show attention by facing them.

Conversely, turning away contemptuously can express the view that the other person is unworthy of attention.

In this use, tilting the cheek becomes a sign of conceit.

This social explanation must not become an absolute rule.

During conversation, people may not look at the other person’s face because of pain, bodily discomfort, another task, behavioral differences, or many other reasons.

In such cases, the face’s direction alone cannot establish arrogance.

What matters in the condemned use of taṣʿīr is turning away with contempt and a sense of superiority.

3. Early Arabic witnesses and uses before the Qur’an

A poetic witness attributed to Al-Mutalammis

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī says:

وَكُنَّا إِذَا الْجَبَّارُ صَعَّرَ خَدَّهُ
أَقَمْنَا لَهُ مِنْ مَيْلِهِ فَتَقَوَّمَا

wa-kunnā idhā al-jabbāru ṣaʿʿara khaddahu / aqamnā lahu min maylihi fa-taqawwamā

Translation: “Whenever a mighty tyrant tilted his cheek arrogantly, we straightened his crookedness until he became straight.”

Ṣaʿʿara khaddahu here describes a powerful, arrogant person.

The poet uses tilting the cheek as a sign of conceit, then pictures that same crookedness being straightened.

The contrast between bending and straightening shows that poetry used the root’s physical image to express social and moral behavior.

Attribution to Al-Mutalammis is not, however, the only transmission.

In Muʿjam al-Shuʿarāʾ, Al-Marzubānī quotes it from a poem attributed to ʿAmr ibn Ḥunayy al-Taghlibī and notes that some also attribute it to Al-Mutalammis or Jābir ibn Ḥunayy.

Different endings are also recorded, including fa-taqawwamā and fa-taqawwami.

The line is therefore a useful witness to the early expression ṣaʿʿara khaddahu, while attribution to one particular poet should remain cautious.

The disputed attribution does not change the relevant meaning: tilting the cheek here expresses arrogant behavior.

Al-Marzubānī’s Muʿjam al-Shuʿarāʾ: disputed attribution and wording

Camels and the inclination of the neck

The ṣuʿr in the following witness describes she-camels, not ostriches. This should be distinguished from the lexicographers’ separate description of the male ostrich as naturally aṣʿar.

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī describes she-camels inclining toward the roar of a male camel, the fanīq:

فَهُنَّ صُعْرٌ إِلَى هَدْرِ الْفَنِيقِ

fa-hunna ṣuʿrun ilā hadri al-fanīqi

Translation: “They incline their necks toward the male camel’s roar.”

Ṣuʿr here concerns inclination and turning toward something.

Lexical explanation relates the preposition ilā to a meaning close to māʾilāt or mawāʾil: those inclining or turning toward something.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Dhuʾayb’s witness and inclination

The witness shows that the root can describe an animal’s natural orientation or movement.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without establishing when this line was composed, it should not be treated as a definitely pre-Qur’anic witness.

The description of the she-camels also carries no moral condemnation.

This distinction further clarifies the boundary between physical inclination and the moral use of taṣʿīr in human behavior.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: fanīq, a prized male camel

Ṣayʿariyyah: a mark on a camel’s neck

Arabic also records al-ṣayʿariyyah for a kind of mark or brand on a camel’s neck.

A poem attributed to Al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas says:

وَقَدْ أَتَنَاسَى الْهَمَّ عِنْدَ احْتِضَارِهِ
بِنَاجٍ عَلَيْهِ الصَّيْعَرِيَّةُ مُكْدَمِ

wa-qad atanāsā al-hamma ʿinda iḥtiḍārihi / bi-nājin ʿalayhi al-ṣayʿariyyatu mukdami

A rendering close to the sense: “At times, when care approaches, I forget it with a swift, sturdy camel bearing the ṣayʿariyyah mark upon its neck.” Here mukdam is understood as sturdy or strong; it should not be translated as a bridle mark.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: mukdam, sturdy and strong

Ṣayʿariyyah here names a mark, neither the neck’s illness nor arrogant behavior.

Lexical sources disagree over whether this mark belonged exclusively to female camels or was also placed on males.

An anecdote also recounts Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd objecting to this word’s use for a male camel.

These disagreements show the mark’s particular use within the language of camel husbandry.

Ibn Fāris treats its connection with the neck’s inclination as a possibility, but this does not establish the name’s historical origin.

Ṣayʿariyyah may therefore be mentioned in studying this family, but its meaning must not be transferred to the Qur’anic verse.

Early lexicons: ṣayʿariyyah and disagreement about the camel brand

4. The Qur’anic use: Luqmān 31:18

وَلَا تُصَعِّرْ خَدَّكَ لِلنَّاسِ وَلَا تَمْشِ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَحًا ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūrin

Translation: Do not tilt your cheek before people, and do not walk upon the earth exultantly. Surely The God does not love any conceited boaster.

The verse belongs to Luqmān’s counsel to his son.

Luqmān 31:17 concerns establishing ṣalāh, enjoining what is right, forbidding what is wrong, and steadfastness amid what befalls a person.

Luqmān 31:18 then addresses conduct toward others and one’s manner of walking.

Luqmān 31:19 concerns moderation in walking and lowering one’s voice.

Alongside social and moral responsibilities, these three verses thus attend to how people behave and present themselves among others.

The meaning of wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi

Tuṣaʿʿir here denotes tilting or turning the cheek aside.

Khaddaka is the verb’s object, specifying the addressee’s cheek or side of the face.

Li-al-nāsi identifies the people before whom this behavior occurs.

The sentence’s direct image is therefore that a person should not tilt the cheek when encountering others in a way expressing disregard and superiority.

In early lexical explanation, Abū ʿUbaydah understands taṣʿīr as turning the face away from people out of arrogance.

Al-Ṭabarī adopts this meaning and transmits early commentators’ accounts describing someone who twists his face while another person speaks to him, treating the speaker with contempt.

Here bodily movement and its social message are connected.

Al-Ṭabarī: contemptuous turning away and the verb’s two readings

Does tuṣaʿʿir itself mean arrogance?

In references to human behavior, taṣʿīr may be performed arrogantly and acquire a contemptuous meaning in this combination.

The root’s general core, however, is crookedness and inclination.

The verse’s concluding expression:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūrin

clearly shows that vanity and boasting are the objects of condemnation.

The moral meaning emerges from the established expression ṣaʿʿara khaddahu and its context, not because every kind of neck crookedness means arrogance in Arabic.

This distinction prevents an involuntary bodily condition from being equated with condemned moral behavior.

Tilting the cheek versus ordinary turning away

Turning the face away is not contemptuous in every circumstance.

Someone may change direction to do a task, attend to danger, respect privacy, or for other reasons.

In its condemned social use, however, ṣaʿʿara khaddahu describes belittling another person by turning away.

The bodily behavior then expresses an inward or social stance.

The Qur’anic prohibition should therefore not become a command requiring the face or gaze to remain in one fixed direction at all times.

The point is to abandon contemptuous behavior and self-superiority.

Wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan

The second part prohibits walking in an exultant, ostentatious manner.

Maraḥan comes from M R Ḥ and here concerns exultance and showing off.

This part relates to the first prohibition but does not merely repeat the same behavior.

The first concerns how people encounter and treat others.

The second concerns movement and walking among them.

Both can outwardly display conceit, but their wording and actions differ.

Tuṣaʿʿir should therefore not be translated as walking arrogantly, since the second part expresses that meaning in its own words.

Mukhtālin fakhūrin

The verse ends with two descriptions:

Mukhtāl and fakhūr.

Mukhtāl comes from KH Y L and here concerns vanity and an inflated view of oneself.

Fakhūr comes from F KH R and denotes boasting and vaunting oneself before others.

These descriptions clarify the moral setting of the preceding prohibitions.

People may inwardly consider themselves superior and reveal this assumption through behavior, speech, looks, or walking.

These descriptions are not part of the lexical formation of Ṣ ʿ R itself.

Their relation to taṣʿīr arises within the verse’s context.

Connections with Luqmān’s preceding and following counsel

Luqmān 31:17 concerns responsibility toward what is right and wrong.

Luqmān 31:18 prohibits contempt for others and showing off.

Luqmān 31:19 addresses moderation in walking and lowering the voice.

The sequence shows that proper social behavior is not limited to having something true to say.

How one treats others also matters.

Someone may speak about truth while accompanying it with contempt, arrogance, and disregard for the listener.

The possible correctness of the content does not then justify self-superiority in conduct.

Conversely, avoiding arrogance does not mean silence before error or abandoning moral responsibility: the preceding verse enjoins what is right and forbids what is wrong.

The counsel therefore brings together steadfast responsibility and humility in dealing with others.

This message follows from the verses’ connections and should not be presented as an independent definition of Ṣ ʿ R.

5. Morphology and other forms of the root

Ṣaʿira and ṣaʿar

In bodily uses, ṣaʿira denotes the neck or face becoming crooked.

Ṣaʿar names that crookedness or condition.

Some instances result from illness; others may be natural.

These two forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Aṣʿar and ṣaʿrāʾ

Aṣʿar is the masculine description of a person or animal with a crooked neck or face.

Ṣaʿrāʾ is its feminine form.

Some behavioral uses also apply aṣʿar to an arrogant person who turns the cheek away from others.

Physical and moral meanings must nevertheless be distinguished according to context.

Ṣaʿʿara and taṣʿīr

Ṣaʿʿara is Form II and concerns tilting or inclining something.

Taṣʿīr is its verbal noun.

Luqmān 31:18 uses this verbal formation.

Tuṣaʿʿir is a second-person masculine singular imperfect, made jussive by prohibitive lā.

Grammatically, khaddaka is its direct object.

The verse therefore describes tilting the cheek as behavior, not a fixed bodily characteristic of the addressee.

Ṣāʿara and muṣāʿarah

Arabic also records ṣāʿara khaddahu for inclining the cheek and turning away from another person.

This is Form III.

The Qur’anic readings also include tuṣāʿir.

Form III should not, however, be taken to require two people mutually tilting their faces in this verse.

Ṣayʿariyyah

Ṣayʿariyyah named a mark on a camel’s neck.

Some lexical accounts also record veering or oblique movement for this form.

It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣuʿrūr and ṣaʿārīr

Arabic sources record ṣuʿrūr and ṣaʿārīr for certain small, twisted pieces of gum, hard round fruits, and objects with a bent or twisted shape.

The quadriliteral verb ṣaʿrara is also recorded for turning and rolling something.

The imagery of bending and turning may connect these uses with Ṣ ʿ R.

Their quadriliteral structure and particular uses nevertheless mean they should not all be treated without evidence as definite, direct derivatives of the same triliteral verb.

These branches do not determine the Qur’anic meaning of tuṣaʿʿir.

6. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and M Y L (māla)

Māla in Arabic denotes inclining or changing direction.

Ṣaʿara likewise has a basic connection with crookedness and inclination.

The established uses of Ṣ ʿ R, however, particularly concern the neck, face, or cheek.

M Y L is broader and can concern bodily movement, inward inclination, and changes in the direction of various things.

Ṣaʿar thus expresses a kind of inclination or crookedness, but not every inclination is ṣaʿar.

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and L W Y (lawā)

Lawā in Arabic concerns twisting and turning something.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:78, yalwūna alsinatahum concerns twisting tongues in speech.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:5 likewise mentions turning heads.

This root can express bodily twisting, while context establishes the behavior’s character and moral meaning.

In its condemned use, ṣaʿʿara khaddahu foregrounds tilting the cheek to express arrogance and contempt.

The roots therefore overlap in bodily movement without having identical uses throughout.

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and Ṣ D D (ṣadda)

Ṣadda in Arabic concerns turning away and preventing.

In some uses, a person turns away from something; in others, another person or agent obstructs their movement.

The behavioral expression ṣaʿʿara khaddahu also concerns a kind of turning away, with the particular image of tilting the cheek or neck.

Ṣadd can therefore occur without any visible facial movement, for example by preventing someone from following a path.

Taṣʿīr al-khadd is originally described through the face’s position and movement.

In Luqmān 31:18, the contemptuous meaning of that movement is prohibited.

Study of Ṣ D D

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and Ṣ D F (ṣadafa)

Ṣadafa ʿan in Arabic concerns turning away or inclining away from something.

In Al-Anʿām 6:46 and Al-Anʿām 6:157, the root concerns turning away from signs.

Ṣaʿʿara may also involve turning away, but in the Qur’anic expression ṣaʿʿara khaddahu, the face is the verb’s direct object.

The families are therefore close in turning away; Ṣ D F in the Qur’an concerns withdrawal from signs as well as two mountain sides, whereas Ṣ ʿ R in Luqmān concerns facial behavior toward people.

Study of Ṣ D F

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and K B R (kabura)

The moral branch of K B R concerns arrogance and conceit.

Ṣ ʿ R generally denotes crookedness and inclination.

In Luqmān 31:18, tilting the cheek occurs in a context of arrogance and vanity.

The roots are thus related as behavior and the moral state accompanying it.

Someone may be arrogant without visibly tilting the face.

Someone may also have a naturally crooked face without being arrogant.

Ṣaʿar and arrogance should therefore not be treated as complete synonyms.

Study of K B R

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and KH Y L (khāla)

Mukhtāl at the end of Luqmān 31:18 belongs to KH Y L and here denotes vanity and an inflated view of oneself.

This may become visible in behavior, including walking or treating others.

The Qur’anic use of Ṣ ʿ R expresses a specific outward manifestation: tilting the cheek contemptuously.

Vanity may therefore underlie taṣʿīr, but the roots’ lexical meanings differ.

Study of KH Y L

Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿara) and Ṣ Y D (ṣayida)

Lexical sources also record ṣayad, with a fatḥah on yāʾ, for a condition involving a tilted neck or face; this differs in vocalization from ṣayd, hunting.

For a camel whose neck is twisted by illness, lexicographers sometimes use both ṣaʿar and ṣayad.

The two therefore overlap in one bodily use.

This ṣayad must not be equated with every use of Ṣ Y D in the sense of hunting.

The words’ proximity also does not prove that either historically derives from the other.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥīṭ, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: ṣayad with a vowel on yāʾ

7. Challenges of understanding and translation

Is taṣʿīr merely a bodily movement?

At its basic level, the verb describes tilting the face.

In the social expression ṣaʿʿara khaddahu, however, the movement can express contempt and arrogance.

Context makes the latter meaning clear in Luqmān 31:18.

A translation saying only “do not tilt your cheek” retains the image, but may not adequately communicate the moral message to a modern reader by itself.

“Do not be arrogant,” conversely, conveys the moral message while removing the word’s particular image.

A direct translation should preferably retain the cheek’s image, while the study explains the contemptuous behavior.

Is every turning away disrespectful?

No.

In language and human behavior, turning away can have various causes.

The verse in Luqmān prohibits turning away with contempt and conceit.

People’s intentions should therefore not be judged from their facial appearance without understanding the situation and their actual behavior.

A naturally crooked neck or face, especially, must not be treated as a sign of moral failing.

Does the root itself mean contempt for poor people?

Some exegetical reports relate the prohibition to despising the needy and favoring the wealthy.

This explanation fits the general message of avoiding arrogance.

The expression tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi itself, however, names no particular group.

Li-al-nāsi has a general scope and should not be restricted to poor people, strangers, or those of lower social status.

The prohibition concerns treating people contemptuously, whether poor or wealthy, familiar or unknown.

Contempt for people who are socially or economically weaker can, of course, be a clear instance.

Does humility mean accepting every statement or demand?

No.

Avoiding arrogance and contempt does not abandon the right to judge, disagree respectfully, or reject an improper demand.

Even concerning parents, Luqmān 31:15 distinguishes refusing an improper demand from treating them well.

Luqmān 31:17 also enjoins what is right and forbids what is wrong.

Luqmān’s counsel therefore does not set a correct stance against respectful treatment of others.

One can oppose someone’s statement or action without belittling them.

This message follows from the verses’ connections; it should not become the independent lexical meaning of Ṣ ʿ R.

8. Translation proposal for Forqan

For the Qur’anic expression:

وَلَا تُصَعِّرْ خَدَّكَ لِلنَّاسِ

wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi

A rendering close to its wording is:

“Do not tilt your cheek before people.”

This retains the word’s main image.

Its behavioral meaning can be explained as:

“Do not turn your face away from people out of arrogance and contempt.”

The second rendering clarifies the expression’s established moral message, but “out of arrogance and contempt” is an explanation drawn from usage and context.

Forqan’s direct translation method therefore favors the first rendering, leaving arrogance and contempt to the word study or verse reflection.

The following equivalents can also be considered for non-Qur’anic forms:

Ṣaʿar: crookedness or inclination of the neck and face; aṣʿar: having a crooked neck or face; ṣaʿʿara khaddahu: he tilted his cheek; taṣʿīr: tilting or inclining the face.

In its condemned social use, taṣʿīr can mean turning away contemptuously.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ ʿ R denotes crookedness and inclination, especially of the neck and face.

Physically, this may be natural or caused by illness. Behaviorally, someone can deliberately turn away from another person, expressing disregard, conceit, or contempt.

Early Arabic knew ṣaʿʿara khaddahu for such arrogant behavior; it appears in poetry attributed to Al-Mutalammis and ʿAmr ibn Ḥunayy.

Only tuṣaʿʿir occurs in the Qur’an, in Luqmān 31:18.

It stands alongside the prohibition of exultant walking and condemnation of vanity and boasting, making the moral meaning of contemptuous turning away clear.

Bodily crookedness, ordinary turning away, and arrogant behavior are nevertheless three different conditions.

Concise root definition: Ṣ ʿ R denotes the neck or face becoming crooked or being inclined to one side; behaviorally, it can mean turning the cheek away from others out of arrogance and contempt. Brief root meaning: Crookedness or tilting of the neck and face; in human behavior, turning away with arrogance and contempt

The direct meaning of wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-al-nāsi is therefore that one should not tilt the cheek before people in a contemptuous manner.

The physical image remains, while moral condemnation concerns conceit and deliberate disregard for others, not natural bodily characteristics or every change in facial direction.
