The Arabic root S Ṭ R concerns things arranged in an orderly, continuous row. Saṭr names a line of writing, trees, palms, or other things placed alongside one another in a definite direction. Saṭara also means to write or record.

The Qur’anic family extends beyond ordinary writing. Yasṭurūna concerns writing; masṭūr and mustaṭar describe what has been recorded; and asāṭīr al-awwalīn is an expression used by opponents to discredit divine messages and promises.

Muṣayṭir and al-muṣayṭirūn are also classified under this family in Qur’anic indexes. Sayṭara has been analyzed on the pattern fayʿala with an added yā. Its four-letter form therefore does not by itself establish an independent four-radical root; the meaning of control must nevertheless be distinguished from ordinary writing.

The study must distinguish arranging in rows, recording, narrating, attributing statements to earlier generations, and claiming control or oversight. These applications are established in Arabic, but not every text is false, nor does recording someone’s actions necessarily confer control over that person.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Saṭr: a row of connected things

Arabic lexical usage records al-saṭr and al-saṭar for a row or line, including writing, trees, and palms.

One central explanation is iṣṭifāf: things standing alongside one another in a recognizable order.

This meaning is readily understood from daily life.

Palms planted in a line form a row of palms. Buildings constructed along a line form a row of buildings. In writing, letters and words are arranged in an orderly sequence to form a line.

The essential feature is not simply that there are many things, but that they occupy a recognizable row.

Scattered trees do not necessarily constitute a saṭr, whereas trees planted successively along a definite line can do so.

An appropriate central meaning is thus “forming a row or occupying an orderly line.”

From rows to writing

Saṭara al-kitāba means to write a book or text.

Here writing is associated with arranging signs and words in lines. Letters and words form an ordered sequence that produces readable content.

Saṭara does not merely mean drawing an empty line. In its written use, spoken content can be converted into writing.

Saṭṭara and istaṭara are also associated with writing and recording in classical Arabic.

The concrete image of forming a row should therefore be distinguished from the established sense of writing. The former explains the semantic association, but writing is itself a real lexical meaning, not a metaphor invented by a modern reader.

Early poetry and its dating

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā, a Qurashī poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, is relevant to this family’s history.

A poem attributed to him concerning a group of Quraysh includes:

أَلْهَى قُصَيًّا عَنِ الْمَجْدِ الْأَسَاطِيرُ
وَرِشْوَةٌ مِثْلَ مَا تُرْشَى السَّفَاسِيرُ

alhā Quṣayyan ʿani al-majdi al-asāṭīru / wa-rishwatun mithla mā turshā al-safāsīru

“Idle tales distracted Quṣayy’s people from honor, and bribes like those paid to brokers.”

Asāṭīr appears alongside bribery and blameworthy conduct. The poet is not praising ancient narratives; he uses it to disparage the people addressed. Quṣayy here names his descendants or people, not necessarily the ancestor personally.

The verse is evidence for a disparaging use of asāṭīr close to the period of revelation.

Attribution to a poet who lived before Islam does not by itself date this particular verse. Historical reports place it in pre-Islamic Qurashī disputes, but it reaches us through later compilations. It should therefore be described as attributed to that period, not as independently and conclusively dated evidence.

Another verse by Jarīr is recorded for saṭr as a row. Jarīr was an Umayyad poet, so his usage demonstrates continuity after the Qur’an rather than pre-Qur’anic evidence.

This chronological distinction matters: meanings recorded centuries later should not automatically be assigned to pre-Islamic Arabic without independent examination.

Saṭr, lines, and rows

Classical Arabic records saṭrun mina al-shajar and saṭrun mina al-nakhl for rows of trees and palms.

Suṭūr and asṭur are established plurals of saṭr.

The tree-row usage shows that the root is not confined to books and writing. Without historical evidence, however, one cannot determine whether rows of trees came first and writing developed later, or whether both branches were present throughout the period accessible to us.

Historical conclusions must stay within the evidence: early Arabic associates this family with rows, writing, and recording, while asāṭīr also disparages accounts considered unreliable by the speaker.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

In the classification adopted here, this family occurs sixteen times in sixteen verses, including the two muṣayṭir forms. Their inclusion is compatible with the analysis of sayṭara as fayʿala with an added yā; their current meaning of control should still be distinguished from writing.

The principal forms are:

Yasṭurūna: a Form I present verb, third-person masculine plural, meaning “they write” or “they record.” It occurs in Al-Qalam 68:1.

Masṭūr: the passive participle of saṭara, “written” or “recorded.” It occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:58, Al-Aḥzāb 33:6, and Al-Ṭūr 52:2.

Mustaṭar: a Form VIII passive participle, meaning recorded or written, in Al-Qamar 54:53.

Asāṭīr: a collective or plural form whose singular and historical formation have received different explanations. It occurs nine times, always with al-awwalīn.

Muṣayṭir / al-muṣayṭirūn: a person or people exercising oversight, authority, or control, in Al-Ṭūr 52:37 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22.

Saṭr, suṭūr, tasṭīr, saṭṭara, and usṭūrah are known Arabic forms but do not themselves occur in the Qur’an.

Asāṭīr has been explained as the plural of usṭūrah and also as a plural of a plural: saṭr, then asṭur or asṭār, then asāṭīr.

Since its singular does not occur in the Qur’an and lexical sources record several proposed singulars, none of these explanations should be treated without sufficient evidence as the sole certain history of the word.

3. Writing, recording, and the persistence of a record

The pen and what is written

Al-Qalam 68:1 states:

ن وَالْقَلَمِ وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ

nūn wa-al-qalami wa-mā yasṭurūna

Al-Qalam 68:1

“Nūn. By the pen and what they write.”

Yasṭurūna refers to writing here.

Its placement beside al-qalam, the pen, establishes the written sense in this context.

Mā can refer to what is written. A verbal-noun interpretation is also grammatically possible: an oath by their writing.

“What they write” is a natural rendering and makes the connection between pen and written content clear.

The identity of those writing is not specified by yasṭurūna itself. The verb is plural, but the phrase does not name its agents.

The root alone therefore cannot establish that they must be angels, human writers, or another group. Identifying them requires broader contextual study.

The lexical point is that the verb here concerns recording speech and content, not merely drawing a line or arranging physical objects in a row.

A written book

Al-Ṭūr 52:2 states:

وَكِتَابٍ مَسْطُورٍ

wa-kitābin masṭūrin

Al-Ṭūr 52:2

“And by a written book.”

Masṭūr is a passive participle describing something written or recorded.

Kitāb identifies the written object, while masṭūr qualifies it.

The next verse, Al-Ṭūr 52:3, adds “فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ”: “on parchment spread open.”

Together they evoke writing recorded on a medium that can be unfolded or spread out.

Masṭūr alone does not specify the book’s name, precise contents, time of writing, or location.

Those questions concern the referent of kitābin masṭūrin rather than the root’s lexical meaning.

A matter recorded in the Book

Al-Isrāʾ 17:58 states:

وَإِنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ إِلَّا نَحْنُ مُهْلِكُوهَا قَبْلَ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ أَوْ مُعَذِّبُوهَا عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا كَانَ ذَٰلِكَ فِي الْكِتَابِ مَسْطُورًا

wa-in min qaryatin illā naḥnu muhlikūhā qabla yawmi al-qiyāmati aw muʿadhdhibūhā ʿadhāban shadīdan kāna dhālika fī al-kitābi masṭūran

Al-Isrāʾ 17:58

“There is no town but that We will destroy it before the Day of Resurrection or punish it severely. That is recorded in the Book.”

Masṭūran describes the stated matter as recorded in the Book.

What is recorded is the preceding statement. Masṭūr can therefore describe a recorded report, ruling, or matter concerning the future.

The word alone cannot explain the entire relationship between divine recording, God’s knowledge, the occurrence of events, and human choice. Those questions involve a broader body of verses.

The same phrase occurs in Al-Aḥzāb 33:6:

كَانَ ذَٰلِكَ فِي الْكِتَابِ مَسْطُورًا

kāna dhālika fī al-kitābi masṭūran

Al-Aḥzāb 33:6

“That is recorded in the Book.”

Here the context concerns the Prophet’s position in relation to believers, his wives, and the priority of relatives within the specified relationships.

An identical form can thus refer to different matters in different verses. In both, masṭūr means recorded; context determines what is recorded.

Mustaṭar: recording the small and the great

Al-Qamar 54:53 states:

وَكُلُّ صَغِيرٍ وَكَبِيرٍ مُسْتَطَرٌ

wa-kullu ṣaghīrin wa-kabīrin mustaṭarun

Al-Qamar 54:53

“Everything small and great is recorded.”

Mustaṭar belongs to the verb istaṭara, which concerns writing and recording.

Kullu ṣaghīrin wa-kabīrin establishes the scope, embracing small and great; mustaṭar states that they are recorded.

The preceding context also matters. Al-Qamar 54:52 states:

وَكُلُّ شَيْءٍ فَعَلُوهُ فِي الزُّبُرِ

wa-kullu shayʾin faʿalūhu fī al-zuburi

Al-Qamar 54:52

“Everything they did is in the records.”

Together, the verses describe the recording of deeds and its comprehensive scope.

The root’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from that of the whole statement.

Mustaṭar does not itself mean “every small and great deed.” That scope is expressed by the preceding words; the derivative means recorded.

Nor does the morphological difference between masṭūr and mustaṭar establish that one is ordinary writing while the other is eternal or unchangeable. Such distinctions require evidence from usage and context, not the verbal pattern alone.

4. Asāṭīr al-awwalīn: earlier writings or discredited tales?

Asāṭīr al-awwalīn is this family’s best-known Qur’anic expression.

It occurs in nine verses: Al-Anʿām 6:25, Al-Anfāl 8:31, Al-Naḥl 16:24, Al-Muʾminūn 23:83, Al-Furqān 25:5, Al-Naml 27:68, Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, Al-Qalam 68:15, and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13.

In every case it belongs to the deniers’ speech. The Qur’an reports their words; it does not itself declare all earlier narratives unreliable asāṭīr.

Lexical meaning and disparagement

Asāṭīr is associated in lexical usage with writings, narratives, and accounts transmitted from earlier generations.

Abāṭīl and aḥādīth lā niẓāma lahā are also recorded explanations: baseless statements or disordered, unreliable accounts.

The disparaging sense is itself an established usage of asāṭīr, reinforced by Qur’anic context; it is not necessarily supplied only from outside the word. It should not, however, be transferred to every derivative of saṭara.

The verb saṭara does not establish that a text is false. A person may write a truth or record an error; writing alone determines neither truth nor falsehood.

In asāṭīr al-awwalīn, speakers disparage the message by reducing it to old, unreliable tales.

“Tales of the ancients” can therefore be a natural rendering, provided that this dismissive judgment is recognized as the speakers’ own.

Merely translating it as “earlier writings” may erase its contemptuous force in context.

The accusation of borrowing earlier writings

Al-Furqān 25:5 states:

وَقَالُوا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ اكْتَتَبَهَا فَهِيَ تُمْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

wa-qālū asāṭīru al-awwalīna iktatabahā fa-hiya tumlā ʿalayhi bukratan wa-aṣīlan

Al-Furqān 25:5

“They said: Tales of the ancients that he has had written down; they are dictated to him morning and evening.”

The connection with writing is evident here.

The opponents allege that the Qur’an’s contents were taken from earlier narratives and then dictated to the Prophet.

Iktatabahā matters because the allegation concerns not just hearing old stories, but procuring a written account and receiving its contents through dictation.

The preceding verse, Al-Furqān 25:4, frames the accusation: opponents call the Qur’an fabricated speech and claim that others helped produce it.

These are their allegations. Reporting them does not establish borrowing or fabrication.

The following verse, Al-Furqān 25:6, answers by attributing its revelation to the One who knows what is hidden in the heavens and earth.

The dispute concerns the source: divine revelation or a collection of old narratives passed to the Prophet?

S Ṭ R helps explain the accusation’s wording but cannot by itself resolve the whole question of the Qur’an’s origin.

Asāṭīr in denial of resurrection

Al-Muʾminūn 23:83 states:

لَقَدْ وُعِدْنَا نَحْنُ وَآبَاؤُنَا هَٰذَا مِنْ قَبْلُ إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

la-qad wuʿidnā naḥnu wa-ābāʾunā hādhā min qablu in hādhā illā asāṭīru al-awwalīna

Al-Muʾminūn 23:83

“We and our fathers were promised this before. This is nothing but tales of the ancients.”

The speakers dismiss the promise of resurrection as old, unreliable narratives.

Al-Naml 27:68 repeats the theme in a similar construction.

Asāṭīr al-awwalīn here is not merely about past events. It dismisses a promise whose fulfillment lies in the future by attributing it to earlier people’s sayings.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, someone uses the same expression against his parents after hearing the promise of being raised again.

The Qur’anic use is therefore not confined to historical stories; it can also discredit promises about the future.

Asāṭīr in response to recited signs

Al-Qalam 68:15 states:

إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ آيَاتُنَا قَالَ أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīru al-awwalīna

Al-Qalam 68:15; Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13

“When Our signs are recited to him, he says: Tales of the ancients.”

The same wording occurs in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13.

In both, asāṭīr al-awwalīn is the person’s response to recitation.

In Al-Anfāl 8:31, after hearing the signs, speakers claim they could produce something similar if they wished and dismiss them as earlier tales.

In Al-Naḥl 16:24, people asked what their Lord has sent down answer: “أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ.”

In Al-Anʿām 6:25, the phrase occurs amid dispute with the Prophet and rejection of the signs.

These uses make asāṭīr part of dismissive, rejecting speech. They do not make everyone who questions a historical account or doubts its reliability an instance of these verses merely for asking or doubting.

The word’s lexical meaning does not reveal a person’s intentions, knowledge, or honesty.

Qur’anic asāṭīr and modern uses of “myth”

Modern Persian sometimes uses usṭūrah for narratives about gods, heroes, and ancient worlds. Literary and historical studies may also use “myth” for a cultural narrative type without simply intending “a lie.”

That modern usage should not be projected into the Qur’an without examination.

In the Qur’an, opponents employ asāṭīr al-awwalīn to discredit the divine message. Its disparagement is inseparable from its wording and context.

This does not imply that the Qur’an regards every ancient story, inherited narrative, or earlier writing as false and worthless.

The Qur’an itself recounts earlier peoples’ histories to convey truth and lessons. Antiquity or transmission from predecessors alone is no proof of falsehood.

5. Muṣayṭir: oversight, authority, and control

The two muṣayṭir occurrences differ in meaning and form from ordinary writing expressions.

Al-Ṭūr 52:37 states:

أَمْ عِنْدَهُمْ خَزَائِنُ رَبِّكَ أَمْ هُمُ الْمُصَيْطِرُونَ

am ʿindahum khazāʾinu rabbika am humu al-muṣayṭirūna

Al-Ṭūr 52:37

“Do they possess your Lord’s treasuries, or are they the controllers?”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22 states:

لَسْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِمُصَيْطِرٍ

lasta ʿalayhim bi-muṣayṭirin

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22

“You are not a controller over them.”

Musayṭir and muṣayṭir describe someone overseeing something, keeping watch over it, or exercising control and authority.

Some early explanations connect the word with recording and keeping track of affairs: someone observing and recording others’ actions can occupy the position of supervisor or watcher.

Muṣayṭir is an active participle associated with sayṭara, not a passive participle from saṭara like masṭūr.

Sayṭara is analyzed in early morphology on the pattern fayʿala with an added yā. It can therefore be placed under S Ṭ R; simply counting its four written letters does not establish four original radicals. This morphological analysis alone, however, does not reconstruct every stage in the historical development from writing to control.

The change from sīn to ṣād near ṭā is also recorded in lexical and recitational accounts. Musayṭir and muṣayṭir therefore need not represent separate concepts.

Control in relation to divine authority

Al-Ṭūr 52:37 asks about the Lord’s treasuries and authority over affairs.

In context, al-muṣayṭirūn refers to people as though they claimed such control.

The question challenges that standing.

This is not equivalent to someone who writes. Writing or recording may inform the lexical explanation, but the word’s current meaning here is authority and control.

The Prophet, reminding, and the absence of control

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:21 states:

فَذَكِّرْ إِنَّمَا أَنْتَ مُذَكِّرٌ

fa-dhakkir innamā anta mudhakkirun

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:21

“Remind, then; you are only a reminder.”

The next verse, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22, states:

لَسْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِمُصَيْطِرٍ

lasta ʿalayhim bi-muṣayṭirin

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22

“You are not a controller over them.”

Together, the verses distinguish reminding and communicating from having coercive control over the audience.

The Prophet is instructed to remind; this address does not appoint him a muṣayṭir over people.

That fits the word’s use for someone exercising controlling oversight and authority.

The derivative alone should not be used to extract every rule concerning social responsibility, judgment, or government. The scope of the Prophet’s responsibilities requires the relevant verses together.

The lexical conclusion is clear: in these two verses muṣayṭir concerns oversight, control, and authority, not ordinary writing.

6. Comparison with related roots

S Ṭ R and K T B: writing in lines and recording

K T B concerns gathering, joining, and writing in Arabic.

Kitāb and its derivatives have a broad Qur’anic range, including writing, commands, rulings, and records.

S Ṭ R overlaps with it in writing, while the image of arrangement in rows helps explain its meaning.

The families occur together in kitābin masṭūrin in Al-Ṭūr 52:2.

Kitāb identifies the written or prescribed object; masṭūr describes it as recorded or written.

The words complement each other here rather than necessarily expressing entirely different types of writing.

S Ṭ R and R Q M: writing and marking

R Q M concerns inscribing, marking, and recording recognizable signs.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 states:

كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ

kitābun marqūmun

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

“An inscribed record.”

This overlaps with kitābin masṭūrin in Al-Ṭūr 52:2.

Both involve writing or recording, but their images and range differ. Raqm is closer to marking and inscription, saṭr to rows and writing.

This distinction does not mean that a masṭūr text must lack marks or a marqūm text must lack lines.

S Ṭ R and Q Ṣ Ṣ: writing and narrating

In Qur’anic narrative uses, Q Ṣ Ṣ concerns following and recounting events.

A story concerns describing and following an event, while saṭr in its written sense concerns recording it.

A story can be spoken or written. Being a narrative and being recorded are distinct qualities.

This matters for asāṭīr al-awwalīn. An old narrative may be written down, but recording it does not make it true or false.

S Ṭ R and N S KH: recording and copying

Some uses of N S KH concern transferring or copying writing.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:29 states:

إِنَّا كُنَّا نَسْتَنْسِخُ مَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

innā kunnā nastansikhu mā kuntum taʿmalūna

Al-Jāthiyah 45:29

“We were recording what you used to do.”

Nastansikhu here concerns the recording of deeds.

Naskh and saṭr are not identical. Saṭr concerns writing and recording in lines; istinsākh can emphasize preparing or transferring a copy.

The distinction should not be made into a conclusive theory about the instruments or physical manner of recording deeds in the unseen.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish writing forms, the disparaging asāṭīr al-awwalīn, and expressions of control.

The following equivalents suit the principal Qur’anic forms:

Yasṭurūna in Al-Qalam 68:1: “they write” or “they record.” In the verse, “what they write” is natural.

Masṭūr in Al-Ṭūr 52:2: “written.”

Masṭūran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:58 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:6: “recorded” or “written.”

Mustaṭarun in Al-Qamar 54:53: “is recorded.”

Asāṭīr al-awwalīn: “tales of the ancients” or “unreliable stories of earlier generations,” reflecting the speakers’ disparaging usage. Its lexical association with earlier writings and narratives should remain clear in the explanation.

Al-muṣayṭirūn in Al-Ṭūr 52:37: “controllers” or “those in authority.”

Bi-muṣayṭirin in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22: “a controller”; the complete statement is “You are not a controller over them.”

Masṭūr should not be expanded into “an absolutely unchangeable decree,” since that meaning is not supplied by the word alone.

Nor should asāṭīr automatically receive the modern scholarly sense of “myth,” or be confused with “mythology” as a field of study.

Rendering muṣayṭir as “writer” or “recorder” would not convey its current meaning in these verses, even though an association with recording and oversight can be considered in lexical explanation.

Conclusion

S Ṭ R concerns things forming an orderly line. Its established applications to writing and recording are readily recognizable within this semantic field.

In the Qur’an, yasṭurūna concerns writing, masṭūr and mustaṭar concern being recorded, and asāṭīr al-awwalīn concerns narratives attributed to predecessors in the opponents’ disparaging speech.

The crucial distinction is that writing and recording prove neither truth nor falsehood by themselves. A text can be accurate or inaccurate; its credibility requires evidence concerning its contents.

In every Qur’anic asāṭīr al-awwalīn passage, speakers dismiss the divine message or promise as old, unreliable sayings. Their allegations should not be represented as the Qur’an’s judgment on all earlier writings and narratives.

Muṣayṭir concerns oversight, authority, and control. Sayṭara has an established analysis as fayʿala with an added yā under S Ṭ R, but morphological relationship does not make control identical to writing.

The final definition should preserve both the central meaning and the limits of its branches:

S Ṭ R concerns an orderly row and, in writing, recording speech and content in lines. Qur’anic asāṭīr is a disparaging label for narratives attributed to earlier generations. Muṣayṭir, morphologically associated with sayṭara, concerns oversight and control.

The study thus distinguishes recording reality, narrating it, judging a narrative’s credibility, and exercising authority over others. These can be connected in language and life, but none should be substituted for another without evidence.
