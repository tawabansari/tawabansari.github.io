Root ه ن أ — Pleasant enjoyment without distress

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَنِيئًا
(hanīʾan)
Agreeably and at ease; four occurrences

From comfortable, agreeable enjoyment to a blessing untroubled by pain, anxiety, or unpleasant consequences

The Arabic triliteral root ه ن أ concerns agreeableness, pleasure, ease of enjoyment, and benefiting from good without distress. It can describe food, drink, property, success, and other blessings enjoyed with satisfaction and ease.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an, always as هَنِيئًا: once concerning property a woman willingly relinquishes from her marital gift, and three times concerning eating and drinking in Paradise.

These uses show that hanīʾ does not simply mean tasty. Something may taste pleasant yet fail to provide untroubled enjoyment because of harm, anxiety, violated rights, or accompanying discomfort.

Conversely, hanīʾ can describe a blessing whose enjoyment is pleasant and comfortable, unspoiled by something distressing.

1. The root in early Arabic

In classical Arabic, hanīʾ describes something agreeable and easy to enjoy, without distress or unpleasantness.

هَنُؤَ الطَّعَامُ and هَنِئَ الطَّعَامُ describe food as pleasant and agreeable.

هَنَأَنِي الطَّعَامُ means that the food was agreeable and enjoyable to me.

هَنَأَنِي خَيْرُ فُلَانٍ describes a benefit received from someone and enjoyed without trouble or difficulty.

Here agreeableness concerns more than taste or food’s material quality. Ease in receiving and using a blessing also belongs to the word’s semantic field.

Lexicons accordingly explain hanīʾ as something that reaches you without hardship.

This should not be restricted to something obtained without any effort. Someone may work hard to prepare food and still eat it comfortably and with satisfaction.

The emphasis is on pleasant enjoyment without distress, not necessarily the absence of effort before acquiring the blessing.

An example from pre-Qur’anic poetry

Al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah, a pre-Islamic Arab poet, addresses the killer in an elegy for al-Muntashir ibn Wahb:

«أَصَبْتَ فِي حَرَمٍ مِنَّا أَخَا ثِقَةٍ
هِنْدُ بْنَ أَسْمَاءَ لَا يَهْنَئْ لَكَ الظَّفَرُ»

Aṣabta fī ḥaramin minnā akhā thiqatin / Hindu bna Asmāʾa lā yahnaʾ laka al-ẓafaru.

“Hind ibn Asmāʾ, you struck down a trustworthy man of ours in the sanctuary; may that victory bring you no enjoyment.”

Here lā yahnaʾ laka al-ẓafaru concerns neither food nor drink. The poet speaks of victory and success.

His opponent may have prevailed in the encounter, but the poet wishes that victory will not become a source of comfortable enjoyment.

This shows that early Arabic could use ه ن أ for the outcome of an action and the enjoyment of success.

Victory is one event; its agreeableness to the victor is another matter. A person may reach a goal yet experience anxiety, regret, or unpleasant consequences.

The verse is attributed to al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah in collections of early poetry, including versions of the Aṣmaʿiyyāt. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet is established in the literary tradition, though the verse’s precise date is not independently established.

It matters lexically because pleasant enjoyment in early Arabic was not limited to food.

An example from ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward

ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward, a recognized pre-Islamic poet, explains to his wife why he strives and takes risks to acquire property:

«وَمُسْتَهْنِئٍ زَيْدٌ أَبُوهُ فَلَا أَرَى
لَهُ مَدْفَعًا فَاقْنَيْ حَيَاءَكِ وَاصْبِرِي»

Wa-mustahniʾin Zaydun abūhu fa-lā arā / lahu madfaʿan fa-qnay ḥayāʾaki wa-iṣbirī.

“A petitioner descended from Zayd comes to us; I see no way to turn him away. So preserve your modesty and be patient.”

Mustahniʾ here denotes someone asking another person for a gift or assistance.

This belongs to another branch of ه ن أ in which hinʾ is recorded as a gift or grant.

Arabic hanaʾtu al-rajula could mean giving something to a person. The family was also used of providing a group with food or livelihood.

Early usage therefore includes a branch concerning giving and receiving benefits and gifts.

This example does not make financial giving the sole meaning of hanīʾ everywhere. ʿUrwah’s verse illustrates giving and requesting assistance, whereas al-Aʿshā’s illustrates agreeable enjoyment of success.

Both may relate to benefiting from good, but their historical order of emergence is uncertain.

2. Other semantic branches

Hanāʾ: comfort and happiness in life

Hanāʾ and hanāʾah express happiness, agreeableness, and ease of living.

Words in this family can describe satisfaction with one’s circumstances and enjoyment untroubled by distressing difficulties.

عَيْشٌ هَنِيءٌ means a pleasant, comfortable life.

This does not mean a life without responsibility, work, or difficulty. It describes a satisfying condition of enjoyment and comfort.

Persian also uses hanāʾ in expressions such as ʿaysh wa hanāʾ, meaning pleasure and ease.

Tahniʾah: offering congratulations

Tahniʾah belongs to the same family.

People offer congratulations when someone experiences something welcome, such as success, a child’s birth, or a desired blessing.

Arabic contrasts tahniʾah with taʿziyah: condolences accompany grief and calamity, congratulations accompany happiness and good fortune.

In this usage, the speaker wishes another person happy enjoyment of a blessing.

The conventional meaning of congratulations does not guarantee future good. Congratulating is a linguistic and social response to a welcome event.

Hinʾ: giving and providing a livelihood

Some Arabic usages employ hinʾ for a gift or grant.

Hanaʾa al-qawma can describe providing a group’s needs or supporting them for a period.

This approaches a blessing from the perspective of receiving and benefiting from it.

It does not mean every financial gift is necessarily hanīʾ. A gift may come with reproaches, harm, imposed dependence, or unpleasant consequences.

Giving and agreeable enjoyment are thus related but distinguishable matters.

Hināʾ: a substance used on camel skin

Arabic also records al-hināʾ for a kind of tar applied to camels’ skin, particularly for skin diseases.

Hanaʾtu al-baʿīra here means coating a camel with that substance.

This is a material branch associated with pastoral life.

A relationship between its therapeutic use and benefit or relief is conceivable, but the evidence does not establish that it developed from agreeable eating, or the reverse.

A single historical origin should therefore not be invented for every branch.

The Qur’an contains no occurrence of this therapeutic branch.

3. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root ه ن أ occurs four times in the Qur’an, in only one lexical form:

  • هَنِيئًا — al-Nisāʾ 4:4: consuming property a woman willingly relinquishes from her marital gift.
  • هَنِيئًا — al-Ṭūr 52:19: the command to eat and drink in Paradise.
  • هَنِيئًا — al-Ḥāqqah 69:24: enjoying heavenly food and drink in return for deeds in the preceding life.
  • هَنِيئًا — al-Mursalāt 77:43: an address to the mindful and those who do good.

Hanīʾ follows the faʿīl pattern and describes the agreeableness of enjoyment in Qur’anic usage.

Hanīʾan is its indefinite accusative form.

Forms such as هَنَأَ, هَنُؤَ, هَنِئَ, يَهْنَأُ, هَنَاء, هَنَاءَة, تَهْنِئَة, مُهَنَّأ, and مُسْتَهْنِئ are established Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

The syntax of hanīʾan

In Qur’anic usage, hanīʾan accompanies eating, and in three verses eating and drinking together.

Several recognized grammatical analyses exist.

One takes it as an adjective qualifying an omitted verbal noun, as though the phrase said “eat an agreeable eating.”

Another treats it as a circumstantial expression describing the manner of enjoyment.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:4, hanīʾan marīʾan can describe consuming or benefiting from property. The other three verses use hanīʾan alone with the command to eat and drink.

The grammatical disagreement does not alter the basic sense of agreeableness. Both analyses describe pleasant, comfortable enjoyment.

The faʿīl pattern alone does not establish everlasting pleasure, freedom from every difficulty, or a particular kind of blessing. Such features must come from context and independent evidence.

4. Hanīʾan marīʾan: enjoying property with its owner’s consent

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4

«وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً ۚ فَإِنْ طِبْنَ لَكُمْ عَنْ شَيْءٍ مِنْهُ نَفْسًا فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا»

Wa-ātū al-nisāʾa ṣaduqātihinna niḥlatan fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾin minhu nafsan fa-kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾā.

“Give women their marital gifts as a grant. Then, if they willingly relinquish any part of it to you, consume it comfortably and agreeably.”

The verse first emphasizes women’s right to receive their marital gifts.

Ṣaduqātihinna refers to the gifts belonging to the women. The pronoun hunna associates that ownership with the women themselves.

It then states a specific condition: if women willingly relinquish some of it, its use is permitted.

The role of ṭibna … nafsan

Ṭibna belongs to ط ي ب and here expresses willingness and contentment.

Grammatically, nafsan is a specification clarifying the state of willingness.

The property must therefore be relinquished with the right-holder’s genuine consent.

This qualification is more than a verbal formality.

A woman may willingly give up part of her marital gift. But agreement obtained through threats, psychological pressure, fear of family consequences, or economic coercion cannot automatically be equated with the willingness described in the verse.

Conversely, requiring consent does not mean that a woman has no right to relinquish her marital gift. The verse recognizes her choice to give part of her property and makes her own willingness the condition.

Fa-kulūhu: eating or using property?

Fa-kulūhu literally means “then eat it.”

Since the subject is property and the marital gift, eating can here mean benefiting from and consuming wealth.

This is familiar Qur’anic usage. In al-Nisāʾ 4:2, consuming orphans’ property means wrongfully appropriating or using their assets, not merely eating food purchased with them.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 likewise concerns benefiting from property willingly relinquished by a woman.

This explanation comes from the financial context and Qur’anic uses of akala, rather than from ه ن أ.

Hanīʾan marīʾan: two related but distinct descriptions

Here hanīʾan accompanies marīʾan.

Hanīʾ emphasizes agreeable, comfortable enjoyment without distress or difficulty.

Marīʾ belongs to another lexical family. In food contexts, it concerns compatibility and the absence of unpleasant consequences.

Lexical explanations distinguish them by treating hanīʾ as pleasant to consume or receive and marīʾ as agreeable and harmless in its consequences as well.

This is a semantic tendency. The words overlap in Arabic and should not be separated by an absolute boundary.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:4, the primary subject is a woman’s property and rights, not material food.

Hanīʾan marīʾan therefore cannot be restricted here to tasty, easily digested food.

When the owner willingly relinquishes property, the recipient can enjoy its use comfortably and without anxiety.

Conversely, property taken coercively or through violating another’s right does not provide the untroubled, legitimate enjoyment permitted by the verse’s condition, however pleasant it may seem.

Hanīʾan is therefore connected here with consent and rights. Consent is not its lexical meaning, however; it is the condition that the verse sets for permitted use of the property.

5. Hanīʾan: agreeable enjoyment in Paradise

Al-Ṭūr 52:19

«كُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا هَنِيئًا بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ»

Kulū wa-ishrabū hanīʾan bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn.

“Eat and drink agreeably and at ease, because of what you used to do.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:17 describes the mindful as being among gardens and blessings.

In al-Ṭūr 52:18, they rejoice in what their Lord has given them and have been protected from the torment of blazing fire.

Verse 19 then commands eating and drinking with the description hanīʾan.

This sequence describes more than the availability of food and drink: it also addresses the quality of enjoyment.

Someone may have good food while suffering illness, fear, threats, or anxiety. Food’s availability alone does not establish complete ease.

Here, however, eating and drinking are described as hanīʾ: pleasant and comfortable enjoyment.

The expression bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūna also matters. The blessing is connected with what they used to do.

Hanīʾ should therefore not be defined exclusively as something obtained without any effort. Here agreeable enjoyment is associated with earlier deeds.

The issue is the quality of enjoyment, not a denial of the effort and responsibility preceding it.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24

«كُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا هَنِيئًا بِمَا أَسْلَفْتُمْ فِي الْأَيَّامِ الْخَالِيَةِ»

Kulū wa-ishrabū hanīʾan bi-mā aslaftum fī al-ayyāmi al-khāliyah.

“Eat and drink agreeably and at ease, because of what you sent ahead in days gone by.”

This follows the description of someone whose record is placed in their right hand.

In al-Ḥāqqah 69:19, that person joyfully invites others to read the record.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:21 describes a satisfying life, while al-Ḥāqqah 69:22 and al-Ḥāqqah 69:23 describe an elevated garden with fruit within reach.

Then comes the command to eat and drink agreeably.

Here hanīʾan forms part of a picture of satisfaction and pleasant enjoyment.

This verse differs from al-Ṭūr 52:19 in its final expression.

Al-Ṭūr speaks of “what you used to do.”

Al-Ḥāqqah speaks of “what you sent ahead in days gone by.”

The distinction comes from ع م ل and س ل ف, not ه ن أ.

Hanīʾ retains the sense of pleasant, comfortable enjoyment in both verses.

The description alone does not determine the biological or physical nature of eating and drinking in the Hereafter. The Qur’an includes food and drink in its portrayal of heavenly blessings, but bodily structure and digestive mechanisms there cannot be derived from hanīʾ.

Al-Mursalāt 77:43

«كُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا هَنِيئًا بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ»

Kulū wa-ishrabū hanīʾan bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn.

“Eat and drink agreeably and at ease, because of what you used to do.”

The wording matches al-Ṭūr 52:19, but the context differs.

Al-Mursalāt 77:41 places the mindful among shade and springs.

Al-Mursalāt 77:42 provides the fruit they desire.

Verse 43 then commands agreeable eating and drinking.

Al-Mursalāt 77:44 also mentions the reward of those who do good.

Here too, hanīʾ belongs to a setting of blessing, satisfaction, and reward.

Later, al-Mursalāt 77:46 speaks in a different tone of another group’s brief eating and enjoyment.

The contrast shows that eating and enjoying things alone do not establish someone’s ultimate condition. Their way of life, actions, and outcome also matter.

It does not make hanīʾ intrinsically exclusive to the Hereafter or believers’ rewards. Al-Nisāʾ 4:4 uses the same word for enjoying property in earthly life.

6. Distinctions from neighboring roots

ه ن أ and م ر أ: agreeable enjoyment and agreeable consequences

The two families occur together in al-Nisāʾ 4:4.

Hanīʾan emphasizes pleasant, comfortable reception or use.

In food contexts, marīʾan concerns compatibility and the absence of unpleasant consequences.

Food may taste pleasant yet cause discomfort afterward. Pleasure while eating and agreeable consequences are distinct matters.

When these descriptions apply to property, however, the image of food must be understood in relation to rights and use.

Property willingly relinquished by its owner can provide comfortable, untroubled enjoyment for the recipient.

The words are close and complementary but not wholly interchangeable.

ه ن أ and ط ي ب: agreeableness and willingness

ط ي ب has a broad Qur’anic range, including purity, goodness, agreeableness, and willingness.

It occurs in ṭibna … nafsan in al-Nisāʾ 4:4.

Ṭibna expresses the women’s willingness.

Hanīʾan describes enjoyment once that condition is met.

The words are related here but perform different roles.

The owner’s consent conditions the transfer; agreeable enjoyment describes using property transferred with that consent.

Ṭayyib and hanīʾ should not be treated as complete synonyms, nor should the woman’s consent be inferred from hanīʾ alone.

ه ن أ and ن ع م: a blessing and enjoyment of it

A blessing concerns good that reaches someone and benefits them.

Hanīʾ emphasizes how agreeably and comfortably that good is enjoyed.

Owning property may be a blessing while also bringing anxiety, conflict, or demanding responsibilities.

By contrast, hanīʾ enjoyment describes benefiting from something without distress or harm.

This does not mean blessings always involve suffering or that hanīʾ concerns only property. Each word has its range, with overlap in some uses.

ه ن أ and م ت ع: use and agreeable enjoyment

م ت ع concerns benefiting from and enjoying something.

Qur’anic matāʿ may be brief or longer-lasting, beneficial or discussed with a warning.

Hanīʾ itself describes pleasure and ease in enjoyment.

Using something is therefore not identical to enjoying it agreeably.

Al-Mursalāt 77:43 describes eating and drinking as hanīʾan; al-Mursalāt 77:46 describes another group’s brief enjoyment.

Not every kind of enjoyment is necessarily hanīʾ.

The root does not determine duration. Something may be pleasant briefly, while another context describes lasting enjoyment.

ه ن أ and ر غ د: agreeableness and abundant provision

Qur’anic raghad concerns abundance, ease, and satisfactory provision.

Al-Baqarah 2:35 describes eating the garden’s blessings as raghadan.

Hanīʾ, by contrast, emphasizes agreeableness and ease of enjoyment.

Abundance is one feature of a blessing; freedom from distress in its use is another.

A little food may be agreeable, while abundant food alone cannot guarantee ease and satisfaction.

These roots are related but not completely interchangeable.

7. Challenges of interpretation and suggested translations

One translation problem is restricting hanīʾan to food’s pleasant taste.

That may seem suitable for some eating contexts, but it is insufficient in al-Nisāʾ 4:4, concerning property and a woman’s rights.

Rendering it as “lawful” does not convey its entire meaning either.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:4, permission to use the property depends on the woman’s consent. Hanīʾan describes agreeable, comfortable enjoyment.

The normative consequence of the verse’s condition should not be presented as the word’s whole lexical meaning.

Nor does hanīʾ alone mean “without effort,” “forever,” or “without any responsibility.”

In the three Paradise verses, agreeable eating and drinking are connected with prior deeds.

Suggested translations for Forqan

For hanīʾan in al-Nisāʾ 4:4, “at ease,” “agreeably,” and “without anxiety” are suitable equivalents.

Since it accompanies marīʾan, their distinction is best retained in the explanation.

A suggested rendering of:

«فَكُلُوهُ هَنِيئًا مَرِيئًا»

Fa-kulūhu hanīʾan marīʾā.

is:

“Then consume it comfortably and agreeably.”

This depends on the woman’s genuine consent in the preceding clause. It must not become unrestricted permission to consume women’s marital gifts.

For the three Hereafter verses, “agreeably and at ease” is natural and clear.

Al-Ṭūr 52:19

“Eat and drink agreeably and at ease, because of what you used to do.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:24

“Eat and drink agreeably and at ease, because of what you sent ahead in days gone by.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:43

“Eat and drink agreeably and at ease, because of what you used to do.”

Persian sometimes uses nūsh-e jān to express pleasant eating. It is natural in everyday speech, but “agreeably and at ease” offers clearer semantic scope for a direct Qur’anic translation.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents must likewise follow their context.

عَيْشٌ هَنِيءٌ can be translated as “a pleasant, comfortable life.”

هَنَأَنِي الطَّعَامُ means “the food was agreeable to me.”

تَهْنِئَة means “offering congratulations.”

مُسْتَهْنِئ in ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward’s poetic example denotes “someone requesting a gift or assistance.”

These forms share a lexical family but should not all receive a single equivalent such as happiness or agreeableness.

8. Conclusion

ه ن أ concerns agreeableness, pleasure, and ease in enjoying good.

The example attributed to al-Aʿshā of Bāhilah shows that pre-Qur’anic usage was not limited to food and could concern success and victory.

ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward’s poem illustrates giving and requesting assistance.

Classical Arabic also records congratulating, providing a livelihood, enjoying food, and applying a therapeutic substance to camel skin. Some branches can be related to benefit and ease, but their collective historical origin is uncertain.

The Qur’an uses this root four times, always as هَنِيئًا.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:4, agreeable enjoyment of property depends on a woman genuinely consenting to relinquish part of her marital gift.

In al-Ṭūr 52:19, al-Ḥāqqah 69:24, and al-Mursalāt 77:43, hanīʾ describes heavenly eating and drinking: pleasant, comfortable enjoyment in the context of reward for deeds.

Final definition for Forqan: In its principal branch, ه ن أ denotes pleasant enjoyment of good without suffering, discomfort, or something that spoils it. Qur’anic hanīʾan describes comfortable, agreeable use of a blessing. The kind of blessing, legitimacy of its use, reason for receiving it, and its duration come from context and the verse’s other elements.

Distinctions from marīʾ, ṭayyib, blessing, matāʿ, and raghad must be preserved. These words share the field of good and enjoyment but can emphasize willingness, compatibility, abundance, the blessing itself, or the manner of benefiting from it.

Agreeableness is not simply possessing something; the manner of enjoyment matters. Someone may own property without enjoying it comfortably, or attain a desire whose consequences spoil its pleasure.

Qur’anic hanīʾ portrays pleasant, comfortable enjoyment: in this life, with respect for the owner’s rights and consent; in the Hereafter, within blessings and reward for good deeds.
