Qur’anic Root KH N S (خ ن س)

Khunūs: drawing back, contracting, and becoming hidden—from stars and wild animals to the retreating whisperer

1. The root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root KH N S concerns drawing back, contracting, retreating, and becoming hidden. خَنَسَ describes someone or something withdrawing from a position or course, moving aside, or disappearing from view. خُنُوس names this retreat or concealment.

Early uses of the family concern someone falling behind or disappearing from their companions, stars becoming hidden from view, animals entering their shelters, and the shape of certain human and animal noses.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an: in the oath by الْخُنَّس in Sūrat Al-Takwīr, and in the description الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ in Sūrat Al-Nās.

These occurrences belong to one semantic family but do not have the same referent. Al-Takwīr concerns beings or bodies that move and become hidden. Al-Nās describes a whisperer who retreats or conceals himself.

Qur’anic forms

  • الْخُنَّس (al-khunnas): the plural of خَانِس, “those that retreat or become hidden”; Al-Takwīr 81:15.
  • الْخَنَّاس (al-khannās): an intensive description, “one who repeatedly or characteristically retreats or becomes hidden”; Al-Nās 114:4.

Other forms belonging to this family are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an:

  • خَنَسَ، يَخْنِسُ / يَخْنُسُ (khanasa, yakhnisu / yakhnusu): to retreat, withdraw, or become hidden.
  • خُنُوس (khunūs): retreating, contracting, or becoming hidden.
  • اِنْخَنَسَ (inkhanasa): to draw oneself back or withdraw.
  • أَخْنَسَ (akhnasa): to hold back, put aside, or remove something from its previous position.
  • خَانِس (khānis): someone or something that retreats or becomes hidden.
  • خَنَّاس (khannās): one who repeatedly or characteristically retreats or conceals himself.
  • أَخْنَس (akhnas): an adjective describing a person or animal whose nose has a particular recession or shortness.
  • خَنْسَاء (khansāʾ): the feminine of akhnas in describing the nose; also known in Arabic as a name for women.

The Qur’anic root KH N S is therefore not confined to Satan’s whispering. The occurrence in Al-Takwīr shows that the family has a meaning involving retreat and concealment independently of moral judgment.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Drawing back and becoming hidden

Arabic lexical explanations define khunūs through contraction, retreat, and concealment.

The verb khanasa can describe someone withdrawing from their companions and no longer being seen. The same family can also describe a person drawing back from a previous position or stepping aside before another.

Two related ideas appear here: withdrawing from a position one occupied, and becoming hidden from others’ view.

They need not be equally prominent in every sentence. Someone may move backward and remain visible. Something may also disappear from view without an observer seeing it move backward.

Khunūs is therefore better understood as a family of retreat, contraction, and concealment than restricted in every use to “becoming invisible.”

A witness from early Arabic speech

In a lexical report, Al-Aṣmaʿī relates hearing a Bedouin of Banū ʿUqayl address a servant travelling with him, after the servant had fallen behind:

لِمَ خَنَسْتَ عَنَّا؟

“Why did you fall behind us and disappear?”

Khanasa here concerns falling behind one’s companions and passing out of their sight.

The example matters because it places the root in an ordinary situation. It is not about evading responsibility, whispering, or morally suspect concealment: someone has fallen behind their companions during a journey.

The verb khanasa alone consequently makes no judgment about whether this retreat is good or bad.

This witness is preserved in the Arabic lexical tradition. Its transmission does not, however, establish that the sentence can certainly be dated to the pre-Islamic period.

A poetic witness

Lexical sources transmit this line from a poem attributed to Al-ʿAlāʾ ibn Al-Ḥaḍramī, a figure of the early Islamic period:

وَإِنْ دَحَسُوا بِالشَّرِّ فَاعْفُ تَكَرُّمًا
وَإِنْ خَنَسُوا عَنْكَ الْحَدِيثَ فَلَا تَسَلْ

“If they do evil secretly, forgive out of generosity; and if they withhold words from you, do not pursue the matter.”

خَنَسُوا عَنْكَ الْحَدِيثَ here concerns keeping words back or concealing them from the person addressed.

The usage shows that khanasa is not confined to bodily movement. Arabic can use the verb for withholding speech as well.

It does not follow that khanasa always means lying or deliberately suppressing the truth. Withholding speech here is one particular use within the broader field of drawing back and keeping something concealed.

Some lexical transmissions leave the poet unnamed; others name Al-ʿAlāʾ ibn Al-Ḥaḍramī, including Al-Ṣāghānī’s identification. These differences in transmission, together with variant wordings of the line, mean that attribution alone cannot establish its precise date of composition or justify presenting it as a securely dated witness preceding Qur’anic revelation.

Holding something back

The transitive أَخْنَسَ also concerns holding something back or putting it aside.

Lexical sources record أَخْنَسْتُ عَنْهُ حَقَّهُ and خَنَسَ عَنِّي حَقِّي for delaying or withholding a due. Transitive use is therefore not confined to Form IV; it is also recorded for the simple verb.

In this use, someone holds back something that should be given or discharged.

Delaying payment of a due is not the fixed meaning of every derivative of KH N S. This construction applies the sense of holding back to a matter of rights.

Nor does the example make الْخَنَّاس in Sūrat Al-Nās someone who withholds people’s financial dues. That verse has a different context and referent.

3. KH N S in the shape of the nose and descriptions of animals

Arabic also records خَنَس for a particular shape of nose. This use has the verb خَنِسَ يَخْنَسُ (khanisa, yakhnasu) and the verbal noun خَنَس (khanas). Its vocalization should be distinguished from خَنَسَ يَخْنِسُ / يَخْنُسُ, concerning retreat.

أَخْنَس describes a person or animal whose nose has a particular shortness, depression, or recession in relation to its usual shape. The feminine form is خَنْسَاء.

Lexical explanations describe this feature variously through a short nasal bridge, a receding tip, or a broad lower portion of the nose.

These differences show that khanas named a recognized visible feature, although the anatomical descriptions are not identical across the sources.

The nasal description in Labīd’s Muʿallaqah

In his Muʿallaqah, part of the tradition of pre-Islamic Arabic odes, Labīd describes a wild cow that has lost her calf:

خَنْسَاءُ ضَيَّعَتِ الْفَرِيرَ فَلَمْ يَرِمْ
عُرْضَ الشَّقَائِقِ طَوْفُهَا وَبُغَامُهَا

“A khansāʾ wild cow that had lost her calf: her wandering and lowing did not leave the stretches of ground among the sands.”

خَنْسَاء here describes the wild cow’s nose. It is neither a woman’s name nor a verb meaning to hide. فَرِير is the calf, and بُغَام the animal’s call. The line supplies poetic evidence for the family’s physical descriptive use; it does not by itself establish that the Qur’anic الْخُنَّس must refer to the same animals.

The connection with retreat

A kind of recession or contraction can also be perceived in the shape of an akhnas nose.

Its relationship with the general sense of drawing back is recognizable in that respect.

This similarity does not establish that all uses of KH N S developed from animal noses, or conversely that the nasal description arose from an older abstract meaning.

The available evidence alone does not establish such a historical sequence.

Uses concerning animals

Arabic describes wild cattle and certain gazelles with words from this family.

This usage has sometimes been connected with their noses. Animals such as gazelles and wild cattle may also enter shelters and disappear from view in response to danger or intense heat.

The two features should not be conflated without examination: nasal shape is a physical characteristic, while entering a hiding place is a behavior.

Both can belong to the lexical family, but each sentence requires us to identify which sense is intended.

The distinction matters when studying الْخُنَّس in Sūrat Al-Takwīr, since some early explanations apply the word to wild animals.

Khansāʾ: from description to personal designation

Arabic khansāʾ describes a nasal feature and is also known as a name or epithet. The celebrated poet Tamāḍur bint ʿAmr is known by the epithet Al-Khansāʾ. In her case, her personal name must be distinguished from her familiar epithet.

When khansāʾ is a person’s name or epithet, its lexical meaning alone does not establish their actual nasal shape. Such a judgment requires independent historical evidence.

Names and epithets may have descriptive origins, but their use to identify someone does not necessarily preserve every feature of the original meaning.

خَنْسَاء also does not occur in the Qur’an and must not be added to its inventory of attested derivatives.

4. فَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِالْخُنَّسِ: the oath by those that retreat

Al-Takwīr 81:15

فَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِالْخُنَّسِ

“So I swear by those that retreat and become hidden.”

This translation understands لَا as emphasizing the oath or rejecting a preceding assumption, rather than negating the oath itself. These are established analyses of لَا أُقْسِمُ. The grammatical analysis of that construction should remain distinct from the lexical meaning of الْخُنَّس.

This is the first occurrence of KH N S in the present order of the muṣḥaf. That ordering should not be equated with the two surahs’ chronology of revelation.

الْخُنَّس is the plural of خَانِس and refers to things that recede, draw back, or disappear from view.

The word alone does not name a particular star, animal, or phenomenon. Its referent must be considered in light of the next verse and the context of the oath.

Al-Takwīr 81:16

الْجَوَارِ الْكُنَّسِ

“Those that run their course and conceal themselves in their retreats.”

This verse supplies two further descriptions: الْجَوَارِ and الْكُنَّسِ.

الْجَوَارِ belongs to J R Y (ج ر ي), concerning movement and running a course.

الْكُنَّسِ belongs to K N S (ك ن س), here involving concealment or entering a hiding place.

The two consecutive verses thus depict things that move, withdraw from their visible position, and become hidden.

The central question remains: what are these moving and concealing things?

The first reading: stars and celestial bodies

Some early explanations understand الْخُنَّسِ الْجَوَارِ الْكُنَّسِ as stars or celestial bodies.

This reading draws upon several observable features of the sky.

Stars appear at night but disappear from ordinary human sight in daylight. Certain celestial bodies also show apparent changes of direction or retrograde motion relative to other stars.

Older explanations sometimes name the five planets visible to the unaided eye: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Venus, and Mercury.

At times these bodies appear to move backward against the stellar background. This is why khunūs has been connected with their apparent retrograde motion.

Another explanation takes the referent to be all stars that disappear from view as daylight arrives.

Even within the celestial reading, there are therefore two possibilities: bodies exhibiting apparent retrograde motion, or the broader class of stars and bodies that become hidden from view.

These possibilities should not be merged without justification.

Must the referent be the five planets?

The five planets are named in early explanations. The Qur’an itself, however, neither gives their number nor names them.

The lexical meaning of الْخُنَّس is also not restricted to five planets.

There is consequently insufficient basis for asserting, without further evidence, that the verse necessarily and exclusively refers to those five celestial bodies.

The planetary reading can be examined through language and observational astronomy. Identifying the verse’s precise referent must nevertheless remain distinct from stating the word’s direct meaning.

The bodies’ apparent retrograde motion should also not be equated with their physically reversing direction in space. What an observer on Earth sees is their apparent displacement against the background sky.

Does الْخُنَّس refer to black holes?

The concealment and movement of celestial bodies may sometimes invite comparison with phenomena studied in modern astrophysics.

The word KH N S itself, however, does not mean a black hole, a body with extremely strong gravity, or an object from which light cannot escape.

Nor does الْجَوَارِ الْكُنَّسِ by itself state the scientific properties of a black hole.

A verbal resemblance involving “becoming hidden” is insufficient for that identification.

Translating الْخُنَّس as “black holes,” or presenting the verse as an explicit account of that modern astrophysical phenomenon, therefore lacks sufficient linguistic and Qur’anic support.

This caution does not deny the possibility of studying the sky scientifically. It preserves the distinction between Qur’anic language and later scientific theories.

5. The second reading of الْخُنَّس: gazelles and wild cattle

A number of early explanations identify الْخُنَّسِ الْجَوَارِ الْكُنَّسِ with gazelles and wild cattle.

This reading also rests on established Arabic usage.

Wild animals move through open country and enter shelters during danger or intense heat. Arabic calls the hiding place of some such animals a كِنَاس (kinās).

الْجَوَارِ can accordingly describe running animals, while الْكُنَّسِ can describe those that conceal themselves in their retreats.

الْخُنَّس would then concern their drawing back or withdrawing.

The connection among the three descriptions

On this reading, the three descriptions convey the animals’ movement and concealment.

An animal moves in the open, then draws back or out of view and takes refuge in its shelter.

This picture accords with desert animals’ lives and established Arabic uses.

That compatibility does not make these animals the verse’s certain referent.

Arabic can apply the three descriptions to other things as well. The verses themselves do not name a gazelle or wild cow.

The role of the nasal description

Some explanations also connect the word’s application to cattle and gazelles with their noses.

The lexical relationship requires a morphological distinction: خُنْس (khuns), with an unvowelled nūn, is the plural of أَخْنَس and خَنْسَاء describing the nose. The Qur’anic خُنَّس (khunnas), however, has a doubled nūn and follows the pattern fuʿʿal as the plural of خَانِس, from khunūs.

Directly treating the doubled Qur’anic form as the plural of the nasal adjective is therefore insufficient without explanation. The animal reading remains possible if khunnas concerns their retreat and concealment. The next two descriptions likewise convey movement and entering a hiding place.

This morphological distinction does not deny the root’s nasal usage. It shows why the two related vocalizations and formations should not be treated as identical.

Contextual study must therefore consider both the Qur’anic form’s structure and its possible application to animals through their behavior. Discarding one of the language’s recorded meanings does not resolve the question.

6. Assessing the two readings in Sūrat Al-Takwīr

The following verses must also be considered when weighing these possibilities.

Al-Takwīr 81:17

وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا عَسْعَسَ

“And by the night as it approaches or retreats.”

Al-Takwīr 81:18

وَالصُّبْحِ إِذَا تَنَفَّسَ

“And by the dawn as its light spreads.”

Night and morning continue the same oath in these verses.

If الْخُنَّس means stars or celestial bodies, their movement and disappearance have a clear connection with the coming and going of night and morning.

Stars appear in the darkness of night and disappear from view with the arrival of daylight.

The immediate context can therefore strengthen the celestial reading.

This clue alone does not make the wild-animal reading impossible: the Qur’an’s oaths need not all concern one kind of being or phenomenon.

Some desert animals also regulate their activity in relation to heat, darkness, and daylight.

The mention of night and morning consequently supports the celestial reading without proving it conclusively.

Why the early disagreement matters

The disagreement shows that the lexical meaning of الْخُنَّس is broader than its proposed referent.

Both readings draw on retreat and concealment. Their main difference concerns which beings the Qur’an designates through that description.

A careful approach preserves the word’s established meaning, explains the reading more compatible with context, and does not eliminate the other possibility without decisive evidence.

For a basic translation, “those that retreat and become hidden” is appropriate.

A contextual explanation can note that stars and celestial bodies accord clearly with the following verses, while the lexical and exegetical traditions also propose gazelles and wild cattle.

The oath and the surah’s message

Al-Takwīr 81:19

إِنَّهُ لَقَوْلُ رَسُولٍ كَرِيمٍ

“Indeed, this is the speech of an honored Messenger.”

This verse clarifies part of the direction and purpose of the preceding oaths.

الْخُنَّس is one of the objects of the oath here. Its presence alone should not be taken to mean that the Qur’an is offering an independent lesson in astronomy or zoology.

Natural images serve the structure of the oath, which leads to a statement about the message.

A study of the root should therefore examine both the word’s actual meaning and its role in the surah’s full structure.

7. الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ: the retreating whisperer

Al-Nās 114:4

مِنْ شَرِّ الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ

“From the evil of the retreating whisperer.”

This is the second and final Qur’anic occurrence of KH N S.

The form here is الْخَنَّاس.

خَنَّاس follows the pattern faʿʿāl, which can convey repetition or the prominence of a behavior in Arabic.

Khannās thus describes someone whose retreat or concealment is a recognized feature of their conduct.

The pattern faʿʿāl alone does not specify the number of retreats, their timing, or their precise cause.

Waswās and khannās: two different descriptions

الْوَسْوَاس and الْخَنَّاس stand together in this verse.

وَسْوَاس belongs to W S W S (و س و س) and here denotes the whisperer.

خَنَّاس belongs to KH N S (خ ن س) and expresses retreat or concealment.

The words are therefore not synonymous.

One identifies the whisperer’s activity; the other describes his withdrawal and concealment.

Rendering both simply as “the whispering Satan” loses the distinct contribution of khannās.

“The retreating whisperer” better preserves both components.

Is the khannās always hidden?

Lexically, khunūs concerns drawing back and becoming hidden.

The word itself does not establish that the agent never appears or remains hidden at every moment.

On the contrary, withdrawal can concern something that was previously visible or active and then drew aside.

That possibility does not establish a fixed schedule on which the khannās necessarily appears and disappears.

The verse states no such timetable.

Khannās describes the whisperer’s conduct, rather than giving a precise account of the timing and number of his appearances.

8. Khannās in relation to the following verses of Sūrat Al-Nās

Al-Nās 114:5

الَّذِي يُوَسْوِسُ فِي صُدُورِ النَّاسِ

“Who whispers in the breasts of humankind.”

This verse explains the whisperer’s activity.

He whispers within a person, but the verse does not describe the physical manner of that influence.

The juxtaposition of خَنَّاس and يُوَسْوِسُ should not be taken to establish that the whispering agent necessarily enters the heart as a bodily being and then leaves it.

Some early religious explanations offer such an image. It must not be turned into the root’s direct meaning or an explicit statement of the Qur’an itself.

Here khannās continues to concern retreat and concealment, while yuwaswisu concerns hidden suggestion.

Khannās and remembering The God

Early explanations of the verse frequently state that the whisperer whispers when a person is heedless and retreats when that person remembers The God.

This explanation is compatible with the semantic field of khunūs, since drawing back and withdrawing are clear meanings of the root.

The Qur’an also instructs people to seek refuge in The God when Satan incites them, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36.

The fourth verse of Sūrat Al-Nās itself does not, however, say precisely when the khannās retreats.

Neither the phrase “upon remembering The God” nor the duration and manner of that retreat is specified there.

Three levels should therefore remain distinct: the lexical meaning of khannās, the description the Qur’an actually uses, and exegetical explanations of the conditions of the whisperer’s retreat.

“The retreating whisperer” is preferable for the basic translation. Discussion of remembering The God belongs in a study of the related verses on remembrance, heedlessness, and seeking refuge; it should not be introduced without explanation as part of khannās’s literal meaning.

Does retreat always mean complete defeat?

Arabic khunūs can express retreat, contraction, or concealment.

It does not necessarily mean destruction or final defeat.

A being may retreat and continue to exist, or disappear from view without being destroyed.

Translating khannās as “one who is destroyed,” “defeated,” or “powerless” therefore goes beyond the root’s direct meaning.

In Sūrat Al-Nās, a person seeks refuge in The God from the whisperer’s evil. The instruction takes the danger seriously without declaring the agent’s certain and permanent destruction.

Whisperers among jinn and humankind

Al-Nās 114:6

مِنَ الْجِنَّةِ وَالنَّاسِ

“From among the jinn and humankind.”

A strong reading of this clause understands the whisperers to be from both jinn and humans.

All descriptions of الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ should therefore not be restricted without justification to one fixed kind of unseen being.

A human too may incite another through deceptive speech and then retreat, withdraw from responsibility for the words, or conceal their influence.

These are examples illustrating how the description could apply to humans. The verse itself does not specify exactly how every human whisperer withdraws.

Khannās should also not be lexically defined as “a socially manipulative person.” Manipulation may involve concealment, but it is not identical to khunūs.

Some early grammatical analyses propose different connections for مِنَ الْجِنَّةِ وَالنَّاسِ. Nevertheless, understanding whisperers from among jinn and humans accords clearly with the surah’s structure and other verses such as Al-Anʿām 6:112.

9. Two Qur’anic occurrences: one root, different referents

In Al-Takwīr 81:15, الْخُنَّس is plural and denotes beings or bodies that retreat or become hidden.

In Al-Nās 114:4, الْخَنَّاس is singular and intensive, describing a characteristic of the whisperer.

Retreat and concealment connect the two.

Their differences in use are also real.

In Al-Takwīr, khunūs concerns movement and concealment in a natural scene. Al-Nās applies the same semantic field to the conduct of a whispering agent.

This shared field does not mean that the whisperer moves exactly like stars or hides in a physical shelter like a gazelle.

The image of withdrawal can apply in different situations without transferring every feature of one referent to another.

Is khunūs always blameworthy?

Arabic usage shows that it is not.

Someone falling behind their companions, stars disappearing in daylight, or an animal entering its shelter is not intrinsically doing something blameworthy.

In Sūrat Al-Nās, however, khannās describes an agent of evil and whispering.

The negative character of this occurrence comes from مِنْ شَرِّ, “from the evil of,” and its connection with the whisperer, rather than the independent meaning of KH N S.

This distinction prevents every concealment or retreat from being treated as evidence of scheming, fear, lying, or wickedness.

10. Comparing related roots

KH N S (خُنُوس) and K N S (كُنُوس): retreat and entering a hiding place

These roots stand together in Al-Takwīr 81:15 and Al-Takwīr 81:16.

Khunūs concerns retreat, withdrawal, and concealment.

Kunūs, in relation to animals, expresses entering a shelter or hiding place.

Explanations concerning stars also use kunūs for their disappearance from view.

The families therefore overlap in the image of concealment.

They should nevertheless not be treated as wholly synonymous.

Khunūs emphasizes drawing back or withdrawing; animal-related kunūs foregrounds the relationship with a hiding place.

No exceptionless boundary should be constructed in which khunūs always precedes kunūs or kunūs is always its final stage. The full range of usage does not establish that fixed sequence.

KH N S (خُنُوس) and KH F Y (خَفَاء): retreat and hiddenness

Arabic khafāʾ concerns being hidden or not apparent.

Khunūs too may lead to concealment, but drawing back or withdrawing also belongs to its principal uses.

Something may be hidden from the outset without first appearing and then retreating.

Conversely, something may retreat while remaining partly visible.

Khunūs and khafāʾ consequently share part of a semantic field without being identical.

KH N S (خُنُوس) and GH Y B (غَيْب / غَيْبَة): becoming hidden and being beyond observation

GH Y B concerns absence, concealment, and being beyond direct observation.

Some uses of khunūs likewise concern disappearing from view.

Absence is not always the result of retreat. Something may be unobservable to a person from the beginning or simply lie beyond their field of vision.

Khunūs may also concern backward movement without complete concealment.

Rendering khannās in Sūrat Al-Nās merely as “absent” would therefore underrepresent its feature of withdrawal.

KH N S (خُنُوس) and R J ʿ (رُجُوع): returning and drawing back

Arabic rujūʿ concerns returning to a previous place or condition.

In some uses, khunūs also concerns moving backward or returning.

This semantic proximity helps explain why khunūs has been connected with the apparent retrograde motion of certain celestial bodies.

Not every return, however, is khunūs.

Someone may return openly and quickly to a previous position without withdrawal or concealment being involved.

“Returning” can consequently be appropriate in some contexts without covering the word’s entire semantic field.

KH N S (خُنُوس) and W S W S (وَسْوَسَة): retreat and hidden suggestion

These roots appear together in Al-Nās 114:4.

Arabic waswasah concerns quiet, hidden sound and, in relation to humans, the suggestion of words or inward thought.

Khunūs expresses retreat and concealment.

One therefore expresses the activity of suggestion; the other describes withdrawal.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ.

Rendering khannās merely as “one who whispers secretly” may leave the meaning of retreat less apparent.

Conversely, khannās should not be defined as someone who always retreats before whispering or necessarily hides after every whisper. The verse states no precise chronological sequence.

KH N S (خُنُوس) and N Z GH (نَزْغ): withdrawal and incitement

Qur’anic nazgh concerns inciting or stirring up.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36 speak of Satan’s incitement and instruct people to seek refuge in The God.

Khunūs in Sūrat Al-Nās also concerns the whispering agent, but the word itself does not mean inciting.

الْخَنَّاس alone should therefore not be translated as “the inciter.”

The description stands beside الْوَسْوَاس and conveys another characteristic of the same agent.

11. Challenges of understanding and translation

Restricting KH N S to Satan

Khannās is very familiar in religious language, which may lead readers to restrict the whole family to Satan’s whispering.

The use in Al-Takwīr and early Arabic witnesses, however, show that the root fundamentally concerns retreat and concealment.

Lexical understanding should therefore begin with the wider linguistic field, rather than only its moral and religious application.

Turning khannās into an invariably invisible being

In Sūrat Al-Nās, khannās concerns concealment and retreat.

The description alone does not establish that the whispering agent is invisible at every moment or never acts openly.

On the reading that the surah’s final verse identifies human and jinn whisperers, their different instances cannot all be explained through one fixed mode of presence and concealment.

Asserting a definite time for the retreat

Early religious explanations describe Satan retreating when The God is remembered.

This interpretation can be examined alongside verses concerning seeking refuge and remembering The God.

The expression الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ itself does not state the precise time of that retreat.

A translation should therefore not insert “when The God is remembered” into the verse without explanation.

Restricting Al-Takwīr’s word to one scientific phenomenon

Understanding الْخُنَّس in Al-Takwīr 81:15 as stars or celestial bodies has linguistic and contextual support.

The word alone does not establish whether the referent is certainly five planets, all stars, or a particular astronomical phenomenon.

The wild-animal reading also appears in early explanations and should not be discarded without examination.

The root’s meaning should remain distinct from proposed identifications of its referent.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

الْخُنَّس in Al-Takwīr 81:15

Proposed basic equivalent:

“Those that retreat and become hidden.”

For the verse:

“So I swear by those that retreat and become hidden.”

This preserves the root’s principal meaning without committing the reader to one particular referent from the outset.

A contextual explanation can identify stars and celestial bodies as an important reading, particularly in relation to movement and disappearance in daylight. Early explanations also propose gazelles and wild cattle.

If the celestial reading is selected after fuller study for the interpretive translation, “stars that retreat and become hidden” can serve as a contextual rendering. “Stars,” however, does not follow from the structure of الْخُنَّس alone.

For a word-by-word box:

“الْخُنَّسِ: those that retreat or become hidden; a description of beings or bodies that withdraw from view.”

الْخَنَّاس in Al-Nās 114:4

Proposed basic equivalent:

“The retreating one” or “one who repeatedly withdraws.”

In the complete expression:

“From the evil of the retreating whisperer.”

“Whisperer” conveys الْوَسْوَاس, while “retreating” conveys الْخَنَّاس.

“The whisperer who hides” is also defensible. “Retreating,” however, makes the connection with drawing back in the KH N S family clearer.

For a word-by-word box:

“الْخَنَّاسِ: one who repeatedly retreats or becomes hidden; here, a description of the whisperer who withdraws.”

الْخَنَّاس alone should not be rendered as “Satan,” “deceiver,” “liar,” or “the agent of evil thoughts.” These identify a referent or attributes understood from context and other evidence, rather than the root’s direct meaning.

13. Conclusion

Arabic KH N S concerns retreat, contraction, withdrawal, and becoming hidden.

These meanings appear in everyday uses such as falling behind one’s companions or withholding words. Animal descriptions also attest uses involving both a particular nasal shape and movement or concealment.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Takwīr 81:15, الْخُنَّس describes beings or bodies that retreat or become hidden. The next verse connects them with movement and entering a retreat. Two early readings propose stars or celestial bodies on one hand, and wild animals on the other. The context of night and morning strengthens the celestial reading, but the word alone does not conclusively identify its referent.

In Al-Nās 114:4, الْخَنَّاس describes the whisperer. The form conveys the prominence or recurrence of retreat, without itself determining its time, cause, or manner.

Khannās and waswās express different concepts. Waswās concerns hidden suggestion, while khannās concerns retreat or concealment. Their combination brings two characteristics together in one agent without turning either word’s meaning into the other’s.

Retreat is also neither good nor bad in its lexical meaning alone. Stars disappearing, animals withdrawing, or a person falling behind companions do not necessarily carry moral judgments. In Al-Nās, blame comes from the context of evil and whispering.

For Forqan, the basic definition is: khunūs is drawing back, contracting, or withdrawing from an exposed position, which may be accompanied by disappearing from view. Khunnas denotes those that retreat or become hidden; khannās describes someone or something whose conduct is characterized by retreat and concealment.

This definition accommodates both Qur’anic occurrences without restricting the root to Satan, stars, wild animals, or a particular scientific theory.
