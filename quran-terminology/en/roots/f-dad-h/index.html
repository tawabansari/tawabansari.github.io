The root f-ḍ-ḥ — Exposure of something shameful, disgrace, and damage to reputation

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَفْضَحُونِ
(tafdahuni)
Do not disgrace me: in a prohibition

فَضِيحَة / اِفْتَضَحَ
(fadihah / iftadaha)
Disgrace and becoming disgraced

أَفْضَح / فُضْحَة
(afdah / fudhah)
Color and brightness senses

The triliteral Arabic root ف ض ح concerns the exposure of something hidden and, in social usage, becoming disgraced or disgracing someone. فَضَحَ فُلَانًا describes exposing someone to shame and dishonor, especially by revealing something people regard as reprehensible.

The same family includes فَضِيحَة, disgrace or scandal; فَاضِح, one who disgraces; and اِفْتَضَحَ, to become disgraced.

The family is not confined to moral wrongdoing. Early Arabic also records فَضَحَ الصُّبْحُ and أَفْضَحَ الصُّبْحُ for the appearance of dawn’s light. Here something becomes visible that could not previously be seen in the darkness.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as تَفْضَحُونِ in Al-Hijr 15:68. Lot says this when a group from the town approaches his guests with wrongful intentions: “These are my guests, so do not disgrace me.”

Disgracing someone does not necessarily mean exposing that person’s own wrongdoing. Others’ misconduct can expose a person to shame and humiliation—for example, a powerless host whose guests are abused in his home.

We must therefore distinguish revealing the truth, disgracing someone, committing wrongdoing, and suffering harm through others’ misconduct.

1. The root in early Arabic

Something hidden becomes apparent

One principal sense of ف ض ح is becoming visible and emerging from concealment.

Early lexicons record فَضَحَ الصُّبْحُ and أَفْضَحَ الصُّبْحُ for the appearance of dawn’s light.

Many things cannot be seen in darkness. When morning brightens, what was hidden becomes visible.

In فَضَحَهُ الصُّبْحُ, dawn’s light makes visible someone previously concealed by darkness.

Dawn is not morally punishing the person; its light simply reveals them.

This image helps explain the relationship between exposure and disgrace: something a person does not want others to know may become apparent and bring them shame.

That relationship does not prove that the social sense of disgrace developed at a particular stage from the expression about dawn. Establishing the chronology requires independent evidence.

Disgracing someone and exposing misconduct

In social usage, فَضَحَهُ means to disgrace someone.

اِفْتَضَحَ الرَّجُلُ can describe a person whose misconduct has become known and brought them disrepute.

In Maqayis al-Lughah, Ibn Faris distinguishes two related branches of this root.

The first concerns exposure, usually associated with something reprehensible.

The second concerns coloration found in certain animals and fruits.

This analysis shows that exposure alone does not exhaust the social meaning: reprehensibility and shame also matter.

When someone’s hidden talent becomes apparent, we do not ordinarily say that they have been disgraced. When their misconduct becomes known, however, the expression may apply.

Thus, in human affairs, فضح usually carries social and evaluative implications absent from the general verb “reveal.”

A pre-Islamic poetic example: al-A‘sha

The renowned pre-Islamic poet al-A‘sha says in a satirical poem:

لَأُمُّكَ بِالْهِجَاءِ أَحَقُّ مِنَّا
لِمَا أَبْلَتْكَ مِنْ شَوْطِ الْفِضَاحِ

La-ummuka bi-al-hija'i ahaqqu minna / li-ma ablatka min shawti al-fidahi

Translation: “Your mother is more deserving of satire than we are, because of the disgrace she has brought upon you.”

The verse belongs to an exchange of satire and response to an opponent.

هِجَاء is poetry used to criticize, reproach, or ridicule someone.

Here الفِضَاح concerns conduct or a situation the poet presents as disgraceful.

The root’s use in this example is not confined to revealing a physical object; it concerns social shame and disrepute.

The poem exemplifies Arabic satire. Without independent evidence, the poet’s accusation should not be treated as historical fact about the person attacked.

The verse appears in al-A‘sha’s collected poetry and early lexical sources, including Kitab al-‘Ayn.

Its wording varies: some quotations have أَوْلَتْكَ, whereas the collected-poetry version has أَبْلَتْكَ. Variations in شَوْطِ الْفِضَاحِ are also reported in some copies.

Despite these differences, الفضاح in a context of satire and disrepute is important evidence for the root’s usage in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It indicates that disgrace and dishonor belonged to the semantic field before the compilation of lexicons, although pre-Islamic poetry itself reaches us through later transmission.

Disgrace and fear of dishonor

Another rajaz passage quoted in lexicons reads:

قَوْمٌ إِذَا مَا رَهِبُوا الْفَضَائِحَا
عَلَى النِّسَاءِ لَبِسُوا الصَّفَائِحَا

Qawmun idha ma rahibu al-fada'iha / 'ala al-nisa'i labisu al-safa'iha

Translation: “A people who, when they feared disgrace concerning their women, put on armor to defend them.”

الفَضَائِح is the plural of فَضِيحَة.

In this context, الصَّفَائِح refers to armor and protective equipment worn in battle.

The passage connects fear of disgrace with a group’s social conduct.

In a society where protecting family and guests was considered a social responsibility, inability to fulfill that responsibility could bring disrepute.

The speaker and precise date of this passage are uncertain, so it should not be labeled conclusive pre-Islamic evidence.

Its value lies chiefly in illustrating the established use of فضائح in early Arabic language and culture.

2. Other branches of the root

The appearance of light

Lexicons record the following expressions:

فَضَحَ الصُّبْحُ — dawn’s light appeared.

أَفْضَحَ الصُّبْحُ — morning became apparent and bright.

فَضَحَ الْقَمَرُ النُّجُومَ — the moon’s light overpowered the stars’ light, making them difficult to see.

The last expression does not morally disgrace the stars; the moon’s brightness outshines their weaker light.

These uses show the family’s extension into visibility, becoming apparent, and the predominance of light.

They should not be transferred to the Qur’anic verse without contextual support. Al-Hijr 15:68 concerns human conduct and Lot’s possible disgrace before his guests, not dawn or moonlight.

أَفْضَح as a color description

Another branch concerns color.

Lexicons record الفُضْحَة for a muted whiteness and also a dusty coloration mixed with darkness, including descriptions of certain animals.

أَفْضَح and فَضْحَاء are also attested as descriptions of such coloration.

أَفْضَحَ الْبُسْرُ is used when redness appears in unripe dates.

In these uses, أفضح does not mean “more disgraceful.”

It describes an aspect of color or appearance.

The distinction matters: shared consonants alone do not make every derivative identical in meaning.

A semantic connection between emerging color and appearance may be explored, but the historical development of the branches should not be asserted without evidence.

3. Linguistic forms and derivatives

The principal attested forms include:

  • فَضَحَ: disgraced someone; exposed reprehensible conduct or a shameful situation.
  • يَفْضَحُ: disgraces.
  • فَضْح: disgracing; exposing something that causes shame.
  • فَضِيحَة: disgrace, scandal, or something discrediting.
  • فَضَائِح: plural of فضيحة; scandals or disgraceful matters.
  • فَاضِح: one who disgraces.
  • مَفْضُوح: someone disgraced or exposed to disgrace.
  • فَضَّحَ: in Form II, disgraced or intensified exposure.
  • تَفْضِيح: the act of disgracing.
  • اِفْتَضَحَ: became disgraced; had something reprehensible exposed.
  • اِفْتِضَاح: becoming disgraced or exposed to shame.
  • فَضُوح and فَضَّاح: one who disgraces others; فَضُوح is also attested for a disgraced person.
  • فُضْحَة: in another branch, muted whiteness or a dusty coloration mixed with darkness.
  • أَفْضَح and فَضْحَاء: in color usage, descriptions of a particular coloration.

The nouns فضيحة, فضح, فضائح, and فاضح do not occur in the Qur’an.

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is تَفْضَحُونِ.

This Qur’anic form must therefore remain distinct from the other forms attested in Arabic.

The core meaning relevant to the Qur’anic usage may be defined as:

Exposing someone to shame and dishonor, especially through the revelation or creation of a situation regarded socially as reprehensible and disgraceful.

Disgracing someone in this sense need not mean exposing their own wrongdoing.

Lot’s story clearly illustrates this distinction.

4. Qur’anic usage: فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

Al-Hijr 15:68

قَالَ إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

Qala inna ha'ula'i dayfi fa-la tafdahun

Translation: “He said: These are my guests, so do not disgrace me.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The speaker is Lot.

He addresses a group from the town who have approached his guests.

Al-Hijr 15:67 describes the townspeople coming joyfully.

In verse 68, Lot asks them not to mistreat his guests and disgrace him.

Three parties must be distinguished to understand the sentence:

First, the guests staying with Lot.

Second, the people approaching them.

Third, Lot, who sees himself as responsible for his guests.

إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي

Lot begins by identifying the guests:

«إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي»

Inna ha'ula'i dayfi

That is: these are my guests.

He is not simply announcing the presence of some people in his home. In context, the identification supports his request that they be treated with respect.

Someone who welcomes a guest ordinarily expects the guest to be safe in their home and free from others’ abuse.

If a group invades a home and threatens its guests, the host may feel ashamed and helpless before them.

That feeling does not necessarily make the host culpable. The host may do everything possible to protect the guests but lack the power to resist the attackers.

In such a situation, the attackers’ conduct creates the prospect of disgrace and humiliation.

فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

Lot asks the people not to disgrace him.

Lot himself is the verb’s object.

But the situation giving rise to that disgrace is the people’s treatment of his guests.

The verse is therefore not simply about disclosing a private secret of Lot’s.

Lot fears that his guests will be assaulted and humiliated in his home and that he will be unable to protect them.

A simpler example can clarify the relationship.

Suppose someone shelters a traveler overnight. A group comes to the house and threatens to take the traveler away by force.

The host says: “This person is my guest; do not shame and dishonor me before him.”

This does not mean the host has done wrong. The host asks the attackers to respect the guest and not place the host in a position of helplessness and shame.

In Lot’s story, too, disgrace must be understood through the relationship between host, guest, and those making threats.

5. The surrounding verses: threats to the guests and Lot’s responsibility

Reading verse 68 alone is insufficient for a precise understanding of فلا تفضحون.

Other Qur’anic passages illuminate Lot’s situation and that of his guests.

Lot’s concern when the guests arrive

Hud 11:77

وَلَمَّا جَاءَتْ رُسُلُنَا لُوطًا سِيءَ بِهِمْ وَضَاقَ بِهِمْ ذَرْعًا وَقَالَ هَٰذَا يَوْمٌ عَصِيبٌ

Wa-lamma ja'at rusuluna Lutan si'a bihim wa-daqa bihim dhar'an wa-qala hadha yawmun 'asib

Translation: “And when Our Messengers came to Lot, he was distressed on their account and felt unable to cope with them, and said: This is a difficult day.”

The verse shows that Lot was concerned about the guests’ safety from the outset.

His concern related to the townspeople’s conduct.

The plea فلا تفضحون is not a sudden response without a background: he faces a threat he had already feared.

The people hurry toward the guests

Hud 11:78

وَجَاءَهُ قَوْمُهُ يُهْرَعُونَ إِلَيْهِ وَمِنْ قَبْلُ كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ ۚ قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ هَٰؤُلَاءِ بَنَاتِي هُنَّ أَطْهَرُ لَكُمْ ۖ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي ۖ أَلَيْسَ مِنْكُمْ رَجُلٌ رَشِيدٌ

Wa-ja'ahu qawmuhu yuhra'una ilayhi wa-min qablu kanu ya'maluna al-sayyi'ati qala ya qawmi ha'ula'i banati hunna atharu lakum fa-ittaqu Allaha wa-la tukhzuni fi dayfi a-laysa minkum rajulun rashid

Translation: “And his people came rushing toward him, having previously committed evil deeds. He said: My people, these are my daughters; they are purer for you. So be mindful of The God, and do not disgrace me concerning my guests. Is there not among you a man of sound judgment?”

The same situation is presented from another angle.

The people hurry toward him, and Lot asks them to observe taqwa and not humiliate him before his guests.

The expression فِي ضَيْفِي matters.

Al-Hijr 15:68 says:

«فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ»

Fa-la tafdahun

Hud 11:78, however, says:

«وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي»

Wa-la tukhzuni fi dayfi

That is: do not humiliate me concerning my guests.

The connection shows that the central issue is the treatment of the guests and Lot’s responsibility toward them.

The people’s intentions toward the guests

Al-Qamar 54:37

وَلَقَدْ رَاوَدُوهُ عَنْ ضَيْفِهِ فَطَمَسْنَا أَعْيُنَهُمْ فَذُوقُوا عَذَابِي وَنُذُرِ

Wa-la-qad rawaduhu 'an dayfihi fa-tamasna a'yunahum fa-dhuqu 'adhabi wa-nudhur

Translation: “And they sought to take his guests from him, so We effaced their eyes. Taste My punishment and My warnings.”

This makes clear that the people had not come merely for an ordinary visit or friendly conversation.

They sought access to Lot’s guests.

Thus فلا تفضحون occurs in a context of threats and assault.

Details of conduct, speech, or the form of assault not stated in the verse should not be invented and added to it.

What is clear is their wrongful intention toward the guests and Lot’s attempt to prevent it.

Concern about lacking the power to resist

Hud 11:80

قَالَ لَوْ أَنَّ لِي بِكُمْ قُوَّةً أَوْ آوِي إِلَىٰ رُكْنٍ شَدِيدٍ

Qala law anna li bikum quwwatan aw awi ila ruknin shadid

Translation: “He said: If only I had strength against you, or could take refuge in a mighty support.”

Lot’s words show that he saw himself as powerless against the group’s pressure.

He wanted to protect his guests but lacked the means to resist those people.

His fear of disgrace is therefore also related to that practical inability.

Yet being unable to prevent others’ oppression does not make someone a participant in it.

In Lot’s story, responsibility for the misconduct lies with those intending to assault the guests, not with the guests or the person trying to defend them.

6. The distinction between فَضْح and خِزْي

The next verse immediately states:

Al-Hijr 15:69

وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ

Wa-ittaqu Allaha wa-la tukhzun

Translation: “And be mindful of The God, and do not humiliate and dishonor me.”

Here تُخْزُونِ belongs to the root خ ز ي.

That root concerns humiliation, disgrace, degradation, and falling into a shameful situation.

ف ض ح and خ ز ي are very close in meaning, but not identical in every use.

In فضح, the exposure or creation of a situation that brings someone disgrace is prominent.

In خزي, humiliation, abasement, and the degrading consequence may be more prominent.

For example, someone’s misconduct may become known and damage their reputation.

The exposure and the resulting disgrace are then related.

But someone may also be humiliated by others without any hidden wrongdoing of their own being exposed.

Thus خزي does not necessarily require the disclosure of a secret or previous wrongdoing.

The two expressions stand alongside one another in Lot’s story:

«فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ»

Fa-la tafdahun

and

«وَلَا تُخْزُونِ»

Wa-la tukhzun

Their combination conveys Lot’s intense concern and his request that the people be restrained from their misconduct.

An artificial boundary should not be imposed, as though one word meant only revealing a secret and the other only formal punishment.

Their semantic fields overlap; the precise distinction depends on construction and context.

7. Does disgrace always indicate personal guilt?

A central issue is the distinction between committing wrongdoing and being exposed to disgrace.

In some Arabic uses, افتضح الرجل describes someone whose own misconduct has become known.

But in Al-Hijr 15:68, Lot asks others not to disgrace him.

He is not asking them to conceal his hidden wrongdoing.

He asks them not to mistreat his guests.

Two situations must therefore remain distinct, both linguistically and logically.

In the first, someone has acted wrongly, and its exposure brings them disrepute.

In the second, someone is exposed to shame and humiliation through others’ oppression or misconduct.

A person subjected to assault, threats, or abuse may, for example, feel ashamed when the incident becomes known.

That feeling does not mean they themselves did anything wrong.

Responsibility for wrongful conduct rests with the person who commits it.

This distinction is especially important in Lot’s story, which concerns efforts to protect guests, not the guilt of either guests or host.

The concept of disgrace must therefore not become a means of blaming people who have been wronged.

8. Revealing truth and disgracing someone: are they always the same?

فضح relates to exposure, but not every disclosure disgraces someone.

Someone may reveal a scientific achievement, a good deed, or an unknown truth.

Such disclosure need not belong to the semantic field of disgrace.

Conversely, revealing misconduct may disgrace its perpetrator.

Even then, several matters must be distinguished:

  • Whether the allegation is true.
  • The need to inform others.
  • The discloser’s intention.
  • The rights of those affected by publication.

For example, someone who knows of misuse of public assets may report it with reliable evidence to prevent further injustice. Disclosure alone does not make that conduct wrong.

Conversely, inventing and spreading a baseless rumor to humiliate someone is more than disclosure: it involves fabricating an accusation and damaging another person’s reputation.

The Qur’an connects these distinctions with other principles governing speech and social conduct.

An-Nisa 4:148 discusses publicly voicing evil and the exception for someone who has been wronged.

Al-Hujurat 49:12 prohibits spying and backbiting.

An-Nur 24:19 also warns against wishing fahsha to spread among believers.

These verses show that publishing statements about others cannot be assessed through one simple rule about revealing or concealing.

None of those verses, however, contains a derivative of ف ض ح. Their relevance is ethical and thematic, not derivational.

Nor can فلا تفضحون itself establish a rule that every unwelcome truth must remain hidden.

The verse’s immediate concern is preventing mistreatment of Lot’s guests and his humiliation before them.

9. Morphology and grammar of تَفْضَحُونِ

ف ض ح is a sound triliteral root.

Its basic verb is:

فَضَحَ يَفْضَحُ

Its verbal noun is:

فَضْح

These are the basic verb and verbal noun of this family.

The Qur’anic تَفْضَحُونِ comes from this basic verb, not Form II or Form IV.

The ordinary form addressing a group is:

تَفْضَحُونَ

That is: you disgrace.

In the verse, however, the verb follows prohibitive لَا:

«فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ»

Fa-la tafdahun

Here لا expresses a prohibition and makes the imperfect verb jussive.

In the five verb forms with an indicative nun ending, the sign of the jussive is removal of that nun.

The remaining nun in تفضحونِ is the protective nun associated with the attached first-person object pronoun.

The object ya is omitted in the verse’s customary written form but remains understood grammatically.

The expression therefore means “do not disgrace me,” not merely “do not disgrace.”

Readings of the object ya

In the common Hafs reading, the form is تَفْضَحُونِ.

In Ya‘qub’s reading, the final ya is pronounced explicitly:

تَفْضَحُونِي

The difference concerns retaining or omitting the object ya and does not change the sentence’s essential meaning.

In both readings, Lot asks the people not to disgrace him.

The initial فَـ in فلا also connects the request to the preceding statement:

«إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي»

Inna ha'ula'i dayfi

Since these are my guests, then, do not disgrace me before them.

The connection clarifies the relationship between hospitality and Lot’s request.

10. Comparison with related roots

ف ض ح (فَضَحَ) and ك ش ف (كَشَفَ)

كَشْف concerns removing a covering and revealing something.

The disclosure may be physical or figurative.

The Qur’an speaks, for example, of removing affliction and making certain matters apparent.

In social usage, however, فضح ordinarily involves shame and disgrace.

Something may become apparent without any reprehensible aspect.

Not every disclosure is therefore an act of disgrace, although disgrace may result from exposing a hidden matter.

ف ض ح (فَضَحَ) and خ ز ي (خَزِيَ)

These appear alongside one another in Lot’s story.

فضح concerns disgracing someone and exposing them to shame.

خزي may emphasize humiliation, abasement, and degrading consequences.

Their conjunction in Al-Hijr 15:68 and Al-Hijr 15:69 indicates semantic closeness, not complete identity.

ف ض ح (فَضَحَ) and ع ي ب (عَابَ)

عَيْب concerns a defect or deficiency.

Al-Kahf 18:79 discusses damaging a boat and making it defective.

Having a defect is not the same as being disgraced.

Something may have a defect that remains hidden.

Someone may also be exposed to disgrace through others’ conduct without having committed a moral wrong.

Thus فضح should not be defined solely as “exposing someone’s actual fault.”

ف ض ح (فَضَحَ) and ب ه ت (بَهَتَ)

بُهْتَان concerns a false accusation: attributing something untrue to another person.

Such an accusation may disgrace someone.

But not every act of disgracing necessarily involves false accusation.

Actual misconduct may become known; in that case, the report is not necessarily false.

Conversely, fabricating an accusation to damage someone’s reputation is false accusation and may cause unjust disgrace.

These concepts may coincide in some situations, but they are not identical.

ف ض ح (فَضَحَ) and س ت ر (سَتَرَ)

سَتْر concerns covering and keeping something concealed.

One branch of فضح concerns the exposure of what was hidden.

In that respect, they stand in semantic opposition.

But not every concealment is commendable, nor every disclosure reprehensible.

Concealing oppression to prevent accountability is not the same as respecting privacy.

Revealing truth to prevent harm is likewise different from circulating rumors to humiliate someone.

The opposition between concealment and exposure alone cannot determine the moral value of every action.

ف ض ح (فَضَحَ) and ف ص ح (فَصُحَ)

These roots are similar in spelling and sound.

Lexicons sometimes record both فَضَحَ الصُّبْحُ and فَصَحَ الصُّبْحُ for dawn becoming apparent.

That similarity does not make all their uses identical.

In its Qur’anic usage, ف ص ح concerns clear and eloquent speech, as in أَفْصَحُ مِنِّي لِسَانًا in Al-Qasas 28:34.

But ف ض ح in Al-Hijr 15:68 concerns disgracing someone and exposing them to shame.

Eloquence and disgrace should therefore not be confused because their Arabic consonants resemble one another.

11. Difficulties in understanding and translation

The first error is equating every appearance or disclosure with disgrace.

Arabic attests uses concerning dawn’s light and emerging color, but not every appearance implies moral wrongdoing.

The second error is assuming that every disgraced person must be guilty.

In Lot’s story, he asks the people not to mistreat his guests and disgrace him.

Someone may therefore be exposed to shame through others’ oppression without being the perpetrator.

The third error is restricting فلا تفضحون to disclosure of a personal secret.

Al-Hijr 15:68 concerns threatening conduct toward guests and the host’s responsibility.

The fourth error is translating تفضحون without its object.

A first-person pronoun is present grammatically: Lot says “do not disgrace me.”

The fifth error is treating فضح and خزي as completely identical.

They are very close, but their constructions and range of use can express different aspects of disgrace, humiliation, and degradation.

The sixth error is turning the verse into a universal command to conceal every wrongful act.

Lot asks the people to stop their aggression against his guests; he does not permit it on condition that nobody hears about it.

The central concern is preventing wrongdoing, not simply keeping it out of public view.

Finally, a wronged person’s shame must not be used to shift the perpetrator’s responsibility onto them.

Understanding this Qur’anic story requires distinguishing the aggressor, the person harmed, and the person trying to prevent that harm.

12. Suggested translations for Forqan

In social usage, فَضَحَ may be rendered “disgraced,” “dishonored,” or “exposed someone to shame.”

فَضِيحَة may be “disgrace,” “scandal,” or “a disgraceful matter.”

اِفْتَضَحَ means “became disgraced” or “had one’s misconduct exposed,” with the precise sense depending on context.

In the light-related usage, فَضَحَ الصُّبْحُ means “dawn appeared” or “the light of morning became apparent.”

The sole Qur’anic occurrence, however, concerns disgracing Lot in connection with his guests.

For Al-Hijr 15:68:

«قَالَ إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ ضَيْفِي فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ»

Qala inna ha'ula'i dayfi fa-la tafdahun

Suggested translation:

“He said: These are my guests, so do not disgrace me.”

This is brief, clear, and faithful to the verse’s construction.

An accompanying explanation of the contextual meaning might say:

“Do not shame and dishonor me before my guests by mistreating them.”

That explanation should not replace the direct translation, since the details of the people’s conduct are understood from the surrounding passages.

A short lexical explanation can distinguish the root’s meaning from this occurrence as follows:

Word form: تَفْضَحُونِ

Root: ف ض ح

Brief root meaning: Exposure of something shameful; disgracing and exposing someone to dishonor.

Meaning in this verse: Do not disgrace me by mistreating my guests; a jussive imperfect governed by prohibitive لا, addressed to a group.

The brief explanation stays focused on this usage; the poetic evidence, comparison with خزي, and ethical responsibility are separate discussions.

13. Conclusion

The Arabic root ف ض ح concerns exposure and becoming apparent. In social usage, that exposure ordinarily involves something reprehensible and the consequences of shame and disrepute.

Al-A‘sha’s poetic example places فضاح in the field of satire, disrepute, and disgrace in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Early lexicons also record uses concerning dawn, moonlight outshining the stars, and emerging color. The historical order of all these branches cannot, however, be established without independent evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Hijr 15:68.

Lot asks the people not to mistreat his guests and disgrace him.

Other passages in the story show that the people sought access to the guests and that Lot feared for their safety.

The disgrace in question does not mean exposure of Lot’s own wrongdoing; it concerns others’ threatening conduct and his responsibility toward his guests.

The core social meaning is the exposure or creation of a situation that subjects someone to shame and dishonor.

The wrongful conduct itself, its exposure, and the person harmed by its consequences must nevertheless be distinguished.

This prevents us from automatically treating everyone who is ashamed or disgraced as guilty.

It also shows that preventing disgrace in Lot’s story is not merely hiding wrongdoing from public view. Lot wants the threatening behavior to stop and his guests’ dignity to be respected.

In context, فلا تفضحون therefore relates to social responsibility, protecting guests, and preventing assault and humiliation alongside its lexical sense of “do not disgrace me”—without artificially inserting all these contextual ideas into the root’s definition.
