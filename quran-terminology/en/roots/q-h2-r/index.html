1. Fundamental meaning and linguistic background

The Arabic root ق ه ر (Q H R) denotes overpowering another so that the other party cannot effectively resist the dominant power or carry out their own will.

In poetry transmitted under the name of al-Mukhabbal al-Saʿdī, who lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, this family describes defeat and loss of social standing. Without establishing when the verse was composed, it cannot prove pre-Qur’anic usage. Arabic dictionaries also record prevailing, bringing under control, and compelling someone against their wishes.

Independent, datable evidence for the precise pre-Qur’anic semantic range is limited. The Qur’an itself nevertheless attests the root in both human domination and descriptions of divine supremacy.

Qur’anic forms and frequencies: ten occurrences in ten verses: one verb, three active participles, and six intensive forms. Case endings do not create a different root or morphological pattern.

  • تَقْهَرْ (taqhar): Form I active imperfect, second-person masculine singular, made jussive by prohibitive lā; once, Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9. Lā taqhar: do not subjugate.
  • الْقَاهِرُ (al-qāhiru): definite nominative singular active participle on the pattern fāʿil; twice, Al-Anʿām 6:18 and Al-Anʿām 6:61, describing The God: dominant.
  • قَاهِرُونَ (qāhirūna): nominative sound masculine plural of qāhir; once, Al-Aʿrāf 7:127, in Pharaoh’s speech: dominant and in control. This is active, not “those who have been subjugated.”
  • الْقَهَّارُ (al-qahhāru): definite nominative singular intensive form on the pattern faʿʿāl; four times, Yūsuf 12:39, Al-Raʿd 13:16, Ṣād 38:65, and Al-Zumar 39:4.
  • الْقَهَّارِ (al-qahhāri): the same intensive form in the genitive; twice, Ibrāhīm 14:48 and Ghāfir 40:16, following li-llāhi l-wāḥidi. All six occurrences describe The God. This article consistently translates the title as “the Invincible,” understood as possessing all-encompassing dominance.

Forms known in Arabic but absent from the Qur’an include qahr, dominance or subjugation; maqhūr, defeated or under domination; inqihār, being subjugated; and qahran, by force and without consent.

2. Core meaning and early Arabic evidence

Ibn Fāris foregrounds prevailing and superiority. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains qahr through prevailing and subduing together, while recognizing usage for either meaning individually.

The Arabic expression أَخَذَهُ قَهْرًا (akhadhahu qahran) means taking someone or something by force and without consent.

One party’s power prevails to such an extent that the other party’s consent does not determine whether the action occurs.

Qahr need not involve a physical blow. Political power, social position, economic dependence, or control of resources can be used to subjugate another.

A verse attributed to al-Mukhabbal al-Saʿdī, satirizing al-Zibriqān ibn Badr, whose name was Ḥuṣayn, reads:

تَمَنَّى حُصَيْنٌ أَنْ يَسُودَ جِذَاعَهُ فَأَمْسَى حُصَيْنٌ قَدْ أُذِلَّ وَأُقْهِرَا

“Ḥuṣayn wished to rule his people, but ended up humiliated and subjugated.”

The wording quoted is passive: he was found humiliated and subjugated. The active vocalization أَذَلَّ وَأَقْهَرَ (adhalla wa-aqhara) is also transmitted. It is explained as his situation turning to humiliation and subjugation, or his companions becoming humiliated and defeated. An active grammatical form in that reading therefore does not necessarily mean that Ḥuṣayn defeated others.

Both readings contrast the ambition to lead with humiliation and subjugation. The verse supports usage concerning power and social standing, although its precise date of composition is unknown.

In some modern Arabic usages, qahr also denotes grief, anger, and distress arising from injustice, failure, or powerlessness. Such an inward state may result from being subjugated, but should not replace the Qur’anic root meaning.

In Persian and Dari, qahr commonly means breaking off relations or refusing to speak to someone because of hurt feelings. This familiar Persian usage is not identical to the Qur’anic meaning.

3. Qahr in human relations

Pharaoh and domination of people

A clear example of human qahr is Pharaoh’s statement about Moses and his people:

وَقالَ المَلَأُ مِن قَومِ فِرعَونَ أَتَذَرُ موسىٰ وَقَومَهُ لِيُفسِدوا فِى الأَرضِ وَيَذَرَكَ وَءالِهَتَكَ قالَ سَنُقَتِّلُ أَبناءَهُم وَنَستَحيۦ نِساءَهُم وَإِنّا فَوقَهُم قٰهِرونَ

Wa-qāla l-malaʾu min qawmi firʿawna a-tadharu mūsā wa-qawmahu li-yufsidū fī l-arḍi wa-yadharaka wa-ālihataka qāla sa-nuqattilu abnāʾahum wa-nastaḥyī nisāʾahum wa-innā fawqahum qāhirūn.

“The chiefs of Pharaoh’s people said: ‘Will you leave Moses and his people to spread corruption in the land, and leave you and your gods?’ He said: ‘We will kill their sons and keep their women alive, and we are dominant over them and in control.’”

Al-A'raf 7:127

Qāhirūna is the sound masculine plural of qāhir, the active participle of Form I qahara. Fawqahum also expresses the superior power of Pharaoh and his government over the people under their domination.

Pharaoh is not merely claiming victory in an encounter; he is asserting his apparatus’s ability to impose its will on others.

Sūrat Al-Qaṣaṣ also describes this situation:

إِنَّ فِرعَونَ عَلا فِى الأَرضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهلَها شِيَعًا يَستَضعِفُ طائِفَةً مِنهُم يُذَبِّحُ أَبناءَهُم وَيَستَحيۦ نِساءَهُم إِنَّهُ كانَ مِنَ المُفسِدينَ

Inna firʿawna ʿalā fī l-arḍi wa-jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa-yastaḥyī nisāʾahum innahu kāna mina l-mufsidīn.

“Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and divided its people into factions, weakening a group among them, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women alive. He was indeed among those who spread corruption.”

Al-Qasas 28:4

Here, weakening people is presented as one means of preserving Pharaoh’s domination. Reducing a group’s capacity to resist enabled his government’s continued subjugation of them.

The Qur’an reports Pharaoh’s allegation that Moses causes corruption, while its own direct description identifies Pharaoh as a corrupter.

Superior power and the ability to subjugate others therefore do not, by themselves, establish the legitimacy of the powerful.

The prohibition on subjugating an orphan

Another human application appears in Al-Ḍuḥā:

فَأَمَّا اليَتيمَ فَلا تَقهَر

Fa-ammā l-yatīma fa-lā taqhar.

“So, as for the orphan, do not subjugate.”

Ad-Duha 93:9

Taqhar is a Form I jussive imperfect addressing a masculine singular addressee. Lā is prohibitive, and al-yatīma is the verb’s object, placed before it.

To understand this command, consider the earlier reminder about Muhammad’s life in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:6:

أَلَم يَجِدكَ يَتيمًا فَـٔاوىٰ

A-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā.

“Did He not find you an orphan and give shelter?”

Ad-Duha 93:6

After recalling the shelter given to Muhammad in orphanhood, The God prohibits him from subjugating an orphan.

Having lost a father and depending on others, an orphan may possess fewer resources and less power to defend their rights. A guardian or person managing their affairs may exploit this imbalance for personal advantage.

Examples of subjugating an orphan include unjustly taking their property, depriving them of rights, humiliating them, compelling them to accept a guardian’s wishes against their interests and rights, and exploiting their inability to defend themselves.

The Qur’an also addresses orphans’ financial rights:

وَءاتُوا اليَتٰمىٰ أَموٰلَهُم وَلا تَتَبَدَّلُوا الخَبيثَ بِالطَّيِّبِ وَلا تَأكُلوا أَموٰلَهُم إِلىٰ أَموٰلِكُم إِنَّهُ كانَ حوبًا كَبيرًا

Wa-ātū l-yatāmā amwālahum wa-lā tatabaddalū l-khabītha bi-l-ṭayyibi wa-lā taʾkulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum innahu kāna ḥūban kabīrā.

“Give the orphans their property; do not exchange the bad for the good, and do not consume their property along with your own. That is indeed a great sin.”

An-Nisa 4:2

The prohibition is more than a command to speak kindly. It concerns the use of power against someone who may be unable to resist its holder.

Guardianship does not mean ownership of an orphan. Power entrusted for protection and upbringing must not become a means of imposing personal wishes or violating rights.

Care, education, and limits appropriate to a child’s age and interests are not, by themselves, blameworthy qahr. The verse addresses abuse of superior power to subjugate the orphan.

4. Al-Qāhir as a divine description

Qāhir is an active participle from Q H R, denoting one who dominates another.

The Qur’an uses its definite form, al-Qāhir, for The God in two verses of Al-Anʿām:

وَهُوَ القاهِرُ فَوقَ عِبادِهِ وَهُوَ الحَكيمُ الخَبيرُ

Wa-huwa l-qāhiru fawqa ʿibādihi wa-huwa l-ḥakīmu l-khabīr.

“He is dominant over His servants, and He is the Wise, the Fully Aware.”

Al-An'am 6:18

Qāhir here stands beside ḥakīm and khabīr. Divine power is accompanied by wisdom and knowledge.

Human superiority of power can coexist with ignorance, selfishness, injustice, and corruption. The Qur’an, however, describes The God as possessing wisdom and awareness alongside authority over His servants.

Fawqa ʿibādihi in this context expresses supremacy over His servants. It does not establish a bodily location or spatial coordinates for The God.

Another verse of the same sūrah says:

وَهُوَ القاهِرُ فَوقَ عِبادِهِ وَيُرسِلُ عَلَيكُم حَفَظَةً حَتّىٰ إِذا جاءَ أَحَدَكُمُ المَوتُ تَوَفَّتهُ رُسُلُنا وَهُم لا يُفَرِّطونَ

Wa-huwa l-qāhiru fawqa ʿibādihi wa-yursilu ʿalaykum ḥafaẓatan ḥattā idhā jāʾa aḥadakumu l-mawtu tawaffathu rusulunā wa-hum lā yufarriṭūn.

“He is dominant over His servants, and He sends guardians over you until, when death comes to one of you, Our Messengers take him, and they do not neglect their duty.”

Al-An'am 6:61

Here divine dominance is discussed in relation to guardianship, life, and the arrival of death.

People can protect their lives, treat illnesses, and overcome many difficulties by learning the laws of creation, but do not possess absolute control over all the conditions of their existence.

Death is a clear instance of the limits of human power. Rulers, the wealthy, and the powerful are not exempt.

Divine and human dominance

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:127, Pharaoh calls himself and his apparatus qāhirūna; in Al-Anʿām, The God is described as al-Qāhir.

Both share the semantic core of prevailing and dominance. The source, scope, character, and moral value of the power differ.

Pharaoh’s power was limited, dependent on resources, and liable to disappear. He used it to weaken and oppress others. Qur’anic divine authority is described as encompassing, independent, and accompanied by wisdom, knowledge, and justice.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:40 expressly states that The God does not wrong anyone even by an atom’s weight.

Translating the divine description qāhir as “oppressor” would therefore be wrong. Injustice cannot be assigned to every occurrence of qahr.

5. Al-Qahhār: encompassing and invincible divine authority

Qahhār follows the intensive pattern faʿʿāl, emphasizing the strength and prominence of qahr.

Every Qur’anic occurrence of al-Qahhār accompanies al-Wāḥid. This collocation connects divine oneness with invincible authority.

Qahhār and the rejection of multiple lords

Joseph addresses his two companions in prison:

يٰصٰحِبَىِ السِّجنِ ءَأَربابٌ مُتَفَرِّقونَ خَيرٌ أَمِ اللَّهُ الوٰحِدُ القَهّارُ

Yā ṣāḥibayi l-sijni a-arbābun mutafarriqūna khayrun ami llāhu l-wāḥidu l-qahhār.

“O my two prison companions, are separate lords better, or The God, the One, the Invincible?”

Yusuf 12:39

Joseph asks this question while he himself is outwardly under other people’s control.

Those in power had imprisoned him, but that power did not make them lords or absolute masters of human beings.

In Yūsuf 12:40, Joseph assigns judgment to The God and calls people to worship Him.

Qahhār here means more than possessing greater force than others. Alongside “the One,” it denies independent, rival lords.

The same relationship appears in Ṣād:

قُل إِنَّما أَنا۠ مُنذِرٌ وَما مِن إِلٰهٍ إِلَّا اللَّهُ الوٰحِدُ القَهّارُ

Qul innamā anā mundhirun wa-mā min ilāhin illā llāhu l-wāḥidu l-qahhār.

“Say: ‘I am only a warner, and there is no deity except The God, the One, the Invincible.’”

Sad 38:65

Muhammad’s position as a warner is distinguished here from The God’s position as the sole deity and the Invincible.

Being a Messenger does not confer absolute divine authority. A Messenger conveys the message and warns, while oneness and invincible authority belong to The God.

Qahhār and creation

Al-Raʿd 13:16 discusses people who have taken protectors besides The God, although those protectors do not even control benefit or harm for themselves. It then asks whether the alleged partners have created anything like His creation, making the creations indistinguishable to them.

The verse ends by identifying The God as the Creator of everything, with the descriptions al-Wāḥid and al-Qahhār.

Other beings’ power depends on resources and conditions that are themselves parts of creation. No creature is independent of divine creation in existence, capacity, or resources.

Al-Zumar also says:

لَو أَرادَ اللَّهُ أَن يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا لَاصطَفىٰ مِمّا يَخلُقُ ما يَشاءُ سُبحٰنَهُ هُوَ اللَّهُ الوٰحِدُ القَهّارُ

Law arāda llāhu an yattakhidha waladan la-ṣṭafā mimmā yakhluqu mā yashāʾu subḥānahu huwa llāhu l-wāḥidu l-qahhār.

“If The God had willed to take a child, He would have selected whatever He willed from what He creates. Exalted is He! He is The God, the One, the Invincible.”

Az-Zumar 39:4

Here oneness and invincibility occur in a context rejecting the attribution of a child to The God. A created being, even if chosen from among other creatures, does not occupy the Creator’s position.

Qahhār and the manifestation of divine sovereignty at resurrection

يَومَ تُبَدَّلُ الأَرضُ غَيرَ الأَرضِ وَالسَّمٰوٰتُ وَبَرَزوا لِلَّهِ الوٰحِدِ القَهّارِ

Yawma tubaddalu l-arḍu ghayra l-arḍi wa-l-samāwātu wa-barazū li-llāhi l-wāḥidi l-qahhār.

“On the day when the earth is changed into another earth, and the heavens too, and they emerge before The God, the One, the Invincible.”

Ibrahim 14:48

This verse describes the transformation of the created order and people’s emergence before The God.

The powers on which people rely in worldly life have no independence in the face of such a transformation.

Ghāfir also says:

يَومَ هُم بٰرِزونَ لا يَخفىٰ عَلَى اللَّهِ مِنهُم شَىءٌ لِمَنِ المُلكُ اليَومَ لِلَّهِ الوٰحِدِ القَهّارِ

Yawma hum bārizūna lā yakhfā ʿalā llāhi minhum shayʾun li-mani l-mulku l-yawma li-llāhi l-wāḥidi l-qahhār.

“On the day when they emerge, nothing about them is hidden from The God. To whom belongs sovereignty today? To The God, the One, the Invincible.”

Ghafir 40:16

Qahhār is linked here to sovereignty and divine knowledge of human beings.

In worldly life, wealth, influence, position, or others’ support may help someone evade accountability. In the scene described here, nothing is hidden from The God and no independent power confronts His sovereignty.

The difference between qāhir and qahhār

Qāhir is an active participle describing one who exercises dominance. The intensive qahhār emphasizes its prominence and encompassing scope.

The Qur’an uses qāhir for The God and its plural for humans; qahhār exclusively describes The God.

Divine invincibility and encompassing authority do not follow from morphology alone. Collocation with oneness, creation, sovereignty, and the rejection of partners also supports that meaning.

The distinction does not establish two separate, limited levels of divine power.

6. Divine authority and human choice

One difficulty in understanding divine qahr is the supposition that it conflicts with human choice.

If The God is qāhir and qahhār, how can a human disobey His command?

We must distinguish divine authority over the created order from voluntary human obedience in ethical and religious life.

The Qur’an says:

وَلَو شاءَ رَبُّكَ لَـٔامَنَ مَن فِى الأَرضِ كُلُّهُم جَميعًا أَفَأَنتَ تُكرِهُ النّاسَ حَتّىٰ يَكونوا مُؤمِنينَ

Wa-law shāʾa rabbuka la-āmana man fī l-arḍi kulluhum jamīʿan a-fa-anta tukrihu l-nāsa ḥattā yakūnū muʾminīn.

“If your Lord had willed, everyone on earth would have believed, all together. Would you then compel people until they become believers?”

Yunus 10:99

It also says:

إِن نَشَأ نُنَزِّل عَلَيهِم مِنَ السَّماءِ ءايَةً فَظَلَّت أَعنٰقُهُم لَها خٰضِعينَ

In nashaʾ nunazzil ʿalayhim mina l-samāʾi āyatan fa-ẓallat aʿnāquhum lahā khāḍiʿīn.

“If We will, We send down upon them a sign from heaven, so that their necks remain bowed before it.”

Ash-Shu'ara 26:4

These verses distinguish divine power to bring people into submission from the manner in which their guidance is provided.

Human choice operates within the possibilities and laws available in creation. People can decide, believe, deny, do good, or commit injustice. Their choice does not mean an ability to leave creation’s domain or escape responsibility for their deeds.

Disobeying a divine ethical command does not mean defeating The God. Nor does divine power to subjugate people imply that all human decisions have been forced upon them.

The Qur’an explicitly says:

إِنّا هَدَينٰهُ السَّبيلَ إِمّا شاكِرًا وَإِمّا كَفورًا

Innā hadaynāhu l-sabīla immā shākiran wa-immā kafūrā.

“We have shown him the way, whether he be grateful or ungrateful.”

Al-Insan 76:3

Divine invincibility therefore cannot justify denying human choice or compelling people to accept religion. Divine authority and the freedom of choice available to humans within His provision are not incompatible.

7. Comparison with related roots

Q H R and Gh L B: dominance and prevailing

Ghalabah denotes prevailing over another in battle, competition, argument, or encounters between different forces.

Qahr more strongly foregrounds superior power and the capacity to bring another under control.

One may defeat another in a competition without placing them under one’s command. Qahr is more closely associated with domination and a reduced capacity to resist.

Nevertheless, the two roots overlap in many Arabic usages.

Q H R and Q M ʿ: subjugation and suppression

Qamʿ concerns restraining, subduing, and suppressing through force.

Qahr more often expresses a condition of dominance, while qamʿ can denote acts used to establish or preserve it.

A despotic government may suppress opposition to keep people under its control. Suppression illustrates qamʿ; the resulting domination illustrates qahr.

Q H R and J B R: dominance, compulsion, and repair

One branch of jabr concerns compulsion and the exercise of power, bringing it close to qahr.

Jabr also has other meanings, including mending a fracture, making up a deficiency, and restoring something to a sound condition.

Al-Jabbār is a divine description in Al-Ḥashr 59:23. Some of its meanings overlap with qahhār, but the whole range of jabr cannot be reduced to dominance.

Q H R and K R H: domination and compulsion against one’s will

Ikrāh means compelling someone to act against their wishes and consent.

Qahr can create the conditions for such compulsion, but is not always limited to forcing a particular act.

Someone may be economically or politically dominated without being compelled to perform a specific action at every moment.

Qahr foregrounds a relationship of power and control; ikrāh emphasizes forcing someone against their will.

Q H R and Ẓ L M: superior power and violation of rights

Ẓulm concerns transgressing what is right and violating others’ rights.

Qahr can serve injustice, as in Pharaoh’s conduct and subjugating an orphan. Not every injustice involves overt domination, however; deception, betrayal, and false testimony can also violate rights.

Nor does every instance of qahr mean injustice: qāhir and qahhār are divine descriptions in the Qur’an.

8. Translation choices for Forqan

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 renders lā taqhar as “do not subjugate.” In the orphan’s context, this prohibits abusing power to violate rights and humiliate. That explanation does not replace the short direct translation.

“Do not hurt” or “do not humiliate” conveys part of the meaning but does not fully highlight the use of superior power against an orphan.

Qāhirūna in Al-Aʿrāf 7:127 is translated here as “dominant and in control.” Maqhūr describes the defeated party and is not equivalent to this active participle.

Al-Qāhiru in Al-Anʿām 6:18 and Al-Anʿām 6:61 is “dominant,” or with the following phrase, “dominant over His servants.” Every quotation here renders qahhār as “the Invincible”; “dominant over all” explains the positive scope of that power, rather than serving as an inconsistently substituted translation.

Both al-Wāḥidu l-Qahhāru and its genitive form, al-Wāḥidi l-Qahhāri, are consistently rendered “the One, the Invincible.” Their different case endings do not change the meanings of the two descriptions.

“Avenger,” “angry,” or “punisher” would unjustifiably narrow qahhār. The description concerns not only punishment but also creation, oneness, and encompassing sovereignty.

9. Conclusion

Q H R denotes dominance through superior power, such that the other party cannot effectively resist or prevent its exercise.

The Qur’an uses it for Pharaoh’s oppressive domination and to prohibit subjugating an orphan. These usages draw attention to how people exercise superior power over others.

Qāhir and qahhār are also divine descriptions. Qāhir denotes divine dominance, while qahhār emphasizes its prominence and invincibility. Qahhār’s constant accompaniment of “the One” underscores the absence of any independent rival power.

Qahr is not inherently anger, vengeance, or injustice. Its quality and value depend on who holds the power, how it is exercised, and the context.

Definition for Forqan: dominance through superior power, bringing another under control, and subjugation; in the divine description qahhār, encompassing and invincible authority confronted by no independent power.
