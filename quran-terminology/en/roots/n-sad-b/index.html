Setting up and establishing; hardship and weariness; shares and portions; erected markers and stones

The Arabic root N Ṣ B produces a family whose meanings initially seem different: setting something up, standing or occupying a visible position, weariness and hardship from effort, an assigned share, and stones or markers erected for a purpose.

These meanings also appear in the Qur’an. Mountains set up, someone called to renewed effort after finishing a task, a traveler weary from the journey, someone receiving a portion of property or deeds, and stones with a special place in polytheistic rites are all described through this family.

Its morphological forms and semantic branches must remain distinct. Connections can be suggested among setting up, exertion, and an assigned share, but every derivative should not be translated through one physical image or abstract expression.

1. Forms, semantic core, and early Arabic

Principal forms

The root occurs 32 times in 29 Qur’anic verses. Its principal forms are:

  • نُصِبَتْ: was set up or established.
  • فَانْصَبْ: strive earnestly; apply yourself to work and effort.
  • نَصَب: weariness, hardship, and exhaustion arising from difficulty.
  • نُصْب: hardship, difficulty, and distress.
  • نَاصِبَة: toiling and weary; suffering exhausting effort.
  • نَصِيب: a share or portion of something belonging or coming to someone.
  • نُصُب and أَنْصَاب: erected objects and markers; in some contexts, stones with a ritual function.

Arabic also has forms such as naṣb, intiṣāb, manṣib, niṣāb, naṣībah, and munāṣabah. They help establish the root’s lexical range, but should not all be presented as actual Qur’anic forms.

Semantic core

A clear physical meaning of naṣaba is setting something up or placing it in a standing or visible position.

Arabic uses expressions such as:

Naṣaba al-rumḥa: he set up the spear.

Naṣaba al-khaymata: he pitched the tent.

Naṣaba al-ʿalama: he erected a marker or flag.

Something changes into an erected position. Sometimes standing upright matters; elsewhere, being placed in a particular visible position is prominent.

Setting something up and placing it in a definite position is one semantic center of this family. Some lexical analyses also explain other uses, including weariness and shares, through it.

In this analysis, hardship and exhaustion relate to continued effort and remaining upright. Naṣīb is likewise understood as a portion determined for someone and, as it were, set before them.

Such connections help explain the semantic field, but differ from certain derivational history. Relating two meanings to one image does not alone establish that one historically developed directly from the other.

Two verbs in particular should be distinguished:

Naṣaba al-shayʾa means setting something up, while naṣiba al-rajulu describes a person becoming weary or worn out.

They share a root, but differ in form and usage.

Pre-Qur’anic Arabic evidence

A clear early witness to the hardship branch is the well-known verse of al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī:

كِلِينِي لِهَمٍّ يَا أُمَيْمَةَ نَاصِبِ
وَلَيْلٍ أُقَاسِيهِ بَطِيءِ الْكَوَاكِبِ

kilīnī li-hammin yā umaymata nāṣibi / wa-laylin uqāsīhi baṭīʾi al-kawākibi

Translation: Leave me, Umaymah, to a wearing sorrow and a night I struggle through, whose stars move slowly.

Nāṣib describes a sorrow that wears someone down. The poet speaks of lost peace and a night difficult to endure.

Before the Qur’an, this family was thus already used for psychological suffering and exhaustion. Weariness cannot be treated solely as a Qur’anic innovation or ritual meaning.

Another witness concerning ritual stones is attributed to al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī, a pre-Islamic poet:

يُطِيفُ بِنُصْبِهِمْ حُجْنٌ صِغَارٌ
فَقَدْ كَادَتْ حَوَاجِبُهُمْ تَشِيبُ

yuṭīfu bi-nuṣbihim ḥujnun ṣighārun / fa-qad kādat ḥawājibuhum tashību

Translation: Little children circle their ritual stone, their eyebrows seemingly almost turning gray.

Early accounts transmit the verse concerning rites and venerated stones. It helps establish nuṣb for a stone occupying a place in ritual practice.

This does not make every erected stone an object of worship, or every nuṣub necessarily an idol in all Arabic uses.

Stones could also be erected for ordinary purposes: marking a route, identifying land, defining a boundary, or constructing a structure.

Being erected is a physical feature. Ritual function, ownership, boundaries, and guidance are understood from the object’s setting and use.

2. Setting up and establishing: from mountains to human effort

Mountains set up

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:19 says:

وَإِلَى الْجِبَالِ كَيْفَ نُصِبَتْ

wa-ilā al-jibāli kayfa nuṣibat

Translation: And the mountains, how they have been set up?

Nuṣibat is a passive perfect verb from naṣaba.

The verse appears alongside invitations to observe camels, the sky, and the earth. It directs attention to visible structures of the world and asks about their condition.

Nuṣibat here concerns the mountains being set up and standing. The word alone does not state their geological formation process, age, or the details of natural forces.

Nor does it establish that every mountain was made like a spear or column. It describes the mountains’ standing and visibility within the earth’s structure; their precise formation cannot be extracted from the root alone.

This is a particularly clear Qur’anic example of the root’s physical branch.

Fa-nṣab: renewed effort after finishing

Al-Sharḥ 94:7 says:

فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَانْصَبْ

fa-idhā faraghta fa-nṣab

Translation: When you have finished, strive earnestly.

The following verse says:

وَإِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَارْغَبْ

wa-ilā rabbika fa-rghab

Translation: And direct your desire toward your Lord. (Al-Sharḥ 94:8)

Fa-nṣab is an imperative, here conveying effort and earnest application to a task.

Fa-nṣab, with a on ṣād, belongs to naṣiba yanṣabu in the field of hardship and effort. It differs from naṣaba al-shayʾa yanṣibuhu, setting something up. In context, the call is to earnest exertion, not self-injury or exhaustion as an end in itself.

Al-Sharḥ 94:7 does not itself specify the activity just completed. Al-Sharḥ 94:8 directs desire toward the Lord.

Translations restricting fa-nṣab to “pray,” “supplicate,” or “perform night worship” replace the verb’s direct meaning with an interpretive instance.

Those actions may be examples of effort in service, but fa-nṣab itself does not name any of them.

The verse should also not become a command to work without interruption or any opportunity for rest. That ruling does not follow from the verb’s meaning alone.

“Strive earnestly,” “apply yourself,” and “devote yourself to effort” are appropriate here; the following verse clarifies the direction of that exertion.

3. Naṣab and nuṣb: hardship, weariness, and exhaustion

Weariness in travel

In Al-Kahf 18:62, Moses says to his companion:

آتِنَا غَدَاءَنَا لَقَدْ لَقِينَا مِنْ سَفَرِنَا هَٰذَا نَصَبًا

ātinā ghadāʾanā la-qad laqīnā min safarinā hādhā naṣaban

Translation: Bring us our midday meal; we have certainly suffered weariness on this journey.

Naṣaban describes weariness and hardship arising from travel.

This use is clearly physical. Travel, movement, and the difficulties of a route can weary the body, a condition described as naṣab.

Naṣab is not restricted to muscular fatigue. As al-Nābighah’s poem shows, wearing distress from sorrow is also expressed through the family.

Hardship in The God’s way

Al-Tawbah 9:120 says:

لَا يُصِيبُهُمْ ظَمَأٌ وَلَا نَصَبٌ وَلَا مَخْمَصَةٌ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

lā yuṣībuhum ẓamaʾun wa-lā naṣabun wa-lā makhmaṣatun fī sabīli allāhi

Translation: No thirst, weariness, or severe hunger afflicts them in The God’s way…

Three separate conditions appear: thirst, weariness, and severe hunger.

Their co-occurrence shows why naṣab should not be restricted to thirst or hunger. Each names a distinct condition.

Naṣab itself also has no fixed moral value. The verse concerns hardship during exertion in The God’s way; its value comes from the setting, direction of action, and continuation of the verse, not from every instance of tiredness inherently being worship or virtue.

No weariness in the Garden

Al-Ḥijr 15:48 says:

لَا يَمَسُّهُمْ فِيهَا نَصَبٌ وَمَا هُمْ مِنْهَا بِمُخْرَجِينَ

lā yamassuhum fīhā naṣabun wa-mā hum minhā bi-mukhrajīna

Translation: No weariness touches them there, and they will not be expelled from it.

Fāṭir 35:35 also says:

لَا يَمَسُّنَا فِيهَا نَصَبٌ وَلَا يَمَسُّنَا فِيهَا لُغُوبٌ

lā yamassunā fīhā naṣabun wa-lā yamassunā fīhā lughūbun

Translation: No hardship touches us there, nor does any exhaustion touch us.

Naṣab and lughūb are different words. Lughūb relates to weariness and exhaustion from exertion and appears beside naṣab here.

The pairing may emphasize the absence of different aspects of hardship and exhaustion. It should not create an unsupported rigid division in which one always means physical fatigue and the other psychological fatigue.

The verse promises freedom from hardship and exhaustion. The bodily or nonbodily nature of that condition cannot be determined from naṣab alone.

Nuṣb and suffering in Job’s story

Ṣād 38:41 says:

أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الشَّيْطَانُ بِنُصْبٍ وَعَذَابٍ

annī massaniya al-shayṭānu bi-nuṣbin wa-ʿadhābin

Translation: Satan has afflicted me with hardship and suffering.

Nuṣb, with u on nūn and a vowelless ṣād, refers here to hardship and difficulty.

Its different vowels from naṣab do not create an entirely separate semantic family. Arabic dictionaries attest both in the field of hardship and distress.

Nuṣb and ʿadhāb appear together. Wearing hardship can be conceptually distinguished from suffering, but the words do not yield every detail of Job’s illness, its medical causes, or the nature of his distress.

The attribution to Satan requires separate study within Job’s story and the connected verses on trial and responsibility. It does not change the lexical definition of nuṣb.

ʿĀmilatun nāṣibah: exhausting toil

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:3 says:

عَامِلَةٌ نَاصِبَةٌ

ʿāmilatun nāṣibatun

Translation: Laboring and weary.

This follows the description of faces humbled that day and precedes entering a fiercely hot fire.

ʿĀmilah belongs to ʿ M L and nāṣibah to N Ṣ B. The first concerns work and action, the second hardship and exhaustion.

Together they convey toil accompanied by hardship. Nāṣibah alone should not become “useless work,” “invalid worship,” or “punishment in the Hereafter.”

Interpretations differ over whether ʿāmilatun nāṣibah refers to earlier fruitless effort or hardship within the resurrection scene itself. The setting is the Day of Resurrection, but determining the precise time and object of the labor requires the surrounding sentences.

The lexical sense of nāṣibah is weary or exhausted from toil. Moral or otherworldly conclusions come from the broader context.

4. Naṣīb: shares and portions in different fields

Naṣīb is among the most extensive Qur’anic branches of this root.

It describes a share or portion of something belonging or coming to a person or group.

The share may be property, the result of deeds, knowledge, power, status, or even an unwelcome consequence.

Persian usage sometimes equates naṣīb with an unalterable destiny. The word itself, however, does not establish who determines a share or whether it can change.

Shares of inheritance and property

Al-Nisāʾ 4:7 says:

لِلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ

li-al-rijāli naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūna wa-li-al-nisāʾi naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūna

Translation: Men have a share in what parents and close relatives leave behind, and women have a share in what parents and close relatives leave behind.

The verse ends with naṣīban mafrūḍan: a prescribed share.

Naṣīb names the share, while mafrūḍ describes its prescribed character.

The distinction matters: mafrūḍ explicitly states that the share is prescribed. Without context, naṣīb alone does not establish that every use means a fixed, divinely obligatory share.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:33 also says:

فَآتُوهُمْ نَصِيبَهُمْ

fa-ātūhum naṣībahum

Translation: Give them their share.

A naṣīb can therefore be an entitlement that others are responsible for paying. Its nature, recipient, basis, and amount require the overall ruling.

A portion of what someone earns

Al-Baqarah 2:202 says:

أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ

ulāʾika lahum naṣībun mimmā kasabū wa-allāhu sarīʿu al-ḥisābi

Translation: They have a portion of what they have earned, and The God is swift in accounting.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:32 also says:

لِلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا اكْتَسَبُوا وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا اكْتَسَبْنَ

li-al-rijāli naṣībun mimmā iktasabū wa-li-al-nisāʾi naṣībun mimmā iktasabna

Translation: Men have a portion of what they have earned, and women have a portion of what they have earned.

Naṣīb is directly connected with earning and acquisition in these verses.

It need not be something received without action or choice. A share sometimes relates to someone’s deeds, effort, and their results.

These examples should not create the opposite absolute rule that every Qur’anic naṣīb results from personal effort. In inheritance, the share concerns property others leave behind; elsewhere the subject is giving, knowledge, power, or consequences of deeds.

The source of a portion is thus not a fixed component of naṣīb and must be examined verse by verse.

A portion of scripture and knowledge

Āl ʿImrān 3:23 says:

الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا نَصِيبًا مِنَ الْكِتَابِ

alladhīna ūtū naṣīban mina al-kitābi

Translation: Those given a portion of the Book.

The same expression appears in Al-Nisāʾ 4:44 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:51.

Naṣīb here concerns a portion of scripture rather than property.

It can thus describe access to something involving knowledge or content.

The word alone does not determine a number of pages, quantity of knowledge, part of the rulings, or degree of understanding. Those questions require context and other evidence.

Nor does having a portion of the Book inherently mean correct understanding, complete practice, or faithfulness to it. The subsequent verses examine people’s conduct.

A share of power and status

Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 says:

أَمْ لَهُمْ نَصِيبٌ مِنَ الْمُلْكِ

am lahum naṣībun mina al-mulki

Translation: Or do they have a share of sovereignty?

Al-Nisāʾ 4:141 also uses naṣīb concerning an advantage or portion coming to disbelievers.

The share need not be divided property; power, victory, or status may be involved.

The word expresses participation or possession of a portion. Its legitimacy, fairness, duration, and moral value require context.

Sharing in good or bad action

Al-Nisāʾ 4:85 says:

مَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً حَسَنَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ نَصِيبٌ مِنْهَا وَمَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً سَيِّئَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ كِفْلٌ مِنْهَا

man yashfaʿ shafāʿatan ḥasanatan yakun lahu naṣībun minhā wa-man yashfaʿ shafāʿatan sayyiʾatan yakun lahu kiflun minhā

Translation: Whoever makes a good intercession will have a portion of it; whoever makes a bad intercession will have a share of it.

Naṣīb is paired with kifl here.

That pairing matters for examining the distinction, but does not make naṣīb always a good portion and kifl always a bad one.

The Qur’an also uses naṣīb in unwelcome settings.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:118, for example, Satan says:

لَأَتَّخِذَنَّ مِنْ عِبَادِكَ نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا

la-attakhidhanna min ʿibādika naṣīban mafrūḍan

Translation: I will surely take an assigned portion of Your servants.

This is Satan’s claim to gain followers. Naṣīb itself has no positive or praiseworthy value here. Reporting Satan’s claim also does not prove that his wish will certainly be fulfilled for everyone.

Al-Anʿām 6:136 and Al-Naḥl 16:56 also describe people assigning portions of provision and produce to their objects of worship.

Naṣīb here names a portion people assign or allocate. The criticism concerns the act and direction of allocation, not the word itself.

Portions in this world and the Hereafter

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:77 says:

وَلَا تَنْسَ نَصِيبَكَ مِنَ الدُّنْيَا

wa-lā tansa naṣībaka mina al-dunyā

Translation: Do not forget your portion of this world.

Naṣīb can thus describe someone’s benefit from worldly life.

Al-Shūrā 42:20 also says:

وَمَا لَهُ فِي الْآخِرَةِ مِنْ نَصِيبٍ

wa-mā lahu fī al-ākhirati min naṣībin

Translation: He has no portion in the Hereafter.

In Ghāfir 40:47, some inhabitants of the Fire ask others:

فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ مُغْنُونَ عَنَّا نَصِيبًا مِنَ النَّارِ

fa-hal antum mughnūna ʿannā naṣīban mina al-nāri

Translation: Can you relieve us of any portion of the Fire?

Naṣīb is clearly not always a blessing, good reward, or something pleasant. A portion can concern something unwelcome.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:37 and Hūd 11:109 likewise use naṣīb for a portion or consequence coming to people.

Its definition should therefore preserve semantic neutrality: a share or portion of something, desirable or undesirable.

Naṣīb alone cannot establish a theory of unalterable destiny, compulsion, predestination, or absence of human choice. A portion may be prescribed, earned, allocated, or a consequence of deeds; the words and relationships of each verse identify those features.

5. Nuṣub and anṣāb: erected markers and ritual stones

Physical and historical meaning

Nuṣub can describe something erected, particularly an upright stone or marker.

The stone may mark something, identify a position, or participate in a custom or rite.

Some erected stones had ritual functions in pre-Islamic Arabia. People might slaughter beside them, venerate them, or use them in ceremonies.

Historical and lexical accounts sometimes distinguish ritual stones from sculpted idols. A nuṣub need not be a statue shaped like a person or animal.

Translating every anṣāb as “idol statues” may therefore restrict the word’s physical character too narrowly.

«وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ»

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 says:

وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ

wa-mā dhubiḥa ʿalā al-nuṣubi

Translation: And what has been slaughtered on erected ritual stones.

Nuṣub accompanies slaughter here.

That connection indicates more than an ordinary stone used for construction or marking. Stones occupying a particular place in slaughtering rites are intended.

The phrase ʿalā al-nuṣubi identifies the stones used in those rites. The motive, name of a deity, and every ceremonial detail cannot be extracted from the word and preposition alone.

The same verse separately mentions what is dedicated to other than The God. Those expressions should not be made entirely identical without examining their construction and setting.

«الْأَنْصَابُ وَالْأَزْلَامُ»

Al-Māʾidah 5:90 says:

إِنَّمَا الْخَمْرُ وَالْمَيْسِرُ وَالْأَنْصَابُ وَالْأَزْلَامُ رِجْسٌ مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ

innamā al-khamru wa-al-maysiru wa-al-anṣābu wa-al-azlāmu rijsun min ʿamali al-shayṭāni

Translation: Wine, gambling, erected ritual stones, and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan’s work.

Anṣāb stands beside wine, gambling, and divining arrows.

Their grouping places the verse among recognized social and ritual practices and implements.

Anṣāb should not absorb every feature of wine, gambling, and divining arrows. Each word has its own field and use.

The prohibition also does not make every upright stone, route marker, building column, or material memorial one of the condemned anṣāb. Ritual function and its relationship to the criticized conduct matter here.

«كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ»

Al-Maʿārij 70:43 says:

يَوْمَ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ سِرَاعًا كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ

yawma yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi sirāʿan ka-annahum ilā nuṣubin yūfiḍūna

Translation: The day they emerge swiftly from the graves, as though rushing toward an erected marker.

This depicts swift emergence at the resurrection through a comparison conveying the speed of movement.

Two recognized lexical interpretations of nuṣub are offered here.

The first understands an erected marker or target toward which people rush, such as a marker for a destination or race.

The second understands a ritual stone or marker toward which people hasten.

The transmitted readings include nuṣub and naṣb, whose different vowels have received lexical discussion.

Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim and Ibn ʿĀmir’s reading have nuṣubin, with u on both nūn and ṣād. The other readers among the seven read naṣbin, with a on nūn and a vowelless ṣād.

Both interpretations concern something erected, but identify the comparison’s precise object differently.

The verse certainly depicts rapid emergence from graves and hurried movement. Speed comes from sirāʿan and yūfiḍūna, not from nuṣub itself meaning haste.

Ritual stones in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 should therefore not become the only possible interpretation of Al-Maʿārij 70:43 without considering the comparison and reading differences.

6. Related roots and the limits of later terminology

N Ṣ B and Q S M: division and shares

Naṣīb and qism approach one another in the field of portions and division.

Q S M in the sense of division emphasizes separating and distributing parts among sections or people.

Naṣīb emphasizes the share itself, belonging or assigned to someone.

A share may result from division, but naṣīb does not itself express the act of dividing or determine the source of every portion.

The distinction is clear in inheritance and nonfinancial uses such as a portion of scripture.

N Ṣ B and Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ: naṣīb and ḥaẓẓ

Ḥaẓẓ and naṣīb can both describe a share or portion.

In some uses, ḥaẓẓ gives greater emphasis to enjoyment or possession of a benefit, and naṣīb to the share or part in question.

This should not become an absolute rule. Both words can overlap in different settings.

Naṣīb should particularly not always be considered predestined while ḥaẓẓ is always accidental or chance. The roots themselves do not establish that division.

Study of Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ

N Ṣ B and L GH B: hardship and exhaustion

Naṣab and lughūb appear together in Fāṭir 35:35.

Both concern hardship and weariness. Lexical uses connect lughūb with exhaustion and inability arising from exertion.

The words reinforce the denial of weariness and exhaustion, but no absolute boundary between them can be established without broader examination.

N Ṣ B and N S B: distinct roots

Naṣab and nasab may be confused because of similar writing and sounds.

N S B is an independent root concerning relationship, family connection, and attribution.

Ansāb, family and genealogical relationships, therefore differs from anṣāb, erected markers or stones.

One different letter distinguishes two independent lexical families.

Study of N S B

Niṣāb, manṣib, and later terminology

Niṣāb describes an origin, base, or place where something is positioned, and in legal and juristic uses a specified threshold of property.

Manṣib can describe a position, office, or standing occupied by someone.

Naṣb in grammar names a case or inflectional state.

These belong to the family’s historical and technical range. The juristic niṣāb, administrative manṣib, and grammatical naṣb should not be transferred without evidence to Qur’anic derivatives.

Nāṣibī also became a particular sectarian term after the Qur’an. That does not make nāṣibah in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:3 refer to followers of a sect or enemies of a particular group. Its lexical and contextual meaning concerns hardship and toil.

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

One Persian equivalent cannot translate this entire family.

Nuṣibat in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:19 is best rendered “have been set up” or “have been established,” preserving its physical meaning.

Fa-nṣab in Al-Sharḥ 94:7 can be rendered “strive earnestly,” “apply yourself diligently,” or “devote yourself to effort.” “Exhaust yourself” is unnatural as a Persian rendering and may turn the verse’s purpose into wearing oneself out.

Naṣab in travel and fatigue verses can be “weariness,” “hardship,” or “exhaustion,” according to the sentence. “Weariness” is clearer in Al-Kahf 18:62; “hardship and weariness” also fits Al-Tawbah 9:120.

Nuṣb in Ṣād 38:41 is “hardship” or “distress.” Since it accompanies ʿadhāb, both need not be translated with one word.

Nāṣibah in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:3 can be “weary” or “exhausted from toil.” Determining the time and nature of the toil belongs to the complete reflection on the verse.

Naṣīb is usually “share” or “portion.” “Share” is natural for inheritance; “portion” suits scripture and deeds; “part” can convey the intended sense in a setting such as Ghāfir 40:47.

Nuṣub and anṣāb in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 and Al-Māʾidah 5:90 can be “erected ritual stones” or, when the setting is clear, “sacrificial stones.” “Idols” may convey some instances, but does not retain all the word’s physical features.

“An erected marker” in Al-Maʿārij 70:43 preserves the comparison’s broader meaning. If a ritual-stone interpretation is preferred, its grounds should be explained in the verse’s reflection.

8. Conclusion

N Ṣ B has distinct Qur’anic semantic branches that should not be equated merely because they share a root.

Setting up and establishing is a clear principal meaning. Nuṣibat concerning mountains and nuṣub concerning erected objects show this physical field.

Hardship and weariness appear in naṣab, nuṣb, and nāṣibah. They may arise from travel, effort, or difficulty. The word itself has neither positive nor negative moral value.

Naṣīb names a share or portion belonging or coming to a person or group. It can concern property, knowledge, social position, results of deeds, or an unwelcome consequence. The word alone does not establish compulsion, unalterable destiny, or legitimacy of the share.

Nuṣub and anṣāb concern erected objects that in some Qur’anic settings, particularly Al-Māʾidah, have a ritual and polytheistic function. Not every erected object belongs to the condemned anṣāb merely because of its physical shape.

Lexical explanation connects these branches with setting up and placing, but the historical unity of all meanings should not be asserted beyond secure evidence.

Thus, N Ṣ B is a family with distinct branches of standing and establishment, exertion and exhaustion, shares and portions, and erected markers. Qur’anic translation should identify the meaning of each derivative through its morphology, construction, and setting, without imposing one branch on the entire family.
