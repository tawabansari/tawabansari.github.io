From roasting meat with heated stones to the effects of heat and sweating

The Arabic root ح ن ذ concerns heating and cooking something through intense heat. حَنَذَ اللَّحْمَ means to roast meat, especially when it is cooked with heated stones or in a heated pit covered with earth. حَنِيذ describes meat prepared in this way or, more generally, meat roasted and cooked by heat.

In the same family, حَنَذَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ describes a person or animal affected by intense sunlight. In early usage, حَنَذَ الْفَرَسَ also denoted covering a horse after exercise to make it sweat.

These usages share a semantic field: exposure to heat and its effects on a substance or body. Cooking meat, the heat of the sun, and an animal’s sweating nevertheless remain different situations; the details of one should not be transferred to another without evidence.

The root ح ن ذ occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَنِيذٍ in Hūd 11:69. It describes the calf Abraham brought for his guests.

Qur’anic form: حَنِيذٍ in Hūd 11:69; an indefinite, masculine singular adjective in the genitive case, from ح ن ذ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of ḥanīdh in Hūd 11:69

Arabic also records حَنَذَ, يَحْنِذُ, حَنْذ, حِنَاذ, حَنِيذ, مَحْنُوذ, أَحْنَذَ, and اسْتَحْنَذَ, but these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic center and early Arabic usages

حَنَذَ: cooking through heat

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for this root: إِنْضَاجُ الشَّيْءِ, bringing something to a cooked or mature state.

He explains this through heating stones and placing them on meat until it cooks.

Al-ʿAyn likewise explains الْحَنْذُ as roasting meat with heated stones.

These witnesses show that the central meaning is not merely warming a substance’s surface, but applying heat until it reaches a cooked or prepared condition.

For example, meat exposed to fire may initially only become warm, but continued heating changes its structure and condition, preparing it for eating.

The established usage of حنذ concerns this process of cooking.

Ibn Fāris’s definition does not mean that every instance of حنيذ in every period or Arabic dialect was necessarily prepared only with hot stones. Early dictionaries record both the specific meaning of roasting with stones and the broader use of roasting meat.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: cooking and heated stones

حَنِيذ: roasted meat

حَنِيذ follows the pattern فَعِيل.

Lexical explanations understand it as مَحْنُوذ: something upon which the action of ḥandh has been performed.

This resembles other Arabic adjectives on the faʿīl pattern that carry a meaning close to a passive participle.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains حَنَذْتُ الشَّاةَ as roasting a sheep and placing heated stones upon it to cook it.

Lisān al-ʿArab also uses لَحْمٌ حَنِيذٌ for roasted meat.

In Hūd 11:69, حنيذ therefore describes the calf and expresses its preparation as food.

The word does not mean the calf itself: عِجْل names the animal, while حَنِيذ describes its prepared meat.

Nor does حنيذ itself mean fat, large, or costly. If such qualities appear elsewhere, they must be identified through the words in that context.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥanīdh and maḥnūdh

Roasting with heated stones

Early lexical explanations record several ways of preparing ḥanīdh meat.

In one method, stones were heated in a fire and the meat placed in contact with them so that their heat would cook it.

In another, a pit was made in the ground and a fire lit in it; after the surroundings became hot, the meat was placed inside and covered over.

Al-Farrāʾ presents this as an established method among desert dwellers.

Ibn Sīdah’s Al-Muḥkam gives a more detailed description of building a stone enclosure, heating it, and placing the meat in the enclosed space.

These accounts show that ḥandh could denote a particular method of cooking through retained heat, transferred to the meat from heated stones and surroundings.

These technical descriptions should not be presented as certain Qur’anic details of Abraham’s cooking.

Hūd 11:69 does not say that Abraham dug a pit, how many heated stones he used, or how long he left the meat inside.

The Qur’an supplies only the description حنيذ. Lexical evidence explains established methods of preparing such food, but does not establish every detail of this historical event.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: different cooking methods

Roasting and the release of moisture from meat

Some early reports use حنيذ for meat whose moisture or juices emerge from its surface after roasting.

Shamir describes meat that remains hot and drips liquid after being roasted.

In explaining عِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ, Ibn ʿArafah also describes roasting with heated stones until moisture emerges.

These accounts help explain the cooking method and the meat’s condition.

Dripping fat or juices should nevertheless not be treated as the only possible definition of حنيذ, since other sources use the word more generally for roasted meat.

Nor does liquid emerging from meat necessarily mean that it is exceptionally fatty. Moisture and fat are different matters.

“Roasted” is therefore lexically better supported than adding definite descriptions such as “very fatty” or “full of oil” to حنيذ itself.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Shamir and Ibn ʿArafah on meat juices

حَنَذَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ: the effects of intense sunlight

Arabic uses حَنَذَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ for a person or animal whose body is affected by intense sunlight.

Al-Jawharī explains the expression through burning and heating by the sun.

In Asās al-Balāghah, Al-Zamakhsharī also places حَنَذَتْنَا الشَّمْسُ alongside expressions describing the sun’s heat as roasting or cooking people.

In such usage, the person has clearly not literally been cooked like meat for eating.

The expression depicts intense heat and its perceptible effect on the body.

It is connected with cooking meat, but should not be translated as the literal roasting of a person.

حِنَاذُ الْخَيْل: making a horse sweat

Another usage of حَنَذَ الْفَرَسَ concerns covering a horse after exercise to make it sweat.

Ibn Fāris explains that the horse was made to run one or two stretches and then covered with several cloths until it sweated.

حِنَاذ names the procedure, and فَرَسٌ مَحْنُوذٌ describes a horse subjected to it.

Some sources also use حَنِيذ for such a horse.

The procedure differs from roasting meat, but heat and retaining warmth matter in both.

For meat, the aim is cooking; for the horse, the concern is making its body sweat.

This does not establish that ḥinādh is necessarily sound or appropriate in modern veterinary practice. Early sources report a linguistic usage and historical practice, rather than proving its scientific safety or benefit.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the historical procedure of making a horse sweat

Other usages and lexical disagreements

Lisān al-ʿArab also records حَنِيذ for hot water.

The expression إِذَا سَقَيْتَ فَأَحْنِذْ also has differing explanations concerning mixing a drink with water.

Al-Farrāʾ explains it as reducing the water added to the drink, whereas Ibn al-Aʿrābī transmits a usage with a different meaning.

Abū al-Haytham also rejects some explanations.

The disagreement shows that these expressions are not understood identically in every source. One explanation should not be declared the root’s central meaning without grounds.

حَنَذ is also recorded as a place-name in geographical sources. The designation alone does not prove that every place bearing this name was naturally very hot.

None of these subsidiary meanings is intended in the Qur’anic usage. حنيذ describes food brought for Abraham’s guests.

Lisān al-ʿArab: hot water, place-names, and disputed drink-mixing usages

2. An early poetic witness: intense sunlight

In selected parts of his rajaz poem, Al-ʿAjjāj describes a male and female donkey exposed to summer heat:

حَتَّى إِذَا مَا الصَّيْفُ كَانَ أَمَجَا
وَرَهِبَا مِنْ حَنْذِهِ أَنْ يَهْرَجَا

ḥattā idhā mā al-ṣayfu kāna amajā / wa-rahibā min ḥandhihi an yahrajā

The selected lines mean: “Until summer became intensely hot, and the two feared that its burning heat would make them dizzy and disoriented.” Here يَهْرَجَا concerns sadar, dizziness caused by intense heat, rather than merely fatigue or loss of strength.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab, H R J: heat-induced dizziness and Al-ʿAjjāj’s witness

In this witness, حَنْذ denotes intense heat affecting animals.

It is not the same as roasting meat; the subject is the natural heat of the sun.

The usages nevertheless share a feature: intense heat can change something’s condition.

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad quotes the line containing حَنْذ in Al-ʿAyn to explain the root, relating it to sunlight heating these animals in stony surroundings.

The witness appears in transmissions of Al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry. Some versions in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Jamharat al-Lughah place another line, about the two animals finishing their grazing, between the selected parts. Two excerpts are therefore quoted here, rather than necessarily consecutive lines. Al-ʿAjjāj belongs to the early Islamic and Umayyad periods; the witness should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic poetry.

Jamharat al-Lughah: the rajaz witness with the intervening grazing line

Its importance lies in recording the root’s use in early literary Arabic.

The witness also shows that حنذ was not solely a cooking term, but could describe natural heat.

Among the evidence examined for ح ن ذ, no independently attributed and securely dated pre-Islamic verse was established that could alone serve as the historical basis of this meaning.

3. بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ in Hūd 11:69

وَلَقَدْ جَاءَتْ رُسُلُنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ بِالْبُشْرَىٰ قَالُوا سَلَامًا قَالَ سَلَامٌ فَمَا لَبِثَ أَنْ جَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ

wa-la-qad jāʾat rusulunā ibrāhīma bi-l-bushrā qālū salāman qāla salāmun fa-mā labitha an jāʾa bi-ʿijlin ḥanīdhin

Translation: “Our messengers came to Abraham with good news. They said, ‘Peace’; he said, ‘Peace’; and before long he brought a roasted calf.”

Hūd 11:69

This is the only Qur’anic verse containing the root ح ن ذ.

حَنِيذٍ modifies عِجْلٍ. Both are indefinite, masculine singular, and in the genitive case.

عجل, from ع ج ل, means a calf: a young bovine.

حنيذ expresses how it has been prepared: a calf roasted for food.

The verse therefore does not concern bringing a live calf for display or sale; the context indicates preparing food for guests.

The meaning of حنيذ in this verse

The accounts of Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, Ibn Fāris, Al-Jawharī, and Ibn Manẓūr indicate that حنيذ denotes roasted, cooked meat.

Roasting with heated stones is one of the word’s most distinctive established usages in early Arabic.

A lexical explanation can therefore describe عجل حنيذ as a roasted calf, prepared in the established method of ḥandh using the heat of stones or a heated enclosure.

In the basic translation, however, “a roasted calf” is preferable: it conveys the word’s clear meaning without adding details of a specific cooking method not stated in the verse.

Nor does the verse specify the number of pieces of meat, the size of the fire, the type of stone, or the cooking time.

These details may be useful for understanding food history and Arabic cooking techniques, but are not the verse’s direct meaning.

فَمَا لَبِثَ and Abraham’s conduct

The verse first describes the messengers’ arrival and exchange of greetings.

It then states: فَمَا لَبِثَ أَنْ جَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ.

The phrase indicates that Abraham did not delay long in bringing food.

Prompt hospitality is conveyed by the verb and construction فما لبث, rather than by حنيذ itself.

حنيذ describes the food’s preparation; how quickly Abraham brought it is a separate matter.

Nor does the sentence imply that the calf was roasted in an instant. It does not state the precise preparation time, but emphasizes the short delay in the narrative.

Why a calf?

عِجْل names the animal, while حنيذ describes its preparation as food.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26, recounting the same scene, also states that Abraham went to his family and brought a fat calf.

Hūd 11:69 describes the calf as حنيذ, and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26 as سمين.

The descriptions are compatible.

The calf could have been fat, and its meat roasted.

Yet the words have different meanings: سمين concerns fatness, and حنيذ roasting.

حنيذ should therefore not be translated merely as “fat,” just as سمين alone does not mean “roasted.”

The comparison shows how parallel passages can provide a fuller picture of an event without erasing lexical differences.

Hospitality toward the guests

In Hūd 11:70, Abraham becomes apprehensive when he sees that his guests’ hands do not reach toward the food.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:27 also describes bringing the food close to the guests and asking whether they will eat.

This continuity shows that the roasted calf was prepared for hospitality.

حنيذ itself, however, cannot establish a general ruling about the amount of food served, the number of guests, or an obligation to provide a particular dish.

Hūd 11:69 also does not explain why the guests do not eat; Abraham’s response and the messengers’ explanation appear in the following verses.

Lexical research should therefore not add unstated narrative details to the word’s meaning.

In this verse, حنيذ has a clear and limited contribution: describing roasted food.

4. Distinctions from related roots and words

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and ش و ي (شَوَى)

In Arabic, شَوَى denotes roasting meat through the heat of fire.

حَنَذَ also belongs to roasting, but heated stones and sometimes cooking in a covered pit are prominent in its established early usage.

شواء can therefore be a broader designation for roasting, while حنذ in some usages denotes a particular method.

Yet some lexicographers also explain حنذ simply as roasting. Not every usage should therefore be declared necessarily distinct from شواء or restricted to a single technique.

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and ن ض ج (نَضَجَ)

نضج concerns becoming cooked or ripe.

Ibn Fāris uses إنضاج الشيء to explain the principle of حنذ.

نضج does not necessarily specify a particular cooking method. Something may become cooked in different ways.

In its use concerning meat, حنذ more specifically indicates roasting through heat.

نضج thus emphasizes the result of becoming cooked, while حنذ is more closely associated with the method or condition of roasting.

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and ط ب خ (طَبْخ)

طبخ concerns cooking food and can encompass various methods of preparation.

In its established usage, حنيذ is roasted meat.

Every ḥanīdh dish has therefore been cooked, but not every cooked dish is necessarily ḥanīdh.

For example, food boiled in water may be thoroughly cooked, but that fact alone does not justify calling it حنيذ.

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and س م ن (سَمِين)

سمين concerns fatness or plumpness.

حنيذ concerns roasting and cooking by heat.

In Hūd 11:69 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26, the descriptions concern a calf in the same story.

Each nevertheless has its own meaning.

“Fat” should therefore not replace “roasted” in translating حنيذ.

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and ح م ي (حَمِيَ)

In its thermal branch, ح م ي concerns becoming warm or hot.

In the method of ḥandh, stones or the cooking enclosure are heated; the semantic fields therefore have a practical connection.

حمي itself, however, does not mean roasting meat.

Iron or water can be heated without any act of ḥandh taking place.

In its culinary use, by contrast, ḥandh concerns preparing meat through heat.

Study of Ḥ M Y

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and ح م م (حَمِيم)

In one branch, حميم denotes hot water; in Qur’anic usages concerning punishment, it denotes very hot water.

حنيذ describes roasted meat.

The words can be close through heat, but their substances and usages differ.

حميم also has another Qur’anic meaning, an intimate friend, which must not be transferred to ح ن ذ.

Study of Ḥ M M

ح ن ذ (حَنِيذ) and ح ن ث (حِنْث)

In the Qur’an, ح ن ث concerns violating an oath and grave sin.

ح ن ذ concerns roasting and the effects of heat.

They share their initial consonants but differ in their final consonant and semantic field.

There are therefore no lexical grounds for connecting حنيذ in Hūd 11:69 with oaths, sin, or breaking a commitment.

Study of Ḥ N TH

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Hūd 11:69, بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ should be rendered as “a roasted calf.”

This equivalent is brief, natural, and consistent with the meaning recorded in early dictionaries.

Forqan’s existing Persian translation also uses this expression:

“Our messengers came to Abraham with good news. They said, ‘Peace’; he said, ‘Peace’; and before long he brought a roasted calf.”

This translation needs no change.

The root explanation can add that حنيذ in established Arabic usage denotes meat roasted with heated stones or in a hot, covered space.

Replacing the direct translation with “a calf cooked in a pit with heated stones” is less suitable, because the verse does not explain the precise cooking method.

Nor is “a fat, fatty calf” an appropriate equivalent for حنيذ. Fatness is conveyed by سمين in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26, rather than by this root itself.

Outside the Qur’an, حَنَذَ اللَّحْمَ can be translated as “he roasted the meat”; لَحْمٌ مَحْنُوذٌ as “roasted meat”; حَنَذَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ as “intense sunlight affected his body” or “the sun scorched him”; and حِنَاذُ الْخَيْلِ as “making a horse sweat by covering its body.”

In each case, the verb’s object and the circumstances must determine whether the subject is cooking food or heat affecting a body.

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Roasting and cooking through heat, especially with heated stones; in other usages, the effects of intense heat and inducing sweat.

Ibn Fāris identifies its principle as إنضاج الشيء. Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad and Al-Jawharī likewise regard roasting meat with heated stones as a principal usage.

Other uses in the same family express intense sunlight and making a horse sweat by covering its body.

The meanings share the field of heat and its effects on a substance or body, but their details differ.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an:

بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ in Hūd 11:69: a roasted calf.

This describes food Abraham brought for his guests. In the parallel passage Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26, the calf is described as سمين; سمين concerns fatness, whereas حنيذ concerns roasting.

The promptness in bringing food is also understood from فَمَا لَبِثَ, rather than from حنيذ itself.

The root’s prominent semantic center is roasting and cooking through heat; its usages concerning sunlight and induced sweating also express heat’s effects. Its Qur’anic use concerns roasted meat. This family should not be generalized to every change of state caused by heat.

The method of cooking with heated stones matters for lexical explanation, but the direct Qur’anic translation need not add its details.

“A roasted calf” is consequently precise and sufficient. Historical explanations of the cooking method, the effects of heat, and connections with other Arabic usages belong in the root article, allowing the verse’s clear, brief meaning to remain distinct from unstated details.
