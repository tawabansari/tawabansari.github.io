Core meaning: illicit sexual intercourse; in related Arabic uses, attributing that act to someone and describing its perpetrator. The precise boundaries of lawful relations require contextual and historical examination beyond the root alone.

1. Introduction and meaning in pre-Islamic Arabic

Z N Y concerns illicit sexual intercourse between a woman and a man, commonly explained in contrast with a lawful marital relationship. زَنَى names the act, زِنًى and زِنَاء are verbal nouns, and زَانٍ and زَانِيَة describe the man and woman who commit it.

The primary meaning is not mere attraction, affection, sexual desire, looking, or emotional connection, but intercourse regarded as illicit. “Outside marriage” is a useful introductory contrast, not an exhaustive historical legal definition: passages such as Al-Muʾminūn 23:6 and Al-Maʿārij 70:30 also require examination when discussing the historical category of those possessed by the right hand. Those questions cannot be settled through this root alone.

The term can apply whether the person is married or unmarried. Limiting it to a married person’s adultery does not capture its whole range.

Lexical explanations sometimes use فُجُور, but that word has a wider field of transgression and wrongdoing. Not every instance of it is zinā.

The particular sense discussed here concerns sexual conduct.

Zinā and marriage in early poetry

Al-Aʿsha, known for his pre-Islamic poetry, says:

وَأَقْرَرْتُ عَيْنِي مِنَ الْغَانِيَاتِ
إِمَّا نِكَاحًا وَإِمَّا أُزَنّْ

wa-aqrartu ʿaynī mina al-ghāniyāti / immā nikāḥan wa-immā uzann

“I delighted in women, either through marriage or—in one reading—by committing zinā; in the other, by being suspected of it.” The final phrase depends on the disputed analysis.

The poet speaks of delight in women and contrasts نِكَاح with أُزَنّ.

Two lexical analyses have been offered for أُزَنّ.

Some explain it as a shortened أُزَنِّي, “I commit zinā.” On that reading, marriage is contrasted with intercourse outside it.

Another derives it from Z N N, concerning suspicion and attribution: what others suspect of the poet, rather than necessarily his direct admission.

The quotation matters to the distinction, but its disputed analysis makes it unsuitable as an unqualified example of the verb زَنَى.

Another Arabic quotation

A verse transmitted in lexical records reads:

أَمَّا الزِّنَاءُ فَإِنِّي لَسْتُ قَارِبَهُ
وَالْمَالُ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ الْخَمْرِ نِصْفَانِ

ammā al-zināʾu fa-innī lastu qāribahu / wa-al-mālu baynī wa-bayna al-khamri niṣfāni

Translation:

“As for zinā, I do not approach it; my wealth is divided equally between me and wine.”

الزِّنَاء names a particular act the poet says he avoids.

It is treated separately from drinking wine, and the word has its sexual sense.

The poet and date are not securely identified in the evidence examined here. It attests recorded Arabic usage, not a firmly dated pre-Islamic composition.

Historical definition and limits of the evidence

Early lexical records distinguish zinā from marriage and identify it as sexual conduct.

A few verses cannot establish the dates of every form or the legal boundaries recognized by all pre-Islamic Arab communities.

A defensible account is:

Zinā names illicit sexual intercourse, commonly contrasted with a recognized marriage bond. It names the act rather than mere desire or affection; derivatives can describe the person committing it or the attribution of it. The historical boundaries of licitness require separate evidence.

The Quran uses the sexual sense within passages concerning prohibition, marriage, chastity, responsibility, accusations, and repentance.

Later juristic definitions, including detailed proof and punishment requirements, should not be presented wholesale as the original pre-Quranic lexical meaning.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَنَى يَزْنِي

A Form I verb meaning to commit zinā.

زَنَى الرَّجُلُ

zanā al-rajulu

“The man committed zinā.”

زَنَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ

zanati al-marʾatu

“The woman committed zinā.”

The verb applies to both sexes, rather than naming only male or female conduct.

زِنًى and زِنَاء

Two recorded verbal-noun forms.

The shortened form زِنًى is associated with Hijazi usage.

The extended زِنَاء, ending in hamzah, is associated in lexical accounts with Najd and Banu Tamim.

In ordinary vocalized spelling, the Quranic noun is الْزِّنَىٰ; its Uthmanic spelling also appears as ٱلزِّنَىٰٓ. These orthographic details should be distinguished from the lexical contrast between the shortened and extended forms.

The difference does not itself designate two different acts.

In Persian, the customary spelling is زنا.

زَانٍ

The active participle of زَنَى.

A man who commits zinā.

One plural is زُنَاة.

The Quran uses definite and indefinite forms, including الزَّانِي and زَانٍ.

The participle describes someone through the attributed act. Its morphology alone does not establish a permanent identity continuing after repentance and reform.

زَانِيَة

The feminine active participle.

A woman who commits zinā.

Recorded plurals include زَوَانٍ.

The Quran uses الزَّانِيَة and زَانِيَةً.

Grammatical gender should be distinguished from actual responsibility in a particular event. A sexual act inflicted upon someone does not itself establish their willing participation.

زَانَى يُزَانِي

A Form III verb also recorded for committing zinā.

زَانَى الْمَرْأَةَ

zānā al-marʾata

“He committed zinā with the woman.”

This use involves a relationship between two people.

Form III alone does not prove consent, equal power, or equal responsibility.

مُزَانَاة

The verbal noun of زَانَى.

In the sexual use, it concerns zinā.

Some lexical reports use تُزَانِي of a woman engaged in prostitution.

Sexual wrongdoing and financial gain can thus coincide, but payment is not inherent in every instance of zinā.

زَنَّى يُزَنِّي

In one use, attributing zinā to someone.

زَنَّاهُ

zannāhu

“He accused him of zinā.”

The verb here reports attribution, not necessarily the commission of the act.

The distinction matters.

An accused person has not necessarily committed the act.

The Quran also distinguishes the conduct from accusing another person of it.

تَزْنِيَة

The verbal noun of زَنَّى in its accusatory use.

Attributing zinā to someone or accusing them of it.

This form does not occur in the Quran.

زَنْيَة and زِنْيَة

These occur in expressions such as ابْنُ زِنْيَةٍ or وَلَدُ زِنْيَةٍ concerning birth from intercourse outside marriage. The complete phrase should be distinguished from a standalone word meaning child.

Another recorded use denotes the last child born to a parent.

Vocalization and context help distinguish the meanings.

A term describing birth circumstances does not make a child responsible for the parents’ conduct.

The Quran likewise does not transfer parents’ guilt to their children.

Distinction from Z N ʾ and Z N N

Some similar-looking Arabic forms concern narrowness, pressure, or climbing a mountain.

Some belong to the hamzated root Z N ʾ.

Z N N concerns suspicion and attribution.

Resemblance in writing does not make these identical with Z N Y.

The Quranic sexual meaning cannot be derived from narrowness, climbing, or suspicion without independent evidence.

3. Quranic occurrences

The root occurs nine times in five verses: Al-Isrāʾ 17:32, Al-Nūr 24:2, Al-Nūr 24:3, Al-Furqān 25:68, and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12.

There are two verbs, one verbal noun, and six active participles.

The occurrences can be discussed under prohibition, the responsibility of women and men, marriage, and abstention as a quality of believers.

A. Al-Isrāʾ 17:32: do not approach zinā

وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا الزِّنَىٰ إِنَّهُ كَانَ فَاحِشَةً وَسَاءَ سَبِيلًا

wa-lā taqrabū al-zinā innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa-sāʾa sabīlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32

Translation:

“Do not approach zinā; it is indeed an indecency and an evil way.”

الزِّنَى names the conduct.

تَقْرَبُوا belongs to Q R B and means approaching.

The wording does not merely say not to commit the act; it prohibits approaching it.

This feature of the command matters.

It can encompass avoiding conduct and circumstances that knowingly lead toward the prohibited act.

It does not make every conversation, look, affection, or relationship between a woman and a man itself zinā.

The noun names a specific act; the command concerns keeping away from it and paths leading to it.

The verse then calls it an indecency and an evil way.

فَاحِشَة has a broader range, referring to gravely objectionable conduct.

Zinā is an instance of it here, but every Quranic occurrence of فَاحِشَة should not be reduced to zinā without examination.

B. Al-Nūr 24:2: women’s and men’s responsibility

الزَّانِيَةُ وَالزَّانِي فَاجْلِدُوا كُلَّ وَاحِدٍ مِّنْهُمَا مِائَةَ جَلْدَةٍ وَلَا تَأْخُذْكُم بِهِمَا رَأْفَةٌ فِي دِينِ اللَّهِ إِن كُنتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَلْيَشْهَدْ عَذَابَهُمَا طَائِفَةٌ مِّنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

al-zāniyatu wa-al-zānī fa-ijlidū kulla wāḥidin minhumā miʾata jaldatin wa-lā taʾkhudhkum bihimā raʾfatun fī dīni allāhi in kuntum tuʾminūna bi-allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri wa-l-yashhad ʿadhābahumā ṭāʾifatun mina al-muʾminīn

Al-Nūr 24:2

Translation:

“The woman and the man who commit zinā: flog each of them one hundred lashes. If you believe in The God and the Last Day, let no pity for them seize you in the religion of The God; and let a group of believers witness their punishment.”

الزَّانِيَةُ and الزَّانِي are respectively feminine and masculine active participles.

The Quran names both in stating the ruling.

The act is not treated solely as a woman’s conduct; the man is independently named as responsible.

The sentence specifies one hundred lashes for each.

The number and punishment come from the full sentence, not the lexical root.

Applying the ruling requires distinguishing an established act from an accusation. The participle alone does not supply rules of proof or authority to impose punishment; those are separate questions beyond a lexical article.

Conduct and proof of an accusation

Al-Nūr 24:4 gives a separate ruling concerning accusers of chaste women who do not produce four witnesses.

Al-Nūr 24:6, Al-Nūr 24:7, Al-Nūr 24:8, and Al-Nūr 24:9 describe reciprocal sworn declarations when a husband accuses his wife and has no witnesses other than himself.

These distinguish committing the act from accusing someone of it.

The descriptions زَانٍ and زَانِيَة should not be equated with suspicion, social rumor, or an unproved accusation.

Willing participation must also be distinguished from coercion or sexual assault.

A victim cannot be treated as a willing participant merely because an act was inflicted upon them.

This relates to individual responsibility and the explicit prohibition of forced prostitution in Al-Nūr 24:33.

C. Al-Nūr 24:3: zinā and marriage

الزَّانِي لَا يَنكِحُ إِلَّا زَانِيَةً أَوْ مُشْرِكَةً وَالزَّانِيَةُ لَا يَنكِحُهَا إِلَّا زَانٍ أَوْ مُشْرِكٌ وَحُرِّمَ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

al-zānī lā yankiḥu illā zāniyatan aw mushrikatan wa-al-zāniyatu lā yankiḥuhā illā zānin aw mushrikun wa-ḥurrima dhālika ʿalā al-muʾminīn

Al-Nūr 24:3

Translation:

“A man who commits zinā marries only a woman who commits zinā or a woman who associates partners; and a woman who commits zinā is married only by a man who commits zinā or a man who associates partners. That is forbidden to the believers.”

The root occurs four times here, all as active participles.

الزَّانِي and زَانٍ refer to the man; زَانِيَةً and الزَّانِيَةُ to the woman.

The main verb يَنكِحُ belongs to another root.

The meanings of zinā and nikāḥ should therefore not be equated.

Arabic uses nikāḥ for marriage and, in some settings, intercourse. Interpretations differ concerning يَنكِحُ here and the referent of ذَٰلِكَ in the concluding clause.

The detailed marriage ruling requires a separate study of N K H, the sentence, and related verses.

For this root study, the verse describes women and men through zinā and relates that condition to nikāḥ and prohibition.

Juxtaposing the descriptions for zinā and associating partners does not make them synonymous.

Associating partners concerns worship and assigning partners to The God; zinā concerns sexual conduct.

They can coexist in one person without having the same meaning.

D. Al-Furqān 25:68: alongside associating partners and wrongful killing

وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَدْعُونَ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ وَلَا يَقْتُلُونَ النَّفْسَ الَّتِي حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ إِلَّا بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا يَزْنُونَ وَمَن يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ يَلْقَ أَثَامًا

wa-alladhīna lā yadʿūna maʿa allāhi ilāhan ākhara wa-lā yaqtulūna al-nafsa allatī ḥarrama allāhu illā bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-lā yaznūna wa-man yafʿal dhālika yalqa athāmā

Al-Furqān 25:68

Translation:

“Those who invoke no other god alongside The God, do not kill the life The God has made inviolable except with right, and do not commit zinā; whoever does that will encounter punishment.”

يَزْنُونَ is a masculine plural imperfect verb.

It names the prohibited sexual conduct beside invoking another deity and wrongful killing.

The context treats it as serious wrongdoing.

The grouping does not make the three acts lexically identical or equal in every condition, consequence, and worldly ruling.

Each has its own subject and treatment.

After warning of their consequences, Al-Furqān 25:70 makes an explicit exception:

إِلَّا مَن تَابَ وَآمَنَ وَعَمِلَ عَمَلًا صَالِحًا فَأُولَٰئِكَ يُبَدِّلُ اللَّهُ سَيِّئَاتِهِمْ حَسَنَاتٍ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا

illā man tāba wa-āmana wa-ʿamila ʿamalan ṣāliḥan fa-ulāʾika yubaddilu allāhu sayyiʾātihim ḥasanātin wa-kāna allāhu ghafūran raḥīmā

Al-Furqān 25:70

Translation:

“Except whoever repents, believes, and does righteous work: The God will replace their evil deeds with good deeds. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Committing a sin does not close the possibility of repentance and reform.

The participles should not be turned into an unchangeable identity confining a person forever to a sin after repentance and reform.

E. Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12: the pledge of believing women

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِذَا جَاءَكَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتُ يُبَايِعْنَكَ عَلَىٰ أَن لَّا يُشْرِكْنَ بِاللَّهِ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَسْرِقْنَ وَلَا يَزْنِينَ وَلَا يَقْتُلْنَ أَوْلَادَهُنَّ وَلَا يَأْتِينَ بِبُهْتَانٍ يَفْتَرِينَهُ بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِنَّ وَأَرْجُلِهِنَّ وَلَا يَعْصِينَكَ فِي مَعْرُوفٍ فَبَايِعْهُنَّ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُنَّ اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu idhā jāʾaka al-muʾminātu yubāyiʿnaka ʿalā an lā yushrikna bi-allāhi shayʾan wa-lā yasriqna wa-lā yaznīna wa-lā yaqtulna awlādahunna wa-lā yaʾtīna bi-buhtānin yaftarīnahu bayna aydīhinna wa-arjulihinna wa-lā yaʿṣīnaka fī maʿrūfin fa-bāyiʿhunna wa-istaghfir lahunna allāha inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīm

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12

Translation:

“O Prophet, when believing women come to pledge to you that they will associate nothing with The God, will not steal, will not commit zinā, will not kill their children, will not bring a slander they fabricate between their hands and feet, and will not disobey you in what is right, accept their pledge and ask The God to forgive them. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

يَزْنِينَ is a feminine plural imperfect verb.

Abstention from zinā is among the commitments made in the pledge.

These include worship of The God alone and abstention from theft, zinā, killing children, slander, and disobedience in what is right.

Here it is conduct the women undertake to avoid.

The particular female audience does not make abstention solely women’s responsibility.

Al-Nūr 24:2 explicitly names both woman and man.

Al-Furqān 25:68 likewise includes abstention among the qualities of servants of the Entirely Merciful.

The feminine form follows the particular audience, rather than limiting the prohibition to one sex.

4. Zinā, nikāḥ, sifāḥ, and bighāʾ

Z N Y (زِنًى) and N K H (نِكَاح)

The Quranic zinā concerns illicit intercourse, distinguished from a lawful relationship. Determining all historical categories of lawfulness requires more than this root.

Nikāḥ concerns forming a marriage bond, spouses, and related rulings.

The terms intersect in human sexual relations but have distinct lexical meanings and roles in the verses.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 speaks of seeking women with wealth in the condition مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ.

This distinguishes a relationship within iḥṣān from one outside that framework.

Desire or emotional attachment alone is not equivalent to nikāḥ, which is not merely the name of a feeling.

Z N Y (زِنًى) and S F H (سِفَاح)

In Quranic sexual uses, sifāḥ concerns relations outside chastity and lawful commitment.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5 contrast مُسَافِحِينَ with iḥṣān.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 uses غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ of women.

Sifāḥ and zinā are therefore closely related.

Overlapping instances do not make their entire lexical fields identical.

Zinā names prohibited intercourse, whereas sifāḥ here characterizes a relation incompatible with iḥṣān and chastity.

Their precise boundaries require an independent study of S F H.

Z N Y (زِنًى) and B GH Y (بِغَاء)

Al-Nūr 24:33 uses bighāʾ in prohibiting the coercion of enslaved women into prostitution.

The Quran says:

وَلَا تُكْرِهُوا فَتَيَاتِكُمْ عَلَى الْبِغَاءِ

wa-lā tukrihū fatayātikum ʿalā al-bighāʾ

Al-Nūr 24:33

“Do not compel your enslaved women into prostitution.”

The same verse mentions seeking worldly financial gain.

Here exploitation, coercion, and profit are supplied by the whole sentence; they should not all be treated as obligatory components of the noun بِغَاء in every use.

The conduct can overlap with zinā, but not every instance involves money or financial benefit.

Coercion is also different from free and willing participation.

Responsibility therefore requires distinguishing the coercer from the person forced.

This ethical and legal distinction should not be erased by treating everyone involved in an event as equally responsible.

Z N Y (زِنًى) and F H SH (فَاحِشَة)

فَاحِشَة has a broader range of grave indecency and deeply objectionable conduct.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32 explicitly calls zinā a فَاحِشَة.

Related forms appear in broader contexts in Al-Aʿrāf 7:28, Al-Aʿrāf 7:33, and Al-Naḥl 16:90.

Zinā is an instance of indecency, but indecency does not necessarily mean zinā in every verse.

This also matters in Al-Nisāʾ 4:15 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:16: the former names الْفَاحِشَة and the latter refers back with a pronoun; identifying the conduct requires contextual examination.

Z N Y (زِنًى) and H S N (إِحْصَان)

Iḥṣān concerns protection and inviolability, and in some uses chastity or marriage.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 has مُحْصَنَاتٍ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ.

Al-Māʾidah 5:5 also discusses chaste women and a bond distinguished from sifāḥ and secret lovers.

Here iḥṣān concerns ordering and preserving sexual boundaries.

It does not mean married in every verse; chastity or marital status may be prominent in different contexts.

One fixed technical definition should not be imposed on all relevant passages without examination.

5. Zinā and human responsibility

Prohibited conduct differs from an unproved accusation

The Quran distinguishes committing zinā from being accused of it.

Al-Nūr 24:2 speaks of the woman and man who commit the act.

Al-Nūr 24:4 addresses accusers who do not bring sufficient witnesses.

Suspicion, reputation, family hostility, or hearsay cannot alone establish the accusation.

An accusation can be false and itself cause injustice.

Alongside prohibition, the Quran protects human honor and dignity.

Al-Nūr 24:12 calls believers to think well of one another when hearing the accusation.

Al-Nūr 24:15 condemns passing along statements without knowledge.

The seriousness of the prohibition does not authorize spreading accusations, spying, or disregarding evidence.

Repentance and reform

Al-Furqān 25:70 explicitly presents repentance, belief, and righteous action after the grave sins mentioned previously.

A past act does not permanently exclude a person from reform and return.

Describing an act should therefore be distinguished from a definitive judgment about someone’s entire character and future.

This does not trivialize the conduct or deny responsibility.

Responsibility for wrongdoing does not abolish repentance and reform.

Nor does a parent’s, spouse’s, or other relative’s wrongdoing make another family member guilty.

Al-Anʿām 6:164 states that no one bears another’s burden of sin.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents distinguish the principal forms. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَنَى يَزْنِي: committed or commits zinā; engaged in illicit sexual intercourse.

زِنًى: zinā; the prohibited sexual act.

زِنَاء: another verbal-noun form in the same semantic field.

زَانٍ: a man who commits zinā.

زَانِيَة: a woman who commits zinā.

زُنَاة: a plural of زَانٍ, people committing the act.

زَوَانٍ: a plural of زَانِيَة.

زَانَى: committed zinā with another person in the sexual use.

مُزَانَاة: engaging in illicit intercourse in the relevant use.

زَنَّاهُ: accused him of zinā.

تَزْنِيَة: attributing zinā to someone.

زَنْيَة / زِنْيَة: used in expressions concerning birth outside marriage; also recorded for a parent’s last child. The phrase and context determine the sense.

Al-Nūr 24:2

A suitable brief root meaning is:

“Illicit sexual intercourse; derivatives also describe its perpetrator or the attribution of it.”

For active participles, the contextual meaning should concern someone who commits the act, not someone merely accused.

Direct translation should not extend zinā to every sexual desire, affection, friendship, or emotional relationship.

7. Conclusion

Z N Y concerns illicit sexual intercourse, commonly contrasted with a lawful marital bond.

Early Arabic distinguishes zinā and nikāḥ, though some poetic forms, including al-Aʿsha’s أُزَنّ, have disputed analyses and should not be presented as unqualified evidence for one root.

زِنًى and زِنَاء are recorded verbal nouns; زَانٍ and زَانِيَة describe the man and woman committing the act.

The root occurs nine times across five Quranic verses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32 prohibits approaching it and calls it an indecency and an evil way.

Al-Nūr 24:2 names both woman and man in stating the ruling.

Al-Nūr 24:3 discusses its relation to nikāḥ.

Al-Furqān 25:68 includes abstention alongside avoiding associating partners and wrongful killing among qualities of servants of the Entirely Merciful.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12 includes abstention in believing women’s pledge.

The root relates to indecency, sifāḥ, iḥṣān, nikāḥ, bighāʾ, and repentance, without being identical to them.

The Quran also distinguishes commission from accusation. Responsibility should not be transferred to a victim of coercion or to someone merely related to the perpetrator.

The central Quranic meaning is illicit sexual intercourse; the root alone does not establish every condition of lawfulness, proof, or responsibility.

The lexical definition must remain distinct from rulings on proof, punishment, marriage, repentance, and judgments about individuals, which require the relevant passages and separate analysis.
