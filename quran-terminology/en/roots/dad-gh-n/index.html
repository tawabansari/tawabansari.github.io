Concealed resentment, inward inclination, and the disclosure of hostility

In early Arabic, ض غ ن concerns resentment, hostility, and an inclination lodged within a person or expressed in a living creature’s behaviour, which may remain hidden. In human relations, ضِغْن and ضَغَن denote inward resentment and hostility; ضَغِينَة likewise denotes a grudge held against another person.

Other Arabic usages connect this family with inclination in a particular direction, resistance in movement, crookedness, and even holding something close. A camel drawn towards its familiar home and resisting movement elsewhere, for example, is described as ذات ضِغْن. Related forms also describe a crooked shaft or spear and an animal reluctant to obey.

The family is thus not restricted to resentment. Nevertheless, the historical claim that all these meanings arose from one original image cannot be established with certainty. Early lexicographers connected them through inclination, crookedness, and concealment, but a semantic explanation does not itself establish a word’s historical development.

The root occurs twice in the Quran, both times as the plural أَضْغَان: أَضْغَانَهُمْ in Muḥammad 47:29 and أَضْغَانَكُمْ in Muḥammad 47:37. Both refer to inward resentments and hostilities whose disclosure is discussed.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In early Arabic, ضِغْن describes resentment and hostility in human relations. A person bearing ضغن towards another is not merely displeased but harbours a hostile inclination.

This condition may remain within, appear in speech, or result in hostile conduct. Concealment is prominent in the human usage, but resentment does not necessarily remain hidden forever.

An early witness concerning human resentment

A verse attributed to Abū Qays ibn Rifāʿah, and also transmitted under other attributions, reads:

وَذِي ضِغْنٍ كَفَفْتُ النَّفْسَ عَنْهُ
وَكُنْتُ عَلَى مَسَاءَتِهِ مُقِيتًا

wa-dhī ḍighnin kafaftu al-nafsa ʿanhu / wa-kuntu ʿalā masāʾatihi muqītan

Translation: “Against one bearing a grudge I restrained myself, though I was able to cause him harm.”

ذِي ضِغْنٍ describes someone harbouring resentment or hostility. The poet speaks of restraint despite possessing the ability to retaliate or hurt him.

The witness shows ضغن used between people, where it could underlie discord and confrontation. The word itself does not yet denote a violent act: harbouring resentment and acting upon it are distinct.

The verse’s attribution varies in the sources: alongside Abū Qays ibn Rifāʿah, Al-Zubayr ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib is also named. Neither its attribution nor its precise date should be presented as certain. It remains useful evidence for an early usage, without independently proving a pre-Quranic date.

An early witness concerning animal inclination and resistance

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim reads:

فَإِنَّكَ وَالشَّكَاةَ مِنْ آلِ لَأْمٍ
كَذَاتِ الضِّغْنِ تَمْشِي فِي الرِّفَاقِ

fa-innaka wa-al-shakāta min āli Laʾmin / ka-dhāti al-ḍighni tamshī fī al-rifāqi

Translation: “You, in complaining of the house of Laʾm, are like the she-camel drawn towards home, walking in restraints.”

ذات الضغن here describes a camel drawn towards a familiar place or home and reluctant to proceed elsewhere.

الرِّفاق denotes a restraint used to limit an animal’s movement. The image is of outward movement at odds with an inward pull.

The explanation here does not require attributing human moral hostility to the animal. The relevant sense is an inclination at odds with the intended direction of movement.

This witness matters because it shows ضغن describing an inward inclination and resistance to an imposed direction.

Although the poet is classified as pre-Islamic, this individual verse’s date has not been independently established here. The antiquity of an attribution should be distinguished from proof of a precise date of usage.

Physical and tangible usages

Classical Arabic records قَنَاةٌ ضَغِنَةٌ for a crooked or twisted spear shaft. فَرَسٌ ضَاغِنٌ describes a horse that does not readily give its full running effort and must be urged to greater movement.

اضْطَغَنَ الشَّيْءَ also denotes taking and holding something beneath the arm or against the body. اضْطِغَان is further recorded for wrapping the body in a garment.

These usages involve crookedness, inclination, resistance, and enclosing. They do not establish that resentment originally derived from a bent spear or something hidden under an arm.

An early account connects this family through concealment accompanied by inclination or crookedness. That may illuminate similarities between branches, but without historical evidence establishing their sequence, it should not be presented as the root’s certain origin.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • ضِغْن and ضَغَن: resentment and inward hostility; in some usages, an inclination in a direction.
  • أَضْغَان: the plural of ضغن, resentments or inward hostilities; the only form of this family occurring in the Quran.
  • ضَغِينَة: a grudge or hostility harboured against another.
  • ضَغَائِن: the plural of ضغينة, grudges or hostilities.
  • ضَغِنَ: to harbour resentment or show hostility; in other constructions, to incline or become crooked.
  • ضَاغِن: someone bearing resentment; of a horse, reluctant to obey or to exert its full running ability.
  • أَضْغَنَ: to harbour hostility.
  • اضْطَغَنَ: to bear a grudge; alternatively, to hold something under the arm or against the body.
  • تَضَاغَنَ: to harbour mutual resentment.
  • مُضَاغَنَة: mutual hostility or antagonism.
  • ضَغُون: an animal difficult to move or direct.

These forms are not identical in meaning. ضغينة identifies a grudge, تضاغن can express a reciprocal relationship, and some usages of اضطغن concern holding something rather than hostility at all.

Prepositions also help determine meaning.

ضَغِنَ عَلَيْهِ highlights hostility directed against someone. ضَغِنَ إِلَيْهِ expresses inclination towards a person or destination. Inclining towards peace is also recorded with this root.

A negative moral meaning cannot therefore be inferred merely from the presence of the three consonants ض غ ن in any construction.

Among these forms, the Quran uses only أَضْغَان. In both occurrences the plural takes an attached pronoun, referring to resentments belonging to a group of people.

3. أَضْغَانَهُمْ: resentments that will not remain hidden

Muḥammad 47:29

أَمْ حَسِبَ الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ أَنْ لَنْ يُخْرِجَ اللَّهُ أَضْغَانَهُمْ

am ḥasiba alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun an lan yukhrija Allāhu aḍghānahum

Muḥammad 47:29

Translation: “Or do those in whose hearts is sickness suppose that The God will not bring their resentments to light?”

أَضْغَانَهُمْ combines the plural of ضغن with هُمْ: their resentments or inward hostilities.

Grammatically it is the object of يُخْرِجَ, whose subject is اللَّهُ. The verse explicitly describes The God bringing their resentments to light.

Resentment within and its disclosure

The description الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ identifies those of whom the Quran speaks as having sickness in their hearts.

Earlier verses describe people whose reactions to divine commands are inconsistent, who turn away after guidance has become clear, or who agree to cooperate in certain matters with opponents of revelation.

Muḥammad 47:26 describes people secretly promising obedience in some matters to opponents of revelation.

The context shows that Muḥammad 47:29 does not concern a merely passing unpleasant feeling. It concerns inward hostility connected with their conduct towards divine guidance.

أَنْ لَنْ يُخْرِجَ اللَّهُ أَضْغَانَهُمْ expresses their supposition that what they harbour will remain hidden.

The verse rejects that supposition.

إخراج here concerns revealing something hidden within a person, rather than physically removing an object from a container. Resentment can become visible in speech, conduct, or decisions.

How is resentment recognized?

The next verse, Muḥammad 47:30, addresses recognition through signs and manner of speech. This connects disclosure of an inward condition with outward indications.

Muḥammad 47:31 also speaks of testing people and disclosing their condition through striving and steadfastness.

Within this context, resentment is not an inward feeling disconnected from action. Responsibility, choice, or trial may bring what a person harbours into outward conduct.

These verses do not, however, give people unlimited authority to pronounce on others’ hidden resentments after hearing an unwelcome statement or disagreement.

The God knows the reality of hearts. People can examine observable indications and conduct, but may misjudge others’ intentions.

Nor should “sickness in the heart” here be converted into a physical or psychiatric diagnosis. In this context it concerns moral disposition and a person’s relationship to truth and guidance.

4. أَضْغَانَكُمْ: resentment disclosed in relation to wealth

Muḥammad 47:37

إِنْ يَسْأَلْكُمُوهَا فَيُحْفِكُمْ تَبْخَلُوا وَيُخْرِجْ أَضْغَانَكُمْ

in yasʾalkumūhā fa-yuḥfikum tabkhalū wa-yukhrij aḍghānakum

Muḥammad 47:37

Translation: “If He were to ask you for it and press you insistently, you would withhold and He would bring your resentments to light.”

أَضْغَان again is the plural of ضغن, now joined to كُمْ and addressing a group directly.

The surrounding verses are necessary to its understanding.

Muḥammad 47:36 states that The God rewards faith and mindfulness and does not ask people for their wealth. Read beside the call to spend in Muḥammad 47:38, this must not be taken as denying every financial responsibility. Muḥammad 47:37 presents a conditional situation: demanding their wealth insistently would lead to withholding and disclosure of resentment. A common explanation concerns an exhaustive demand for all their wealth.

Muḥammad 47:38 then calls people to spend in The God’s way and describes those who withhold in response.

This sequence places Muḥammad 47:37 within people’s relationship to wealth, giving, and financial responsibility.

Why can wealth bring resentment to light?

Wealth provides necessities, security, comfort, and provision for the future. Losing it, especially under a heavy demand, can provoke anxiety and resistance.

The verse depicts an insistent demand for wealth that brings withholding and inward hostility into the open.

فَيُحْفِكُمْ concerns pressing insistently in a demand. This qualification must not be dropped. The Quran does not equate an ordinary request for help or giving with such pressure.

Grammatically, تَبْخَلُوا is the result of the condition, and وَيُخْرِجْ is coordinated with it.

Several grammatical explanations identify the subject of يُخْرِجْ: the implicit subject may refer to The God, already the subject of the preceding verbs, or to the demand or withholding understood from the wording. These are alternative analyses of the subject in the same wording, not necessarily different Quranic readings. Their shared point is the disclosure of inward hostility in the stated situation.

ضغن is not identical with withholding or miserliness.

Miserliness concerns withholding gifts and retaining wealth; ضغن concerns inward hostility. Someone may be reluctant to part with wealth without bearing a grudge against a particular person. Conversely, resentment may concern a dispute unrelated to money.

Here the concepts are connected by the specific situation of an insistent demand for wealth. The verse does not establish that everyone declining a financial request necessarily resents the requester.

Someone may genuinely lack the means to give, question a demand’s legitimacy, or need to provide for their family’s necessities. Those circumstances do not by themselves constitute ضغن.

Different addressees in the two verses

Muḥammad 47:29 specifically concerns those with sickness in their hearts. Muḥammad 47:37 addresses a group within a discussion of faith, mindfulness, and wealth.

This difference prevents identifying all the addressees of Muḥammad 47:37 with the group in Muḥammad 47:29 without further evidence.

The first describes disclosure of a particular group’s resentment; the second presents a conditional financial situation and the addressees’ anticipated reaction.

The second verse likewise does not issue a general command to press people for their wealth. Its conditional form and context show that severe financial pressure can have harmful consequences for conduct and relationships.

5. The connection between the two Quranic usages

أَضْغَانَهُمْ and أَضْغَانَكُمْ share a feature: both accompany forms of أَخْرَجَ, bringing out or disclosing.

In Muḥammad 47:29, يُخْرِجَ اللَّهُ أَضْغَانَهُمْ concerns The God disclosing a particular group’s resentments.

In Muḥammad 47:37, وَيُخْرِجْ أَضْغَانَكُمْ places disclosure within a conditional situation concerning wealth.

Both usages therefore concern something within a person that may become outwardly apparent.

Permanent concealment is not essential to the word. Resentment remains resentment after appearing in speech or behaviour. إخراج describes its disclosure in these verses.

Nor is every negative feeling ضغن. People may be upset by a statement, criticize a decision, or oppose someone’s conduct without nurturing hostility.

In these Quranic usages, ضغن goes beyond ordinary displeasure and concerns inward resentment and hostility.

6. Comparison with related roots

ض غ ن (ḍighn) compared with غ ل ل (ghill)

غِلّ in Arabic and the Quran concerns resentment, ill will, and hostility within the heart. It is close to the Quranic usage of ضغن.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 and Al-Ḥijr 15:47 describe removing غِلّ from the breasts of the people of the Garden. In Al-Ḥashr 59:10, believers ask The God not to place resentment towards believers in their hearts.

أضغان in Muḥammad, by contrast, accompanies disclosure of resentment.

Both words can denote inward hostility. The salient distinction in these passages comes chiefly from context and accompanying verbs: removal of resentment in the former passages, disclosure in the two أضغان verses.

This is not an absolute lexical rule. غل does not inherently mean resentment that can be removed, nor ضغن resentment that can be disclosed. Their semantic fields overlap.

The غ ل ل family also includes separate branches concerning fetters, chains, and breach of trust, which must not be conflated with ض غ ن.

ض غ ن (ḍighn) compared with ب غ ض (bughḍ)

بغض concerns aversion to or hatred of a person or thing. The Quran uses بَغْضَاء for intense hatred.

Āl ʿImrān 3:118 describes people whose hatred has appeared through their mouths, while what their breasts conceal is greater.

Thus بغضاء too can exist inwardly and subsequently appear in speech.

An absolute boundary between outward بغضاء and concealed ضغن cannot be maintained.

Nevertheless, harbouring a grudge is prominent in the familiar usage of ضغن, whereas بغضاء more directly expresses aversion and intense hatred itself.

Someone may detest an action without developing a lasting grudge against its agent.

ض غ ن (ḍighn) compared with غ ي ظ (ghayẓ)

غَيْظ concerns intense anger and its inward pressure.

Āl ʿImrān 3:119 describes people biting their fingers in rage when alone.

Such rage may be an intense response to an event, whereas ضغن, in its resentment sense, is a hostile inclination that may persist within.

The distinction is not absolute. Resentment can accompany rage, and anger can develop into lasting hostility.

But being angry in a particular situation does not alone establish that someone bears a grudge.

ض غ ن (ḍighn) compared with ح س د (ḥasad)

حسد concerns displeasure at another’s advantage and, in some usages, wishing that advantage removed.

ضغن denotes inward resentment and need not concern another person’s success or blessings.

Envy may produce resentment, but a dispute, injustice, family feud, or other experience can do so without the other person’s advantages being at issue.

The two may coexist but are not complete synonyms.

ض غ ن (ḍighn) compared with ض غ ث (ḍighth)

These roots differ only in their final consonant, but are semantically distinct.

ض غ ث concerns bundles of stalks or gathered components and, figuratively, intermingled things. أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ in Yūsuf 12:44 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5 belongs to that family.

أَضْغَان, however, is the plural of ضغن and denotes inward resentments in the two verses of Muḥammad.

Resemblance between أَضْغَاث and أَضْغَان must not transfer the meaning of confused dreams to human resentment.

7. Resentment, disagreement, and moral responsibility

Understanding ضغن matters in human relationships because disagreement is not necessarily resentment.

Two people may disagree on a scientific, social, or religious question. One may find the other’s argument mistaken and criticize it openly without harbouring hostility.

Likewise, someone harmed by injustice may seek redress. Seeking justice does not by itself constitute ضغن.

Resentment is at issue when hostility towards a person or group takes hold inwardly and shapes one’s orientation. Someone may wish another harm even where the original dispute is irrelevant.

Resentment alone does not explain every action either. Assessing moral responsibility requires considering conduct, intention, awareness, and consequences separately.

Both verses of Muḥammad discuss disclosure of resentments, but neither commands probing others’ hidden hearts or accusing them on suspicion.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 forbids much suspicion and spying. This principle prevents turning ضغن into a tool for accusing people merely because they disagree with us.

Where hostility becomes observable injustice, corruption, or harm, responsibility should be assessed on real evidence. A concealed intention does not exempt observable conduct and its consequences from moral assessment.

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

For أَضْغَان in both Quranic occurrences, “resentments” is concise, natural, and defensible.

“Hidden hostilities” clarifies part of the sense but may add more explanation than a direct translation needs, especially as يُخْرِجَ or يُخْرِجْ already expresses disclosure.

“Hatreds” is another close equivalent, though it conveys less of the tendency distinguishing بغضاء from ضغن.

“Concealed grudges” can explain the context, but concealment must not be treated as so inseparable from the word that an exposed grudge ceases to be ضغن.

For Muḥammad 47:29, the key phrase may be rendered:

أَنْ لَنْ يُخْرِجَ اللَّهُ أَضْغَانَهُمْ — “that The God will not bring their resentments to light.”

In Muḥammad 47:37:

وَيُخْرِجْ أَضْغَانَكُمْ — “and He would bring your resentments to light.”

This keeps the noun’s meaning distinct from إخراج: resentment belongs to the noun, while disclosure comes from the accompanying verb.

“Inward hostilities” can provide a fuller explanation, but “resentments” is clearer and more economical in the base translation.

Other derivatives require appropriate equivalents: ضغينة, a grudge or concealed resentment; ethical اضطغان, harbouring a grudge; تضاغن, mutual resentment. Animal, shaft, or enclosing usages instead require contextual equivalents such as inclination, resistance, crookedness, or holding against the body.

9. Conclusion

ض غ ن is an early Arabic family with several related usages. Its principal human sense is resentment lodged within. Other branches describe inclination, resistance in an animal’s movement, crookedness, and holding something close.

Inclination, resistance, crookedness, and concealment can explain similarities among these usages, but their historical sequence is not securely established. None should be asserted without evidence as the original and sole meaning of every derivative.

The Quran uses only the plural أَضْغَان, in two verses of Muḥammad.

Muḥammad 47:29 concerns those with sickness in their hearts who suppose The God will not disclose their resentments.

Muḥammad 47:37 presents an insistent demand for wealth as a conditional situation exposing withholding and inward resentments.

Both concern resentment and its possible disclosure. ضغن is nevertheless distinct from every instance of displeasure, anger, envy, withholding, or disagreement.

The root’s range can be summarized in two branches:

1. In human relations: resentment and hostility towards a person or group, lodged within and either remaining concealed or appearing in speech and behaviour.

2. In other Arabic usages: inclination in a direction, crookedness and resistance in movement, and in some derivatives, holding or enclosing something.

The first meaning applies in the Quran. أَضْغَان denotes inward resentments and hostilities that a person may hide from others, but cannot hide from The God.
