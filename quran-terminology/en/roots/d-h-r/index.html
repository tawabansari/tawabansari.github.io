Driving away, removing, and repelling from a particular place or position

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D Ḥ R denotes driving away, removing, or repelling someone or something. It concerns more than distance or being far away: an action causes someone or something to be driven from a place, domain, or position.

This matters. Something may have been distant from the outset, whereas daḥr involves driving away. Lexical sources consequently explain it through expulsion, repulsion, distancing, and removal.

Some Arabic uses associate daḥr with force, humiliation, or exclusion from a position. These should not all become fixed components of the root. Whether expulsion involves blame, disgrace, punishment, or force depends on subject and context.

The family occurs four times in the Qur’an: three as madḥūran and once as duḥūran. Its uses concern Iblīs’ expulsion, the outcome of people responding wrongly to guidance, and devils being driven from listening to the highest assembly.

Their common feature is being excluded or kept away from a position or access. What one is expelled from, why, and with what consequences must follow from the verses themselves.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic meaning

Qur’anic forms and derivatives

D Ḥ R occurs four times in four verses, in two morphological forms.

مَدْحُورًا: a passive participle of Form I daḥara, on the mafʿūl pattern, meaning driven away or removed. It occurs three times:

  • Al-Aʿrāf 7:18
  • Al-Isrāʾ 17:18
  • Al-Isrāʾ 17:39

دُحُورًا: a verbal noun meaning driving away or removing, in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9.

The basic Arabic verb is دَحَرَ يَدْحَرُ. Both daḥr and duḥūr are recorded verbal nouns.

Other family members include dāḥir, one driving away, and madḥūr, one driven away.

اِدْحَرْ is the imperative, meaning drive away, remove, or repel.

Later and present-day Arabic also uses indaḥara and indiḥār for being repelled, defeated, or driven from the field. This description does not establish the precise date these forms arose; they are not Qur’anic derivatives.

The Qur’an uses only duḥūr and madḥūr from this family. Daḥara itself and its imperative do not occur.

Qur’anic meaning should therefore be established primarily from these four uses rather than every possible or later use of the family.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four occurrences in two forms

The semantic core: expelling and removing

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle:

الطَّرْدُ وَالْإِبْعَادُ

al-ṭardu wa-al-ibʿādu

“Expelling and removing.”

This brief definition identifies two related aspects: the act of expelling, and removal from the position or point of reference from which something is excluded.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: expelling and removing

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, attributed to Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, explains daḥartuhu through بَعَّدْتُهُ وَنَحَّيْتُهُ: I put him at a distance and removed him.

Al-Azharī likewise emphasizes distancing something from something else.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab associate daḥr with repelling and removing. Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt similarly explains it through expelling and distancing.

These sources largely agree on the core: daḥr is driving away that results in removal or exclusion.

Al-ʿAyn: distancing and removing

Removing versus simply being distant

In Arabic, something may be distant without anyone having expelled it.

Two cities, for example, may be far apart; that distance alone is not daḥr.

By contrast, driving someone from somewhere or preventing their approach involves an act of repulsion.

Daḥr therefore expresses more than a state of distance: the action’s direction and result also matter.

An obvious bodily movement need not occur in every use. Someone may be excluded from status, access, or a position without every detail being explained as spatial displacement.

This matters in eschatological passages, where expulsion cannot be restricted without evidence to bodily travel between earthly locations.

3. Daḥr in early Arabic usage

Driving away and removing

Dictionaries record daḥarahu for driving someone or something away.

An agent removes something from a domain or position.

Daḥartuhu ʿani al-shayʾ makes the relationship clearer: someone has been driven or distanced from something.

Lexical explanations consequently use tanḥiyah, removal or setting aside.

This does not inherently determine whether the act is rightful. Someone may repel an intruder or unjustly expel another person from their position.

Its ethical significance follows from agent, cause, and circumstances, rather than the root alone.

Forceful and humiliating expulsion

Explaining one use, Lisān al-ʿArab emphasizes forceful repulsion accompanied by humiliation and abasement.

Daḥr can therefore describe harsh, humiliating removal in some circumstances.

Yet the same dictionary also gives the general meanings of repelling and distancing.

The basic sense should therefore be distinguished from features of a particular use.

If someone is driven away with blame and disgrace, those features may belong to the whole sentence without all deriving from daḥr alone.

Qur’anic madḥūr likewise accompanies separate descriptions—madhʾūm, madhmūm, and malūm—in three verses. Their independent meanings should not all be folded into madḥūr.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the general sense and forceful uses

Historical witnesses and their limits

Early dictionaries establish daḥr as driving away and removing. This study, however, did not obtain a securely attributed, independently reliable pre-Qur’anic poetic text that could be confidently assigned to a pre-Islamic poet.

Later sources transmit expressions about Satan being expelled or driven away. They show continued usage but cannot establish pre-Qur’anic language without investigating attribution and date.

Rather than inventing a pre-Islamic poetic example, this study therefore relies on documented lexical evidence and four clear Qur’anic occurrences.

4. Madḥūran: driven away and excluded from a position

A. Al-Aʿrāf 7:18: Iblīs expelled

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18

قَالَ اخْرُجْ مِنْهَا مَذْءُومًا مَدْحُورًا لَمَنْ تَبِعَكَ مِنْهُمْ لَأَمْلَأَنَّ جَهَنَّمَ مِنْكُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

qāla ukhruj minhā madhʾūman madḥūran la-man tabiʿaka minhum la-amlaʾanna jahannama minkum ajmaʿīna

“He said: Leave it, blameworthy and expelled. Whoever among them follows you—I will certainly fill Hell with all of you.”

The command ukhruj minhā and the description madḥūran occur together.

Ukhruj commands departure; minhā identifies the reference from which departure is required.

Madḥūran describes the addressee’s state: expelled and removed from his former position.

The distinction matters.

Khurūj can mean leaving a place, but daḥr here shows more than voluntary movement: Iblīs is expelled by divine command.

Earlier verses explain why. Iblīs disobeys the command given to him and, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:12, justifies himself by claiming superiority over Adam.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:13 also commands departure alongside al-ṣāghirīn.

His expulsion follows his stance and conduct; daḥr itself does not mean disobedience or arrogance.

How do madhʾūman and madḥūran differ?

Madhʾūman belongs to DH ʾ M; madḥūran belongs to D Ḥ R.

The former concerns censure, fault-finding, and a blameworthy condition; the latter expresses expulsion and removal.

They do not merely repeat one concept.

One foregrounds Iblīs’ blameworthy condition; the other his exclusion.

He is not only censured but also driven from a position.

Madḥūr does not itself mean cursed. Other Qur’anic verses discuss a curse, but cursing and daḥr are separate semantic families.

Their relationship can be studied in Iblīs’ story without replacing one meaning with the other.

Does daḥr always mean exclusion from The God’s mercy?

Lexical and exegetical explanations sometimes describe Iblīs’ expulsion as exclusion from divine mercy.

That reading can be examined within his story, alongside other verses concerning his curse and condition.

Mercy, however, is not part of daḥr’s lexical meaning.

Someone may be expelled from a place, position, or access. What they are expelled from requires sentence and context.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 identifies the reference through minhā, but the pronoun does not fully explain the nature of the position from which Iblīs is driven.

D Ḥ R alone should therefore not establish the unseen world’s spatial structure or the bodily manner of his expulsion.

B. Al-Isrāʾ 17:18: blameworthy and expelled in the outcome

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18

مَنْ كَانَ يُرِيدُ الْعَاجِلَةَ عَجَّلْنَا لَهُ فِيهَا مَا نَشَاءُ لِمَنْ نُرِيدُ ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَا لَهُ جَهَنَّمَ يَصْلَاهَا مَذْمُومًا مَدْحُورًا

man kāna yurīdu al-ʿājilata ʿajjalnā lahu fīhā mā nashāʾu li-man nurīdu thumma jaʿalnā lahu jahannama yaṣlāhā madhmūman madḥūran

“Whoever desires the fleeting life, We hasten within it whatever We will for whomever We intend; then We appoint Hell for him, where he burns, blameworthy and expelled.”

Madḥūran concludes the sentence alongside madhmūman.

Madhmūman belongs to DH M M and denotes being blamed.

Madḥūran is another description, conveying expulsion or exclusion.

They are related but not completely synonymous. Blame expresses negative judgment of someone or their conduct; daḥr foregrounds being excluded from position or access.

Expelled from where?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 identifies the reference through ukhruj minhā.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18, however, does not specify the precise place from which someone is expelled.

It names Hell as the outcome, then describes him as madhmūman madḥūran.

Madḥūran cannot mean being expelled from Hell here, since the construction concerns entering punishment and burning there.

Excluded from a desirable position or denied acceptance is more appropriate, but precisely identifying the object of exclusion requires independent evidence.

“Expelled” preserves this limit without adding an unnamed spatial origin or destination.

Does daḥr mean failing to obtain worldly success?

The verse describes those who desire al-ʿājilah, the immediate life.

Yet it expressly says that The God grants whatever fleeting benefit He wills to whomever He chooses. Their worldly benefit is therefore not necessarily entirely denied.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:19 introduces another group: those who desire the Hereafter and strive appropriately for it while believing.

The contrast clarifies the two orientations and outcomes.

Daḥr in verse 18 does not itself mean poverty, economic failure, or failure in every worldly aim. Someone may enjoy worldly benefits yet ultimately face blame and exclusion.

This should not become the claim that every wealthy or successful person is expelled. The situation follows from all the verse’s conditions and context.

C. Al-Isrāʾ 17:39: warning against associating another deity with The God

Al-Isrāʾ 17:39

ذَٰلِكَ مِمَّا أَوْحَىٰ إِلَيْكَ رَبُّكَ مِنَ الْحِكْمَةِ وَلَا تَجْعَلْ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ فَتُلْقَىٰ فِي جَهَنَّمَ مَلُومًا مَدْحُورًا

dhālika mimmā awḥā ilayka rabbuka mina al-ḥikmati wa-lā tajʿal maʿa allāhi ilāhan ākhara fa-tulqā fī jahannama malūman madḥūran

“This is among the wisdom your Lord has revealed to you. Do not place another deity alongside The God, lest you be cast into Hell, blamed and expelled.”

Madḥūran again concludes a warning, here alongside malūman.

Malūman belongs to L W M and means being reproached.

Madḥūran retains the meaning of being expelled and excluded.

Each of the three occurrences accompanies a different description:

Madhʾūman in Al-Aʿrāf 7:18, madhmūman in Al-Isrāʾ 17:18, and malūman in Al-Isrāʾ 17:39.

These associations link expulsion with blame and reproach without making madḥūr identical in meaning to those descriptions.

Each retains its independent contribution.

The command’s addressee and the warning’s scope

Al-Isrāʾ 17:39 directly addresses Muhammad.

Wa-lā tajʿal is a singular prohibition continuing the address identified by ilayka at the verse’s beginning.

That direct address should not be emptied of meaning through a prior assumption that a warning to Muhammad cannot be real.

Nevertheless, avoiding another deity alongside The God appears repeatedly in the Qur’an and is not confined to this individual.

For root research, madḥūr describes the warned-of outcome rather than meaning association of deities itself.

The verse specifies the prohibited conduct through وَلَا تَجْعَلْ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ, then describes a reproachful outcome and expulsion.

Does daḥr mean annihilation?

The prohibition is followed by fa-tulqā fī jahannam: being cast into Hell.

Madḥūran itself does not mean annihilation or ceasing to exist.

Driving away, repelling, and excluding do not necessarily mean destroying existence in Arabic.

A being may be expelled from one position without being destroyed, even entering another situation afterward.

Daḥr alone should therefore not prove absolute annihilation, the end of existence, or a particular mode of punishment in eschatological discussions.

Those questions require independent verses concerning death, life, punishment, and final outcome.

5. Duḥūran: repulsion and preventing access

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9

دُحُورًا وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ وَاصِبٌ

duḥūran wa-lahum ʿadhābun wāṣibun

“To drive away; and for them is a continuing punishment.”

Syntactically, this verse depends on the preceding verse.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:7 describes protection against every rebellious devil.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8 then says:

لَا يَسَّمَّعُونَ إِلَى الْمَلَإِ الْأَعْلَىٰ وَيُقْذَفُونَ مِنْ كُلِّ جَانِبٍ

lā yassammaʿūna ilā al-malaʾi al-aʿlā wa-yuqdhafūna min kulli jānibin

“They cannot listen to the highest assembly, and they are pelted from every side.”

Duḥūran follows this.

The word should therefore be understood in connection with keeping devils from access to the highest assembly.

The role of duḥūran in the sentence

Duḥūran is an accusative verbal noun.

Several syntactic explanations have been proposed.

One takes it as the purpose of the pelting and repulsion: to drive away and exclude.

Another relates it to an implied verb of the same root: they are driven away, a driving away. A verbal noun functioning circumstantially as “expelled” is also transmitted in exegetical explanations.

In Maʿānī al-Qurʾān, Al-Farrāʾ examines duḥūran with initial ḍammah and daḥūran with initial fatḥah. He explains the familiar former as a verbal noun and proposes another analysis for the latter. In his reported analysis, the fatḥah form can name that which repels; this alternative-reading analysis should remain distinct from duḥūran as a verbal noun in Ḥafṣ.

Al-Zajjāj likewise explains the resulting meaning as being driven away and excluded.

These syntactic and reading differences matter without changing the root’s core of expulsion and keeping away.

Al-Qurṭubī and the Al-Farrāʾ report: verbal noun and reading variation

“To drive away” naturally conveys the connection to the previous sentence in Persian.

Al-Ṭabarī: expelled and the meanings of wāṣib

Distinguishing qadhf and daḥr

Two separate linguistic families occur here.

Qadhf means throwing or casting.

Daḥr means driving away or removing.

Throwing can therefore serve expulsion without making the words synonymous.

Something can be thrown toward someone without intending to drive them away; someone can also be expelled through other means without anything being thrown.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 combine these meanings in one image.

Yuqdhafūn—يُقْذَفُونَ—here describes devils being pelted, while duḥūran foregrounds repulsion or its purpose according to the syntactic analysis. The passive construction should not make the devils themselves the thrown projectiles.

Duḥūr should therefore not be translated as stone, meteor, arrow, or a particular projectile.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 mentions a piercing flame, but that is a separate expression rather than D Ḥ R’s own meaning.

Is daḥr merely spatial distance?

In these verses, expulsion concerns preventing access.

Devils are prevented from listening to the highest assembly; pelting and exclusion are described in connection with that situation.

Daḥr here therefore concerns more than spatial distance: prevention of access follows from the connected verses.

That contextual conclusion should not become the root’s invariable lexical meaning.

Nor does duḥūr alone determine the unseen world’s physics, devils’ routes, their substance, or the precise relationship with celestial phenomena.

The Qur’an presents protection, preventing listening, and expulsion. Scientific or ontological explanation of its manner is a separate question requiring further evidence.

Duḥūran and a continuing punishment

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 follows duḥūran with وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ وَاصِبٌ.

These statements are related but not identical in meaning.

Duḥūr conveys driving away and removing.

ʿAdhāb names suffering and punishment; wāṣib specifies a quality of it.

The punishment’s duration or quality should therefore not be transferred into duḥūr itself.

Their juxtaposition does not make daḥr necessarily an everlasting punishment in every Arabic use.

6. Distinguishing related roots

D Ḥ R and Ṭ R D: expelling and driving away

Ṭ R D is the closest semantic family to D Ḥ R.

Dictionaries often explain daḥr through ṭard.

Qur’anic ṭard also concerns expelling believers from an assembly or a prophet’s presence, for example in Hūd 11:29 and Al-Anʿām 6:52.

Ṭard can mean excluding someone from a group or position.

Daḥr likewise conveys expulsion, with removal and exclusion prominent in its Qur’anic uses.

Some early uses involve greater force, but this does not make every ṭard gentle and every daḥr violent.

Expulsion itself carries no necessarily positive or negative ethical judgment.

Hūd 11:29 denies that the prophet will expel believers. Iblīs’ expulsion, however, occurs in a context of disobedience and arrogance.

Rightfulness follows from cause and circumstance rather than the verb alone.

D Ḥ R and D KH R: expulsion and abasement

The roots resemble each other in writing but differ in their second letter: ḥāʾ in D Ḥ R and khāʾ in D KH R. Their letters have not exchanged positions.

D Ḥ R concerns driving away and removing.

Qur’anic D KH R relates to abasement, lowliness, and submission.

Al-Naḥl 16:48 has دَاخِرُونَ; Ghāfir 40:60 has دَاخِرِينَ.

These belong to D KH R, not D Ḥ R.

Expulsion can accompany abasement without making the concepts identical.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 itself distinguishes censure from expulsion through separate descriptions.

Rendering madḥūr merely “abased” would lose the sense of expulsion.

D Ḥ R and KH S ʾ: driving away and humbling

Arabic KH S ʾ also concerns expelling and distancing with abasement.

Al-Baqarah 2:65 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:166 contain قِرَدَةً خَاسِئِينَ.

In some uses, this family approaches exclusion accompanied by lowliness and humiliation.

Its established uses can emphasize the state of abasement and being kept back, whereas daḥr’s clear core is driving away and removing.

The roots overlap without being interchangeable without examination.

Study of KH S ʾ

D Ḥ R and R J M: driving away and stoning

Rajm concerns throwing stones and, in some uses, expelling or excluding.

Al-Ḥijr 15:34 and Ṣād 38:77 describe Iblīs as rajīm.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 describes him as madḥūran.

These descriptions connect within the story but differ in linguistic form and semantic field.

Daḥr concerns expulsion; rajm has a principal connection with throwing stones and, in certain constructions, being driven away.

Al-Ṣāffāt likewise distinguishes pelting from the purpose of repulsion.

Daḥr should not always be rendered “stoning,” nor should rajīm make madḥūr primarily mean someone hit by stones.

D Ḥ R and D Ḥ Ḍ: expulsion and loss of validity

D Ḥ Ḍ resembles D Ḥ R in writing but ends in ḍād.

Daḥḍ concerns slipping, instability, and, in argumentation, a proof becoming invalid or losing standing.

Al-Shūrā 42:16 has حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ.

It denotes a proof lacking validity and firmness.

Dāḥiḍah belongs to D Ḥ Ḍ and should not be confused with madḥūr or duḥūr of D Ḥ R.

Someone might be excluded after their argument fails, but that possible connection does not establish a shared root.

D Ḥ R and B ʿ D: being distant and being driven away

B ʿ D has a broad Arabic field covering spatial, temporal, and abstract distance.

Daḥr, however, centrally describes an action removing someone or something.

Being far away and being driven away therefore differ.

Something can be distant without having been expelled; someone may be excluded from a position without the resulting distance being bodily or measurable.

This matters for eschatological translation. Madḥūr is better rendered “expelled” rather than merely “distant,” since the passive participle also conveys the act being performed upon someone.

7. Suggested translations for Forqan

Equivalents for all four Qur’anic occurrences should preserve driving away and removal.

Madḥūran

A suitable equivalent for the passive participle is “expelled.”

“Banished” is also defensible; the recommended Persian equivalent is natural and combines well with the accompanying descriptions.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:18:

“Blameworthy and expelled.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:18:

“Blameworthy and expelled.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:39:

“Reproached and expelled.”

These preserve expulsion while conveying the accompanying description separately.

“Abased” alone is insufficient, potentially deleting expulsion from the translation.

“Cursed,” “deprived of mercy,” and “destroyed” should not be fixed equivalents. They may relate to someone’s fate in particular contexts without being madḥūr’s direct meaning.

Duḥūran

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, the recommended Persian equivalent means “to drive away.”

It naturally expresses the word’s connection with the preceding verb.

“Harsh expulsion” or “driving away forcefully” may be discussed in some syntactic interpretations, but a particular intensity should not follow from the verbal noun alone.

Nor is throwing alone its equivalent: that comes from the separate verb yuqdhafūn.

Forqan’s current Persian “to drive away” is therefore suitable, without needing a more complicated replacement.

Equivalents within the Arabic family

Natural equivalents for Qur’anic and non-Qur’anic family members include:

Daḥara: drove away, removed, repelled.

Dāḥir: one driving away, removing, or repelling.

Madḥūr: expelled or banished.

Daḥr and duḥūr: expulsion, removal, or repulsion.

Indiḥār in later usage: being repelled or defeated.

Choice depends on the sentence’s subject. In military or political contexts, defeating an enemy can result from daḥr, but the root should not invariably mean destroying the enemy.

8. Conclusion

Arabic D Ḥ R denotes driving away and removing. Unlike simply being distant, it involves an action excluding someone or something from a position.

Early dictionaries agree on this core. Some uses accompany force, humiliation, and abasement, which should not be generalized without regard to context.

Madḥūran occurs three times. Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 commands Iblīs to leave and describes him as expelled; Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:39 use it for a blameworthy, reproachful outcome.

All three pair madḥūr with a separate description of blame or reproach. Expulsion can thus connect with ethical judgment and punishment without the root itself meaning blame, sin, curse, or annihilation.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, duḥūran connects with preventing access to the highest assembly and repelling devils. Pelting and repelling are related but distinct acts and linguistic families.

Daḥr should therefore not be reduced to stoning, throwing, exclusion from mercy, absolute annihilation, or everlasting punishment. Such concepts require independent words and verses wherever they apply.

Proposed final definition: D Ḥ R denotes driving away, repelling, and removing someone or something from a place, domain, or position. This may be bodily or involve exclusion from position and access. Qur’anic daḥr accompanies blame or prevention of access, but context determines its cause, manner, and outcome rather than the root alone. Brief root meaning: Driving away, repelling, and removing from a place, domain, or access.
