Opening, spreading, and dispersing: from unfolded writings and movement through the land to revival after death and resurrection

1. Introducing the root and its semantic centers

The Arabic root ن ش ر concerns opening, spreading, and dispersing, especially when something emerges from a gathered, closed, or restricted condition into a broader space or extent.

One clear use is unfolding cloth or writing previously folded. نَشَرَ الثَّوْبَ means opening and spreading a cloth; نَشَرَ الْكِتَابَ can mean opening and unfolding a writing.

Another use concerns people, animals, or objects spreading through an area. اِنْتَشَرَ الْقَوْمُ describes a group leaving a gathered condition and dispersing in different directions.

Another branch uses نَشَرَ and أَنْشَرَ for returning or restoring to life after death. Qur’anic نُشُور commonly denotes rising and living again after death.

Arabic also uses the family for plants emerging again after rain, fragrance spreading, news circulating, and cutting wood with a saw.

These uses cannot be confined to one physical action without respecting their differences.

Opening writing, people dispersing, mercy extending, and the dead reviving are different events, though semantically connected.

Opening writing emphasizes unfolding something closed; people dispersing involves movement from concentration into a wider area; spreading news brings it to more people; revival concerns returning to life after death.

Opening and spreading form an important semantic center. Revival is also an established early Arabic and Qur’anic use, which must not be reduced to physically opening something.

Nor does every occurrence mean distributing something among people. That familiar modern Persian and Arabic sense is only one branch of the family.

2. Early Arabic: unfolding, dispersal, and returning to life

Opening something closed or folded

One lexical explanation introduces the root as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى فَتْحِ شَيْءٍ وَتَشَعُّبِهِ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā fatḥi shayʾin wa-tashaʿʿubihi

Meaning:

“An established root indicating opening something and the branching or spreading of its parts.”

Opening writing and a bird’s feathers spreading illustrate this sense.

نَشَرْتُ الثَّوْبَ means “I spread out the cloth.”

نَشَرْتُ الْكِتَابَ can describe opening writing previously rolled or closed.

This contrasts with طَيّ, folding or gathering up.

The Qur’an uses manshūr and nushirat for writings, making this branch directly relevant to several verses.

Opening a writing must nevertheless be distinguished from circulating its contents through society.

Someone can open a scroll without distributing copies. News can also circulate without any physical writing being unfolded.

Modern “publishing a book” generally concerns printing, offering, or making it available, whereas opening a book or scroll was an independent established early sense.

People and animals dispersing

اِنْتَشَرَ الْقَوْمُ means that people dispersed.

اِنْتَشَرَتِ الْغَنَمُ likewise describes sheep spreading through pasture.

Living beings move from concentration into a wider area.

Grazing, work, or travel may provide a purpose, but the root does not identify it.

Dispersal need not therefore signify disorder or confusion.

People can disperse after a gathering to attend to their affairs, or animals spread out to graze.

Al-Rūm 30:20, Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, and Al-Jumuʿah 62:10 clearly illustrate this sense.

News circulating and reputation spreading

نَشَرَ الْخَبَرَ means making news circulate.

اِنْتَشَرَ الْخَبَرُ concerns news spreading among people.

This extends from physical spreading to the dissemination of information.

Circulation does not itself establish a report’s truth.

True, false, or incomplete news can spread.

The root concerns reaching more recipients, rather than credibility, usefulness, or moral value.

Nor does spreading news necessarily involve writing or printing: it can circulate orally.

Nashr and fragrance

Early Arabic also uses نَشْر for fragrance diffusing through the air.

The pre-Islamic poet al-Muraqqish says:

النَّشْرُ مِسْكٌ وَالْوُجُوهُ دَنَانِيرُ
وَأَطْرَافُ الْأَكُفِّ عَنَمْ

al-nashru miskun wa-al-wujūhu danānīru / wa-aṭrāfu al-akuffi ʿanam

A translation conveying the sense:

“Their scent is like musk, their faces like gleaming dinars, and their fingertips like a red plant.”

Nashr here concerns scent.

The image fits fragrance spreading, without making every instance of nashr fragrant.

Lexical traditions also record the word more generally for odor.

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays says:

كَأَنَّ الْمُدَامَ وَصَوْبَ الْغَمَامِ
وَرِيحَ الْخُزَامَى وَنَشْرَ الْقُطُرْ

ka-anna al-mudāma wa-ṣawba al-ghamāmi / wa-rīḥa al-khuzāmā wa-nashra al-quṭur

“As though wine, the showering cloud, lavender’s scent, and fragrant aloeswood…”

Wine, rain, and aromatic plants form a shared poetic image, with nashr denoting diffusing scent.

Early Arabic nashr was thus not confined to visible objects.

These verses alone do not establish the precise historical relation between scent and unfolding or dispersal. Diffusing fragrance provides a semantic connection, without becoming the certain origin of every use.

Returning to life after death

Early Arabic attests نَشَرَ الْمَيِّتُ for a dead person returning to life.

Likewise:

أَنْشَرَ اللَّهُ المَيِّتَ

anshara allāhu al-mayyita

Meaning:

“The God restored the dead person to life.”

نُشُور concerns revival and rising again after death.

The pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says:

حَتَّى يَقُولَ النَّاسُ مِمَّا رَأَوْا
يَا عَجَبًا لِلْمَيِّتِ النَّاشِرِ

ḥattā yaqūla al-nāsu mimmā raʾaw / yā ʿajaban li-al-mayyiti al-nāshiri

Translation:

“Until people say, at what they have seen: How astonishing, this dead person who has returned to life!”

The line uses a poetic image of revival and helps explain nāshir.

Nāshir here denotes someone alive again after death, rather than a publisher of books or news.

The example witnesses an established revival sense in pre-Islamic Arabic.

It does not make death and life the original subject of every use.

Spreading clothes or news, people dispersing, and the dead rising are each recorded in their own lexical setting.

The earth’s renewed vegetation

نَشَرَتِ الْأَرْضُ describes the earth producing vegetation again after rain and the growing season’s arrival.

Some lexical explanations use nashr for plants becoming green again after drying and receiving fresh rain.

This approaches revival semantically: the land or plant manifests signs of life after dryness.

A plant drying and a human dying should not, however, be treated as biologically identical.

The Qur’an also invokes dead land and its revival to recall the possibility of human resurrection.

The comparison forms part of Qur’anic reasoning without erasing every difference between the events.

Sawing wood and other uses

نَشَرَ الْخَشَبَةَ بِالْمِنْشَارِ means sawing a piece of wood.

مِنْشَار names the saw, while نُشَارَة denotes the particles or shavings separated during sawing.

This can be connected with opening a split in wood or separating its parts.

It does not establish sawing as the source of every meaning in the family.

Lexical traditions also record نُشْرَة for a type of treatment or removal of a supposed spell, نَشْر for certain plant conditions, and نَوَاشِر for visible veins on the inner forearm.

These forms show the family’s breadth without supplying one fixed meaning for every Qur’anic occurrence.

3. Unfolded writings: from opening a scroll to revealing the record of deeds

A. Al-Isrāʾ 17:13: an opened record

وَكُلَّ إِنْسَانٍ أَلْزَمْنَاهُ طَائِرَهُ فِي عُنُقِهِ وَنُخْرِجُ لَهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ كِتَابًا يَلْقَاهُ مَنْشُورًا

wa-kulla insānin alzamnāhu ṭāʾirahu fī ʿunuqihi wa-nukhriju lahu yawma al-qiyāmati kitāban yalqāhu manshūran

Translation:

“We have bound each person’s assigned lot to his neck, and on Resurrection Day We bring out for him a record he finds opened out.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13

مَنْشُورًا is the passive participle of the Form I verb نَشَرَ.

It describes the record encountered on Resurrection Day.

Its direct sense here is opened or unfolded.

The next verse says:

اقْرَأْ كِتَابَكَ كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكَ حَسِيبًا

iqraʾ kitābaka kafā bi-nafsika al-yawma ʿalayka ḥasīban

“Read your record. Today you yourself suffice as your own reckoner.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:14

The sequence connects the record’s openness with reading it and confronting its contents.

Manshūr does not establish printing or the distribution of copies among people.

Nashr itself does not mean reckoning or recording deeds.

Those ideas come from the record, Resurrection Day, and the surrounding passage.

B. Al-Ṭūr 52:2 and Al-Ṭūr 52:3: writing on an unfolded sheet

وَكِتَابٍ مَسْطُورٍ

wa-kitābin masṭūrin

“And by a written book.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:2

فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ

fī raqqin manshūrin

“On an unfolded, spread-out sheet.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:3

مَنْشُورٍ describes رَقٍّ.

Raqq concerns writing material, such as prepared skin or parchment.

Manshūr expresses its opened, spread-out condition.

The word itself does not identify the particular book.

Interpretations include the record of deeds, a revealed book, or writing significant within the sūrah’s oaths.

The complete oath sequence and other verses must be considered to examine its referent.

Lexically, “opened and spread out” is the clear sense of manshūr.

C. Al-Muddaththir 74:52: demanding individual sheets

بَلْ يُرِيدُ كُلُّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ أَنْ يُؤْتَىٰ صُحُفًا مُنَشَّرَةً

bal yurīdu kullu imriʾin minhum an yuʾtā ṣuḥufan munashsharatan

Translation:

“Rather, each of them wants to be given unfolded, spread-out sheets.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:52

مُنَشَّرَةً is a Form II passive participle.

Its morphology differs from manshūr in Al-Isrāʾ and Al-Ṭūr, while remaining within the opening and spreading of writings.

The context concerns people responding improperly to divine reminder and message.

Their demand should not become evidence of faith or truth-seeking without considering who speaks and the setting.

Munashsharah cannot simply be rendered as “record of deeds” here.

The verse describes requested writings, whose precise nature and contents require contextual study.

D. Al-Takwīr 81:10: when the records are opened

وَإِذَا الصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-ṣuḥufu nushirat

Translation:

“And when the records are opened out.”

Al-Takwīr 81:10

نُشِرَتْ is a passive Form I perfect verb.

الصُّحُف semantically receives the opening action. One grammatical analysis treats the noun following idhā as the passive subject of an omitted verb explained by نُشِرَتْ; the expressed verb has an implicit passive-subject pronoun referring to the records.

Ḥafṣ reads the verb without a doubled shīn.

Some other established readings have نُشِّرَتْ with doubling.

Both fit the records being opened and spread out.

The verse belongs to the description of Resurrection events.

Other passages about the record of deeds support connecting the opening with the deeds’ disclosure.

The verb itself does not mean judgment, reckoning, or punishment.

Those come from the wider Resurrection passages.

All four writing-related occurrences should be distinguished from modern printing and book distribution.

4. Dispersal through the land and everyday life

A. Al-Rūm 30:20: humans spreading abroad

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ أَنْ خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ إِذَا أَنْتُمْ بَشَرٌ تَنْتَشِرُونَ

wa-min āyātihi an khalaqakum min turābin thumma idhā antum basharun tantashirūna

Translation:

“Among His signs is that He created you from dust; then suddenly you are human beings spreading abroad.”

Al-Rūm 30:20

تَنْتَشِرُونَ is a Form VIII imperfect verb.

It concerns humans spreading across the earth.

The verse moves from human creation to humanity’s widening presence.

The verb alone does not mean population growth.

A growing population may accompany dispersal, but the word highlights spreading and movement over a wider area.

Nor does it mean re-creation after death here.

The otherworldly sense of nushūr must not be imposed on every form in the family.

B. Al-Aḥzāb 33:53: dispersing after a meal

وَلَٰكِنْ إِذَا دُعِيتُمْ فَادْخُلُوا فَإِذَا طَعِمْتُمْ فَانْتَشِرُوا

wa-lākin idhā duʿītum fa-udkhulū fa-idhā ṭaʿimtum fa-intashirū

Translation:

“But enter when invited; then, when you have eaten, disperse.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, part of the verse

فَانْتَشِرُوا is a Form VIII imperative.

It concerns leaving the gathering after eating.

The following passage discusses lingering in conversation and troubling the host.

Dispersal here means departing from the gathering place.

The verb does not require guests to disperse in every direction at equal distances; such details are not part of its meaning.

Nor does nashr here concern revival after death or the publication of writings.

C. Al-Jumuʿah 62:10: dispersal after prayer

فَإِذَا قُضِيَتِ الصَّلَاةُ فَانْتَشِرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَابْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِ اللَّهِ وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

fa-idhā quḍiyati al-ṣalātu fa-intashirū fī al-arḍi wa-ibtaghū min faḍli allāhi wa-udhkurū allāha kathīran laʿallakum tufliḥūna

Translation:

“When the prayer is concluded, disperse through the land, seek The God’s bounty, and remember Him often, so that you may prosper.”

Al-Jumuʿah 62:10

فَانْتَشِرُوا follows the prayer’s conclusion.

The preceding verse instructs leaving trade at the call to prayer.

Dispersal and seeking divine bounty follow its completion.

People can thus return to the activities and affairs of life after gathering.

Dispersal itself does not mean trading or earning an income.

وَابْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِ اللَّهِ separately expresses seeking bounty.

وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا is another independent instruction.

These ideas should not all be merged into intashirū.

Dispersal need not be blameworthy: it is presented here as permissible and appropriate after the gathering.

D. Al-Qamar 54:7: scattered locusts

يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ كَأَنَّهُمْ جَرَادٌ مُنْتَشِرٌ

yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi ka-annahum jarādun muntashirun

Translation:

“They come out of the graves as though they were scattered locusts.”

Al-Qamar 54:7

مُنْتَشِرٌ is a Form VIII active participle.

It describes the locusts.

The simile conveys widespread dispersal and collective movement in the Resurrection scene.

Muntashir itself does not mean fear, punishment, or psychological disorder.

Any such contextual features must be explained separately from the literal dispersal sense.

Nor does comparison with locusts imply biological transformation into them.

The likeness concerns movement and dispersal.

5. Spreading mercy, the land, and winds

A. Al-Kahf 18:16: extending mercy

The youths seeking refuge in the cave say:

فَأْوُوا إِلَى الْكَهْفِ يَنْشُرْ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُمْ مِنْ رَحْمَتِهِ وَيُهَيِّئْ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَمْرِكُمْ مِرْفَقًا

faʾwū ilā al-kahfi yanshur lakum rabbukum min raḥmatihi wa-yuhayyiʾ lakum min amrikum mirfaqan

Translation:

“Seek refuge in the cave; your Lord will spread some of His mercy for you and prepare ease for you in your affair.”

Al-Kahf 18:16, part of the verse

يَنْشُرْ is a jussive Form I imperfect verb.

Divine mercy is what is extended.

Mercy is not a physical cloth literally laid out.

Nashr conveys the broadening reach or availability of mercy here.

يُهَيِّئْ then concerns preparing suitable conditions.

Nashr itself does not mean preparing a place or resolving every problem; those ideas involve the second verb and context.

B. Al-Shūrā 42:28: extending mercy after despair

وَهُوَ الَّذِي يُنَزِّلُ الْغَيْثَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا قَنَطُوا وَيَنْشُرُ رَحْمَتَهُ وَهُوَ الْوَلِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ

wa-huwa alladhī yunazzilu al-ghaytha min baʿdi mā qanaṭū wa-yanshuru raḥmatahu wa-huwa al-waliyyu al-ḥamīdu

Translation:

“He sends down rain after they have despaired and spreads His mercy. He is the protecting guardian, the praiseworthy.”

يَنْشُرُ again concerns mercy.

Unlike Al-Kahf 18:16, the setting is rainfall after despair.

Its extension can be connected with rain’s effects, relief from dryness, renewed hope, and the possibility of life.

Nashr itself does not mean raining.

يُنَزِّلُ الْغَيْثَ expresses the rainfall; nashr concerns mercy extending.

C. Al-Mursalāt 77:3: وَالنَّاشِرَاتِ نَشْرًا

وَالنَّاشِرَاتِ نَشْرًا

wa-al-nāshirāti nashran

A close rendering:

“And by those that spread, spreading.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:3

النَّاشِرَات is a feminine plural active participle; نَشْرًا is an accusative verbal noun.

The expression does not explicitly identify who or what these spreaders are.

Several established interpretations exist.

One understands winds spreading clouds across the sky.

Another understands angels opening and distributing writings or commands.

Some accounts also mention rain, the earth’s vegetation spreading, or mercy extending.

These possibilities arise because Arabic uses nashr for wind, writing, and renewed vegetation.

Al-Mursalāt 77:1, Al-Mursalāt 77:2, Al-Mursalāt 77:3, Al-Mursalāt 77:4, and Al-Mursalāt 77:5 form a sequence of descriptions:

وَالْمُرْسَلَاتِ عُرْفًا

wa-al-mursalāti ʿurfan

“By those sent forth in succession.”

فَالْعَاصِفَاتِ عَصْفًا

fa-al-ʿāṣifāti ʿaṣfan

“Then those that blow violently, a violent blowing.”

وَالنَّاشِرَاتِ نَشْرًا

wa-al-nāshirāti nashran

“And those that spread, a spreading.”

فَالْفَارِقَاتِ فَرْقًا

fa-al-fāriqāti farqan

“Then those that separate, a separating.”

فَالْمُلْقِيَاتِ ذِكْرًا

fa-al-mulqiyāti dhikran

“Then those that deliver a reminder.”

Some descriptions readily fit winds; others can be connected with conveying and separating a message or command.

Nāshirāt itself nevertheless names no particular referent.

One exegetical reading, finding no decisive specification, extends the expression to whatever spreads rather than restricting it to one referent.

The lexical sense “spreaders” should therefore remain distinct from identifying winds or angels.

“Spreading winds” selects an interpretive reading. It is defensible, but should not be presented as the sole literal meaning.

6. Nushūr: returning to life and rising after death

A. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:21: do the gods restore life?

أَمِ اتَّخَذُوا آلِهَةً مِنَ الْأَرْضِ هُمْ يُنْشِرُونَ

ami ittakhadhū ālihatan mina al-arḍi hum yunshirūna

Translation:

“Have they adopted gods from the earth who restore life?”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:21

يُنْشِرُونَ is Form IV.

Its subject matter is restoring life.

The question challenges the ability of gods people have adopted.

Nashr here does not mean opening a book or dispersing people through the land.

It illustrates the established use of أَنْشَرَ for restoring the dead to life.

B. Al-Furqān 25:3: no control over death, life, or resurrection

وَلَا يَمْلِكُونَ مَوْتًا وَلَا حَيَاةً وَلَا نُشُورًا

wa-lā yamlikūna mawtan wa-lā ḥayātan wa-lā nushūran

Translation:

“They control neither death, nor life, nor rising again.”

Al-Furqān 25:3, part of the verse

Nushūr appears alongside death and life.

The association identifies returning to life after death.

It should not be restricted to people dispersing through the land.

This does not make every other Qur’anic use of nushūr describe precisely the same setting.

C. Al-Furqān 25:40: rejecting resurrection

بَلْ كَانُوا لَا يَرْجُونَ نُشُورًا

bal kānū lā yarjūna nushūran

Translation:

“Rather, they did not hope for or expect a rising again.”

Al-Furqān 25:40, the verse’s closing part

The context concerns people passing the remains of an earlier people’s destruction without taking heed.

Nushūr here concerns resurrection and return after death.

يَرْجُونَ belongs to ر ج و and requires separate examination. Expectation and, in some uses, fear have been discussed in explaining it.

Hope or fear should not become part of nushūr itself.

D. Fāṭir 35:9: the earth’s revival and resurrection

فَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا كَذَٰلِكَ النُّشُورُ

fa-aḥyaynā bihi al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā ka-dhālika al-nushūru

Translation:

“Through it We revived the earth after its death. Such is resurrection.”

Fāṭir 35:9, the verse’s closing part

Revival of the earth through rain recalls resurrection.

The comparison does not equate every biological stage of plant growth with human re-creation.

Nushūr at the verse’s end concerns resurrection.

The verse nevertheless establishes an argumentative connection between the earth’s revival and human life after death.

E. Al-Zukhruf 43:11: reviving dead land

وَالَّذِي نَزَّلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً بِقَدَرٍ فَأَنْشَرْنَا بِهِ بَلْدَةً مَيْتًا كَذَٰلِكَ تُخْرَجُونَ

wa-alladhī nazzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan bi-qadarin fa-ansharnā bihi baldatan maytan ka-dhālika tukhrajūna

Translation:

“He sent down water from the sky in due measure; through it We revived a dead land. Thus you are brought forth.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:11

فَأَنْشَرْنَا describes reviving dead land.

Form IV of ن ش ر is consequently not confined to human re-creation.

Rain restores the land’s growth and life, and the passage connects this with humanity’s return.

Ansharnā alone does not explain the details of vegetation or human resurrection.

F. Al-Dukhān 44:35: denying revival

إِنْ هِيَ إِلَّا مَوْتَتُنَا الْأُولَىٰ وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُنْشَرِينَ

in hiya illā mawtatunā al-ūlā wa-mā naḥnu bi-munsharīna

Translation:

“There is nothing but our first death, and we shall not be raised to life again.”

Al-Dukhān 44:35

مُنْشَرِينَ is a Form IV passive participle.

The verse quotes those denying re-creation.

The sentence expresses their position, rather than the Qur’an endorsing that denial.

Munsharīn here means those raised to life again.

G. ʿAbasa 80:22: restoring life after death

ثُمَّ إِذَا شَاءَ أَنْشَرَهُ

thumma idhā shāʾa ansharahu

Translation:

“Then, whenever He wills, He restores him to life.”

ʿAbasa 80:22

The preceding verses mention human creation, death, and burial.

Ansharahu then concerns revival after death.

Context makes the sense clear.

It should not be confused with أَنْشَأَ from ن ش أ: similar letters belong to independent roots.

H. Al-Mulk 67:15: the final return

فَامْشُوا فِي مَنَاكِبِهَا وَكُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِهِ وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ

fa-imshū fī manākibihā wa-kulū min rizqihi wa-ilayhi al-nushūru

Translation:

“Walk through its expanses and eat of His provision. To Him is the return after death.”

Al-Mulk 67:15, the verse’s closing part

The verse concerns both movement and provision on earth, and resurrection toward The God.

Nushūr at its end is not merely dispersal through the land.

إِلَيْهِ and the final-return setting indicate resurrection.

Walking is separately expressed by فَامْشُوا.

Earthly movement and return after death should not be merged into one lexical meaning.

7. Al-Furqān 25:47: daily rising must not be overlooked

وَهُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ اللَّيْلَ لِبَاسًا وَالنَّوْمَ سُبَاتًا وَجَعَلَ النَّهَارَ نُشُورًا

wa-huwa alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-layla libāsan wa-al-nawma subātan wa-jaʿala al-nahāra nushūran

Translation:

“He made night a covering for you, sleep a rest and cessation of activity, and day a time of rising and spreading abroad.”

Al-Furqān 25:47

Nushūr is particularly significant here because its setting is sleep, waking, and daily activity.

Elsewhere it commonly refers to resurrection after death.

Here, however, day as nushūr contrasts with night and sleep.

Two related readings

One reading understands waking from sleep.

Sleep involves stillness and reduced activity; waking restores movement and ordinary awareness.

Another reading understands people dispersing during the day for work, travel, and daily affairs.

This connects with the expression “disperse through the land.”

Following the mention of sleep, waking has strong contextual support; dispersal for livelihood is also recorded in exegetical tradition.

The readings need not conflict.

People rise after sleep and attend to their daytime activities.

The verse itself describes the familiar order of night, sleep, and day, rather than the dead returning at the world’s end.

Nushūr cannot therefore be translated without exception as the final resurrection in every Qur’anic occurrence.

This verse is important evidence for preserving the family’s semantic breadth.

8. Variant readings and related roots

ن ش ر and ب ش ر in wind passages

Ḥafṣ reads forms from ب ش ر concerning winds in Al-Aʿrāf 7:57, Al-Furqān 25:48, and Al-Naml 27:63.

For example:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي يُرْسِلُ الرِّيَاحَ بُشْرًا بَيْنَ يَدَيْ رَحْمَتِهِ

wa-huwa alladhī yursilu al-riyāḥa bushran bayna yaday raḥmatihi

“He sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57, part of the verse

بُشْرًا concerns winds bringing good tidings before mercy and rain.

Other established readings include نُشُرًا, نُشْرًا, and نَشْرًا.

These belong to ن ش ر and concern winds spreading or dispersing clouds.

The readings must therefore be distinguished:

بُشْرًا: bringing good tidings.

نُشُرًا or نَشْرًا: spreading or dispersing.

The variant readings do not make the roots identical.

Wind can herald rain and spread clouds, but heralding and spreading remain different concepts.

These readings matter to the study, although excluded from the 21 Ḥafṣ occurrences.

Study of B SH R

Al-Baqarah 2:259: نُنْشِزُهَا or نُنْشِرُهَا?

Ḥafṣ reads:

وَانْظُرْ إِلَى الْعِظَامِ كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا

wa-unẓur ilā al-ʿiẓāmi kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥman

A close rendering:

“Look at the bones: how We raise them, then clothe them with flesh.”

Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Abū Jaʿfar, and Yaʿqūb instead read:

كَيْفَ نُنْشِرُهَا

kayfa nunshiruhā

Meaning:

“How We restore them to life.”

The distinction is significant.

نُنْشِزُهَا belongs to ن ش ز and concerns raising or putting upright.

نُنْشِرُهَا belongs to ن ش ر and here concerns restoring life.

Both relate to re-creation, while belonging to different roots.

Any occurrence count must therefore specify the reading examined.

The distinction between rāʾ and zāy is not merely an insignificant pronunciation difference: the roots and direct meanings differ.

ن ش ر and ن ش أ: opening and bringing into being

The roots are close in writing and sound.

ن ش أ more particularly concerns emergence, beginning, growth, and origination.

ن ش ر is established for opening, spreading, dispersal, and revival after death.

Both can concern creation and life in some verses.

أَنْشَأَهَا in Yā Sīn 36:79 concerns first origination, whereas أَنْشَرَهُ in ʿAbasa 80:22 concerns revival after death.

The verbs must not be confused.

Study of N SH A

ن ش ر and ن ث ر: spreading and scattering components

ن ث ر concerns strewing or scattering components.

Al-Infiṭār 82:2 says:

وَإِذَا الْكَوَاكِبُ انْتَثَرَتْ

wa-idhā al-kawākibu intatharat

“And when the stars are scattered.”

ن ش ر includes dispersal but has a broader field encompassing opened writings, revival, and spreading through the land.

Nathr may involve scattering, but not every nashr is nathr.

Study of N TH R

ن ش ر and ن س ف: spreading and uprooting

ن س ف concerns detaching or uprooting from a place and may result in dispersal.

Nashr can also mean dispersing, without necessarily first tearing something from its position by force.

Opening a book or extending mercy ordinarily requires no uprooting.

The roots approach one another in some results without being complete synonyms.

Study of N S F

ن ش ر and ب ع ث: revival and raising

ب ع ث concerns raising, sending, and restoring life after death in the Qur’an.

Nushūr likewise often concerns rising after death.

Baʿth has a wider field including sending messengers or rousing people to an activity.

Nashr has independent uses for writings, mercy, winds, and dispersal.

In resurrection passages they may refer to one event, without being complete lexical synonyms.

Study of B ʿ TH

9. Translation difficulties and proposals for Forqan

The chief challenge is avoiding one imposed meaning for every occurrence.

Always translating nashr as scattering would inadequately express revival passages.

Always translating it as reviving would misrepresent opened books and people dispersing.

Restricting it to modern publication would erase much of its early Arabic and Qur’anic field.

Morphology and context must therefore be distinguished.

The meaning in each verse must nevertheless be specified separately.

Manshūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:13 means opened out.

Intishār in Al-Jumuʿah 62:10 concerns dispersing through the land.

Nashr in Al-Shūrā 42:28 concerns mercy extending.

Anshara in ʿAbasa 80:22 concerns revival after death.

Nushūr in Al-Furqān 25:47 concerns waking and daytime activity.

Al-Mursalāt 77:3 names spreaders without specifying their precise referent in that phrase.

Winds or angels should not replace nāshirāt in the basic translation without independent explanation and argument.

Nor should resurrection passages be confined to the physical unfolding of bodily parts. Such imagery may explain semantic connections without determining the physical manner of re-creation.

10. Conclusion

ن ش ر encompasses opening, laying out, dispersing, extending, circulating news, and returning to life after death.

Opening and spreading are important in its lexical explanation, with clear connections to cloth and writing, dispersed beings, and diffused fragrance.

Revival is independently established in early Arabic, with a poetic example in al-Aʿshā. It is not merely an unsupported hypothetical image.

The root occurs 21 times in 20 Qur’anic verses.

Some concern books and records opening.

Others concern people spreading through the land, leaving gatherings, and mercy extending.

Al-Mursalāt swears by spreaders without naming their precise referent in that phrase.

Other passages concern revival after death, human re-creation, and the land revived by rain.

Al-Furqān 25:47 nevertheless uses nushūr for waking and daytime activity.

This prevents restricting every occurrence to the final resurrection.

Variant readings also place ن ش ر beside ب ش ر in some wind passages; Al-Baqarah 2:259 contrasts نُنْشِرُهَا from ن ش ر with نُنْشِزُهَا from ن ش ز.

These distinctions matter for precise lexical study.

Nashr can express opening something, extension through an environment, circulation among people, or rising into life again. The precise sense depends on construction, object, and context.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

An important branch of ن ش ر denotes opening, spreading, and dispersing something, as it leaves a closed, gathered, or restricted state and extends over a wider area or scope.

Another established Arabic and Qur’anic branch concerns returning or restoring to life after death.

Nushūr often denotes rising after death, but Al-Furqān 25:47 uses it for waking and expanding daytime activity.

Qur’anic intashara concerns dispersing and spreading through the land; manshūr and munashshar describe opened and unfolded writings.

Opening and spreading are an important general center, while revival after death is an established semantic branch. Neither should replace every other use without attention to context.
