The Qur’anic Root DH W D (dhāda)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَذُودَانِ
(tadhūdāni)
They hold back; sole Qur’anic form; third person feminine dual imperfect

ذَادَ يَذُودُ
(dhāda yadhūdu)
Drove away or kept back; lexical Form I verb

ذَوْد
(dhawd)
Driving away and restraining; non-Qur’anic verbal noun

ذِيَاد
(dhiyād)
Repelling and keeping away; non-Qur’anic verbal noun

ذَائِد
(dhāʾid)
Defender or one who drives away; non-Qur’anic active participle

مَذُود
(madhūd)
Driven away or restrained; non-Qur’anic passive participle

مِذْوَد
(midhwad)
A means of defense; tongue or horn in lexical uses; non-Qur’anic

ذَوْد
(dhawd)
A small group of camels; non-Qur’anic nominal branch

Keeping away, holding back, driving away, and defending; tending and directing livestock; a small group of camels

The Arabic root DH W D concerns moving someone or something away from a place, preventing entry or approach, and resisting encroachment or unwanted movement. It has uses in livestock husbandry, protecting people and property, defending against intrusion, and tending animals.

In Arabic, «ذَادَ عَنْهُ» can mean defending someone and keeping danger away from them, whereas «ذَادَ إِبِلَهُ» may concern driving or directing one’s camels, or preventing them from moving in a particular direction. Both involve intervention to keep something away or turn it back, but the purpose and direction differ.

Another branch of this family is «ذَوْد», a small group of camels. This use is known in early Arabic, but its historical connection with the verb of driving away and holding back is not certain.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as «تَذُودَانِ» in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23. The verse describes Moses after his arrival in Midian: a group of people are watering their animals, while two women stand apart, holding back or keeping something away.

What makes this use significant is that the verb tadhūdāni has no expressed object. Early lexical and exegetical explanations therefore offer two readings: were the women keeping their animals away from the water and the crowd, or keeping people from approaching their animals?

Understanding the distinction requires separating the root’s meaning, what the sentence explicitly states, and what is inferred from the rest of the story.

1. Core Meaning and Forms of the Root

Moving away and holding back

Two semantic branches can be distinguished within this family:

أَحَدُهُمَا تَنْحِيَةُ الشَّيْءِ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ، وَالْآخَرُ جَمَاعَةُ الْإِبِلِ

aḥaduhumā tanḥiyatu al-shayʾi ʿani al-shayʾi wa-al-ākharu jamāʿatu al-ibil

That is: moving something aside or away from something else, and a group of camels.

These two branches may be related in origin, but that connection is not certain.

Conceptual proximity must therefore be distinguished from proof of a historical connection between the two uses.

In the first branch, the central meaning is clear: someone or something is kept away from another place, route, or object.

Arabic expressions include:

«ذَادَهُ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ»: he drove him away from something or kept him from it.

«ذَادَ عَنْ حَوْضِهِ»: he defended his watering trough and prevented others from encroaching on it.

«ذَادَ إِبِلَهُ»: he drove or directed his camels, or turned them away from a direction.

«ذَادَ عَنْ أَهْلِهِ»: he defended his family and kept harm away from them.

In its verbal branch, «ذَوْد» is explained through three concepts: «سَوْق», «طَرْد», and «دَفْع»—driving or leading onward, driving away, and pushing back or repelling.

These meanings overlap in different uses, but they are not identical.

Someone driving a herd may be taking the animals to pasture or returning them to their enclosure. Someone defending a watering trough aims to prevent another person’s entry or encroachment.

Thus, dhawd involves more than causing movement: in many uses, directing or restricting movement to keep something away from something else is significant.

This should not become an absolute rule. The Arabic verb dhāda can also mean driving or directing, without a defensive purpose or an enemy being involved in every instance.

Important forms in the family

In Arabic, «ذَادَ يَذُودُ» is the family’s basic, Form I verb.

«ذَوْد» and «ذِيَاد» denote driving, repelling, and keeping away.

«ذَائِد» is an active participle, used for someone who defends a person, right, or boundary, or drives something away.

«ذُوَّاد» and «ذَادَة» are plural forms referring to defenders and those who hold others back.

«مَذُود» is a passive participle, describing someone or something driven away or held back.

«مِذْوَد» can name an instrument or bodily organ used for repelling and defending.

«أَذَادَ» is also recorded in Arabic for helping another person drive or tend livestock.

None of these forms occurs in the Qur’an except the imperfect verb «تَذُودَانِ».

The structure of tadhūdāni

«تَذُودَانِ» is an imperfect form of «ذَادَ يَذُودُ».

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 it is third person feminine dual. Its subject is the two women Moses sees at the water of Midian.

The dual alif refers to the women, and the final nūn marks the indicative mood in this class of five imperfect verb forms.

The clause «تَذُودَانِ» describes the women’s situation when Moses sees them.

The important syntactic point is that the verb here has no expressed object. The phrase itself does not say whom or what they were keeping away.

The root identifies the kind of action, while its precise object must be examined through the context.

2. Early Arabic Evidence: Defending a Watering Trough and Tending Livestock

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā: defending the watering trough

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a pre-Islamic poet, says in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

وَمَنْ لَمْ يَذُدْ عَنْ حَوْضِهِ بِسِلَاحِهِ
يُهَدَّمْ وَمَنْ لَا يَظْلِمِ النَّاسَ يُظْلَمِ

wa-man lam yadhud ʿan ḥawḍihi bi-silāḥihi / yuhaddam wa-man lā yaẓlimi al-nāsa yuẓlami

Translation: Whoever does not defend his watering trough with his weapons will have it demolished; and whoever does not wrong people will himself be wronged.

In this verse, «يَذُدْ» is a jussive imperfect verb from DH W D.

«حَوْض» denotes a watering trough or basin. Access to a watering place was especially important in pastoral life and areas where water was scarce.

In this image, defending the trough can mean protecting rights of access, property, and an area for whose care a person or group is responsible.

Here, dhawd relates to «كَفّ» and «رَدْع»: holding back and restraining.

The watering trough can also stand for one’s protected sphere and what one is responsible for safeguarding.

Dhawd here therefore does not mean storing water in a vessel or simply standing beside a basin. It means preventing encroachment and keeping away those who would cause harm.

The second half, about wronging others and being wronged, reflects the poet’s view of power relations and tribal conflict. The poem does not establish that defending one’s rights requires wronging others according to the Qur’an.

The lexical witness illustrates dhāda as defending and restraining; the poet’s entire ethical judgment does not thereby become part of the root’s meaning.

Tending and driving a herd

In pastoral Arabic, «ذَادَ الْإِبِلَ» is used for driving and tending camels.

An animal may stray from the herd toward a direction the herder does not want. The herder turns it back and prevents it from separating.

A herd may also need to be kept away from a particular watering place or pasture until it is time to enter.

Both situations can be described with dhawd.

Keeping animals from approaching the water is one established explanation of this root’s Qur’anic use.

However, dhawd can also apply to people in Arabic. Restricting it absolutely to livestock would conflict with the range of evidence.

Some early explanations distinguish the use of “holding back” for animals from its application to people. This concerns the verb’s usage and the kind of object it takes, rather than implying that repelling or keeping away cannot apply to humans.

Midhwad: a means of defense

Arabic lexical usage also applies «مِذْوَد» to the human tongue, since speech can defend a person’s honor and rights.

Ḥassān ibn Thābit says:

لِسَانِي وَسَيْفِي صَارِمَانِ كِلَاهُمَا
وَيَبْلُغُ مَا لَا يَبْلُغُ السَّيْفُ مِذْوَدِي

lisānī wa-sayfī ṣārimāni kilāhumā / wa-yablughu mā lā yablughu al-sayfu midhwadī

Translation: My tongue and my sword are both sharp, and my defending tongue reaches where the sword cannot reach.

In this verse, «مِذْوَدِي» refers to the poet’s tongue.

This use shows that repelling and defending in Arabic need not involve physical force alone. Speech, argument, and response can also serve as means of defense.

Ḥassān lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The precise date of this verse is not established in the witness examined, so it cannot be called securely pre-Qur’anic evidence.

«مِذْوَد» is also recorded for a bull’s horn, with which the animal can repel danger.

These uses have related imagery, but they do not mean that DH W D itself denotes a tongue, sword, or horn.

A means of defense differs from the act of repelling or holding back.

3. Dhawd: A Small Group of Camels

Another branch of this family appears in the noun «ذَوْد».

In early Arabic, dhawd denotes a small group of camels.

The group is often said to comprise three to ten camels, but other ranges are also reported, and some usages restrict the word to female camels.

That restriction is disputed as well. In some witnesses the word describes a group that includes a male camel.

A fixed number or composition by sex should therefore not be treated as an unchanging definition of dhawd in every Arabic use.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

وَنَحْنُ ثَلَاثَةٌ وَثَلَاثُ ذَوْدٍ
لَقَدْ جَارَ الزَّمَانُ عَلَى عِيَالِي

wa-naḥnu thalāthatun wa-thalāthu dhawdin / laqad jāra al-zamānu ʿalā ʿiyālī

Translation: We are three people and have three camels; truly, time has dealt harshly with my dependents.

Here, dhawd refers to camels, rather than driving or defending. In this usage, «ثَلَاثُ ذَوْدٍ» means three camels, not three groups each comprising several camels.

Some transmissions give the first half as «ثَلَاثَةُ أَنْفُسٍ وَثَلَاثُ ذَوْدٍ».

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived both before and after Islam, and the precise date of this verse is uncertain. It illustrates early Arabic usage, but does not by itself establish that the verse predates the Qur’an.

The name for this group of camels may be connected with driving and gathering livestock, but that possibility does not prove its historical derivation.

This caution should be maintained.

The Qur’an does not use dhawd for a group of camels. Thus, tadhūdāni in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 must not be turned into “the two women owned a few camels.”

4. Tadhūdāni in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23: Two Women at the Water of Midian

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23:

وَلَمَّا وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ وَجَدَ عَلَيْهِ أُمَّةً مِنَ النَّاسِ يَسْقُونَ وَوَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُمَا قَالَتَا لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

wa-lammā warada māʾa madyana wajada ʿalayhi ummatan mina al-nāsi yasqūna wa-wajada min dūnihimu imraʾatayni tadhūdāni qāla mā khaṭbukumā qālatā lā nasqī ḥattā yuṣdira al-riʿāʾu wa-abūnā shaykhun kabīr

Translation: When he reached the water of Midian, he found there a group of people watering, and beside them he found two women holding back. He said, “What is your business?” They said, “We do not water until the shepherds return, and our father is a very old man.”

In this verse, Moses reaches the watering place after leaving Egypt and traveling toward Midian.

He sees two different situations: a group of people are watering, while two women stand apart, holding back or keeping something away.

«مِنْ دُونِهِمُ» locates the women relative to the group, but does not specify their distance or the site’s precise layout.

«يَسْقُونَ» shows that the first group is watering. In contrast, «تَذُودَانِ» describes a different action.

The women’s answer shows that they have not yet watered their animals and are waiting for the shepherds to leave the watering place.

Were the women holding back their animals?

The usual explanation takes tadhūdāni to mean keeping the women’s animals from approaching the water.

On this reading, they kept their herd aside until the other shepherds finished, allowing them to water their own animals.

Here the verb is explained through ḥabs: holding or keeping the animals back.

The rest of the verse supports this understanding.

An important contextual clue is the continuation of the women’s reply:

لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ

lā nasqī ḥattā yuṣdira al-riʿāʾ

That is: we do not water until the shepherds take their animals back from the water. In the common Ḥafṣ reading, «يُصْدِرَ» is transitive and refers to taking the animals away; the shorter rendering “until the shepherds return” leaves that object unexpressed.

Their answer shows that they are waiting for the watering place to clear. Keeping their herd from entering it therefore fits the rest of the verse well.

On this reading, dhawd means tending the animals and preventing their movement toward the watering place until the appropriate time.

However, the word “sheep” or “herd” does not occur in tadhūdāni itself. What is being held back is inferred from the pastoral setting and the continuation of the story.

Were the women keeping people away from their animals?

Some early explanations offer another reading: the women were preventing people from approaching their animals.

This possibility rests on the established use of dhāda ʿan: keeping someone or something away from what is being protected.

The verb could then refer to defending the animals against crowding or the approach of others.

This reading is possible within the root’s general meaning, but relates less directly to the following statement that they wait for the shepherds to leave before watering.

If their main concern were only to keep people away from the herd, their answer about when they water would need further explanation.

For this reason, the first reading—keeping the animals away from the watering place—fits the immediate context more closely.

Nevertheless, the two possibilities show that the verb’s meaning alone does not specify the precise direction of restraint or its object.

Why were the two women waiting?

The women’s reply includes:

وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

wa-abūnā shaykhun kabīr

That is: and our father is a very old man.

This sentence explains their family circumstances. Their father is elderly, and they themselves are present at the watering place.

The scene as a whole suggests that watering their animals was difficult for them at that time and that they had to wait until the other shepherds finished.

However, the Qur’an does not explicitly say that the shepherds threatened them, denied them water, or enforced a rule forbidding women to approach the watering place.

Such details may appear in historical or exegetical accounts, but should not be added to the Qur’anic story without evidence.

The verse also does not name the women’s father. Identifying him conclusively with a particular figure goes beyond the information this verse provides.

What is clear is that their position differs from that of the group already watering, and that waiting for the shepherds to leave delayed their work.

Moses’ action in the next verse

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:24 states:

فَسَقَىٰ لَهُمَا ثُمَّ تَوَلَّىٰ إِلَى الظِّلِّ فَقَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي لِمَا أَنْزَلْتَ إِلَيَّ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَقِيرٌ

fa-saqā lahumā thumma tawallā ilā al-ẓilli fa-qāla rabbi innī limā anzalta ilayya min khayrin faqīr

Translation: So he watered for the two of them, then turned back to the shade and said, “My Lord, I am in need of whatever good You send down to me.”

This makes the preceding situation clearer: the women’s practical need was watering, and Moses did it for them.

Thus, tadhūdāni in the preceding verse does not mean watering. The women were holding back or keeping aside, whereas Moses watered for them.

The distinction between dhawd and saqy must be preserved in translation.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:25 describes the invitation for Moses to come to the women’s father, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:26 describes the proposal to employ him. One of the women identifies strength and trustworthiness as qualities that make Moses suitable for the work.

These ethical and social qualities cannot all be extracted from tadhūdāni alone. They emerge from the rest of the story and the characters’ actions.

What these verses clearly depict is Moses encountering two people who have difficulty carrying out their work, and meeting their need without imposing a demand on them.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

DH W D (dhāda) and M N ʿ (manaʿa): holding back and preventing

Study of M N ʿ

Arabic manaʿa concerns preventing an action or access to something.

Dhāda can also involve restraint or prevention, but its established uses emphasize moving people or animals away, driving them away, or turning them back.

For example, someone may forbid another person to do something without moving them away physically. In many early examples, however, dhāda involves movement, turning back, or keeping away.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary. In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, early lexical explanations gloss tadhūdāni with taḥbisāni and tamnaʿāni: the two women hold back or prevent.

DH W D (dhāda) and D F ʿ (dafaʿa): keeping away and repelling

Study of D F ʿ

Arabic dafʿ has a broad range of uses, including moving aside, pushing back, and preventing something.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 speaks of repelling some people by means of others.

Dhawd can likewise be used for defending and pushing back, but pastoral language gives it a particular use in tending and directing a herd.

Dafʿ and dhawd therefore overlap in some situations, but not every act of repelling is dhawd in its familiar sense of driving livestock.

DH W D (dhāda) and Ṭ R D (ṭarada): driving away and expelling

Study of Ṭ R D

Ṭard emphasizes driving or moving someone or something away.

In the Qur’an, «تَطْرُدِ» in Al-Anʿām 6:52 occurs in the prohibition against driving away those who call upon their Lord.

Dhāda can also approach the meaning of expulsion or driving away, but sometimes its purpose is to keep animals in position and prevent premature entry into a particular place.

Thus, tadhūdāni in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 does not necessarily mean violently expelling someone from the watering place.

DH W D (dhāda) and S W Q (sāqa): directing and driving

Sawq concerns driving or directing someone or something onward along a route.

Some Arabic uses of dhawd also concern driving livestock. But when a herder keeps a herd away from water, restraining and controlling movement is more prominent than driving toward a destination.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, the context suggests that the women’s concern is not taking the herd to a distant location, but gaining the opportunity to water and knowing when they can approach the water.

“Holding back” is therefore more suitable here than “driving forward.”

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

The root’s only Qur’anic form is «تَذُودَانِ» in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23.

At the level of direct meaning, “they were holding back,” “they were keeping away,” and, in some uses, “they were driving away” are defensible equivalents.

Forqan’s current rendering is:

“And beside them he found two women holding back.”

This preserves the verb’s central meaning without silently adding an object that the Arabic leaves unexpressed.

To clarify the contextual sense, one could explain:

“The two women were keeping their animals away from where the others were watering.”

This explanation fits the rest of the verse and the usual lexical and exegetical reading. However, the animals are not named in tadhūdāni itself. The explanation therefore belongs in the verse’s interpretation and contextual discussion, rather than replacing the direct translation without an indication of the addition.

“Two women were watering” is not an accurate translation, since the verse distinguishes those watering from the two women.

“Two women were driving people away by force” also goes beyond the text, which does not specify the verb’s object or the manner of their action.

For Forqan, “they were holding back” is a defensible equivalent; the more precise contextual meaning and probable object can be explained in the interpretation.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root DH W D has two established branches: moving away, holding back, and defending; and dhawd, a small group of camels.

In the first branch, the verb can describe defending rights and protected boundaries, keeping danger away, preventing entry, and driving or tending livestock.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s verse shows that pre-Islamic poetry used dhawd for defending a watering trough and preventing encroachment. Pastoral uses also show that the verb could refer to controlling a herd’s movement and keeping it away from a place or direction.

Only «تَذُودَانِ» occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23.

There, two women at the water of Midian stand apart from the group already watering, performing an action involving restraint or keeping away.

Their reply shows that they are waiting for the shepherds to leave before watering their own animals. Keeping the animals from approaching the watering place until the right time is therefore a strong reading that fits the context.

However, because the verse leaves the verb’s object unexpressed, this inference should not be presented as the independent literal meaning of tadhūdāni itself.

Final definition: In its verbal branch, DH W D means moving, holding, or driving someone or something away from another place, route, or object. The action may serve defense, protection, or the directing and tending of livestock. In its nominal branch, dhawd denotes a small group of camels; that noun does not occur in the Qur’an.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, tadhūdāni means holding back or keeping away. Identifying what is being restrained and why depends on the context and the women’s reply.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing the act of keeping away, its object, and its purpose or effect. None should be made to stand for another’s meaning without evidence.
