Core meaning: a thorn or sharp, potentially injurious projection; in warfare, the sharpness and power of weapons and the capacity to confront an opponent.

The Arabic root sh w k concerns thorns and the sharpness and hardness of something capable of piercing or injuring the body.

شَوْك names plant thorns; شَوْكَة is a single thorn or sharp projection. The latter also names a scorpion's sting and certain pointed implements.

In extended usage, شَوْكَة denotes armed strength, fighting capacity, and the ability to injure an opponent. The thorn's sharpness and harmful effect provide an image for military strength.

A small thorn may seem insignificant, yet injures someone who steps on it. Weapons and fighting forces likewise can harm an opponent and make resistance difficult.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as الشَّوْكَةِ in Al-Anfal 8:7. There it means armed strength and fighting capacity, rather than a plant thorn.

1. The root in early Arabic

شَوْك: thorns and sharp projections

Early Arabic uses شَوْك for plant thorns.

A thorn is generally a hard, pointed growth capable of piercing human or animal skin.

Hardness alone does not characterize it: its sharp point and capacity to injure also matter.

A branch may be hard but, without a sharp point, does not pierce like a thorn.

A small thorn, by contrast, can penetrate the skin and cause pain because of its point.

شَاكَتْهُ الشَّوْكَةُ describes a thorn piercing or wounding someone.

The usage connects two closely related features: sharpness and injury.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Hajar says:

كَأَنَّ أَطَاوِلَ شَوْكِ السِّيَالِ
تَشُكُّ بِهَا مَضْجَعِي شَاجِرَهْ

ka-anna aṭāwila shawki al-siyāli / tashukku bihā maḍjaʿī shājirah

“As though the longest thorns of the siyal were piercing my resting place.”

سِيَال names a thorny plant; the poet uses its long thorns to evoke pain and restlessness.

شَوْك names physical plant thorns, while تَشُكُّ describes piercing.

تَشُكُّ comes from sh k k, not sh w k. Its combination with شَوْك nevertheless creates a vivid image of piercing thorns.

The verse is transmitted in Aws ibn Hajar's collected poetry and attributed to pre-Islamic verse. The general attribution is established in literary tradition, but this particular verse has no independently determined date.

Its lexical evidence is clear: شَوْك named plant thorns in early Arabic, with sharpness and the capacity to pierce part of the familiar image.

شَوْكَة: a single thorn

In its physical use, شَوْكَة means a thorn.

شَوْك can be a collective name, with شَوْكَة naming one member.

شجرة ذات شوك, for example, means a tree with thorns.

أصابتني شوكة describes being struck or pierced by a thorn.

Lexicons also record شَوْكَةُ الْعَقْرَبِ for a scorpion's sting.

The word thus extends beyond plants to another creature's sharp, injurious projection.

Both uses share sharpness and the potential to injure.

Extension to roughness and pointed growth

شَوَّكَ and related forms also describe emerging sharp projections or parts of the body becoming rough.

شَوَّكَ رِيشُ الْفَرْخِ, for example, describes a chick's newly emerging, rough and pointed feathers.

شَوَّكَ شَارِبُ الْغُلَامِ describes the appearance of coarse moustache hair on a youth.

Neither person nor bird literally grows plant thorns. The image describes the roughness, prominence, and sharpness of new growth.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhali says:

وَأَكْسُو الْحُلَّةَ الشَّوْكَاءَ خِدْنِي
وَبَعْضُ الْخَيْرِ فِي حُزَنٍ وِرَاطِ

wa-aksū al-ḥullata al-shawkāʾa khidnī / wa-baʿḍu al-khayri fī ḥuzanin wirāṭ

“I clothe my companion in a new, rough garment; some good is reached through hardship and difficult terrain.”

الشَّوْكَاء qualifies the garment here.

Lexicons explain it as a garment retaining the roughness of newness or its weave.

It need not be a garment with actual thorns attached: the texture of the cloth is in view.

The verse has variant readings, including “some of the people” instead of “some good.” The common lexical explanation of حُلَّة شَوْكَاء concerns the roughness of new cloth; differences in transmission should remain distinct from this explanation.

2. From thorns and sharpness to armed strength

An important use of شَوْكَة concerns military strength and the ability to injure an opponent.

Lexicons explain it through weapons, their sharpness, and fighting strength.

They also record شَائِكُ السِّلَاحِ and شَاكِي السِّلَاحِ for someone equipped with sharp weapons and ready to fight.

The connection with the thorn image is intelligible.

Thorns injure through their points; blades, spears, arrows, and other weapons can likewise harm an opponent.

Within this imagery, military شَوْكَة denotes effective fighting capacity.

One should nevertheless distinguish having weapons from possessing fighting strength.

A group may own several weapons but lack the numbers, readiness, or resources to confront a large force.

A group with trained members, suitable weapons, and resilience may possess substantial military strength.

Military شَوْكَة can therefore express effective fighting capacity beyond the mere presence of a sword or spear.

In an expression such as شَوْكَةُ الْمُقَاتِلِ, it can indicate a combatant's fighting strength.

ذُو شَوْكَةٍ similarly describes a person or group possessing strength to confront an opponent.

Classical lexicons record this sense, and the Quran itself attests it. This article does not present an independently dated pre-Islamic poetic witness for the military usage; the Quran establishes its currency in the period of revelation without fixing its historical origin.

3. Forms and derivatives

Important forms in the Arabic family include:

شَوْك: thorns; a collective noun.

شَوْكَة: a thorn or sharp projection; militarily, armed strength and fighting capacity.

أَشْوَاك: a plural referring to thorns.

شَوِك: thorny or bearing thorns.

شَائِك: thorn-bearing; with weapons, equipped with sharp arms.

شَاكِي السِّلَاحِ: equipped with sharp weapons and ready for confrontation.

شَاكَتْهُ الشَّوْكَةُ: a thorn pierced or injured him.

أَشْوَكَتِ الشَّجَرَةُ: the tree developed thorns or became abundantly thorny.

شَوَّكَ: produced thorns, made thorny, or developed rough and pointed projections.

تَشْوِيك: making thorny or the appearance of thornlike projections.

شَوْكَاء: a feminine adjective describing something thorny or rough.

Forms such as شائك and شاكي have closely related meanings in weapon expressions, but their different structures and spellings still matter.

Only الشَّوْكَةِ occurs in the Quran.

شَوْك, شَائِك, أَشْوَك, and the other lexical forms help explain the family but are not Quranic occurrences.

4. Quranic use: the group possessing armed strength

Al-Anfal 8:7

وَإِذْ يَعِدُكُمُ اللَّهُ إِحْدَى الطَّائِفَتَيْنِ أَنَّهَا لَكُمْ وَتَوَدُّونَ أَنَّ غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ تَكُونُ لَكُمْ وَيُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَنْ يُحِقَّ الْحَقَّ بِكَلِمَاتِهِ وَيَقْطَعَ دَابِرَ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-idh yaʿidukumu allāhu iḥdā al-ṭāʾifatayni annahā lakum wa-tawaddūna anna ghayra dhāti al-shawkati takūnu lakum wa-yurīdu allāhu an yuḥiqqa al-ḥaqqa bi-kalimātihi wa-yaqṭaʿa dābira al-kāfirīn

Al-Anfāl 8:7

“And when The God promised you that one of the two groups would be yours, you wished that the group without armed strength would be yours. But The God intended to establish the truth by His words and cut off the last remnant of the disbelievers.”

Al-Anfal 8:7

الشَّوْكَة occurs in ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ.

ذَات here expresses possessing something.

ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ therefore describes the group possessing shawkah.

In context, that is military strength and fighting capacity, not plant thorns.

غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ denotes the group without that armed strength or substantial fighting capacity.

The two groups and their different capabilities

إِحْدَى الطَّائِفَتَيْنِ means one of the two groups.

The verse then says the addressees wished to obtain the group without armed strength.

The groups were thus not alike in their ability to resist or the difficulty of confronting them.

One had military strength; the other lacked comparable fighting capacity.

Historical accounts of Badr commonly identify them as the Quraysh trading caravan and the armed force that came out to confront the Muslims.

This identification also fits the surah's context.

Al-Anfal 8:42 describes the two sides' positions in the valley and the caravan below them.

الشوكة itself nevertheless means neither caravan nor army.

It describes armed strength; the groups' historical identity comes from the broader surah and related evidence.

Does غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ mean completely unarmed?

In context, the phrase concerns the group lacking the other group's military capacity.

The word alone does not establish that no individual carried even one weapon.

A trading caravan, for example, may have armed guards but lack substantial strength compared with an army ready for battle.

In that comparison, the caravan lacks the army's fighting power.

The phrase indicates a difference in military capacity rather than a precise inventory of swords and spears.

“Unarmed” is an established rendering associated with the lexical sense of weapons. It should not, however, be treated as a precise count of every individual's arms.

“The group without armed strength” or “the group lacking substantial fighting capacity” makes this contextual distinction explicit.

Why did the addressees prefer the group without shawkah?

The verse says:

وَتَوَدُّونَ أَنَّ غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ تَكُونُ لَكُمْ

wa-tawaddūna anna ghayra dhāti al-shawkati takūnu lakum

Al-Anfāl 8:7

They wished that the group without armed strength would be theirs.

تَوَدُّونَ expresses their desire.

The preceding verse helps explain that preference.

Al-Anfal 8:6 describes people disputing with the Prophet about the truth, as though being driven toward death while watching.

The image conveys apprehension about a dangerous confrontation.

A preference for the militarily weaker group is understandable in that setting.

Faced with a choice between a battle-ready force and a less capable group, someone may prefer the less dangerous encounter.

الشوكة itself does not mean fear, danger, or easy victory.

Fear and preference for less danger arise from the situation and context; shawkah identifies the opponent's armed strength.

The addressees' wishes and divine purpose

The verse continues:

وَيُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَنْ يُحِقَّ الْحَقَّ بِكَلِمَاتِهِ

wa-yurīdu allāhu an yuḥiqqa al-ḥaqqa bi-kalimātihi

Al-Anfāl 8:7

That is, God intends to establish the truth by His words.

The verse distinguishes their wishes from divine purpose.

They preferred the group whose military confrontation would be less difficult.

The verse instead describes God's purpose of establishing truth and cutting off the disbelievers' last remnant.

This does not mean every difficult choice is automatically God's will, or every easier course is less worthy.

The verse addresses a particular historical and military situation.

Nor does shawkah name a moral virtue here.

A militarily powerful group can be in the wrong.

A poorly equipped or militarily weak group can be in the right.

Fighting strength and moral rightness are different matters.

Al-Anfal 8:7 expresses military power through الشوكة and the establishment of truth through other phrases.

They should not be merged into one lexical definition.

5. Comparison with related roots

sh w k and ḥ d d

حِدَّة, from ḥ d d, concerns sharpness and cutting ability.

The physical image of شَوْكَة also involves sharpness.

حِدَّة may describe a knife, sword, or cutting edge, whereas شَوْك names thorns and pointed projections.

A knife blade can be sharp without being called a thorn.

Military شَوْكَة can concern sharp weapons and their harmful force, but Al-Anfal 8:7 goes beyond one blade's edge to a group's capacity to resist.

sh w k and s l ḥ

سِلَاح names the tools of fighting and defense.

Military شَوْكَة can express effective strength in warfare.

These concepts overlap without being identical in every use.

Someone can own a weapon without being able to use it effectively.

A group with trained members and appropriate equipment may have greater fighting strength.

Weapons contribute to military power, but that power is not always reducible to owning a few implements.

The wider sense of fighting capacity is appropriate for شَوْكَة in Al-Anfal 8:7.

sh w k and b ʾ s

بَأْس concerns hardship and severity, and in some Quranic and Arabic contexts the strength and intensity of warfare.

Military شَوْكَة also concerns confrontational strength.

Expressions such as شَوْكَةُ الْقِتَالِ and شِدَّةُ الْبَأْسِ describe fighting strength and forceful confrontation.

بأس has a wider range, also covering hardship, severity, and punishment.

Quranic شَوْكَة, by contrast, belongs to a family linking thorns and weapons with armed power.

It cannot replace every use of بأس.

sh w k and q w y

قُوَّة is a broad word for strength and capacity.

That strength can be physical, social, economic, or, where appropriate, military.

شَوْكَة in Al-Anfal 8:7 identifies military strength and the ability to confront an opponent.

A group can possess great wealth without a large fighting force.

Another may have fewer economic resources but an organized, ready military force.

Armed strength is one kind of power, not the equivalent of every use of قُوَّة.

sh w k and sh k k

The roots resemble one another in writing but are distinct.

شَوْكَة belongs to sh w k and concerns thorns and sharpness.

شَكّ belongs to sh k k and in familiar Quranic usage means doubt or lack of certainty.

Arabic sh k k also has physical piercing uses, such as thrusting a spear into a body.

The families can therefore overlap in imagery without being the same root.

شَائِك in a weapon expression also differs from شَاكّ, “one who doubts.”

Similarity of sound or spelling should not erase these distinctions.

6. Military and political power: lexical limits

Persian shawkat also denotes grandeur, authority, and power.

The shawkat of a government, for example, may describe its authority and strength.

This relates to the Arabic branch involving power and the ability to confront others.

Al-Anfal 8:7, however, concerns a military encounter.

الشوكة should therefore not be rendered merely as splendor, fame, wealth, or social importance.

An outwardly magnificent government might have little battlefield strength.

A capable fighting force might have little luxury or outward grandeur.

“Armed strength” is thus more precise here than “splendor and greatness.”

The word's use in a battle account does not itself establish a general rule legitimizing force.

It describes the capacity to harm or resist. Questions of who may fight, under what conditions, and within what limits require separate consideration of passages on warfare and justice.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Quranic word: الشَّوْكَةِ

Root: sh w k

Word type: a definite feminine singular noun, genitive in the possessive construction ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ.

Brief root meaning: a thorn or sharp, potentially injurious projection; militarily, weapons and fighting strength.

Meaning in this verse: a group's military strength and capacity to confront an opponent.

Suggested rendering of ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ: “possessing armed strength.”

Suggested rendering of غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ: “the group without armed strength.”

Within Al-Anfal 8:7, the phrase may be rendered:

“And you wished that the group without armed strength would be yours.”

“The unarmed group” is also an established translation. It should not be taken as a precise statement about every individual's equipment.

“The powerless group” is too broad: the verse concerns a particular kind of power, not the absence of all resources and abilities.

“The group without armed strength” or “the group lacking substantial fighting capacity” clarifies the contextual sense.

A proposed word-box explanation should distinguish the physical root meaning from its sense in this verse:

Brief root meaning: “Thorns and injurious sharpness; figuratively, armed strength.”

Meaning in this verse: “Military strength and capacity to resist.”

8. Conclusion

The physical meanings of Arabic sh w k include thorns and sharp projections.

شَوْك denotes plant thorns and شَوْكَة one thorn. The latter also names a scorpion's sting and other sharp, injurious projections.

Extended uses concern roughness, pointed growth, and the power of weapons.

In warfare, شَوْكَة can express effective fighting strength.

The root occurs once in the Quran, in Al-Anfal 8:7.

ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ describes the group with armed strength; غَيْرَ ذَاتِ الشَّوْكَةِ identifies the group lacking that military capacity.

The contrast concerns fighting strength, not possession of every kind of power on one side and absolute helplessness on the other.

Military power does not itself establish rightness. الشوكة describes fighting capacity, while separate phrases address truth and falsehood.

Final definition of sh w k:

“A thorn or hard, pointed projection capable of causing injury, with extended uses involving roughness, sharpness, and harmful force. In warfare, شَوْكَة describes the power of weapons and effective fighting capacity. In Al-Anfal 8:7, الشَّوْكَة means armed strength rather than a physical thorn.”
