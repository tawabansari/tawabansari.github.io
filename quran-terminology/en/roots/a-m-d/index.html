Qur’anic Root A M D

Limits, endpoints, and temporal extent: from a race’s finish line to waiting periods and the fulfillment of a promise

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَمَدًا
(amadan)
Qur’anic indefinite accusative noun; three occurrences, Āl ʿImrān 3:30, Al-Kahf 18:12, Al-Jinn 72:25

الْأَمَدُ
(al-amadu)
Qur’anic definite nominative noun; Al-Ḥadīd 57:16

أَمَد
(amad)
Base noun: limit, goal, duration, or interval, according to context

آمَاد
(āmād)
Recorded lexical plural of amad; absent from the Qur’an

أَمَّدَ / تَأْمِيد

(āmid / āmidah)

The Arabic root A M D concerns the limit or endpoint toward which a movement, distance, or period extends. Amad can denote the end of a course, the duration elapsed up to a particular point, or an interval between two situations. In temporal use, it refers to a period for which an endpoint can be envisaged, even when that endpoint is unknown to the speaker or listener.

The Qur’an uses this root four times. In Āl ʿImrān 3:30, a person on the Day of Reckoning wishes for a great distance from their evil deeds. In Al-Kahf 18:12, amad concerns the duration of the Companions of the Cave’s stay. In Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, the lengthening of al-amad accompanies the hardening of the hearts of a group previously given the Scripture. In Al-Jinn 72:25, it concerns a period the Lord may appoint before a promise is fulfilled.

These uses show that amad does not always mean a long period. In some verses, length or remoteness comes from accompanying words such as baʿīdan, “far,” and ṭāla, “grew long.” Amad itself concerns an extent or limit that may be near or distant, short or long, known or unknown.

Amad also overlaps semantically with ajal, madā, and abad, without being interchangeable with any of them in every use. Recognizing these distinctions matters for accurate translation.

1. Qur’anic forms and the root’s semantic core

The root A M D occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses, exclusively as the noun amad.

Its Qur’anic forms are:

  • Amadan baʿīdan in Āl ʿImrān 3:30.
  • Amadan in Al-Kahf 18:12.
  • Al-amadu in Al-Ḥadīd 57:16.
  • Amadan in Al-Jinn 72:25.

There are therefore three indefinite accusative occurrences, amadan, and one definite nominative occurrence, al-amadu.

These are forms of a single noun. Their differences concern definiteness and grammatical function, rather than independent derivatives.

In Arabic, amad denotes a limit, goal, or endpoint. The plural āmād is also recorded.

Lexicons record ammada and its verbal noun taʾmīd for declaring or clarifying the limit or endpoint of a period. This lexical report alone does not establish that the verb was used before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Another branch records amida ʿalayhi, “became angry with someone.” In certain uses, āmid also describes a laden ship or something filled with good or evil.

None of these verbs or descriptive forms occurs in the Qur’an. All Qur’anic occurrences belong to the noun amad, denoting a limit, duration, or interval.

The semantic core of this branch may be expressed as follows:

A limit or endpoint reached by a distance or extent; temporally, a duration or interval measured with reference to a beginning, an end, or an anticipated point.

The endpoint or limit matters in this definition, but the length of the distance or duration need not be known in advance.

For example, someone may know that a journey has a destination without knowing when they will reach it. Similarly, they may know that an event will eventually occur without knowing the interval before it.

Amad can be used in both situations.

2. Amad in early Arabic: from the finish line to the end of life

Amad: the limit and goal of a course

In early Arabic usage, amad denotes the endpoint of a course or the limit toward which movement proceeds.

Horse racing provides a particularly clear setting for this use.

A horse starts at one point and strives to reach the goal or end of the course before its competitors.

Here, amad may denote the race’s endpoint or finish line.

A poem attributed to Al-Nābighah Al-Dhubyānī contains the following:

إِلَّا لِمِثْلِكَ أَوْ مَنْ أَنْتَ سَابِقُهُ
سَبْقَ الْجَوَادِ إِذَا اسْتَوْلَى عَلَى الْأَمَدِ

illā li-mithlika aw man anta sābiquhu / sabqa al-jawādi idhā istawlā ʿalā al-amadi

“Except for someone like you, or someone whom you surpass, as a swift horse outstrips its rival when it gains the finish line.”

Al-jawād in this line is a swift horse; al-amad is the limit or endpoint of the racecourse.

The poet speaks of reaching the endpoint, where precedence becomes evident.

In this witness, amad therefore does not directly denote time. It refers to a course’s endpoint or spatial goal.

However, the authenticity of some lines in this part of the poem attributed to Al-Nābighah has been questioned. This line is an important witness to usage transmitted in Arabic literary and lexical tradition, but its composition in the pre-Islamic period should not be treated as securely established.

Amad and the interval remaining before a goal

Where a course has an endpoint, the distance remaining before that endpoint can also be considered.

Accordingly, amad may denote not only the endpoint itself but also the interval or extent leading up to it.

This subtle distinction matters.

One use focuses on the limit itself.

Another focuses on the interval between the beginning and that limit.

For example, the amad of a race may be its finish line. In amadan baʿīdan, however, the remoteness of the goal or the distance before it becomes prominent.

Consequently, “end” is not an adequate translation in every instance.

“Limit,” “interval,” “distance,” or “duration” may be more natural, depending on context.

Amad and the end of human life

Arabic records the expression mā amaduka? as a question about a person’s life or its limit.

In one use, it concerns the end of a person’s life: the endpoint toward which life proceeds.

In another, the same expression asks about life’s beginning or the time of birth.

This variation shows that amad can be understood relative to a span with a beginning and an end, about which a question may be asked from either direction.

However, this does not mean that amad denotes death in every Qur’anic verse.

Al-Kahf 18:12 concerns the duration of the Companions of the Cave’s stay.

Al-Jinn 72:25 concerns the timing of a promised event whose date is unknown to the Prophet.

Thus, amad can refer to the end of life, but its meaning is not restricted to human life.

A temporal witness: a lifetime reaching its end

A poem attributed to Al-Ṭirimmāḥ, an Umayyad poet, reads:

كُلُّ حَيٍّ مُسْتَكْمِلٌ عِدَّةَ الْعُمْرِ
وَمُودٍ إِذَا انْقَضَى أَمَدُهُ

kullu ḥayyin mustakmilun ʿiddata al-ʿumri / wa-mūdin idhā inqaḍā amaduhu

“Every living being completes the numbered span of life and perishes when its term expires.”

In this witness, amaduhu concerns the end of a lifetime.

Inqaḍā expresses passing or expiration; amad identifies the limit or period that has ended.

The line has variant readings. Al-Ṭabarī’s transmission and certain lexical sources read amaduhu, whereas the dīwān text and its reported manuscript reading have ʿadaduhu, “its numbered span.”

The line should therefore be read with its variant readings acknowledged. Amaduhu is a documented traditional reading, but not the dīwān’s only securely transmitted wording. Nor can this Umayyad witness alone establish usage before the Qur’an’s revelation.

In any case, lexical sources clearly record amad for a period’s limit and the end of life.

Other branches: anger and fullness

Arabic records amida ʿalayhi for becoming angry with someone.

This differs from amad meaning an endpoint or goal.

Lexicons also record āmid and āmidah for a laden ship, and āmid for something filled with good or evil. The precise dates of these uses cannot be inferred from the lexical reports alone.

One might imagine a connection between filling a vessel and reaching its limit, but such an explanation does not establish these words’ historical origin.

The same caution applies to the branch concerning anger.

Shared consonants alone do not justify deriving anger from the expiration of a period.

For Qur’anic study, the branch concerning limits and extent has direct support. The other branches help identify the wider range of Arabic usage.

3. Amadan baʿīdan: wishing to be far from evil deeds

Āl ʿImrān 3:30 states:

يَوْمَ تَجِدُ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ مُحْضَرًا وَمَا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ سُوءٍ تَوَدُّ لَوْ أَنَّ بَيْنَهَا وَبَيْنَهُ أَمَدًا بَعِيدًا وَيُحَذِّرُكُمُ اللَّهُ نَفْسَهُ وَاللَّهُ رَءُوفٌ بِالْعِبَادِ

yawma tajidu kullu nafsin mā ʿamilat min khayrin muḥḍaran wa-mā ʿamilat min sūʾin tawaddu law anna baynahā wa-baynahu amadan baʿīdan wa-yuḥadhdhirukumu allāhu nafsahu wa-allāhu raʾūfun bi-l-ʿibādi

“On the day when every person finds whatever good they have done brought before them; and concerning whatever evil they have done, they wish there were a vast distance between themselves and it. The God warns you of Himself; and The God is kind to His servants.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:30

Here, amadan is qualified by baʿīdan, “far.”

Amad expresses the interval or extent; baʿīdan specifies its remoteness.

Great remoteness is therefore not an inherent component of the root’s meaning. It comes from the accompanying adjective.

The interval between a person and their deeds

The verse describes a day when a person confronts what they have done.

Their good deeds are present, while their evil deeds become the subject of a painful wish.

The person wants a great distance between themselves and the evil they have done.

In this context, amadan baʿīdan can express the distance they wish would separate them from their evil deeds.

This remoteness can also be understood spatially: a person does not want to face the consequences of wrongdoing and wishes the deed were far away.

Arabic also uses amad for temporal extent. A temporal reading is therefore possible: the person wishes that a very long period separated them from the deed or from confronting it.

Some grammatical analyses allow the pronoun in baynahu, “between it,” to refer to the Day of Reckoning: the person wishes that day were still far away.

Nevertheless, the proximity of mā ʿamilat min sūʾ, “whatever evil it has done,” and the presentation of deeds make reference to the evil deed a clear and strong reading.

A base translation can therefore retain the interval between a person and their evil deed, leaving the temporal or spatial imagery to the explanation.

Does amad here mean punishment?

No.

The verse concerns confronting one’s deeds and their consequences, but amad does not directly mean punishment.

Nor does the word name sin or regret.

Tawaddu expresses the person’s wish.

Mā ʿamilat min sūʾ identifies the evil deed.

Amadan baʿīdan expresses the distant interval the person desires.

The sentence as a whole conveys regret and remorse, but these ideas should not all be incorporated into amad’s lexical definition.

Does distance from a deed imply its erasure?

In earthly life, a person may regret an action and seek to make amends.

In the scene described here, however, the person is confronting their earlier deed.

Wishing for distance from it does not, by itself, announce that it has been erased or forgiven.

Conversely, this verse alone does not exclude the possibility of divine forgiveness. Its subject is the encounter with one’s deeds and the desire to be far from evil.

The person’s wish, the presence of their deeds, and The God’s final judgment must therefore be distinguished.

Amad expresses only the desired interval or extent within this scene.

4. Aḥṣā li-mā labithū amadan: the duration of the Companions of the Cave’s stay

Al-Kahf 18:12 states:

ثُمَّ بَعَثْنَاهُمْ لِنَعْلَمَ أَيُّ الْحِزْبَيْنِ أَحْصَىٰ لِمَا لَبِثُوا أَمَدًا

thumma baʿathnāhum li-naʿlama ayyu al-ḥizbayni aḥṣā li-mā labithū amadā

“Then We awakened them, to know which of the two groups had more accurately reckoned the duration of their stay.”

Al-Kahf 18:12

Here, amadan concerns the duration of the Companions of the Cave’s stay.

Labithū describes their remaining or staying.

Aḥṣā belongs to the root Ḥ Ṣ Y and concerns counting or accurate reckoning.

The subject is therefore the period they spent in that condition and the issue of knowing or reckoning it.

Does amad here mean a duration or its endpoint?

In early usage, amad concerns a limit or endpoint.

When the subject is remaining in a place, however, the temporal endpoint becomes associated with the duration elapsed.

For example, if someone has remained somewhere from the beginning to the end of a period, one can ask how long that stay lasted.

Amadan concerns this temporal extent in the verse.

“Duration” is therefore a clear, natural contextual translation.

This does not mean that amad is exclusively a name for a span of time throughout Arabic. The horse-racing witness also shows a spatial endpoint.

Distinguishing amad from aḥṣā

In this verse, amadan concerns the duration, while aḥṣā expresses reckoning or accuracy in determining it.

Amad should therefore not be equated directly with “number.”

Some early explanations gloss amadan with ʿadadan, “a number,” because the verse concerns reckoning the duration of the stay.

This contextual explanation does not erase the distinction between the two concepts.

A number is a counted quantity; amad is the duration or limit whose quantity is being considered.

Replacing amad with “number” alone obscures its temporal extent.

Grammatical disagreement over aḥṣā

There are two well-known analyses of aḥṣā in this verse.

One treats it as a past-tense verb: which of the two groups correctly reckoned the duration.

The other treats it as an elative: which group was more accurate in reckoning that duration.

In the verbal analysis, amadan is taken as the object of aḥṣā. In the elative analysis, both forming an elative from a Form IV verb and accounting for the accusative amadan have been debated; proposals include an additional understood verb. These analyses should not be presented as grammatically identical or undisputed.

In both readings, however, the main subject remains the duration of the Companions of the Cave’s stay and accuracy in knowing or reckoning it.

Thus, “which group had more accurately reckoned the duration of their stay” conveys the general sense of the sentence.

The grammatical explanation should acknowledge that the comparative translation is not the only possible analysis of aḥṣā.

Who were the two groups?

The verse says al-ḥizbayn: “the two groups.”

Al-Kahf 18:12 itself does not name or precisely identify them.

Early explanations offer several possibilities: two groups disputing the duration; some of the young men and another group of people; or people assessing the duration after the story became known.

Determining these groups’ identities with certainty is unnecessary for understanding amad.

What the sentence directly establishes is that the duration of the stay was an object of reckoning and knowledge.

After the young men awaken, Al-Kahf 18:19 also raises the question of how long they remained.

Al-Kahf 18:25 expresses their stay in years.

These verses clarify the story’s temporal setting. The historical and interpretive debates surrounding the Companions of the Cave should not all be imported into the definition of A M D.

Li-naʿlama and divine knowledge

The verse begins with li-naʿlama, “so that We may know.”

This verb is attributed to The God and occurs within the account of awakening the Companions of the Cave.

The wording must be taken seriously as it stands. However, it does not by itself establish that The God previously lacked knowledge of the duration of their stay.

The relationship between divine knowledge and an event becoming manifest in the world extends beyond a study of A M D.

For this article, the distinction between the two expressions is sufficient: li-naʿlama concerns knowledge and the disclosure of the outcome; amadan concerns the duration being reckoned.

5. Fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu al-amadu: passing time and hardened hearts

Al-Ḥadīd 57:16 states:

أَلَمْ يَأْنِ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْ تَخْشَعَ قُلُوبُهُمْ لِذِكْرِ اللَّهِ وَمَا نَزَلَ مِنَ الْحَقِّ وَلَا يَكُونُوا كَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَطَالَ عَلَيْهِمُ الْأَمَدُ فَقَسَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَكَثِيرٌ مِنْهُمْ فَاسِقُونَ

a-lam yaʾni li-lladhīna āmanū an takhshaʿa qulūbuhum li-dhikri allāhi wa-mā nazala mina al-ḥaqqi wa-lā yakūnū ka-lladhīna ūtū al-kitāba min qablu fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu al-amadu fa-qasat qulūbuhum wa-kathīrun minhum fāsiqūna

“Has the time not come for those who believe that their hearts should humble themselves before the remembrance of The God and the truth that has come down, and that they should not be like those given the Scripture before, over whom a long period passed and whose hearts hardened? Many of them are defiantly disobedient.”

Al-Ḥadīd 57:16

Here, al-amadu occurs with the verb ṭāla.

Ṭāla belongs to the root Ṭ W L and denotes growing long or being lengthy.

The lengthening of the period therefore comes from the accompanying verb. Amad by itself does not necessarily mean a long period.

What period had grown long?

The verse refers to people previously given the Scripture.

It then says fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu al-amadu, “a long period passed over them.”

Here, al-amadu concerns the period or temporal extent that had passed over that group.

It may be understood as the long interval between their receiving Scripture and revealed guidance, and their later condition.

Amad itself does not name a particular prophet, scripture, or historical era.

Nor does the verse specify this period in years or generations.

No historical number should therefore be invented for al-amadu here, or its meaning restricted to a supposedly certain calendar interval.

Does the passage of time itself harden hearts?

The verse states that the period grew long for them and their hearts hardened.

This association matters.

It does not establish that passing time alone necessarily hardens hearts, irrespective of human conduct.

The verse itself begins by questioning the humility of believers’ hearts.

Its subject is the human relationship with remembrance of The God and the truth that has come down.

The warning therefore concerns more than a count of years. It concerns becoming distant from the remembrance and truth that should keep hearts attentive and humble.

Someone may have known Scripture and guidance for a long time while their presence in that person’s life and conduct gradually diminishes.

In that situation, passing time can accompany heedlessness and hardening of the heart.

This conclusion comes from the whole verse, rather than amad’s lexical meaning.

Amad expresses the duration; qasat qulūbuhum describes the hardening of their hearts.

Who receives this warning?

The verse begins with alladhīna āmanū, “those who believe.” Believers therefore receive the warning directly.

A group of earlier recipients of Scripture is then presented as a cautionary example.

This construction shows that the Qur’an is not merely criticizing a historical group. It warns believers lest they too fall into the same condition over time.

The verse concludes: wa-kathīrun minhum fāsiqūn.

Kathīrun minhum means “many of them,” rather than all of them.

The verse should therefore not be turned into an indiscriminate judgment about all People of the Scripture in every age.

Its central concern is the danger of hardened hearts and distance from truth, a danger against which the believing addressees themselves are warned.

Is the hardening of the heart irreversible?

The next verse, Al-Ḥadīd 57:17, speaks of bringing the earth to life after its death.

This sequence can suggest the possibility of transformation and renewed life.

However, this connection does not turn al-amadu into a lexical expression for “the death of the heart.”

In verse 16, the long period and the hardening of hearts are related but distinct elements.

Verse 17 describes The God’s power to revive the earth.

The relationship between these verses may matter for spiritual reflection, but the lexical definition of A M D still concerns duration and extent.

6. Am yajʿalu lahu rabbī amadan: the unknown timing of a promise

Al-Jinn 72:25 states:

قُلْ إِنْ أَدْرِي أَقَرِيبٌ مَا تُوعَدُونَ أَمْ يَجْعَلُ لَهُ رَبِّي أَمَدًا

qul in adrī a-qarībun mā tūʿadūna am yajʿalu lahu rabbī amadā

“Say: I do not know whether what you are promised is near, or whether my Lord appoints a period for it.”

Al-Jinn 72:25

Amadan here concerns the interval before the promise is fulfilled.

A-qarībun asks whether it is near.

The alternative, am yajʿalu lahu rabbī amadan, allows for a period before that same promise is fulfilled.

Here, amadan may denote a temporal endpoint that the Lord appoints for the event.

Must amad here mean a very long period?

Am, “or,” following a-qarībun, “is it near?”, shows that the event might not be near.

“A more distant time” or “a longer interval” may therefore explain the context.

Amadan itself, however, is not qualified by baʿīdan, “far,” or ṭawīlan, “long.”

This distinction should be preserved.

Āl ʿImrān 3:30 explicitly includes the adjective baʿīdan.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:16 expresses lengthening through the verb ṭāla.

Al-Jinn 72:25, however, specifies neither the amount nor the length of the period.

Translations such as “my Lord postpones it for thousands of years” or “has appointed a very distant time for it” therefore exceed what the word itself says.

The verse directly states uncertainty about the promise’s nearness or the interval before its fulfillment.

What is the promise?

Al-Jinn 72:24 describes the addressees seeing what they were promised and discovering who has weaker supporters and fewer in number.

In verse 25, Muḥammad is commanded to say that he does not know whether the promise’s fulfillment is near or how long the interval will be.

Early interpretations associate this promise with punishment or the Resurrection.

Neither referent, however, should be extracted from amad alone as its definitive lexical meaning.

Amad expresses the time or endpoint associated with the event. The nature of the promise comes from context.

In adrī: the limit of the Prophet’s knowledge

This part of the verse is particularly significant.

Here, in adrī is negative and means “I do not know.”

Muḥammad is therefore commanded to state plainly that he does not know when the promise will be fulfilled.

This direct statement should not be neutralized solely by later theological assumptions that the Prophet knows all unseen matters.

Al-Jinn 72:26 describes The God’s knowledge of the unseen, while Al-Jinn 72:27 raises the disclosure of some unseen knowledge to chosen Messengers.

Receiving revelation and being informed of unseen matters is therefore distinct from independently knowing every detail of the future.

Verse 25 explicitly limits knowledge concerning the timing of the promise’s fulfillment.

Does an unknown date make the promise itself uncertain?

No.

The verse distinguishes the promise itself from the time of its occurrence.

The question is not whether the divine promise is true, but whether its fulfillment is near or further away.

This distinction also matters for understanding amad.

Amad can denote the temporal endpoint of an anticipated event, even when a person does not yet know when that endpoint will be reached.

Not knowing the duration therefore need not imply doubting the event itself.

7. How the four Qur’anic uses relate

Reading the four verses together reveals one shared concept serving four different functions.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:30, amad is the interval a person wishes would separate them from their evil deeds.

In Al-Kahf 18:12, the duration of a group’s stay is an object of knowledge and reckoning.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, the lengthening of a period accompanies heedlessness and hardened hearts.

In Al-Jinn 72:25, the subject is the interval before a promise is fulfilled, whose length is unknown to the Prophet.

In all four, amad concerns extent, interval, or endpoint.

This common element does not mean that all four verses discuss the same kind of time.

Al-Kahf focuses on elapsed duration.

Al-Ḥadīd emphasizes the lengthening of a past period.

Al-Jinn concerns a future interval before a promise is fulfilled.

Āl ʿImrān concerns a desired distance in the scene of reckoning.

The root can therefore express different temporal relationships without being restricted to past or future.

These uses also show that amad does not itself specify shortness or length. Those qualities come from an adjective, an accompanying verb, or a contrast within the sentence.

8. Distinguishing A M D from related roots and words

A M D (amad) and A J L (ajal)

Study of A J L

In the Qur’an, ajal concerns an appointed term for the end or occurrence of something.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:34 assigns each community a term: once it arrives, it cannot be advanced or postponed.

Amad too can refer to the end of a period, but its Qur’anic uses also emphasize the extent or interval leading up to that limit.

In Al-Jinn 72:25, amad concerns a period or endpoint whose precise timing is unknown to the Prophet.

Ajal and amad are therefore close in the semantic field of endings and appointed terms, without being exact synonyms.

Nor can one claim that every ajal is necessarily known to humans and every amad necessarily unknown. Qur’anic usage does not establish such an absolute division.

A M D (amad) and M D Y (madā)

In Arabic, madā concerns extent, limit, or endpoint.

Early explanations sometimes define amad and madā through one another.

Both can denote the end of a course or the limit of a distance.

In the witnesses discussed here, amad sometimes emphasizes the goal itself and sometimes the extent leading to it; madā too denotes limit and extent. Since lexicons explain these words through one another, an exclusive, fixed distinction cannot be imposed on them.

The familiar lexical contrast between attention to an endpoint and a general name for time is principally stated in comparing amad with zamān. It is not a rule invariably separating amad from madā.

In the horse-racing witness, amad denotes the course’s endpoint. In the Qur’an, it can concern a duration or interval before a limit.

A M D (amad) and Z M N (zaman)

Zaman is a general name for time or a period.

Amad usually relates to an extent or an envisaged limit.

Saying that time has passed may focus simply on its passage.

Speaking of amad can focus on a particular interval or an endpoint by which the duration is measured.

Nevertheless, amad sometimes has a meaning close to “duration” or “time.” An artificial distinction should not be imposed on every sentence.

“A long period passed over them” is entirely natural in Al-Ḥadīd 57:16.

A M D (amad) and A B D (abad)

Study of A B D

In Arabic, abad concerns temporal extension without a specified limit and, in certain uses, unending permanence.

The Qur’an uses abadan in various constructions expressing permanence and continuity.

Amad, however, concerns a limit or period for which an endpoint can be envisaged, even when the sentence does not specify it.

Amadan baʿīdan therefore does not by itself mean “forever.”

In Āl ʿImrān 3:30, a person wishes for great distance from their evil deeds. Emotionally, this may convey a desire for complete separation, but amad itself does not definitively mean endlessness.

Nor should every use of abad be treated, without contextual examination, as settling every question about the duration of the hereafter. Each word must be examined within its construction.

A M D (amad) and Ḥ Y N (ḥīn)

Study of Ḥ Y N

In the Qur’an, ḥīn denotes a time or period that may be short or long, as context indicates.

Amad too can express duration, but with greater prominence given to limit and extent.

Al-Kahf 18:12 concerns more than an event occurring at some ḥīn: it concerns the duration of a stay and its reckoning.

Amad is therefore more precise here than a general “time.”

This does not mean that every use of ḥīn lacks a limit or endpoint.

A M D (amad) and D H R (dahr)

Study of D H R

Dahr concerns time and the extent of the ages, and can denote a long period.

Amad may also be long, but does not itself name the whole course of time.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, al-amadu accompanies ṭāla. The lengthening of that period relates to the condition of the group under discussion.

The word does not mean the entire history of the world or the endlessness of time.

“A long age” may explain some sentences appropriately, but it is not amad’s basic definition.

A M D (amad) and A M L (amal)

Study of A M L

Amal concerns human hope and expectation about the future.

Amad concerns a duration, interval, or limit.

The words are very similar in writing, but belong to two independent roots.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:3, al-amalu occurs in the context of people being distracted by hopes.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, however, al-amadu concerns a long period.

Fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu al-amadu should therefore not be changed without justification into “their hopes grew long.”

People may develop far-reaching hopes during their lives, but this psychological observation is not amad’s lexical meaning.

A M D (amad) and M D D (amadda)

Study of M D D

This distinction is particularly important for root analysis.

Amad, with one mīm and one dāl, belongs to A M D.

Amadda, “supplied, increased, or supported,” however, is formed from the doubled root M D D.

The initial hamzah of amadda marks Form IV. It is not the root consonant of A M D.

Āl ʿImrān 3:124 and Āl ʿImrān 3:125 use forms of this verb in the context of providing assistance.

Elsewhere, numiddu and amdadnā belong to the same M D D family.

Amad, “limit” or “duration,” must therefore be distinguished from imdād, “providing assistance.”

Similarity of appearance does not prove that two forms share a root.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

English equivalents for A M D should be selected according to context.

“Limit,” “endpoint,” “duration,” “interval,” “distance,” and “term” are close equivalents, but none is equally suitable for every verse.

Āl ʿImrān 3:30

For amadan baʿīdan, the proposed translation is:

“A vast distance”

Within the sentence:

“They wish there were a vast distance between themselves and that evil deed.”

This preserves baynahā wa-baynahu, “between it and it,” and conveys the desire for remoteness without definitively specifying a temporal or spatial interval.

“A very long period” is also possible under a temporal reading. In a base translation, however, it may shift attention from confronting one’s evil deeds to the passage of time.

Al-Kahf 18:12

For amadan, the proposed equivalent is:

“Duration”

Within the sentence:

“Then We awakened them, to know which of the two groups had more accurately reckoned the duration of their stay.”

This accords with labithū and aḥṣā.

“Number” alone is insufficient: reckoning comes from the verse’s other verb, while amad is the duration being reckoned.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:16

For fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu al-amadu, the proposed translation is:

“A long period passed over them.”

This naturally conveys both duration and its lengthening.

“Their respite grew long” is possible in some readings, but may suggest a legal deadline or specified allowance that the verse does not explicitly name.

“A long period passed over them” is therefore more precise and introduces less interpretation.

Al-Jinn 72:25

For am yajʿalu lahu rabbī amadan, the proposed translation is:

“Or whether my Lord appoints a period for it.”

The explanation can clarify that the period may concern a more distant time before the promise is fulfilled.

Its length and date, however, cannot be derived from the word itself.

“An appointed term” is also possible, provided that appointment by The God is not confused with human knowledge of its timing.

10. Conclusion

In Arabic, A M D concerns the limit, goal, or endpoint toward which a course, distance, or period extends.

Early usage records amad for the end of a racecourse and, temporally, for the duration or limit of life.

These witnesses show that the word does more than name passing time. It also contains the concepts of goal and extent.

The Qur’an uses this root four times, all as the noun amad.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:30, a person wishes to be far from their evil deeds.

In Al-Kahf 18:12, the duration of the Companions of the Cave’s stay is an object of knowledge and reckoning.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, the lengthening of a period accompanies the hardening of the hearts of some earlier recipients of Scripture.

In Al-Jinn 72:25, the timing of a promised event is discussed, without the Prophet knowing whether it is near or distant.

Amad is therefore not restricted to past or future. It may express elapsed duration, a desired distance, or the temporal endpoint of an event.

Nor does amad inherently mean a long period. Remoteness and length come from accompanying adjectives and verbs.

The root overlaps with ajal, madā, abad, and zaman without being fully identical to any. In particular, amad, “end” or “duration,” must not be confused with amadda, “provided assistance,” from M D D.

The word’s direct meaning must remain distinct from contextual consequences. Regret on the Day of Reckoning, accuracy in counting time, the danger of hardened hearts, and uncertainty about a promise’s timing each belong to their respective verses. Throughout, amad’s lexical meaning remains centered on limit, interval, and extent.

Forqan’s final definition of A M D:

“A limit, goal, or endpoint reached by a distance or extent; temporally, a duration or interval measured relative to a beginning, an end, or an anticipated point. In the Qur’an, amad may express a desired distance, an elapsed duration, or the unknown timing of an event’s fulfillment. Shortness, length, and whether it is known or unknown are established by context and accompanying words, rather than by the root alone.”
