Neighborhood, refuge, and protection; deviation from a path and injustice

Arabic ج و ر has several important branches. Jār names a neighbor living near someone; jiwār can mean neighborhood or the refuge and protection someone receives; ajāra concerns sheltering and protecting. Jāra–yajūru, by contrast, means deviating from the right path and, in ethical or legal settings, acting unjustly and departing from justice.

The Qur’an itself exhibits this breadth: neighbors’ rights, adjoining land, seeking refuge and providing safety, and a way deviating from the straight course.

The root occurs in 13 words across ten verses, in six groups: jār, jāʾir, mutajāwirāt, the verb yujāwiru, forms of ajāra, and istajāra.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: thirteen occurrences in six groups

The family must not be reduced to injustice, nor neighborhood and protection treated simply as forms of deviation. Sharing a root does not place all its branches in every word. Each form must be understood through its structure and context.

1. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • جَارَ ـ يَجُورُ: departed from the straight way; acted unjustly in judgment or conduct.
  • جَوْر: deviation, leaving the correct course, and injustice opposed to justice.
  • جَائِر: deviating from the right way; unjust where human conduct or judgment is discussed.
  • جَار: neighbor; also a protector or someone under another’s protection.
  • جَارَة: a female neighbor; in some literary uses, a woman or wife.
  • جِيرَان، جِيرَة، أَجْوَار: recorded plurals for neighbors.
  • جِوَار: neighborhood and adjacency; in another use, refuge and protection.
  • جَاوَرَ ـ يُجَاوِرُ: lived or stayed beside someone as a neighbor.
  • مُجَاوَرَة: neighborhood and living alongside another.
  • تَجَاوَرَ ـ يَتَجَاوَرُ: were situated alongside one another or became neighbors.
  • مُتَجَاوِر: adjoining, situated alongside another.
  • أَجَارَ ـ يُجِيرُ: granted refuge, protected, preserved from danger or harm.
  • إِجَارَة: granting refuge and establishing protection and security in this semantic family.
  • اسْتَجَارَ ـ يَسْتَجِيرُ: requested refuge and protection.
  • مُسْتَجِير: someone seeking refuge and requesting safety.
  • مُجِير: someone granting refuge and protection.
  • جَوَّرَ: attributed injustice to someone; in other uses, threw down or overturned.

The Qur’an has jār three times, jāʾir once, mutajāwirāt once, yujāwirūnaka once, istajāra once, and forms of ajāra six times.

These must not be confused with similar words from other roots. Al-jawāri describing ships or moving celestial bodies belongs to ج ر ي. Ajr, wages or reward, belongs to أ ج ر and differs from ajirhu, “grant him protection.”

2. Semantic cores and early Arabic usage

Deviating from the straight course

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris gives the root’s core as الْمَيْلُ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ: turning or departing from the way.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab also contrast jawr with qaṣd. Here qaṣd concerns a straight course and proceeding correctly; jawr departs from it.

جَارَ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ describes someone deviating from a route. Its immediate subject is movement or a path, not necessarily ethical wrongdoing.

Transferred to human judgment and behavior, جَارَ فِي حُكْمِهِ means acting unjustly in judgment.

The semantic relationship is clear: an unjust judge departs from justice and right. Ethical and legal Arabic therefore contrasts jawr with ʿadl.

This explains the relationship between these uses; it does not establish that every other branch, including neighborhood, historically arose from the same image of deviation.

Lexical sources: departure from a route and injustice

An early poetic witness to deviation

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes a verse attributed to ʿAmr ibn ʿAjlān:

وَقُولَا لَهَا لَيْسَ الطَّرِيقُ أَجَارَنَا
وَلَكِنَّنَا جُرْنَا لِنَلْقَاكُمْ عَمْدًا

wa-qūlā lahā laysa al-ṭarīqu ajāranā / wa-lākinnanā jurnā li-nalqākum ʿamdan

“Tell her: the road did not lead us astray; we ourselves deliberately turned aside to meet you.”

Jurnā here means departing from the route: a deliberate change of course to reach another destination, rather than oppression. Ajāranā in this witness means “caused us to turn aside,” not granting refuge; Form IV alone does not settle the verb’s particular sense.

Lisān: the ʿAmr ibn ʿAjlān witness and ajāranā as causing deviation

The witness establishes the use of physical deviation in Arabic independently of legal injustice.

Attribution in a dictionary alone nevertheless does not establish the composition’s precise date. It is a transmitted witness in Arabic linguistic and poetic tradition, not secure evidence for a particular pre-Qur’anic date.

Neighborhood and being alongside another

Another branch is jār, neighbor.

Jār describes someone living near another’s dwelling. Jāwarahu means living or staying beside them; tajāwarū describes people situated alongside each other.

This is not confined to human life: land parcels, houses, or regions can also be mutajāwir, adjoining.

Proximity and adjacency matter here, rather than leaving a route, injustice, or seeking refuge.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn describes jār as someone neighboring your dwelling, and also includes someone asking to come under your protection.

Neighborhood can thus relate to protection and social responsibility as well as spatial proximity.

Neighbor and seeker of protection

In early Arabian social structures, jiwār could extend beyond nearby houses. Someone under the protection of a person or group could take refuge with them.

Dictionaries record jār not only for a neighbor but also an ally, a protected person, and someone whose security another undertakes.

The same field includes ajāra, granting protection, and istajāra, requesting it.

Someone saying اسْتَجَرْتُ بِفُلَانٍ meant that they had taken refuge with someone and sought their protection.

The relationship is more than physical approach: protection against danger is expected.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah transmits a report about tribal protection in which an influential person’s grant of refuge could bind their group. It helps explain social jiwār, but its details must not be generalized to every tribe and period without historical examination.

Lisān and Tahdhīb: protective uses of jiwār

A mujīr provides protection; a mustajīr requests it.

The Qur’an displays this relationship particularly clearly in Al-Tawbah 9:6.

A literary witness to neighborhood

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ attributes the following verse to Al-Aʿshā:

أَجَارَتَنَا بِينِي فَإِنَّكِ طَالِقَهْ
كَذَاكَ أُمُورُ النَّاسِ غَادٍ وَطَارِقَهْ

a-jāratanā bīnī fa-innaki ṭāliqah / kadhāka umūru al-nāsi ghādin wa-ṭāriqah

“Our neighbor, depart from me, for you are divorced; such are people’s affairs—some arrive in the morning and others by night.”

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and jārah as wife

Jārah here describes a woman whose relationship to the speaker extends beyond ordinary neighborhood.

Lexical sources also record jārah for a wife or woman. Adjacency could thus extend to close family relationships in Arabic usage.

This literary sense must not be transferred to al-jār in Al-Nisāʾ 4:36, whose context concerns rights and kindness toward different groups.

Can every branch be traced to one historical meaning?

Neighborhood and protection have a comprehensible relationship: someone alongside another may receive their protection and security.

Deviation and injustice also relate clearly: injustice can depart from the way of justice.

The historical relationship between these two groups is less clear.

Dictionary grouping under ج و ر does not prove that injustice and neighborhood necessarily began from one historical image.

Ibn Fāris emphasizes departure from a route, while Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab also independently report neighborhood and sheltering.

Qur’anic research is more precise when it preserves both attested branches rather than inventing an unsupported etymological story.

3. Qur’anic neighborhood

Al-Nisāʾ 4:36: the near and distant neighbor

Verse text:

وَاعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُشْرِكُوا بِهِ شَيْئًا وَبِالْوَالِدَيْنِ إِحْسَانًا وَبِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْجَارِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْجَارِ الْجُنُبِ وَالصَّاحِبِ بِالْجَنْبِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ وَمَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ مَنْ كَانَ مُخْتَالًا فَخُورًا

wa-uʿbudū Allāha wa-lā tushrikū bihi shayʾan wa-bi-al-wālidayni iḥsānan wa-bi-dhī al-qurbā wa-al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīni wa-al-jāri dhī al-qurbā wa-al-jāri al-junubi wa-al-ṣāḥibi bi-al-janbi wa-ibni al-sabīli wa-mā malakat aymānukum inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu man kāna mukhtālan fakhūran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Worship The God and associate nothing with Him. Show kindness to parents, relatives, orphans, those in need, the near and distant neighbor, the companion beside you, the stranded traveler, and those your right hands possess. God indeed does not love anyone self-admiring and boastful.”

Al-jār appears twice: الْجَارِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ and الْجَارِ الْجُنُبِ.

Both mean neighbor, while the descriptions following them distinguish relationships of neighborhood.

Dhī al-qurbā concerns nearness, which may be kinship or spatial proximity. Al-junub from ج ن ب describes someone to another side or more distant in relationship.

Some lexical and interpretive reports explain the first as a related neighbor and the second as an unrelated or unfamiliar neighbor. Another explanation concerns the dwellings’ proximity and distance.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings of the near and distant neighbor

Both relate to the words’ semantic fields. What is clear in the verse is that kindness is not confined to one kind of neighbor.

After parents, relatives, orphans, and those in need, neighbors join the recipients of good treatment.

Al-jār has no stated religious qualification. The verse does not restrict kindness to a neighbor sharing one’s beliefs.

Its meaning must not be restricted to a religious or ethnic group through an added condition absent from the expression.

Al-jāri al-junubi also differs from al-ṣāḥibi bi-al-janbi. They occur together, but are not identical.

Jār expresses neighborhood; ṣāḥib bi-al-janb concerns companionship and being beside another. This may occur in travel, work, or other situations.

Both adjacency and companionship ground ethical responsibility here, while retaining their particular names and relationships.

Al-Raʿd 13:4: adjoining land

Verse text:

وَفِي الْأَرْضِ قِطَعٌ مُتَجَاوِرَاتٌ وَجَنَّاتٌ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَزَرْعٌ وَنَخِيلٌ صِنْوَانٌ وَغَيْرُ صِنْوَانٍ يُسْقَىٰ بِمَاءٍ وَاحِدٍ وَنُفَضِّلُ بَعْضَهَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي الْأُكُلِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-fī al-arḍi qiṭaʿun mutajāwirātun wa-jannātun min aʿnābin wa-zarʿun wa-nakhīlun ṣinwānun wa-ghayru ṣinwānin yusqā bi-māʾin wāḥidin wa-nufaḍḍilu baʿḍahā ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-ukuli inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yaʿqilūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“In the earth are parcels alongside one another, gardens of grapes, crops, and palms sharing roots and not sharing roots, watered with one water. We favor some over others in their produce and food. These indeed contain signs for people who reason.”

Mutajāwirāt is the feminine plural active participle of Form VI tajāwara.

قِطَعٌ مُتَجَاوِرَاتٌ concerns parcels of land situated alongside each other.

The root here concerns location and neighboring land, not injustice or justice.

The verse names grape gardens, crops, and palms, noting different yields despite watering with one water.

Mutajāwirāt establishes adjacency alone. It does not establish identical soil composition, sunlight, slope, minerals, or every other natural condition.

Being neighbors does not mean sharing every feature.

Al-Ṭabarī: neighboring land with different properties

Spatial proximity accompanies varied yield. The image draws attention to natural differences within nearby environments, without presenting one scientific theory for every difference.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60: لَا يُجَاوِرُونَكَ

Verse text:

لَئِنْ لَمْ يَنْتَهِ الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ وَالْمُرْجِفُونَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ لَنُغْرِيَنَّكَ بِهِمْ ثُمَّ لَا يُجَاوِرُونَكَ فِيهَا إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

la-in lam yantahi al-munāfiqūna wa-alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun wa-al-murjifūna fī al-madīnati la-nughriyannaka bihim thumma lā yujāwirūnaka fīhā illā qalīlan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“If the hypocrites, those whose hearts contain disease, and those spreading alarm in the city do not desist, We will certainly stir you against them. Then they will remain your neighbors there only a little.”

Yujāwirūnaka is a Form III imperfect verb, expressing neighboring or living alongside the addressee.

The addressee is Muḥammad; fīhā refers to the city.

The sentence concerns the named groups’ continuing presence and residence in that city.

The verb means remaining your neighbors or residing beside you, rather than protecting or oppressing you.

Context directs a serious threat toward groups whose conduct the verse specifies. The verb alone does not establish details of response, punishment, or a universal ruling about everyone holding a different belief.

It expresses residence and adjacency; the response’s subject and limits require the whole context and other verses.

4. Qur’anic refuge and protection

Al-Tawbah 9:6: an associationist seeking protection

Verse text:

وَإِنْ أَحَدٌ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ اسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ حَتَّىٰ يَسْمَعَ كَلَامَ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ أَبْلِغْهُ مَأْمَنَهُ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-in aḥadun mina al-mushrikīna istajāraka fa-ajirhu ḥattā yasmaʿa kalāma Allāhi thumma ablighhu maʾmanahu dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yaʿlamūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“If one of the associationists seeks your protection, grant it so that he may hear God’s speech. Then convey him to his place of safety. That is because they are people who do not know.”

This is a particularly clear use of the protection branch.

Two derivatives occur together: istajāraka and fa-ajirhu.

Istajāraka is a Form X perfect verb. Its agent requests refuge and protection.

Fa-ajirhu is an imperative of ajāra: grant that person protection.

One person requests safety, and the other is commanded to shelter them.

The context concerns associationists, covenants, and conflict. This is therefore more than ordinary hospitality: it concerns personal safety amid danger.

The continuation is decisive: ثُمَّ أَبْلِغْهُ مَأْمَنَهُ.

After hearing God’s speech, the person must be conveyed to their place of safety.

The verse does not condition safe passage upon accepting faith. The person may listen and retain their previous belief; the command to convey them to safety remains.

Al-Ṭabarī: the protection seeker and safe passage

Ijārah here is more than admission or permission to hear a message. Protection must continue until reaching a safe location.

Maʾman belongs to أ م ن and clarifies safety; istajāra and ajāra express requesting refuge and providing protection.

The paired families clarify protection’s practical meaning.

Al-Anfāl 8:48: Satan’s claim of protection

Verse text:

وَإِذْ زَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ وَقَالَ لَا غَالِبَ لَكُمُ الْيَوْمَ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَإِنِّي جَارٌ لَكُمْ فَلَمَّا تَرَاءَتِ الْفِئَتَانِ نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ وَقَالَ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِنْكُمْ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ مَا لَا تَرَوْنَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

wa-idh zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu aʿmālahum wa-qāla lā ghāliba lakumu al-yawma mina al-nāsi wa-innī jārun lakum fa-lammā tarāʾati al-fiʾatāni nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi wa-qāla innī barīʾun minkum innī arā mā lā tarawna innī akhāfu Allāha wa-Allāhu shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When Satan made their deeds attractive to them, saying: No one among people will defeat you today, and I am your protector. When the two groups saw one another, he turned upon his heels, saying: I disown you. I see what you do not see; I fear God, and God is severe in punishment.”

Jārun lakum is quoted as Satan’s speech.

Here jār means supporter and protector, rather than the inhabitant of a neighboring house.

Satan promises that his audience will not be defeated and that he will support them. When confrontation appears, he retreats and disowns them.

Jār demonstrates a protective meaning, not the truth of Satan’s claim.

Meaning and truth must be distinguished. The Qur’an reports a deceptive promise; reporting does not endorse it.

إِنِّي جَارٌ لَكُمْ shows that someone can call themselves another’s jār without spatial neighborhood being at issue.

The witness establishes the word’s wider social and protective uses.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:88: يُجِيرُ وَلَا يُجَارُ عَلَيْهِ

Verse text:

قُلْ مَنْ بِيَدِهِ مَلَكُوتُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ وَهُوَ يُجِيرُ وَلَا يُجَارُ عَلَيْهِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

qul man bi-yadihi malakūtu kulli shayʾin wa-huwa yujīru wa-lā yujāru ʿalayhi in kuntum taʿlamūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Say: In whose hand is the dominion of everything—He who grants protection, against whom no one can be protected—if you know?”

Two forms of ajāra occur together.

Yujīru is active: grants refuge and protection.

Yujāru is passive. Lā yujāru ʿalayhi concerns the impossibility of protection against God’s power.

The contrast is that God can protect, while no power can shield someone against His judgment and might.

It does not mean that God needs another’s protection but nobody grants it. The subject is effective protection and its absence against God.

Al-Ṭabarī: protecting and the absence of protection against Him

Malakūtu kulli shayʾ reinforces this: universal dominion and others’ inability to oppose it.

Ijārah extends beyond protection between people into describing divine power, without confining divine protection to a material mechanism or human contract.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:31: refuge from punishment

Verse text:

يَا قَوْمَنَا أَجِيبُوا دَاعِيَ اللَّهِ وَآمِنُوا بِهِ يَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ مِنْ ذُنُوبِكُمْ وَيُجِرْكُمْ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

yā qawmanā ajībū dāʿiya Allāhi wa-āminū bihi yaghfir lakum min dhunūbikum wa-yujirkum min ʿadhābin alīmin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Our people, answer God’s caller and believe in him, so that He may forgive some of your sins and protect you from painful punishment.”

Yujirkum from ajāra means preserving and sheltering from punishment.

Min in مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ identifies what the person is protected from.

Yaghfir and yujirkum occur together: forgiving sins and protecting against punishment.

Related though they are, they are not the same words. Forgiveness is stated separately from the consequence of protection against punishment.

Al-Mulk 67:28: who can protect those who deny?

Verse text:

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَهْلَكَنِيَ اللَّهُ وَمَنْ مَعِيَ أَوْ رَحِمَنَا فَمَنْ يُجِيرُ الْكَافِرِينَ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

qul a-raʾaytum in ahlakaniya Allāhu wa-man maʿiya aw raḥimanā fa-man yujīru al-kāfirīna min ʿadhābin alīmin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Say: Have you considered—if God destroys me and those with me, or shows us mercy—who will protect those who deny from painful punishment?”

Yujīru concerns the ability to protect against punishment.

The question turns attention from the Messenger and his companions’ fate to the audience’s own deliverance.

Min ʿadhābin alīm identifies the danger against which protection is at issue.

As in Al-Muʾminūn 23:88, effective protective power is prominent.

Al-Jinn 72:22: nobody protects against God

Verse text:

قُلْ إِنِّي لَنْ يُجِيرَنِي مِنَ اللَّهِ أَحَدٌ وَلَنْ أَجِدَ مِنْ دُونِهِ مُلْتَحَدًا

qul innī lan yujīranī mina Allāhi aḥadun wa-lan ajida min dūnihi multaḥadan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Say: No one will ever protect me against God, and I will never find a refuge apart from Him.”

Yujīranī means sheltering and protecting the person.

Muḥammad is commanded to declare that nobody can protect him against God.

Multaḥadan belongs to another root and here concerns a refuge or place to turn and escape.

The paired expressions clarify protector and refuge.

Even regarding the Messenger himself, reliance upon independent power against God is denied.

This fits Al-Muʾminūn 23:88: God protects, while nobody supplies independent, effective refuge against Him.

5. Deviation and injustice in the Qur’an

Al-Naḥl 16:9: the straight and deviating ways

Verse text:

وَعَلَى اللَّهِ قَصْدُ السَّبِيلِ وَمِنْهَا جَائِرٌ وَلَوْ شَاءَ لَهَدَاكُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

wa-ʿalā Allāhi qaṣdu al-sabīli wa-minhā jāʾirun wa-law shāʾa la-hadākum ajmaʿīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Upon God rests showing the straight way; among the ways is one that deviates. Had He willed, He would have guided you all.”

Jāʾir is an active participle of jāra–yajūru.

Here it means deviating and leaving the correct course.

Qaṣdu al-sabīl contrasts with jāʾir. Qaṣd concerns the way’s straightness and correctness; jāʾir describes departure from that course.

This matches early jawr as departure from a route.

The pronoun hā in wa-minhā jāʾir refers to al-sabīl. Arabic sabīl can be feminine and can here carry a generic or collective sense of ways.

The verse contrasts ways in terms of guidance and deviation.

Grammatically, jāʾir describes a way, rather than naming an ethnic or religious group.

Some early interpretations apply the deviating way to particular religions or groups. The word jāʾir itself contains no ethnic, religious, or sectarian name.

Al-Ṭabarī: the right course and deviation

The direct meaning is departure from the correct way. Identifying a particular belief, conduct, or course as an instance requires clear Qur’anic criteria rather than simply labeling others.

Jāʾir and ẓālim can overlap ethically, but here jāʾir first belongs to the image of a route.

“An oppressive road” is consequently unnatural. “Deviating” or “crooked way” better expresses the construction.

This matches the distinction between جَارَ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ and جَارَ عَلَيْهِ فِي الْحُكْمِ: leaving a route versus unjust judgment.

Injustice as departure from justice

Although jawr means injustice in Arabic, Qur’anic jāʾir appears only in Al-Naḥl 16:9 and describes a deviating way.

The noun jawr, injustice, does not itself occur in the Qur’an.

This does not erase its established Arabic meaning; it distinguishes lexical attestation from actual Qur’anic use.

Dictionaries record jāra ʿalayhi for wronging someone and jāra fī al-ḥukm for unjust judgment.

The person departs from right and justice.

The Qur’an also discusses injustice through other families, including ظ ل م, ب غ ي, and ع د ل, each requiring its own study.

Not every Qur’anic instance of injustice is an occurrence of ج و ر without its derivative or specific evidence.

6. Comparing related roots

ج و ر (جَوْر) and ظ ل م (ظُلْم): departure from justice and wrongdoing

Study of Ẓ L M

Ethical jawr concerns departing from justice and acting unfairly.

Qur’anic ẓulm has a broad field: wronging others or oneself, associationism, and other forms of wrong.

The words can be synonymous in some uses without necessarily sharing the same primary image.

Jawr emphasizes leaving the correct course; the full field of ẓulm requires independent examination of ظ ل م.

ج و ر (جَائِر) and ق ص د (قَصْد): crooked and straight courses

Al-Naḥl 16:9 brings both families together.

Qaṣd concerns straightness here; jāʾir describes a way departing from it.

Their contrast is especially clear evidence for Qur’anic jāʾir.

Qaṣd does not always mean a straight road in Arabic. Persian “qaṣd kardan” can similarly mean intending or setting out to do something.

The contrast must remain contextual.

ج و ر (جَوْر) and م ي ل (مَيْل): deviation and inclination

Mayl concerns inclining from one direction toward another and need not always be negative.

Jawr regarding a correct route and justice generally describes undesirable deviation.

Every jawr may be an inclination away from the right course, but not every mayl is oppression or ethical deviation.

ج و ر (جَار) and ق ر ب (قُرْب): neighborhood and nearness

Study of Q R B

Qurb broadly means nearness: spatial, temporal, familial, or spiritual.

Jār ordinarily identifies neighborhood.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:36 combines the fields in al-jāri dhī al-qurbā.

Someone can be a neighbor and also have an additional kind of proximity.

Jār and qarīb are therefore not complete synonyms.

ج و ر (إِجَارَة) and أ م ن (أَمْن): protection and safety

Study of ʾ M N

Amn concerns safety and the absence of fear or danger.

Ajāra concerns granting refuge and protection.

Al-Tawbah 9:6 pairs the fields: sheltering first, then conveying the person to a maʾman.

Maʾman is a safe place; ajirhu is the act of protecting.

Protection can enable safety, while the words remain different.

ج و ر (اسْتَجَارَ) and ع و ذ (عَاذَ): seeking protection and taking refuge

Study of ʿ W DH

ʿĀdha also concerns taking refuge and seeking protection.

Istajāra and istaʿādha can be close in Arabic usage.

ج و ر also includes neighborhood and social protection; ع و ذ focuses more on taking refuge and being sheltered from something.

This distinction must not become absolute. Danger, protector, and context determine the precise meaning in both families.

ج و ر (جَار) and ج ر ي (جَارٍ): neighbor and flowing

Study of J R Y

Confusing ج و ر with ج ر ي is a possible error in reading Qur’anic words.

Al-jawāri describing moving ships, and al-jawāri al-kunnasi in Al-Takwīr 81:16, belong to ج ر ي.

Al-jār in Al-Nisāʾ 4:36, however, means neighbor and belongs to ج و ر.

Similar forms and changes or omissions of weak letters must not merge the families.

ج و ر (أَجَارَ) and أ ج ر (أَجْر): protection and wages

Study of ʾ J R

Without vowel marks, ajirhu may be confused with أ ج ر.

Fa-ajirhu in Al-Tawbah 9:6, however, comes from ajāra and concerns protection.

Ajr means wages and reward, from independent أ ج ر.

Granting protection here must not be equated with paying wages, employment, or a lease.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For al-jār in Al-Nisāʾ 4:36, “neighbor” is appropriate: “the near neighbor” for dhī al-qurbā and “the distant neighbor” for al-junub. Lexical explanation should clarify that proximity and distance may also concern kinship or the relationship’s closeness.

For mutajāwirāt in Al-Raʿd 13:4, “alongside one another” or “adjoining” fits. “Land attached without gaps” is too restrictive: adjacency need not mean uninterrupted contact.

For yujāwirūnaka in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60, “remain your neighbors” or “reside beside you” preserves the verb.

For istajāraka in Al-Tawbah 9:6, “seeks your protection” is direct and natural; fa-ajirhu means “grant him protection.”

For innī jārun lakum in Al-Anfāl 8:48, “I am your protector” better expresses the contextual meaning than “I am your neighbor.”

For yujīru in Al-Muʾminūn 23:88 and other divine-protection passages, “grants refuge” or “protects” is appropriate.

For lā yujāru ʿalayhi, “against Him no one can be protected” preserves the passive construction and contrast with yujīru.

For jāʾir in Al-Naḥl 16:9, “deviating” or “crooked” is preferable to “oppressive,” since the word describes a way.

For non-Qur’anic jawr meaning injustice, “oppression,” “unfairness,” or “departure from justice” is natural. This must not be transferred to every family member.

8. Conclusion

Arabic ج و ر contains several attested branches. Brief root meaning: Neighborhood and adjacency; granting refuge and protection; jawr concerns departure from a right course or justice.

Jāra–yajūru concerns leaving the right course and, ethically or legally, injustice. Jār means neighbor and can socially mean supporter or protector. Ajāra grants protection; istajāra requests it.

The Qur’an applies the root to human relationships, adjacent land, refuge and protection, and a deviating way.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:36 associates neighborhood with kindness; Al-Raʿd 13:4 makes adjoining land a natural sign; Al-Tawbah 9:6 answers protection sought with shelter and safe passage. In Al-Anfāl 8:48, Satan claims to protect but abandons his companions during confrontation.

In passages about divine power, God protects, while no power can provide independent refuge against Him.

In Al-Naḥl 16:9, jāʾir describes a way departing from the correct course.

Consequently, ج و ر has no single fixed equivalent such as injustice or neighborhood. Its branches must be preserved, with form and context governing each translation.

The family demonstrates varied human and spatial relationships and distinguishes protection, safety, and deviation. A root begins the understanding of a word; the sentence and context establish each occurrence’s final meaning.
