A clear and evident path, a way of proceeding, and other uses in Arabic and the Qur’an

In Arabic, N H J is best known for a clear, evident path whose course can be recognized. Hence نَهْج (nahj), مَنْهَج (manhaj), and مِنْهَاج (minhāj) denote a path and a way of proceeding along it. This usage extends beyond a physical route to the course a person follows in life, conduct, or work.

However, the root has more than one semantic branch. Early lexicons also record breathlessness, exhaustion, and wearing out. This branch should neither be ignored nor derived from the clear-path sense without sufficient evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as مِنْهَاجًا (minhājan) in Al-Māʾidah 5:48. This occurrence belongs to the clear-path branch.

1. Important forms and derivatives

Different forms in this Arabic family have related or distinct meanings.

نَهْج (nahj): a clear, evident path whose course can be recognized. It is also recorded for a straight path.

نَهْج (nahj): an adjective describing a clear and evident path, as in طَرِيقٌ نَهْجٌ (ṭarīqun nahjun).

مَنْهَج (manhaj): a clear path or a way followed to reach an objective.

مِنْهَاج (minhāj): a clear, identifiable path, close to manhaj in lexical meaning.

مَنَاهِج (manāhij): a plural of manhaj and minhāj in lexical usage.

نَهَجَ الطَّرِيقَ (nahaja al-ṭarīqa): he followed the path; in another use, he made the path clear.

أَنْهَجَ الطَّرِيقُ (anhaja al-ṭarīqu): the path became clear and evident.

اسْتَنْهَجَ (istanhaja): recorded for a path becoming clear, or for following another person’s path or manner.

نَاهِج (nāhij): depending on usage, a description of a clear path or of a panting person or animal.

نَهَجٌ (nahaj) and نَهْجَة (nahjah): in the other branch, breathlessness and repeated breathing, especially from fatigue or exertion.

أَنْهَجَ الثَّوْبُ (anhaja al-thawbu): the garment became old or showed wear.

Only minhājan occurs in the Qur’an. Nahj, manhaj, manāhij, and the family’s verbs, despite being familiar in Arabic, do not occur as separate forms in the Qur’anic text.

2. Semantic cores in early Arabic

A clear, recognizable path

One lexical analysis identifies the first branch through nahj, “path,” and explains نَهَجَ لِي الْأَمْرَ (nahaja lī al-amra) as making a matter clear.

A concise definition of this use is:

النَّهْجُ: الطَّرِيقُ الْوَاضِحُ

al-nahju: al-ṭarīqu al-wāḍiḥu

That is, “Nahj is a clear, evident path.”

طريق نهج (ṭarīq nahj) denotes a clear, evident path. نهجت الطريق (nahajtu al-ṭarīqa) is recorded both for making a path clear and for following it.

Making a path clear and following it are distinguishable uses. Someone may clarify a route for another person or follow a route they know. Each verb must be understood through its construction and context.

The documented physical image is a path whose course is apparent and recognizable. Where a route is visible on the ground, a traveler can distinguish it from the surrounding land or other routes.

Three ideas should nevertheless be distinguished: a path being clear, being straight, and leading to a right destination.

Some lexical records also use nahj for a straight path. However, visibility alone does not establish that every clear path is morally or religiously right. Context supplies that assessment.

From a physical route to a way of acting

In Arabic, a path is not restricted to the movement of feet. A person can also follow a course in work or conduct.

For example, early Arabic records اعْمَلْ عَلَى مَا نَهَجْتُهُ لَكَ (iʿmal ʿalā mā nahajtuhu laka): “Act according to what I have made clear to you.”

No route on the ground is required here. The point is that a way of doing something has been clarified.

This extension explains why manhaj and minhāj can be used in conduct, religion, scholarship, and education.

However, the modern technical meaning of manhaj as an organized set of methods, research rules, experimental stages, and scientific criteria is not wholly contained in its early lexical meaning. These are developed, specialized uses.

Modern usage alone therefore does not justify translating Qur’anic minhāj as a detailed, staged program.

The second branch: breathlessness and wearing out

One lexical analysis identifies two distinct bases for N H J:

أَصْلَانِ مُتَبَايِنَانِ

aṣlāni mutabāyināni

“Two distinct bases.”

The first concerns a path; the second is described as الِانْقِطَاع (al-inqiṭāʿ), interruption or giving out.

An example is أَتَانَا فُلَانٌ يَنْهَجُ (atānā fulānun yanhaju): someone came to us breathless, breathing with difficulty.

Nahaj and associated vocalizations and constructions are recorded for breathlessness and repeated breathing. أَنْهَجْتُ الدَّابَّةَ (anhajtu al-dābbata) concerns exhausting an animal by riding or driving it excessively.

The family also includes clothing wearing out, as in أَنْهَجَ الثَّوْبُ (anhaja al-thawbu). The simple verb’s vocalization is disputed: some lexical accounts reject نَهَجَ (nahaja), with a, for a garment and prefer نَهِجَ (nahija), with i. This is not simply a preference for anhaja over the simple verb.

The disagreement shows that not all forms were equally accepted in lexical accounts.

Breathlessness and a garment wearing out may be connected through semantic analysis, but such a resemblance does not establish one historical origin for every branch.

More particularly, there is no basis for importing breathlessness or wearing out into Qur’anic minhāj.

3. Early Arabic and pre-Islamic poetic evidence

An evident path in poetry attributed to Yazīd ibn Khadhdhāq

A verse attributed to Yazīd ibn Khadhdhāq al-ʿAbdī, a pre-Islamic poet of ʿAbd al-Qays, reads:

وَلَقَدْ أَضَاءَ لَكَ الطَّرِيقُ وَأَنْهَجَتْ
سُبُلُ الْمَكَارِمِ وَالْهُدَى تُعْدِي

wa-laqad aḍāʾa laka al-ṭarīqu wa-anhajat / subulu al-makārimi wa-al-hudā tuʿdī

“The path has become bright for you, the ways of noble conduct have become clear, and guidance gives support.”

Here أَنْهَجَتْ (anhajat) concerns routes becoming clear. However, سُبُلُ الْمَكَارِمِ (subulu al-makārim), “ways of noble conduct,” are not physical tracks on the ground; they concern honorable behavior.

The example matters because ethical and behavioral uses of “path” are not solely products of modern technical developments.

The verse occurs in early transmissions, but its wording varies. Some versions have سُبُلُ الْمَسَالِكِ (subulu al-masālik) in place of subulu al-makārim, with variations in the ending too. The wording above is one transmitted version; the variants should not be disregarded.

Literary accounts place the poet in the pre-Islamic period. However, the attribution and wording of an individual verse must be considered separately from the poet’s lifetime.

The physical path in poetry of Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī

A verse attributed to Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī reads:

فَأَجَزْتُهُ بِأَفَلَّ تَحْسَبُ أَثَرَهُ
نَهْجًا أَبَانَ بِذِي فَرِيغٍ مَخْرَفِ

fa-ajaztuhu bi-afalla taḥsabu atharahu / nahjan abāna bi-dhī farīghin makhrafi

“I crossed that expanse with a chipped sword whose blade-pattern you would take for a clear path, a visible track in well-trodden ground.”

This image concerns a chipped sword and the clear pattern on its blade. أَثَرَهُ (atharahu) denotes the sword’s pattern or watered markings, rather than a footprint or a mark left on the ground by someone passing. The poet compares the pattern’s visibility to an evident path.

نَهْجًا (nahjan) denotes a recognizable, visible path. The physical path is the image to which the sword’s pattern is compared. The image is consistent with the root’s clear-path range; atharahu should not be interpreted as a traveler’s footprint.

Literary accounts identify Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī as a pre-Islamic poet. As with other attributed poetry, each verse’s reliability must be considered separately.

Another witness of uncertain attribution

An early explanation of minhāj quotes the following verse without identifying its poet in that account:

مَنْ يَكُ فِي شَكٍّ فَهَذَا فَلْجُ
مَاءٌ رَوَاءٌ وَطَرِيقٌ نَهْجُ

man yaku fī shakkin fa-hādhā falju / māʾun rawāʾun wa-ṭarīqun nahju

“Whoever doubts, here is Falj: sweet, thirst-quenching water and a clear path.”

Here طَرِيقٌ نَهْجٌ (ṭarīqun nahjun) denotes an evident path. The verse helps establish Arabic usage, but the date and attribution are not clear in this transmission, so it cannot be treated as securely pre-Islamic evidence.

Together, these witnesses provide lexical support for nahj as a clear path, both in physical descriptions and in expressions about conduct and ways of life.

4. Qur’anic مِنْهَاج (minhāj) in Al-Māʾidah 5:48

Al-Māʾidah 5:48

وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَمُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ ۖ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ بِمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ عَمَّا جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْحَقِّ ۚ لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا ۚ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ فِي مَا آتَاكُمْ ۖ فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ ۚ إِلَى اللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ جَمِيعًا فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

wa-anzalnā ilayka al-kitāba bi-al-ḥaqqi muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayhi mina al-kitābi wa-muhayminan ʿalayhi fa-uḥkum baynahum bi-mā anzala allāhu wa-lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾahum ʿammā jāʾaka mina al-ḥaqqi li-kullin jaʿalnā minkum shirʿatan wa-minhājan wa-law shāʾa allāhu la-jaʿalakum ummatan wāḥidatan wa-lākin li-yabluwakum fī mā ātākum fa-istabiqū al-khayrāti ilā allāhi marjiʿukum jamīʿan fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūna

“We sent down the Book to you in truth, confirming the scripture before it and guarding over it. So judge between them by what The God has sent down, and do not follow their desires away from the truth that has come to you. For each of you We have appointed a prescribed way and a clear path. Had The God willed, He would have made you one community; but He intends to test you in what He has given you. So compete in good works. To The God you will all return, and He will inform you about that over which you differed.”

The lexical meaning and structure of مِنْهَاجًا (minhājan)

Minhāj is a noun from N H J on the pattern مِفْعَال (mifʿāl). In the verse, minhājan is indefinite, in the accusative, and joined to شِرْعَةً (shirʿatan) by the conjunction وَ (wa-).

The initial mīm belongs to the word’s structure. It should not be confused with the preposition مِنْ (min).

The established lexical meaning of minhāj is a clear path. Here, discussion of divine scriptures, judgment, and communities extends the physical image to a clear course in religious life.

The pattern mifʿāl alone does not establish that this course must be a collection of detailed rules, fixed stages, or a predetermined program in the modern sense.

The relationship between شِرْعَة (shirʿah) and مِنْهَاج (minhāj)

Two related words occur together:

شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا

shirʿatan wa-minhājan

“A prescribed way and a clear path.”

Shirʿah comes from SH R ʿ and minhāj from N H J. Both concern a path, but their backgrounds and semantic ranges are not identical.

In Arabic, شريعة (sharīʿah) is also used for a route leading to water. The sense of an appointed course and ruling develops within this field.

Minhāj, by contrast, foregrounds the course being clear and evident.

An early explanation interprets both words as a clear way and course. Expressions such as سُنَّةً وَسَبِيلًا (sunnatan wa-sabīlan) and سَبِيلًا وَسُنَّةً (sabīlan wa-sunnatan) are also recorded. Their reversed order shows that a rigid, uniform distinction between the words was not established across all explanations.

Their combination can thus emphasize an appointed, clear course. However, the construction alone does not prove that shirʿah necessarily names one particular body of rules while minhāj names a separate source of law.

In particular, explaining minhāj as sunnah in some early accounts does not turn the word itself into a body of reports or rulings compiled in later centuries. That is an interpretive explanation, rather than a meaning established by the root alone.

Different paths and a shared destination

After shirʿatan wa-minhājan, the verse says:

وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً

wa-law shāʾa allāhu la-jaʿalakum ummatan wāḥidatan

“Had The God willed, He would have made you one community.”

The sequence indicates real differences among the addressees. The preceding verses discuss the Torah, the Gospel, and the scripture revealed to the Prophet. Reading the passage in relation to communities and the courses appointed for them therefore has clear textual support.

The passage does not conclude by inviting hostility over differences. It continues with the command:

فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ

fa-istabiqū al-khayrāti

“So compete in good works.”

It then returns final judgment over disagreements to The God.

The order matters. Differences of course are acknowledged within a framework of testing and responsibility. Yet this does not establish that every course people devise is necessarily approved by The God. Earlier in the verse, adherence to truth and what The God has revealed is emphasized.

Minhāj here therefore denotes a clear, appointed path. Its validity, precise contents, and relation to revelation require a broader inquiry than the lexical meaning of this one word.

5. Minhāj and related Qur’anic concepts

SH R ʿ (sharaʿa) and N H J (nahj)

Part of Al-Shūrā 42:13 reads:

شَرَعَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا وَصَّىٰ بِهِ نُوحًا وَالَّذِي أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ وَمَا وَصَّيْنَا بِهِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ أَنْ أَقِيمُوا الدِّينَ وَلَا تَتَفَرَّقُوا فِيهِ

sharaʿa lakum mina al-dīni mā waṣṣā bihi nūḥan wa-alladhī awḥaynā ilayka wa-mā waṣṣaynā bihi ibrāhīma wa-mūsā wa-ʿīsā an aqīmū al-dīna wa-lā tatafarraqū fīhi

“He has prescribed for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah, what We have revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: uphold the religion and do not divide within it.”

This passage emphasizes shared religious foundations among the prophets. Al-Māʾidah 5:48 also speaks of differences in appointed courses.

Reading the two together helps distinguish a shared religious call from differences in some courses and rulings concerning communities. Identifying every instance of agreement and difference requires a separate study of sharīʿah and dīn.

N S K (nusuk) and N H J (nahj)

Part of Al-Ḥajj 22:67 reads:

لِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ

li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan hum nāsikūhu

“For every community We have appointed rites that they perform.”

Its structure resembles Al-Māʾidah 5:48: something has been appointed for each community. However, مَنْسَك (mansak) and مِنْهَاج (minhāj) are distinct words.

Mansak belongs to the field of rites and acts of worship. Minhāj denotes a clear path and is not lexically restricted to rites.

The structural resemblance alone therefore does not establish that minhāj in Al-Māʾidah 5:48 means only worship practices or a collection of rites.

Study of N S K

S B L (sabīl), Ṭ R Q (ṭarīq), and N H J (nahj)

Sabīl and ṭarīq also denote a path and can occur in physical or figurative contexts.

Lexical definitions of nahj emphasize a path being clear and recognizable. “Path” is a general equivalent; “clear path” better preserves the feature emphasized in early Arabic.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary. In Arabic, ṭarīq and sabīl can also be qualified as clear or straight.

Study of S B L; Study of Ṭ R Q

Distinction from N H Y (nahy)

N H Y occurs in the Qur’an for forbidding, restraining, ceasing, and related ideas. Despite sharing the first two consonants, it is a different root from N H J.

N H R, which concerns a river and other uses, is likewise independent.

The visible similarity of these letters does not by itself establish a semantic or historical relationship.

6. Proposed translation for Forqan

“Clear path” is a natural and lexically accurate equivalent for minhāj.

For لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا (li-kullin jaʿalnā minkum shirʿatan wa-minhājan), the following rendering is possible:

“For each of you We have appointed a prescribed way and a clear path.”

This retains the two words’ semantic closeness, although the precise rendering of shirʿah can also be explored in a separate SH R ʿ study.

“A clear way” is also defensible in context, while “clear path” remains closer to the early lexical sense.

Renderings such as “detailed legal code,” “jurisprudential system,” “body of transmitted reports,” or “staged program of worship” introduce more than the word itself establishes.

Such concepts can be discussed in separate interpretation or research. They should not replace the direct meaning of minhāj without evidence.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic records two semantic branches for N H J. The first concerns a clear, evident path, clarifying a route, and following it. Nahj, manhaj, and minhāj belong here and also denote nonphysical courses of action.

The second concerns breathlessness, exhaustion, and wearing out. One lexical analysis treats the branches as distinct. Semantic resemblances can be investigated, but they do not sufficiently establish one original historical image for every use.

Only minhājan occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Māʾidah 5:48, meaning a clear path. The context places that path within revelation, divine scriptures, and courses appointed for communities.

Shirʿatan wa-minhājan brings differences of course together with human responsibility. The verse then calls people to compete in good works. Minhāj alone does not identify all the rules and practices included in each course; that requires a broader reading of the verse and passages concerning religion and sharīʿah.

A concise practical definition for Forqan is therefore:

N H J: in the path branch, a course being clear and evident, clarifying it, and following it; in another branch, breathlessness and wearing out. Qur’anic minhāj means a clear path.
