Obscuring and debasing: the soul’s corruption in relation to purification, and the derivation of dassāhā

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic lexical sources connect the triliteral root D S W with becoming hidden, obscuring, becoming low, and falling into corruption. One recorded use is dasā–yadsū, contrasted with zakā–yazkū.

In this contrast, zakā concerns purity, growth, and flourishing, while dasā describes a condition in which a person moves away from purity and flourishing toward abasement or corruption.

The root’s historical independence is disputed. Some early lexicographers record dasā as an independent D S W verb; others connect it with D S S, inserting and concealing.

This disagreement matters especially for the Qur’anic word dassāhā.

In Al-Shams 91:10, dassāhā contrasts with zakkāhā in the preceding verse. The Qur’an speaks of the success of one who purifies the soul and the failure of one who subjects it to tadsiyah.

Two matters therefore stand out in Qur’anic usage: the soul’s condition, and its orientation toward purity and growth or toward obscuring and corruption.

Nevertheless, dassāhā’s entire meaning should not be extracted solely from its opposite. First examine this family’s Arabic uses, its relation to D S S, and the evidence for each analysis.

Nor should dassāhā initially be restricted to a particular sin, concealing a crime from people, or absolutely annihilating the soul. Each of these requires independent evidence.

2. Root forms and Qur’anic usage

The Qur’anic form

The Quranic Arabic Corpus indexes D S W once, in Al-Shams 91:10:

دَسَّاهَا

The word consists of a third-person masculine singular past verb and the object pronoun -hā.

The pronoun refers to nafs, the soul, in Al-Shams 91:7.

Under this morphological analysis, the three root consonants are D S W. However, the doubled sīn and the form dassā have occasioned disagreement about the verb’s historical formation.

One analysis connects it with the final-weak verb dasā–yadsū.

Another treats it as an altered form of dassasa from the doubled root D S S.

Thus, indexing the word under D S W describes that index’s classification; it does not by itself resolve every derivational disagreement.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence at Al-Shams 91:10

Recorded Arabic forms

Lexical sources report the following forms:

Dasā–yadsū: became debased or corrupt, or moved away from purity and growth.

Dasw: the verbal noun associated with this verb.

Dusūw and daswah: recorded verbal-noun forms of dasā.

Dasā–yadsā: another variant, with final yāʾ, close in meaning to dasā–yadsū.

Dasiya: a form appearing opposite zakā in some sources.

Dāsin: someone in a condition of abasement or corruption, contrasted with zākin.

Dassā: obscured, debased, or brought to corruption, depending on derivation and context.

Tadassā: in some lexical reports, became hidden or placed oneself in a debased and obscured condition.

Forms belonging to D S S have also been connected with this family. Distinguish forms recorded under each root from historical analysis of their relationship.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Only dassāhā in Al-Shams 91:10 is assigned to D S W.

3. The semantic core in early Arabic sources

Dasā in contrast with zakā

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, attributed to Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, introduces dasā as follows:

دَسَا يَدْسُو دَسْوًا ... وَهُوَ نَقِيضُ زَكَا

dasā yadsū daswan ... wa-huwa naqīḍu zakā

This places dasā opposite zakā.

The same source gives dāsin in contrast with zākin.

This opposition matters for understanding the root’s semantic field.

Arabic zakā can concern becoming pure, growing, and flourishing.

In contrast, dasā concerns a condition in which growth and purity are not realized, or a person moves in the opposite direction.

This does not mean that every use of dasā simply describes cessation of bodily growth.

The condition is broader and may concern abasement, corruption, and reduced standing.

Al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam also records dasā al-rajulu opposite zakā.

This pairing shows that the opposition was recognized in Arabic lexicography, even while the independence of dasā remained disputed.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, volume seven: dasā contrasted with zakā

Becoming hidden and obscuring

Ibn al-Aʿrābī also reports concealment as a meaning of dasā:

دَسَا إِذَا اسْتَخْفَى

dasā idhā istakhfā

That is, dasā describes a person concealing themselves.

This use closely resembles D S S, whose basic meaning likewise involves inserting and concealing something.

Nevertheless, becoming hidden differs from becoming debased.

Someone may hide through caution, fear of danger, or another necessity without acting immorally.

Conversely, someone may lower their moral standing through misconduct even when that conduct is publicly visible.

Concealment and moral corruption should therefore not be equated in every instance merely because the two families are related.

For Al-Shams 91:10, the contrast with purification and the context of the soul become decisive.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: concealment and the D S S connection

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies concealment and covering as the central field of D S W.

Discussing dassāhā, he explains that many language authorities consider its original form to be dassasahā, as though something were obscured.

This analysis connects the two families.

On that account, an image of inserting and concealing something extends to the soul’s condition.

Ibn Fāris also reports leading astray and enticing into ugly conduct as explanations.

Two explanations therefore appear in the same source: obscuring and bringing to corruption.

These may be related in an ethical context without being entirely synonymous.

Obscuring emphasizes an image of covering something. Leading astray and corruption identify conduct’s wrong direction and consequences.

Root study should distinguish what comes from the basic meaning from what comes from the ethical context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: concealment, leading astray, and hospitality imagery

4. Is D S W an independent root?

First analysis: dasā as an independent verb

In the report attributed to Al-Khalīl, dasā–yadsū is presented as a verb with final wāw.

It is used in a sense opposite to zakā.

Under this analysis, D S W may constitute an independent family concerning abasement, corruption, and departure from purity and growth.

Forms such as dasw and daswah are also compatible with a verb having final weak wāw.

A dictionary’s recording of a form does not by itself prove that every derivative assigned to it was historically independent of a similar family.

Such a conclusion requires independent usage evidence, especially from pre-Qur’anic Arabic or language close to the period of revelation.

Second analysis: dassāhā from dassasahā

Al-Farrāʾ, Al-Zajjāj, and other lexicographers and grammarians connect dassāhā with dassasahā.

In this analysis, the original form contained several successive sīn consonants.

To ease pronunciation, one sīn became yāʾ, which became alif in this construction. On this analysis, dassā developed from dassasa.

Morphological sources record other such substitutions in Arabic, including changes to doubled forms when several identical or similar consonants occur consecutively.

This provides a defensible account of the morphological formation.

Dassāhā’s semantic connection with concealment also fits the established core of D S S.

This analysis does not establish that every recorded use of dasā or its yāʾ variant must be a distorted form of a single verb.

The question concerns the forms’ historical origin and requires independent evidence.

Al-Azharī’s assessment

Al-Azharī addresses the disagreement in Tahdhīb al-Lughah.

He recognizes Al-Khalīl’s report about dasā but questions its independence from doubled D S S.

On his analysis, dasā may have developed from the same family as dassa.

Al-Azharī explicitly says he does not know or recognize an independent dassā apart from its derivation through alteration of the doubled root.

This matters because the derivational disagreement is longstanding, already discussed among early lexicographers.

Conversely, Al-Azharī’s lack of independent evidence does not conclusively prove that the usage never existed anywhere in Arabic.

Thus, neither the independence of D S W nor its complete removal merely because it resembles D S S should be treated as established without sufficient evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Azharī’s assessment of the verb’s independence

Morphological conclusion

Keeping separate D S S and D S W entries in Forqan’s root list is defensible for research, because there is a real historical and morphological disagreement.

The articles should nevertheless avoid needless duplication.

The D S S article centers on the tangible meaning of inserting and concealing.

The D S W article centers on dassāhā in Al-Shams, its contrast with purification, and the derivational question.

This distinction does not prove the roots’ historical independence; it allows the evidence and disagreement to be examined clearly.

5. Early Arabic witnesses and their reliability

Concealing one’s dwelling from others

Lexical sources explain a connection between dass and diminished standing through an example of hospitality in early Arabic life.

In this account, a generous person lives in an exposed, elevated place so that travelers and those in need can find them.

A miser, by contrast, may choose a concealed dwelling so others will not request hospitality.

This image connects the visibility or concealment of a place with the person’s social and ethical conduct.

It should not become the direct and universal definition of dasā.

A hidden home is not inherently evidence of miserliness. Someone may live out of sight for security, climate, or personal reasons.

The example simply illustrates how language and social imagery can associate obscuring with diminished standing or concealed misconduct.

The witness dassayta ʿamran

Dictionaries quote a verse containing dassayta ʿamran and cite it for leading astray and corrupting.

Its historical reliability is problematic.

When quoting it in Jamharat al-Lughah, Ibn Durayd reports Abū Ḥātim’s judgment that the verse was fabricated.

Other sources attribute it to a man of Ṭayyiʾ, but the poet’s name and precise date remain unclear.

It cannot therefore independently and reliably establish dassā in the sense of leading astray in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Leading astray and corruption are recorded lexical explanations. Their validity must be assessed through the combined linguistic and Qur’anic evidence, rather than a verse whose authenticity was seriously questioned.

Jamharat al-Lughah: Abū Ḥātim’s judgment that the witness was fabricated

Limits of pre-Qur’anic evidence

Among the sources examined for this article, no pre-Qur’anic poetic witness for independent dasā–yadsū with reliable wording and attribution was found that could establish the root’s date and independence conclusively.

Works such as Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Muḥkam, Maqāyīs al-Lughah, and Lisān al-ʿArab record its uses. Recording a classical meaning nevertheless differs from proving its independent presence before Islam.

This limitation should not be remedied by inventing examples or attributing an unsupported expression to an early poet.

The Qur’an itself, at Al-Shams 91:10, is the most important witness for the form under discussion. Its historical derivation nevertheless remains subject to two analyses.

The research conclusion must reflect that degree of certainty.

6. Qur’anic usage: Al-Shams 91:10

Al-Shams 91:10

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ دَسَّاهَا

wa-qad khāba man dassāhā

“And failed has the one who obscured it.”

This is the sole occurrence indexed under D S W in the Quranic Arabic Corpus.

Dassāhā is a past verb with the object pronoun -hā.

The pronoun refers to nafs in Al-Shams 91:7.

Al-Shams 91:8 speaks of inspiring the soul with its wickedness and mindfulness.

Two contrasting statements follow:

Al-Shams 91:9

قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَنْ زَكَّاهَا

qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā

“Indeed, successful has been the one who purified it.”

Al-Shams 91:10

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ دَسَّاهَا

wa-qad khāba man dassāhā

“And failed has the one who obscured it.”

This contrast is fundamental to understanding the word.

Zakkāhā and dassāhā

Zakkāhā belongs to the family Z K W / Z K Y.

This family concerns purity and growth. Applied to the soul, tazkiyah can mean purifying it and helping it flourish.

Dassāhā expresses the opposite direction in the verse’s context.

Under the D S S analysis, this direction is explained as obscuring the soul.

Under the recorded opposition between dasā and zakā, it concerns debasing, defiling, or corrupting the soul.

The two analyses may converge in their consequences.

Through misconduct, someone may obscure their good capacities and orientation, thereby moving away from purity and flourishing.

This does not establish that dassāhā means preventing growth in every Arabic use.

That sense becomes prominent in Al-Shams through the contrast with zakkāhā.

What is obscured?

The object of dassāhā in this verse is the soul.

What is obscured therefore pertains to the person themselves, rather than an external object such as a stone, garment, or document.

This expression does not mean that the human soul is physically placed beneath a covering.

Here the image of concealment extends into human conduct and moral condition.

Through choices and behavior, people may diminish or disregard what supports their purity and growth, or obscure it beneath layers of habit and misconduct.

This explanation clarifies the semantic connection between dassāhā and dass.

It should not be presented as a definitive scientific theory of the mind’s structure. The passage speaks of the soul, wickedness, mindfulness, purification, and obscuring, rather than a particular neurological mechanism or biological structure explaining that process.

Does obscuring the soul mean hiding sin?

Hiding sin from others may be a human behavior, but the verse’s meaning is not limited to it.

Someone may commit wrongdoing openly and persist in it.

In that case, the absence of social secrecy does not necessarily indicate a pure soul.

Conversely, someone may conceal something to maintain privacy or avoid mistreatment without becoming morally corrupt.

The criterion here is therefore not simply whether conduct is visible or hidden from people.

The contrast with purification and the presence of wickedness and mindfulness show that the soul’s orientation and relation to purity and corruption are at issue.

In this context, obscuring the soul may thus concern suppressing the expression of its purity and growth through misconduct, rather than merely hiding an action from society.

Does dassāhā mean annihilating the soul?

Khāba expresses the outcome of the conduct in the verse.

Khāba belongs to KH Y B and concerns failure and not attaining success.

Dassāhā itself, however, does not mean annihilating the soul’s existence.

In Arabic, debasing, obscuring, or corrupting something’s condition does not necessarily equal completely destroying its existence.

Someone may lower their moral standing and depart from the right path without that alone proving that return or reform has become impossible.

The Qur’an discusses repentance and reform in numerous passages.

Al-Shams 91:10 should therefore not be taken to mean that everyone who morally harms their soul is destroyed forever without any possibility of reform.

The verse warns of the failure associated with that orientation. Repentance and return require examination across other verses.

The agent of zakkāhā and dassāhā

On the reading that takes a human as the agent, man refers to the person to whom purification or obscuring of the soul is attributed. However, the presence of man alone does not conclusively identify the agent of these two verbs.

On that account, success and failure relate to the person’s own conduct and responsibility.

Some early explanations nevertheless propose another identification of the agent, attributing purification and obscuring to divine action.

The disagreement requires examination of the syntax and context.

The nearby construction includes fa-alhamahā fujūrahā wa-taqwāhā. Success and failure are then expressed in two contrasting statements.

Thamūd and its rebellion are discussed afterward.

This continuity supports a reading in which human conduct and orientation genuinely contribute to purifying or corrupting the soul.

Recognizing human responsibility does not deny divine guidance, mercy, or enabling grace; recognizing God’s role should likewise not negate human moral responsibility.

Lexically, dasā itself does not settle the entire question. Agency and human responsibility must be examined through sentence structure and the wider network of verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: derivation and the divine-agency reading

7. Dassāhā in relation to wickedness and mindfulness

Al-Shams 91:8 states:

فَأَلْهَمَهَا فُجُورَهَا وَتَقْوَاهَا

fa-alhamahā fujūrahā wa-taqwāhā

“Then inspired it with its wickedness and its mindfulness.”

Wickedness and mindfulness are related to the soul in this verse.

Purification and obscuring are subsequently presented as two different directions.

This continuity shows that the passage concerns more than an outward condition: a person faces two behavioral and ethical orientations.

Nevertheless, fujūr and dasā should not be treated as complete synonyms.

Fujūr belongs to F J R and has its own semantic field.

Taqwā belongs to W Q Y.

Tazkiyah and tadsiyah are two further expressions concerning the soul’s condition and the direction of action upon it.

Wickedness and mindfulness help identify the ethical setting of purification and obscuring, but no term’s lexical meaning substitutes for another’s.

The example of Thamūd

Al-Shams 91:11, Al-Shams 91:12, Al-Shams 91:13, and Al-Shams 91:14 describe Thamūd’s rebellion and rejection of God’s messenger.

Their conduct is not merely an inner condition: rebellion leads to actual action against the she-camel.

This illustrates how an ethical orientation can become visible in outward conduct and its consequences.

Dassāhā itself does not mean rejecting the messenger or hamstringing the she-camel.

Those actions come from the verses about Thamūd and exemplify responsibility and rebellion’s consequences in continuity with the preceding discussion.

Al-Qurṭubī: obscuring, agency, and hospitality imagery

8. Differences from related words

D S W (dasā) and D S S (dassa): abasement and obscuring

This is the closest and most important comparison for the present root.

In its clear use, D S S concerns inserting and concealing something beneath or among something else.

The expression yadussuhu fī al-turābi in Al-Naḥl 16:59 directly illustrates this meaning.

Lexical sources connect D S W with becoming hidden and entering a condition opposed to purification.

Al-Shams 91:10 applies that meaning to the soul and moral conduct.

Historically, dassāhā may be an altered form of dassasahā, as a number of lexicographers explain.

The separate reporting of dasā–yadsū in early sources should nevertheless remain visible.

These two articles should therefore cross-reference each other, while preserving the derivational disagreement rather than merging the roots without sufficient reason.

Study of D S S

D S W (dasā) and Z K W (zakā): corruption and flourishing

These two families contrast in Al-Shams 91:9 and Al-Shams 91:10.

Zakā concerns purity and growth.

In the contrasting context, dasā concerns obscuring, abasement, and departure from that condition.

It does not follow that every historical use of dasā is precisely and exclusively the opposite of every meaning of zakā.

The Qur’anic contrast concerns the soul and should remain tied to that subject.

Nor does purification mean mere bodily growth; this context concerns the soul’s purity and flourishing.

Conversely, obscuring should not be understood merely as reducing a body’s size or lowering its physical position.

Study of Z K W

D S W (dasā) and KH F Y (khafiya): becoming hidden and obscuring

One recorded use of dasā means becoming hidden.

Khafiya also concerns lack of visibility.

Khafiya may simply express a hidden condition, whereas the analysis connecting dasā with dass also involves inserting or obscuring.

This distinction is not absolute across all uses.

In Al-Shams 91:10, concealment must be examined in relation to the soul and its contrast with purification, rather than physical disappearance from people’s sight.

Study of KH F Y

D S W (dasā) and K T M (katama): obscuring and withholding

Qur’anic kitmān can concern concealing truth or knowledge of which a person is aware.

Dassāhā, however, applies to the soul rather than a particular report or document.

Someone may damage their moral condition by knowingly withholding truth. That relationship does not make the roots synonymous.

Kitmān emphasizes concealing information or truth.

In obscuring the soul, according to Al-Shams’s context, the person’s own condition and orientation are at issue.

Dassāhā should therefore not be translated as “concealed the truth.” Such conduct may instead be examined separately as one possible instance of harming the soul.

Study of K T M

D S W (dasā) and GH W Y (ghawā): corruption and straying

Some lexical sources explain dasā through ghawā.

Qur’anic ghawā likewise concerns straying and departure from the right path.

These fields may overlap in the consequences of misconduct.

Nevertheless, ghawāyah does not name every kind of obscuring.

Nor should dassāhā in Al-Shams 91:10 be limited to misleading another person without considering its contrast with purification.

The verb’s object is the soul.

Even if leading astray or corruption is accepted as an explanation, the main subject is damage to the soul’s condition.

D S W (dasā) and F S D (fasada): debasement and corruption

Qur’anic fasād has a broad field encompassing corruption of conduct, social relationships, the earth, and other conditions.

Some lexical accounts explain dasā through corruption.

Corruption does not inherently mean concealment.

Conversely, concealing something is not invariably corruption.

In Al-Shams 91:10, “corrupting the soul” may explain the ethical outcome of tadsiyah, without reducing the root’s entire meaning to fasād.

Study of F S D

D S W (dasā) and KH Y B (khāba): obscuring and failure

These two roots occur together in Al-Shams 91:10 itself.

Dassāhā expresses conduct or a condition concerning the soul.

Khāba identifies the outcome of failure.

Dasā itself should therefore not be directly translated as “failed.”

Just as zakkāhā and aflaḥa have independent roles in the preceding verse, dassāhā and khāba must remain distinct here.

Study of KH Y B

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Dassāhā is the sole Qur’anic form assigned to this root.

Three main semantic directions can be considered in translation:

“Obscured” follows the derivational analysis connecting it with D S S and the image of concealment.

“Debased” follows dasā’s opposition to zakā and lexical explanations concerning diminished standing.

“Corrupted” follows the ethical reading of its contrast with purification and lexical accounts of corruption and leading astray.

These renderings have related consequences, but each emphasizes a different aspect.

Al-Shams 91:10

Arabic text:

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ دَسَّاهَا

wa-qad khāba man dassāhā

The current Persian Forqan translation, rendered in English:

“And failed has the one who obscured it.”

This translation fits the lexical analysis relating it to D S S and preserves the linguistic image of obscuring.

Alongside the translation of Al-Shams 91:9, “Indeed, successful has been the one who purified it,” it also keeps the two directions clearly contrasted.

There is consequently no need to change the base translation.

The root explanation should nevertheless make clear that the meaning is not simply hiding the soul from people’s sight.

In this context, obscuring concerns a condition in which human conduct debases or corrupts the soul, in opposition to purity and growth.

Should “corrupted” be selected in a future translation, that would express preference for one semantic reading, rather than establishing that dassāhā has only that exact meaning in every Arabic use.

Nor should the base translation pile several meanings into one expression, such as “concealed, debased, defiled, and corrupted.”

One clear equivalent suffices for translation; differences among defensible readings belong in the root article.

10. Conclusion

Arabic lexical sources connect D S W with concealment, abasement, and corruption.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn introduces dasā–yadsū opposite zakā–yazkū. This opposition acquires particular ethical significance when applied to the soul.

The root’s historical independence is nevertheless disputed.

Some lexicographers record dasā as an independent D S W verb.

Al-Farrāʾ, Al-Zajjāj, and Al-Azharī, by contrast, connect Qur’anic dassāhā with dassasahā from D S S.

Their analysis offers an image of inserting and concealing for understanding the word.

Recorded uses concerning abasement, corruption, and leading astray also show that dassāhā’s ethical meaning is not restricted to hiding a physical object.

The familiar poetic witness dassayta ʿamran is historically unreliable as proof of leading astray, since Ibn Durayd reports Abū Ḥātim’s judgment questioning its authenticity as a fabricated verse.

Dassāhā occurs once in the Qur’an, in Al-Shams 91:10.

The verb applies to the soul in direct contrast with zakkāhā.

In this context, purification concerns the soul’s purity and flourishing, while tadsiyah indicates the opposite direction.

Obscuring the soul should therefore not be reduced to concealing sin from people. The subject is damage to the soul’s ethical condition and orientation.

Nor does tadsiyah inherently mean the soul’s absolute annihilation or the permanent loss of any possibility of reform.

Proposed final root definition: Recorded Arabic uses connect D S W with becoming hidden, obscurity, and a condition opposed to purity and flourishing. In Al-Shams 91:10, dassāhā denotes obscuring the soul or bringing it to abasement and corruption. Historically, however, its connection with D S S is strong and well documented; the two families’ complete independence is uncertain. “Obscured” is a defensible image in Qur’anic translation, with its ethical direction made clear through the contrast with purification and the surah’s context. Brief root meaning: Becoming hidden and obscuring; of the soul, debasing and corrupting in opposition to purity and flourishing, with a debated derivational connection to D S S.
