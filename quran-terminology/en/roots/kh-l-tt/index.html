Mixing and intermingling: from materials and plants to association, partnership, and human deeds

1. Forms and semantic core

The basic verb in this family is خَلَطَ ـ يَخْلِطُ. In Arabic, خَلَطَ الشَّيْءَ بِالشَّيْءِ means mixing one thing with another. This construction can concern materials, people, property, conduct, and nonphysical matters.

Important derivatives include khalṭ, mixing; khalīṭ, a companion, associate, or partner; khulaṭāʾ, its plural; mukhālaṭah, association and intermingling; ikhtilāṭ, becoming mixed; akhlāṭ, mixed components; and takhlīṭ, mixing things together.

Early Arabic also records mikhlāṭ for someone who associates frequently with people. خَلَّطَ فِي كَلَامِهِ can describe speech whose contents have become disordered and inconsistent.

The Qur’an contains four principal forms from this root: خَلَطُوا, تُخَالِطُوهُمْ, اِخْتَلَطَ, and الْخُلَطَاءِ. فَاخْتَلَطَ is the same Form VIII verb with the prefixed conjunction fa-, rather than an independent derivative.

Morphologically, the family has three verbal uses: Form I خَلَطَ, Form III خَالَطَ, and Form VIII اِخْتَلَطَ. The noun khulaṭāʾ denotes people connected through intermingling, association, or partnership. There are six occurrences in six verses and six surahs: one Form I verb, one Form III verb, three Form VIII verbs, and one noun khulaṭāʾ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six occurrences in four derived categories

The underlying meaning in early sources

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one foundation for this root, opposing it to خ ل ص.

He writes:

خَلَطْتُ الشَّيْءَ بِغَيْرِهِ فَاخْتَلَطَ

khalaṭtu al-shayʾa bi-ghayrihi fa-ikhtalaṭa

Meaning: “I mixed something with something else, and it became mixed.”

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the root’s foundation opposed to khulūṣ

This simple definition has several essential features.

First, mixing requires at least two components or parties: something enters a relationship with something else.

Second, this relationship may arise through the components of one entering another, or through association and connection.

Third, mixing does not necessarily mean completely erasing the boundaries between components.

For example, when water and milk are mixed, separating them may be difficult. When two owners’ sheep are kept in one flock, however, each sheep and its owner may remain identifiable.

Both situations can be called khalṭ, although the kind of intermingling differs.

The Cairo Arabic Language Academy likewise specifies that some components remain distinguishable after mixing, as with animals, whereas separating others is not easy, as with certain liquids.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: components remaining distinguishable after mixing

This matters for the Qur’anic uses. Mixing good and bad deeds in Al-Tawbah 9:102 differs in the kind of relationship involved from fat mingling with bone in Al-Anʿām 6:146.

The root’s basic meaning can therefore be expressed as:

Bringing two or more things into a relationship of mixing, interpenetration, or association, without making the destruction of their identities or their corruption necessary parts of that meaning.

2. Physical and social uses in early Arabic

Mixing materials and components of a whole

The clearest physical image is mixing two things, such as water with milk, food ingredients with one another, or different components of a medicine.

Lisān al-ʿArab uses خِلْط and أَخْلَاط for mixed components. Akhlāṭ al-ṭīb, for instance, denotes different ingredients blended to make a fragrant composition.

Akhlāṭ al-dawāʾ is likewise recorded for the different ingredients of a medicine.

In these uses, mixing can be purposeful and beneficial. Different materials are combined to achieve a particular result.

Unlike some modern Persian uses of “mixing things up,” which commonly imply confusion, the Arabic root has no fixed negative value from the outset.

Early medical sources also use akhlāṭ al-insān for the body’s four humors. This is an ancient medical technical term; its underlying medical theory should not be treated as the root’s basic meaning.

People mingling and sharing life

A familiar Arabic use is خَالَطَ الْقَوْمَ: he mingled with a group, entered their company, or associated with them.

The principal subject here is a social relationship rather than the physical mixing of bodies.

People may live in one place, work together, travel, or take part in a shared activity. Such association can make them khulaṭāʾ or khalīṭ.

Lexical sources sometimes apply khalīṭ to a neighbor, companion, associate, or partner. In certain uses it also denotes a group connected through living or migrating together.

The important distinction is that company and association do not necessarily establish joint ownership.

Two neighbors may be one another’s khalīṭ without owning each other’s property. Two owners may keep their sheep in one flock while each owner’s property remains identifiable.

Intermingling and partnership in property

Arabic records khulṭah, with ḍammah on khāʾ, for partnership, whereas khilṭah, with kasrah, concerns social association in certain uses.

This distinction shows that the root’s social field covers more than one kind of relationship.

In pastoral life, several people may keep their animals together, use one pasture, or entrust the flock to one shepherd.

Their assets then form one group for management and use, although individual ownership may remain separate.

Early legal explanations distinguish jointly owned property from property mixed only in use or management.

This linguistic and social background helps explain khulaṭāʾ in Ṣād 38:24 and tukhāliṭūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:220.

Later legal and administrative rules about partnership or livestock alms, however, should not be inserted into the root’s own meaning. The word conveys intermingling and association; the relevant legal structure and context establish the limits of ownership and responsibility.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Mughrib: association, partnership, and distinguishable ownership

Mixing and disorder

Arabic uses اِخْتَلَطَ عَقْلُهُ for someone whose thinking or discernment has become disordered.

Likewise, خَلَّطَ فِي كَلَامِهِ can describe disordered speech whose parts do not fit together coherently.

In these uses, intermingling leads to confusion and disturbance, but this outcome should not be generalized to every form of khalṭ.

The same root used for disturbed thought also describes commendable association, useful combinations of materials, and social cooperation.

Corruption and disorder are thus possible consequences of mixing, rather than its necessary meaning.

3. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry and life

Companions and associates in Bashāmah ibn al-Ghadīr’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bashāmah ibn al-Ghadīr reads:

إِنَّ الْخَلِيطَ أَجَدُّوا الْبَيْنَ فَابْتَكَرُوا
لِنِيَّةٍ ثُمَّ مَا عَاجُوا وَمَا انْتَظَرُوا

inna al-khalīṭa ajaddū al-bayna fa-ibtakarū / li-niyyatin thumma mā ʿājū wa-mā intaẓarū

Translation:

“The companions resolved to part and departed early for a distant destination; they neither turned back nor waited.”

Al-khalīṭ here denotes companions or associates whose departure the poet describes.

The continuation mentions loading camels, the caravan’s movement, and the travelers receding into the distance. The poem’s context therefore shows a social relationship and actual association.

The witness does not establish that these people necessarily had joint ownership or a financial partnership.

Its principal emphasis is the social connection of a group now broken apart by migration and separation.

The poem is also recorded in the collected dīwān of Bashāmah ibn al-Ghadīr, providing a traceable witness to khalīṭ in Arabic attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Umm Al-Qurā University poetry collection: Bashāmah ibn al-Ghadīr’s poem

Combining and separating affairs in Aws’s poetry

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris attributes a verse to Aws:

وَإِنْ قَالَ لِي مَاذَا تَرَى يَسْتَشِيرُنِي
يَجِدْنِي ابْنَ عَمٍّ مِخْلَطَ الْأَمْرِ مِزْيَلَا

wa-in qāla lī mādhā tarā yastashīrunī / yajidnī ibna ʿammin mikhlaṭa al-amri mizyalā

Translation:

“If he asks my advice, saying, ‘What do you think?’ he will find me a cousin who knows how to engage with affairs and to separate them.”

Mikhlaṭ stands alongside mizyal in this verse. Their vocalizations should not be confused with mukhliṭ and muzīl. Maqāyīs and other lexical sources use this expression for someone capable both of engaging in affairs and of withdrawing or separating from them.

Ibn Fāris explains مِخْلَط as someone skilled at entering and handling affairs, and sets مِزْيَل opposite it.

The witness therefore concerns neither merely mixing materials nor aimlessly jumbling things; it describes practical competence in handling tasks and situations.

Its precise meaning becomes clear through advice and assistance to a relative. The speaker presents himself as someone able both to engage and associate in affairs and to separate or set things aside.

This shows that early Arabic could use the family in the field of practical and social ability.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Aws’s witness and the vocalizations mikhlaṭ and mizyal

Tribal migration and shared residence

Early lexical explanations relate the frequency of khalīṭ in Arabic poetry to nomadic and pastoral life.

Groups from different tribes gathered in one place for pasture and water. During their stay, relationships of neighborhood, association, and acquaintance developed.

When migration season arrived, these groups separated. Accordingly, firāq al-khalīṭ, the parting of companions, became a familiar theme in Arabic poetry.

In this use, intermingling does not erase tribal or family identities. Groups retain their identities while sharing an environment, then part from one another.

This social example clearly distinguishes intermingling from complete fusion.

4. Qur’anic uses

A. Association and shared life with orphans — Al-Baqarah 2:220

فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْيَتَامَىٰ قُلْ إِصْلَاحٌ لَهُمْ خَيْرٌ وَإِنْ تُخَالِطُوهُمْ فَإِخْوَانُكُمْ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ الْمُفْسِدَ مِنَ الْمُصْلِحِ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَأَعْنَتَكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-yatāmā qul iṣlāḥun lahum khayrun wa-in tukhāliṭūhum fa-ikhwānukum wa-allāhu yaʿlamu al-mufsida mina al-muṣliḥi wa-law shāʾa allāhu la-aʿnatakum inna allāha ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“In this world and the Hereafter. They ask you about orphans. Say: ‘Putting their affairs right is better. If you mingle with them, they are your brothers.’ The God knows the corrupter from the reformer. Had The God willed, He would have caused you hardship. Indeed, The God is mighty and wise.”

Al-Baqarah 2:220

تُخَالِطُوهُمْ is a second-person plural Form III imperfect; hum refers to the orphans. The verb is jussive after conditional in, marked by the omission of nūn. Wāw is its subject and hum its object.

Khalṭ concerns people here, rather than simply two materials or objects.

The question concerns orphans. The reply first states إِصْلَاحٌ لَهُمْ خَيْرٌ, then raises the possibility of associating with them.

Lexically, mukhālaṭah can include association, shared life, cooperation, and the intermingling of daily affairs.

Because the context concerns orphans and their care, this association can include material and financial affairs. The verb alone, however, does not mean appropriating their property or transferring its ownership.

The distinction between association and appropriation is crucial.

A guardian may share a house with orphans, prepare their meals together with the family’s food, and participate in managing everyday affairs. This can be an instance of mukhālaṭah.

The relationship itself does not establish the guardian’s ownership of the orphan’s property.

This excerpt from Al-Baqarah 2:220 further illuminates the distinction:

وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ الْمُفْسِدَ مِنَ الْمُصْلِحِ

wa-allāhu yaʿlamu al-mufsida mina al-muṣliḥi

The issue is therefore more than keeping affairs separate or mixing them: corruption and reform in dealing with orphans are at stake.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:2 describes consuming orphans’ property along with one’s own as wrongful conduct. Al-Baqarah 2:220, however, connects association with orphans to reform and brotherhood.

The two verses must be distinguished. Association and shared life do not themselves amount to unjustly consuming another’s property, while the label of association does not authorize exploiting an orphan’s assets.

The closing expression وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَأَعْنَتَكُمْ also shows attention to the difficulties that arranging such relationships can create, alongside the responsibility to put affairs right.

Tukhāliṭūhum here therefore means mingling in life and associating with orphans. The financial and practical limits in each situation must be assessed through the related principles concerning their rights.

B. Fat mingled with bone — Al-Anʿām 6:146

وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ هَادُوا حَرَّمْنَا كُلَّ ذِي ظُفُرٍ وَمِنَ الْبَقَرِ وَالْغَنَمِ حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ شُحُومَهُمَا إِلَّا مَا حَمَلَتْ ظُهُورُهُمَا أَوِ الْحَوَايَا أَوْ مَا اخْتَلَطَ بِعَظْمٍ ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَاهُمْ بِبَغْيِهِمْ وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

wa-ʿalā alladhīna hādū ḥarramnā kulla dhī ẓufurin wa-mina al-baqari wa-al-ghanami ḥarramnā ʿalayhim shuḥūmahumā illā mā ḥamalat ẓuhūruhumā awi al-ḥawāyā aw mā ikhtalaṭa bi-ʿaẓmin dhālika jazaynāhum bi-baghyihim wa-innā la-ṣādiqūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“For those who became Jews, We prohibited every creature with a claw; and of cattle and sheep We prohibited their fats, except what their backs carry, or the entrails, or what is mingled with bone. Thus We recompensed them for their transgression, and indeed We are truthful.”

Al-Anʿām 6:146

اِخْتَلَطَ is Form VIII in this verse.

مَا اخْتَلَطَ بِعَظْمٍ denotes something mingled with, or closely joined to, bone.

The context concerns the fats of cattle and sheep. Accordingly, the expression concerns fat connected or mingled with bone.

This is a clear example of physical intermingling.

It need not resemble one liquid dissolving in another. Fat can be on or beside bone, connected to surrounding tissues.

Ikhtalaṭa therefore has a broader meaning here than completely blending the components of two liquids. Close material attachment can also fall within its field.

Structurally, مَا اخْتَلَطَ بِعَظْمٍ belongs among the exceptions concerning fats. A familiar grammatical explanation coordinates it with the material following illā.

Another point is the restriction’s particular addressees. The verse begins وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ هَادُوا and describes a restriction imposed on a specific group.

This restriction is not part of ikhtalaṭa’s meaning; it comes from the ruling and its addressees.

Nor should the verse be made to imply that everything attached to bone has an identical dietary ruling in every religious law.

For lexical study, the principal conclusion is more limited: ikhtilāṭ can describe the material attachment of components that retain distinct identities.

C. Mixing righteous and bad deeds — Al-Tawbah 9:102

وَآخَرُونَ اعْتَرَفُوا بِذُنُوبِهِمْ خَلَطُوا عَمَلًا صَالِحًا وَآخَرَ سَيِّئًا عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَتُوبَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-ākharūna iʿtarafū bi-dhunūbihim khalaṭū ʿamalan ṣāliḥan wa-ākhara sayyiʾan ʿasā allāhu an yatūba ʿalayhim inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Others acknowledged their sins; they mixed a righteous deed with another that was bad. It may be that The God will turn toward them; indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Tawbah 9:102

خَلَطُوا is a plural Form I perfect.

The intermingling here is not physical: two kinds of deeds coexist within people’s lives and conduct.

The verse first says that they acknowledged their sins. This excerpt from Al-Tawbah 9:102 then describes their deeds:

خَلَطُوا عَمَلًا صَالِحًا وَآخَرَ سَيِّئًا

khalaṭū ʿamalan ṣāliḥan wa-ākhara sayyiʾan

Grammatically, ʿamalan is the object of khalaṭū and ākhara is coordinated with it; the verb does not take two independent complements here like a verb requiring two objects. Arabic can say خَلَطْتُ الْمَاءَ وَاللَّبَنَ: “I mixed water and milk together.”

The absence of bi- between the two kinds of deeds therefore does not prevent the meaning of mixing.

Ākhara sayyiʾan denotes another deed that is bad. The word ʿamal is not repeated in the second part but is understood from the first.

The verse does not say that a good deed became a bad deed, or that a bad deed disappeared because it accompanied a good one.

The two kinds retain their different descriptions: one righteous and the other bad.

Mixing here therefore does not destroy the moral distinction between good and bad.

Someone may act rightly in one part of life and commit a wrong in another. The presence of both kinds makes that person’s record a mixed one.

Khalaṭū alone, however, does not establish the quantity of good and bad deeds, their equivalence in value, or the final outcome of someone’s account.

The verse concerns people who have acknowledged their sins and for whom a divine turning toward them in mercy is raised as a possibility.

This differs from a situation in which someone denies a wrong or knowingly persists in it.

Khalṭ here thus concerns righteous and bad deeds coexisting in a person’s record. Each deed retains its ethical character; acknowledgment, repentance, and the divine turning are conveyed by other parts of the verse.

D. Water and earthly vegetation intermingling — Yūnus 10:24

إِنَّمَا مَثَلُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَاءٍ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ مِمَّا يَأْكُلُ النَّاسُ وَالْأَنْعَامُ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَخَذَتِ الْأَرْضُ زُخْرُفَهَا وَازَّيَّنَتْ وَظَنَّ أَهْلُهَا أَنَّهُمْ قَادِرُونَ عَلَيْهَا أَتَاهَا أَمْرُنَا لَيْلًا أَوْ نَهَارًا فَجَعَلْنَاهَا حَصِيدًا كَأَنْ لَمْ تَغْنَ بِالْأَمْسِ كَذَٰلِكَ نُفَصِّلُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

innamā mathalu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-māʾin anzalnāhu mina al-samāʾi fa-ikhtalaṭa bihi nabātu al-arḍi mimmā yaʾkulu al-nāsu wa-al-anʿāmu ḥattā idhā akhadhati al-arḍu zukhrufahā wa-izzayyanat wa-ẓanna ahluhā annahum qādirūna ʿalayhā atāhā amrunā laylan aw nahāran fa-jaʿalnāhā ḥaṣīdan ka-an lam taghna bi-al-amsi kadhālika nufaṣṣilu al-āyāti li-qawmin yatafakkarūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The likeness of worldly life is only that of water We sent down from heaven; the earth’s vegetation mingled with it, of what people and livestock eat. When the earth took on its adornment and became beautiful, and its people thought they had power over it, Our command came to it by night or by day, and We made it a harvested field, as though it had not flourished there yesterday. Thus We explain the signs for people who reflect.”

Yūnus 10:24

فَاخْتَلَطَ is a Form VIII perfect.

Grammatically, nabātu al-arḍ is its subject, and the pronoun in bihi refers to the water sent down.

The direct construction can therefore be rendered:

“The earth’s vegetation mingled with that water.”

The expression has two related exegetical and grammatical analyses; the difference concerns more than describing one natural process:

In the first, bi- expresses direct mingling with water, as in Al-Ṭabarī’s explanation of Al-Kahf 18:45, where earthly vegetation mingles with water.

In the second, bi- is causal: through the water, different kinds of plants grow and intermingle with one another. Al-Ṭabarī’s explanation of Yūnus 10:24 emphasizes different kinds of intermingled vegetation growing because of rain.

Both images can coexist in nature. Nevertheless, “the earth’s vegetation mingled with that water” is this article’s proposed translation following the first analysis, rather than the only possible rendering of the Arabic construction. The causal analysis must also remain in the explanation.

Al-Ṭabarī: different intermingled plants growing through rain

Ikhtalaṭa itself does not mean becoming green, increasing in number, or flourishing. These concern water’s role, plant growth, and the continuation.

More significantly, after describing growth and the earth’s adornment, the verse describes the scene’s disappearance.

Intermingling and flourishing vegetation form part of the image of worldly life, but the parable’s entire meaning should not be placed inside ikhtalaṭa.

In this article’s proposal, the verb describes vegetation mingling with water; in the causal analysis, plants intermingle through the water’s effect. In both, the subsequent change and disappearance come from the continuation.

E. Plants and water in the parable of worldly disappearance — Al-Kahf 18:45

وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَاءٍ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقْتَدِرًا

wa-iḍrib lahum mathala al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-māʾin anzalnāhu mina al-samāʾi fa-ikhtalaṭa bihi nabātu al-arḍi fa-aṣbaḥa hashīman tadhrūhu al-riyāḥu wa-kāna allāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqtadiran

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Set forth for them the likeness of worldly life: water We sent down from heaven, with which the earth’s vegetation mingled; then it became dry fragments scattered by the winds. The God has power over every thing.”

Al-Kahf 18:45

فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ has the same construction as in Yūnus 10:24.

In both, earthly vegetation is the subject of ikhtalaṭa, and bihi refers to water.

The different continuations, however, deserve attention.

Yūnus 10:24 moves from intermingling to the earth’s adornment and its people’s confidence in their power.

Al-Kahf 18:45 proceeds directly from intermingling to vegetation becoming hashīm and being scattered by winds.

Both employ a shared natural image to express worldly life’s impermanence, but their descriptive details differ.

Lexically, the different continuations do not change ikhtalaṭa’s meaning: it concerns intermingling in both.

Al-Ṭabarī: vegetation mingling with water and its subsequent disappearance

Reflectively, however, the verses show that growth and flourishing, despite their attraction and abundance, do not last.

Ikhtalaṭa concerns plants here, rather than human morality or deeds. These natural uses therefore do not establish that mixing is inherently corrupting.

F. Khulaṭāʾ and justice in partnership — Ṣād 38:24

قَالَ لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِ وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْخُلَطَاءِ لَيَبْغِي بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَقَلِيلٌ مَا هُمْ وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ

qāla laqad ẓalamaka bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājihi wa-inna kathīran mina al-khulaṭāʾi la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti wa-qalīlun mā hum wa-ẓanna dāwūdu annamā fatannāhu fa-istaghfara rabbahu wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāba

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He said: ‘He has wronged you by asking to add your ewe to his ewes. Many partners indeed transgress against one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds, and how few they are.’ David thought that We had tested him; he sought his Lord’s forgiveness, fell down bowing, and turned back.”

Ṣād 38:24

Al-khulaṭāʾ is the plural of khalīṭ.

It occurs in a dispute between two parties, one complaining that the other wanted to add his single ewe to his own many ewes.

Khulaṭāʾ therefore denotes partners or people dealing with one another in a financial and proprietary relationship.

This agrees with khalīṭ’s early Arabic social meaning, while the context emphasizes partnership specifically.

The word itself does not establish that all their assets were jointly owned and indivisible. In pastoral uses, too, khulaṭāʾ could keep animals together while retaining separate ownership.

Understanding khulaṭāʾ therefore requires distinguishing association, partnership in managing assets, and joint ownership.

Why is injustice mentioned among khulaṭāʾ?

This excerpt from Ṣād 38:24 states:

وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْخُلَطَاءِ لَيَبْغِي بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ

wa-inna kathīran mina al-khulaṭāʾi la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin

Kathīran shows that the statement concerns many khulaṭāʾ, rather than all of them.

Baghy belongs to a separate root, conveying transgression, injustice, or infringement in context.

Injustice is thus not part of khalīṭ’s own meaning. It is conduct that may arise within partnership and intermingled affairs.

Connecting people’s assets, interests, and decisions creates opportunities for cooperation and mutual benefit. Such proximity can also create opportunities for exploitation and infringing another’s rights.

In the story, the person with ninety-nine ewes wants to add another ewe to his property, although it belongs to the other party.

The central issue is consequently more than mixing flocks: it is a demand and conduct connected to injustice within the story.

The continuation excepts those who believe and do righteous deeds from such conduct.

This shows that partnership need not be corrupting. Injustice and infringement of the other party’s rights make the relationship wrongful.

Lexically, khulaṭāʾ are people whose affairs intermingle; ethically, their conduct determines whether the relationship is just or oppressive.

5. Shared morphological and semantic observations

Khalṭ, mukhālaṭah, and ikhtilāṭ

The three Qur’anic verbal forms share a semantic field, while their constructions differ.

Khalaṭū in Al-Tawbah 9:102 is transitive: people mixed two kinds of deeds.

Tukhāliṭūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:220 is Form III, used for social association and sharing life with others.

Ikhtalaṭa in Al-Anʿām 6:146, Yūnus 10:24, and Al-Kahf 18:45 is Form VIII, describing becoming mixed or connected.

One word family can therefore express both the act of mixing and the resulting condition.

We should not, however, make an absolute rule that Form III always means mutual cooperation, or Form VIII always means involuntary intermingling.

The subject, object, preposition, and context are needed to determine the actual relationship.

The role of bi- in ikhtilāṭ

In Al-Anʿām 6:146, bi-ʿaẓmin identifies what the fat is mingled with.

In Yūnus 10:24 and Al-Kahf 18:45, bihi refers to water.

Bi- helps establish the relationship in all three, but a causal analysis also exists in the two vegetation passages. The relationship cannot be identified without considering the connected things, construction, and context.

Fat mingling with bone is not the same process as vegetation mingling with water.

A shared root meaning should therefore not be confused with an identical instance of a relationship.

Mixing without erasing differences

Al-Tawbah 9:102 and Ṣād 38:24 illuminate this from another angle.

In Al-Tawbah 9:102, righteous and bad deeds coexist in people’s records but remain separately described as righteous and bad.

In Ṣād 38:24, partners and associates stand in a relationship, yet each party’s rights and the possibility of one wronging another remain relevant.

Mixing therefore does not erase responsibility or differences in people’s rights.

This agrees with the physical use in Al-Anʿām 6:146: fat is connected with bone, but fat and bone can still be named as distinct things.

Khalṭ should consequently not be defined so narrowly that it covers only completely blending two liquids.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots

KH L Ṭ (khalṭ) compared with M Z J (mazj)

Mazj also concerns mixing and can approach khalṭ in many uses.

The Qur’an uses this family for drinks, as in كَانَ مِزَاجُهَا كَافُورًا in Al-Insān 76:5 and وَمِزَاجُهُ مِنْ تَسْنِيمٍ in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27.

Mizāj here concerns something mingled with a drink or present in its composition.

Qur’anic khalṭ, by contrast, encompasses social relationships and partnership as well as physical mixing.

Its Qur’anic uses therefore extend beyond combining materials, but this does not mean that mazj throughout Arabic is restricted to liquids.

KH L Ṭ (khalṭ) compared with L B S (labs)

In one semantic branch, Qur’anic ل ب س concerns mixing and obscuring distinctions between matters.

Al-Baqarah 2:42 states:

وَلَا تَلْبِسُوا الْحَقَّ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَتَكْتُمُوا الْحَقَّ وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-lā talbisū al-ḥaqqa bi-al-bāṭili wa-taktumū al-ḥaqqa wa-antum taʿlamūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do not mix truth with falsehood or conceal the truth while you know.”

Al-Baqarah 2:42

تَلْبِسُوا الْحَقَّ بِالْبَاطِلِ belongs to ل ب س, rather than خ ل ط.

Here mixing truth and falsehood can obscure their distinction.

Khalṭ itself, however, does not necessarily entail that result.

In Al-Tawbah 9:102, for instance, righteous and bad deeds are mixed, yet the Qur’an retains their distinction.

Although labs and khalṭ may sometimes receive the same Persian equivalent, their meanings should not be assumed identical from the outset.

In the truth-and-falsehood use, labs approaches obscuring and confusing distinctions as well as mixing, whereas khalṭ can emphasize simply accompanying and intermingling.

Study of L B S

KH L Ṭ (khalṭ) compared with KH L Ṣ (khulūṣ)

Ibn Fāris sets خ ل ط opposite خ ل ص.

خ ل ص concerns separating from mixtures and becoming clear or pure.

For example, milk is described as خَالِصًا in Al-Naḥl 16:66.

Here khulūṣ emphasizes something’s being separate and pure from other materials.

Khalṭ, conversely, denotes intermingling and components coexisting in a whole.

This opposition clarifies the roots’ physical cores, but it does not make every mixture impure or wrong, or every purity morally commendable.

Combining food ingredients or living with others may be beneficial, just as purity in a particular use may describe only a material condition.

Study of KH L Ṣ

KH L Ṭ (khalṭ) compared with SH R K (shirk)

ش ر ك concerns having or appointing a partner.

In Ṣād 38:24, khulaṭāʾ denotes partners in a financial context and thus approaches the field of sharīk.

Khulaṭāʾ can also involve association and intermingled affairs, whereas sharīk emphasizes a share or participation in something.

Nor should the social use of partner be equated with shirk in attributing divine standing to something other than The God.

Cooperation and partnership in daily human affairs are lexically distinct from assigning equals in divine standing.

Khulaṭāʾ in Ṣād 38:24 should therefore be understood within people’s rights and relationships, without importing the theological weight of every use of ش ر ك.

Study of SH R K

KH L Ṭ (khalṭ) compared with KH L L (khilāl)

خ ل ل is an independent root concerning, in certain Qur’anic uses, intervals, what lies between things, and close relationships.

Khalīl likewise belongs to that family rather than خ ل ط.

A khalīṭ and khalīl may in some social situations be the same person, for instance someone who is both a companion and a close friend.

Lexically, however, the roots differ.

Khalīṭ emphasizes intermingling, association, or partnership, whereas khalīl in its familiar use concerns friendship and a close bond.

Mere proximity between two people therefore does not make the words identical in meaning.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Three kinds of use should be distinguished when translating this root.

In physical intermingling, as in Al-Anʿām 6:146, “becoming mingled” or “being closely joined and intermingled” is appropriate.

In social relationships, as in Al-Baqarah 2:220, “associate,” “mingle with one another,” or “share life together” can convey the meaning.

In the ethical use of Al-Tawbah 9:102, “mixing good and bad deeds” is more accurate than “confusing good and bad deeds”: both kinds are present, rather than someone being unable to distinguish them.

“Partners” is appropriate for khulaṭāʾ in Ṣād 38:24, although Arabic khalīṭ has a broader field than partnership alone.

In Yūnus 10:24 and Al-Kahf 18:45, “the earth’s vegetation mingled with that water” retains the direct construction. Abundant growth and plants intermingling can be explained in commentary rather than substituted for the verb in the base translation. This choice follows the accompaniment analysis; under the causal analysis, “through that water the earth’s plants grew intermingled” is also a rendering to consider.

Brief root meaning

Mixing and intermingling two or more things; socially, association, companionship, and partnership.

Conclusion

In Arabic, خ ل ط denotes mixing and intermingling two or more things. This may be physical or occur within social relationships, property, and human conduct.

Early Arabic witnesses show that the root extends beyond combining materials to association, companionship, neighborhood, and partnership. Khalīṭ in pre-Islamic poetry expresses the social connection of groups living or migrating together, without necessarily merging their ownership or identities.

The Qur’an displays the same breadth. Fat mingles with bone; vegetation mingles with water; orphans can share daily life with guardians; partners may infringe one another’s rights; and someone’s record may contain both righteous and bad deeds.

Khalṭ itself means neither corruption nor complete fusion of components.

In some situations, mixing is beneficial and natural; in others, it can create conditions for harm or exploitation. Context and the action’s characteristics establish the difference.

Mixing does not itself abolish legal or ethical boundaries. Partners retain separate rights, and righteous and bad deeds retain their different descriptions within one record.

The root’s basic meaning can consequently be expressed as:

“Material components, matters, or people intermingling and becoming connected through interpenetration, association, or a shared relationship, without necessarily becoming inseparable, corrupt, or deprived of their identities.” Brief root meaning: Mixing and intermingling two or more things; socially, association, companionship, and partnership.
