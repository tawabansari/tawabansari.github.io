Ascending, rising in rank, and reciting protective charms

Brief root meaning: This linguistic family has two important, distinct branches: ascending to a higher place or rank; and reciting words or protective formulas intended to avert harm and seek recovery. Early Arabic also attests رَقْوَة for a small rise of ground; this wāw-final form needs separate morphological consideration.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root R Q Y is final-weak: its last radical is a weak letter whose form changes in inflection. It appears, for example, as yāʾ in رَقِيَ and as alif maqṣūrah in رَقَى.

The family has two familiar uses that are close in outward form but differ in meaning.

First, رَقِيَ يَرْقَى means to ascend, as when climbing a ladder or mountain or reaching a higher position.

Second, رَقَى يَرْقِي means to recite a ruqyah or protective formula over someone exposed to illness, a bite, or injury.

The second use does not concern bodily ascent. Words are recited to which the speaker or person requesting them attributes protective or healing properties.

The distinction is especially important in Al-Qiyāmah, since رَاقٍ can in form relate to either verb.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

R Q Y occurs four times in the Qur’an, in three verses and four forms:

  • تَرْقَىٰ: A Form I imperfect verb, “you ascend”; Al-Isrāʾ 17:93.
  • لِرُقِيِّكَ: The verbal noun رُقِيّ with the second-person pronoun and the preposition li-, “your ascent”; Al-Isrāʾ 17:93.
  • فَلْيَرْتَقُوا: A jussive Form VIII imperfect with the imperative lām and plural subject, “then let them ascend”; Ṣād 38:10.
  • رَاقٍ: An active participle, with the lexical possibilities of a reciter of ruqyah or someone ascending; on the second contextual interpretation, someone ascending with the soul; Al-Qiyāmah 75:27.

Other recognized Arabic derivatives include رُقْيَة, رُقًى, رَاقِي, رُقَاة, رَقَّاء, مَرْقَى, مَرْقَاة, ارْتِقَاء, تَرَقِّي, and اسْتِرْقَاء, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Ascent is thus clearly present in the Qur’anic uses, while one verse contains a word that may concern reciting ruqyah or taking someone upward.

2. Meanings in early Arabic

Ascending: رَقِيَ يَرْقَى

رَقِيَ فِي السُّلَّمِ means to climb a ladder. The verb also describes climbing a mountain or reaching a high place.

رَقْي, رُقِيّ, and رِقِيّ are attested as verbal nouns of ascent.

ارْتَقَى belongs to the same family and denotes ascending or reaching a higher rank.

مَرْقَاة and مِرْقَاة denote a step or means of ascent. Their plural is مَرَاقٍ.

The concrete image is clear: someone moves from a lower position to a higher one, whether climbing a ladder or mountain or, by extension, reaching a higher rank.

In bodily use, this concerns place and height. In expressions such as تَرَقَّى فِي الْعِلْمِ or تَرَقَّى فِي الْمَنْزِلَةِ, ascent extends to growth in knowledge or advancement in standing.

The verb does not necessarily imply moral progress or perfection. A person can climb a mountain without becoming more knowledgeable or ethically better.

A witness from Al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A poem transmitted under the name of Al-Aʿshā, the renowned pre-Islamic poet, includes:

لَئِنْ كُنْتَ فِي جُبٍّ ثَمَانِينَ قَامَةً
وَرَقِيتَ أَسْبَابَ السَّمَاءِ بِسُلَّمِ

la-in kunta fī jubbin thamānīna qāmatan / wa-raqīta asbāba al-samāʾi bi-sullami

Translation: “Even if you were in a well eighty human heights deep, and climbed the paths of heaven by a ladder…”

The poetic hyperbole pictures movement from the depths of the earth to the height of heaven.

The witness matters because the verb appears alongside a ladder and the means of reaching heaven, making the sense of ascent clear.

أَسْبَابَ السَّمَاءِ is also useful for considering a similar Qur’anic expression. In this image, asbāb concerns paths or means of reaching a high place.

It does not follow that asbāb always means ladders in Arabic. The ladder is an instrument named separately in this line.

Gradual ascent and advancement

تَرَقَّى is also attested for ascending step by step or passing from one stage to another.

For example, تَرَقَّى فِي الْعِلْمِ refers to advancing in knowledge, while تَرَقَّى فِي الْمَنْزِلَةِ can describe rising in status or position.

These uses relate semantically to height. A person may rise spatially or reach a higher level of rank, ability, or knowledge.

Yet all the positive modern associations of “progress” should not be transferred into the Qur’anic passages.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:93 concerns a demand to ascend into heaven. Ṣād 38:10 also concerns ascent along means of access. Neither uses the word for civilizational, scientific, or moral progress.

Reciting ruqyah: رَقَى يَرْقِي

The second branch is known through رُقْيَة.

Ruqyah denotes words, a prayer, or a protective formula recited over someone to avert injury, illness, a bite, or another unwelcome condition.

Its plural is رُقًى, and a practitioner is called رَاقٍ or رَقَّاء.

اسْتَرْقَى means to request ruqyah from another person.

Traditionally, ruqyah could involve recited words and sometimes blowing. The words, accompanying beliefs, and practitioner’s purpose were not identical in every case.

The lexical meaning of reciting ruqyah is therefore distinct from demonstrating an actual therapeutic effect.

Historically, ruqyah is not limited to recitation of Qur’anic verses. Poetry transmitted from before the Qur’an also attests the word and practice.

A witness from Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

Poetry transmitted under the name of the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes a venomous snake:

تَنَاذَرَهَا الرَّاقُونَ مِنْ سُوءِ سَمِّهَا
تُطَلِّقُهُ طَوْرًا وَطَوْرًا تُرَاجِعُ

tanādharahā al-rāqūna min sūʾi sammihā / tuṭalliquhu ṭawran wa-ṭawran turājiʿu

Translation: “The reciters warned one another of its dangerous venom; at times it released the bitten person, and at times returned to him.” The latter half pictures the venom’s pain subsiding and returning, rather than the snake physically leaving and coming back.

The snake is so dangerous in this image that even those who recite ruqyah for bites fear it.

الرَّاقُونَ denotes people associated with reciting ruqyah and attempting treatment, not mountaineers or people ascending somewhere.

This witness attests the ruqyah sense in poetry transmitted from the pre-Islamic period.

It does not show that all such practitioners were physicians or that their methods were medically effective. It attests a name and a recognizable social practice.

Did reciting ruqyah derive from ascending?

Despite the shared radicals, the historical relationship between these branches is uncertain.

Some early lexical classifications distinguish three fields in discussing the rāʾ–qāf–weak-letter family: ascent, protective recitation, and a kind of raised ground.

That distinction matters because it prevents forcing every derivative into a single image.

One can consider a possible connection between protective recitation and lifting or averting an unwelcome condition, but imagining such a link does not supply historical evidence for it.

A claim that ruqyah originated in raising a person’s spirit or energy would require independent evidence.

رَقْوَة and رَقْو are also attested for a small rise of earth or sand, especially near a valley. These wāw-final forms are also treated under R Q W and should not be placed unconditionally under R Q Y.

These terrain terms do not occur among the four Qur’anic uses examined here, and there is no need to derive every branch from them.

3. Ascending into heaven in Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

Verse text:

أَوْ يَكُونَ لَكَ بَيْتٌ مِنْ زُخْرُفٍ أَوْ تَرْقَىٰ فِي السَّمَاءِ وَلَنْ نُؤْمِنَ لِرُقِيِّكَ حَتَّىٰ تُنَزِّلَ عَلَيْنَا كِتَابًا نَقْرَؤُهُ ۗ قُلْ سُبْحَانَ رَبِّي هَلْ كُنْتُ إِلَّا بَشَرًا رَسُولًا

aw yakūna laka baytun min zukhrufin aw tarqā fī al-samāʾi wa-lan nuʾmina li-ruqiyyika ḥattā tunazzila ʿalaynā kitāban naqraʾuhu qul subḥāna rabbī hal kuntu illā basharan rasūlan

Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

Translation:

“Or that you have a house of gold, or ascend into heaven; and we will not believe in your ascent until you bring down to us a book that we can read. Say: Glorified is my Lord! Have I been anything but a human messenger?”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

Tarqā: you ascend

تَرْقَىٰ is a second-person singular imperfect of رَقِيَ يَرْقَى. Here it is subjunctive through coordination with يَكُونَ, with the mood ending not pronounced on the final alif maqṣūrah.

Its meaning in this construction is clear: you ascend.

فِي السَّمَاءِ specifies the sphere of movement. In Arabic, the verb can occur with fī for ascent on a ladder or along a route.

Fī therefore should not be taken to mean only that someone is already in heaven and merely moves within it.

The word also does not specify the means of ascent. No ladder, wings, flying apparatus, or other instrument is named here.

The verse reports a demand to ascend into heaven, without explaining the physical method.

Ruqiyyika: the act of ascending

Later in the verse, the same root appears as a verbal noun:

“وَلَنْ نُؤْمِنَ لِرُقِيِّكَ”

رُقِيّ is the verbal noun of ascending; the pronoun -ka associates it with the addressee.

لِرُقِيِّكَ therefore concerns your ascent.

The distinction is clear: tarqā expresses ascending as a verb, while ruqiyyika presents the same act as a noun.

The repetition identifies the demand: they require the messenger to ascend into heaven, yet do not consider even that event sufficient for belief.

The context of demanding a miracle

Al-Isrāʾ 17:90, Al-Isrāʾ 17:91, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:92 present several demands: making a spring flow from the ground, possessing a garden and rivers, making portions of heaven fall, and bringing The God and the angels before them.

Verse 93 continues with a house of gold or ascent into heaven.

Even after ascent, they impose a further condition: a book must be brought down for them to read.

The issue is therefore not merely an inability to observe an event. The speakers make their belief conditional on successive demands.

The reply هَلْ كُنْتُ إِلَّا بَشَرًا رَسُولًا clarifies the messenger’s position: he is a human messenger, not an independent controller of every event in existence.

For the root study, the main point is that tarqā and ruqiyyika both mean ascending. Nothing in this verse connects either form with ruqyah, prayer, or treatment.

Does the verb here mean miʿrāj?

Miʿrāj and ʿurūj belong to another family, ʿ R J.

Both families can concern ascent, but they are not the same root.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:93, tarqā occurs in the words of those demanding a sign. Their demand should not be identified without evidence with the separate account of the night journey in Al-Isrāʾ 17:1.

The name or details of a particular heavenly journey also cannot be extracted from R Q Y itself.

Its clear meaning in this verse is ascending.

4. Fa-l-yartaqū: ascending along means of access

Ṣād 38:10

Verse text:

أَمْ لَهُمْ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ فَلْيَرْتَقُوا فِي الْأَسْبَابِ

am lahum mulku al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā fa-l-yartaqū fī al-asbābi

Ṣād 38:10

Translation:

“Or do they possess the sovereignty of the heavens and earth and what is between them? Then let them ascend by the ways of access.”

Ṣād 38:10

The structure of fa-l-yartaqū

The word derives from ارْتَقَى, Form VIII.

يَرْتَقُونَ is the plural imperfect. Here it follows the imperative lām; the jussive removes the final nūn, producing فَلْيَرْتَقُوا.

The complete word includes the connective fa-, imperative lām, verb, and plural subject marker.

Its direct meaning is “then let them ascend” or “then they should ascend.”

Form VIII overlaps semantically with the Form I verb here: both concern ascending.

Form VIII alone does not establish difficult exertion, competition, complex equipment, or multiple stages. Such features require the sentence or other evidence.

Fī al-asbāb: paths and means of access

الْأَسْبَاب is the plural of سَبَب.

Sabab can denote a rope, a path, a means, or something through which a person reaches an objective.

Here, فَلْيَرْتَقُوا combined with فِي الْأَسْبَابِ creates an image of ascent along ways of access.

Early lexical and interpretive explanations also understand asbāb here as the paths or gates of heaven.

Sabab itself, however, is not limited to a gate. No particular apparatus or architecture should be added to the base translation without evidence.

The idea of a path or means comes from asbāb; irtiqāʾ itself denotes ascending.

Context: a claim to sovereignty and authority

Ṣād 38:8 reports people objecting that the reminder has been sent down to one particular person among them.

Ṣād 38:9 asks whether the treasuries of the Lord’s mercy are in their possession.

Verse 10 then asks about sovereignty over heaven and earth and states فَلْيَرْتَقُوا فِي الْأَسْبَابِ.

In this sequence, the imperative is a challenge rather than a practical instruction to climb.

Those objecting to the choice of a messenger and the sending of the reminder face a question challenging their authority and sovereignty.

If sovereignty over heaven and earth belongs to them, let them ascend by the ways of access.

The statement exposes the limits of their authority.

This context does not declare every human ascent into the sky or every scientific access to outer space impossible.

The verse concerns claims to control and sovereignty, not the limits of human technology for all ages.

Fa-l-yartaqū lexically means ascending; in context, the sentence challenges a claim to authority.

5. Rāqin: a reciter of ruqyah or someone taking the soul upward?

Al-Qiyāmah 75:27

Verse text:

وَقِيلَ مَنْ رَاقٍ

wa-qīla man rāqin

Al-Qiyāmah 75:27

Translation:

“And it is said: Who can heal?”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:27

This verse is the principal point of lexical disagreement in the Qur’anic uses of R Q Y.

The structure of rāqin

رَاقٍ is a singular indefinite active participle.

Its underlying form is رَاقِي. In this kind of final-yāʾ noun, the yāʾ is omitted in the indefinite nominative and genitive; the nominative form here is therefore رَاقٍ.

The same participial form can derive from two verbs:

رَقَى يَرْقِي: to recite ruqyah over someone.

رَقِيَ يَرْقَى: to ascend.

The outward form alone therefore does not determine whether reciting ruqyah or ascending is intended.

مَنْ asks who performs the act. Precisely which act is meant must be understood through language and context.

First reading: a request for a reciter or healer

On this reading, rāqin is someone who recites ruqyah for an afflicted person or attempts to bring relief and recovery.

The immediate context supports this possibility.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:26 speaks of reaching the collarbones; in Al-Qiyāmah 75:28 the person realizes that this is separation; Al-Qiyāmah 75:29 describes one leg entwining with the other.

The scene concerns the approach of death.

In such a situation, مَنْ رَاقٍ can be spoken by those around the person: is there anyone to recite ruqyah or find a way to bring relief and recovery?

Early interpretive explanations also express this reading through phrases such as هَلْ مِنْ طَبِيبٍ and هَلْ مِنْ مُدَاوٍ: is there a physician or someone to treat him?

Physician, however, is not the direct lexical meaning of rāqin. A physician treats illness, whereas a rāqī is specifically associated here with reciting ruqyah.

Translating rāqin as “healer” thus broadens the particular practice of ruqyah into the more general attempt to treat someone.

That broadening can be defensible in a fluent translation, but the distinction between lexical meaning and contextual intent should remain clear.

A more direct rendering is:

“And it is said: Who will recite ruqyah for him?”

This retains the specific act more clearly, but readers unfamiliar with ruqyah need an explanation.

Second reading: who will take the soul upward?

On the alternative reading, rāqin belongs to the ascent branch.

The question then concerns who takes the person’s soul upward at death.

Some interpretive accounts attribute the words to angels, as if they were asking who would take the soul upward.

The reading is linguistically possible because رَاقٍ can mean someone ascending. In this interpretation, the sense is “who ascends with the soul?”; “taking upward” renders that fuller interpretation rather than proving that the isolated word names the soul as its object.

Contextually, however, this reading requires further explanation.

The verse does not say that the speakers are angels, nor does it name the soul as the object of rāqin.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:26 does connect reaching the collarbones with dying, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:30 speaks of being driven toward the Lord.

These connections provide a setting for examining the second reading, but do not prove that rāqin must be an angel taking the soul upward.

The alternative is linguistically open to consideration; identifying its speaker and object depends on further indications.

Which reading is preferable?

In the sequence of Al-Qiyāmah 75:26, Al-Qiyāmah 75:27, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:28, a request for a healer or reciter of ruqyah seems more natural.

The scene pictures approaching death, when hope of treatment and rescue is fading.

A question about someone able to do something for the person directly fits that situation.

The soul’s ascent is also compatible with the word’s structure, but identifying angels as speakers and the soul as the object requires additional explanation.

The base translation can therefore choose the appeal for treatment while retaining the alternative in the lexical discussion.

The second reading should not be declared linguistically impossible merely because the first is better known.

Does this verse endorse ruqyah?

Al-Qiyāmah 75:27 reports speech in a scene of approaching death.

Reporting it is not itself a command to perform ruqyah or a guarantee of its effects.

Nor does the verse establish that every kind of ruqyah is prohibited.

It concerns the human condition at death’s threshold and the inability to prevent separation. It does not legislate a method of treatment.

مَنْ رَاقٍ therefore does not establish a particular method of Qur’anic recitation, blowing, repeating phrases a fixed number of times, or charging for ruqyah.

Those are separate questions requiring their own evidence.

Does al-tarāqī belong to the same root?

Al-Qiyāmah 75:26 contains التَّرَاقِي.

It is the plural of تَرْقُوَة and denotes the collarbones at the upper chest near the neck and shoulders.

The resemblance between al-tarāqī and tarqā should not lead to treating them as the same word.

Tarqā concerns ascending; al-tarāqī here names parts of the body.

In the morphological classification used for this Qur’anic count, al-tarāqī is assigned to T R Q and excluded from the four R Q Y occurrences. Earlier lexical discussions nevertheless differ over the status of its initial tāʾ, so this classification should not be presented as unanimous historical derivation.

بَلَغَتِ التَّرَاقِي should therefore not be translated as “reached the stages of advancement” or “reached spiritual heights.”

The context concerns dying and reaching the collarbones.

6. Related roots and translation proposals

R Q Y (ruqiyy) and Ṣ ʿ D (ṣuʿūd)

Both roots concern ascending.

Ṣuʿūd is used in the Qur’an for upward movement and also for traversing a difficult ascent.

In Fāṭir 35:10, يَصْعَدُ describes the ascent of good speech.

R Q Y likewise denotes ascending; its Qur’anic uses include a demand to ascend into heaven and a challenging imperative to ascend along means of access.

They share a field, but one cannot establish an absolute rule that ṣuʿūd always involves difficulty while ruqiyy always means easy or gradual ascent.

R Q Y (ruqiyy) and ʿ R J (ʿurūj)

ʿUrūj denotes ascending in the Qur’an.

Al-Maʿārij 70:4 speaks of the ascent of the angels and the spirit; Al-Sajdah 32:5 contains يَعْرُجُ إِلَيْهِ.

R Q Y and ʿ R J can be close in the direction of movement, but remain distinct roots.

This overlap does not make every R Q Y usage concern angelic ascent, or every ʿ R J usage mean climbing a ladder.

R Q Y (ruqiyy) and D R J (daraj)

Daraj and darajāt concern levels and stages.

Al-Anʿām 6:132 speaks of degrees corresponding to people’s deeds.

Marqāh can also denote a step or means of ascent in Arabic.

The presence of steps and stages in an image does not mean that R Q Y necessarily expresses gradual, stepwise movement in every use.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:93, such details cannot be extracted from the verb itself.

R Q Y (ruqyah) and Sh F Y (shifāʾ)

These families can meet in discussions of treatment but have different meanings.

Ruqyah denotes reciting words or a protective formula to avert harm and seek recovery.

Shifāʾ concerns recovery and the removal of illness or injury.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:82 describes the Qur’an as healing for believers.

That does not make ruqyah and shifāʾ synonymous, or turn every use of shifāʾ into a reference to a ruqyah ritual.

Performing ruqyah does not itself establish that recovery has occurred.

Translation proposals for Forqan

تَرْقَىٰ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

Suitable rendering: “you ascend.”

“Or ascend into heaven” is a natural and clear translation of أَوْ تَرْقَىٰ فِي السَّمَاءِ.

لِرُقِيِّكَ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

The Persian equivalents are “in your ascent” or “for your ascent,” depending on the construction; in English the complete clause is most naturally expressed with “in your ascent.”

وَلَنْ نُؤْمِنَ لِرُقِيِّكَ can be rendered “and we will not believe in your ascent.”

فَلْيَرْتَقُوا — Ṣād 38:10

Suitable renderings: “then let them ascend” or “then they should ascend.”

In the complete sentence, “then let them ascend by the ways of access” preserves the verb and its relation to al-asbāb.

رَاقٍ — Al-Qiyāmah 75:27

Preferred contextual rendering: “a healer” or “someone who recites ruqyah for healing.”

Forqan’s current “Who can heal?” conveys the general intention of the first reading well.

For greater lexical specificity, “Who will recite ruqyah?” is also defensible, but ruqyah needs to be explained.

“Who will take the soul upward?” is presented in the root study as another possibility, not a conclusive rendering of the verse.

7. Synthesis and final definition

R Q Y has two principal Arabic branches, both important to its Qur’anic uses.

The first concerns ascending. رَقِيَ, ارْتَقَى, and their derivatives describe upward movement or reaching a higher rank.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:93, tarqā and ruqiyyika concern a demand for the messenger’s ascent into heaven. In Ṣād 38:10, fa-l-yartaqū challenges those speaking as though they had a claim or objection concerning divine authority and sovereignty.

The second branch, ruqyah, concerns reciting words or protective formulas to avert harm and seek treatment. It is also attested in transmitted pre-Islamic poetry through forms such as rāqin and ruqāh.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:27, the structure of rāqin can relate to either branch.

The approaching-death scene favors the request for a healer or reciter. The soul’s ascent is also linguistically open to consideration, but identifying the speaker and the object requires further indications.

Final definition of R Q Y:

This linguistic family has two distinct branches:

1. Ascending to a higher place or rank, whether through bodily movement or by extension to status and standing.

2. Reciting ruqyah or protective formulas for someone afflicted by illness or injury: a recognized Arabic linguistic and cultural practice whose actual therapeutic efficacy is not part of the word’s meaning.

Early lexical discussions also mention terms for raised ground, including the wāw-final raqwah. Their morphological distinction should be preserved, and neither principal branch should be given a certain origin in these terrain terms.

An apparently comprehensive definition such as “raising and improving a condition” would therefore be imprecise: it could mistakenly turn the ruqyah branch into guaranteed recovery or spiritual advancement.

Each derivative should be understood separately: tarqā and irtaqā concern ascent in the Qur’anic uses; ruqiyy names that act; rāqin in Al-Qiyāmah is a point where two interpretations are examined through morphology and context.

Keeping the distinction preserves the clear meaning of the ascent passages and prevents imposing a therapeutic or supernatural interpretation on the whole family without evidence.
