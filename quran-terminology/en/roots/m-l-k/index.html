Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root M L K

Ownership, kingship, authority, sovereignty, power of disposal, and the dependence of every ownership and dominion upon The God

Forms Covered from This Root

In this page, the root م ل ک (m l k) is examined semantically, then the most important Qur’anic forms from this root are explained separately:

  • مَلَكَ / يَمْلِكُ (malaka / yamliku) — Form I verb; he owned, possessed, had power of disposal, owns, or has authority
  • مَالِك / مَٰلِك (mālik / maālik) — active participle; owner, master, authority-holder, true possessor
  • مَلْك (malk) — noun; rule, kingship, or governing authority in a specific usage
  • مَلِك / مُلُوك (malik / mulūk) — noun; king, ruler, kings
  • مَلِيك (malīk) — noun/adjective; mighty sovereign, one possessing kingship and authority
  • مُلْك (mulk) — noun/verbal noun; dominion, kingdom, sovereignty, kingship, broad ownership, or domain of authority
  • مَلَك / مَلَائِكَة (malak / malāikah) — noun; angel / angels, beings commissioned within the system of divine command
  • مَلَكُوت (malakūt) — noun; malakūt, the hidden and deep domain of divine sovereignty and authority
  • مَمْلُوك (mamlūk) — passive participle; one owned by another, a servant, or one who does not possess independent authority

Note: Some words such as مَكَانَة، مَكَّنَ، يُمَكِّنُ (makānah، makkana، yumakkinu) may be semantically close to power, establishment, and enabling, but in Qur’anic root classification they belong under the root م ك ن (m k n), not م ل ک (m l k). Therefore, they are not counted in this page as direct forms of the root م ل ک (m l k).

Also, مِلْك (milk) exists as a known Arabic word, but in the Qur’anic forms of this root, the central Qur’anic form connected to dominion and broad ownership is مُلْك (mulk). Our focus is on the Qur’anic forms of the root م ل ک (m l k).

General Meaning of the Root M L K

The root م ل ک (m l k) in the Qur’an points to the semantic field of ownership, kingship, authority, power, power of disposal, sovereignty, and having something in one’s hand. This root is not limited to simple “having.” In the Qur’an, ownership is connected with authority, responsibility, power, command, and accountability.

When someone owns something, the relation is not merely outward. A certain right of disposal, governance, or authority over that thing is implied. But the Qur’an makes a fundamental distinction between human ownership and the ownership of The God. Human ownership is limited, temporary, dependent, and entrusted; but the ownership of The God is real, complete, independent, and final.

From the Qur’anic view, no human being owns anything absolutely: not his own life, not his wealth, not his power, not his time, and not even the opportunities placed in his hand. Whatever the human being has has in reality been entrusted to him. He may dispose of it, but he must answer for it.

In relation to The God, the root م ل ک (m l k) points to one of the foundations of tawḥīd: dominion, ownership, sovereignty, and final return all belong to The God. If the human being forgets this truth, he may see himself as an independent owner, and from there oppression, arrogance, tyranny, and corruption begin.

Ownership: Beyond Simple Having

In everyday language, ownership is usually understood as “having.” A person says: my house, my wealth, my body, my time, my power, my position. But the Qur’an raises the human gaze beyond this level.

In the Qur’an, ownership always receives meaning in relation to The God. Even if the human being has something in his possession, that possession is not absolute. Wealth, body, intellect, family, social position, and political power all ultimately stand under the ownership of The God.

For this reason, human ownership must be understood as trust. The human being owns, but he is not an independent owner. He has authority, but his authority is limited and answerable. He may use what is in his hand, but he has no right to oppress, waste, humiliate, or see himself as the final possessor.

When ownership is separated from remembrance of The God, it becomes an illusion of independence. The human being imagines, “This is mine,” while neither its arrival was by his own absolute control, nor its remaining, nor its departure, nor its final accounting.

So the root م ل ک (m l k) returns the human being to a great truth: every human ownership is a limited and temporary shadow; true ownership belongs to The God.

Malaka / Yamliku: Having Authority and Power of Disposal

The verbal forms مَلَكَ (malaka) and يَمْلِكُ (yamliku) point to owning, possessing, or having power of disposal. This usage shows that ownership in the Qur’an is not merely a title; power, authority, and responsibility are present within it.

Sometimes the Qur’an speaks of those who “do not own” anything: meaning they do not possess the power to repel harm, bring benefit, control death and life, or hold final command. This kind of expression breaks the illusion of false powers.

People may imagine that kings, idols, intermediaries, wealth, or social powers own destiny. But the Qur’an repeatedly shows that other than The God, before the final truth, owns nothing except what The God has permitted or placed in their hands in a limited way.

So the verbs of this root pull the human being out of deception by the appearance of power. Whoever has something in his hand should ask: Is this authority essential or given? Is it permanent or temporary? Is it used for justice or for oppression?

Mālik: Owner, Authority-Holder, and Final Possessor

مَالِك (mālik) means owner: the one who has something in his possession and has a right of disposal over it. But when this word is used for The God, its meaning becomes complete and absolute.

The God is Mālik because everything is originally from Him, depends on Him, returns to Him, and has no independence without Him. The ownership of The God is not the ownership of one thing among other things; His ownership is ownership over the root of existence, time, life, death, living, accountability, and return.

In relation to the human being, ownership may be real but limited. A person may own a house, wealth, tools, or a social right, but this ownership is not essential. He is not the creator of that thing, not its final preserver, and not the owner of its return.

So when مَالِك (mālik) is used for The God, it pulls the human being out of the illusion of absolute ownership. The human being understands that what he has is a trust, and final ownership returns to The God.

Mālik Yawm al-Dīn in Surah Al-Fatihah

In Surah Al-Fatihah, after al-raḥmān al-raḥīm, the expression mālik yawm al-dīn appears:

مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ

This expression is very deep. The God is not only the owner of the world; He is also the Owner of the Day of Dīn. The Day of Dīn is the day of accountability, recompense, judgment, and the disclosure of the result of deeds. On that day, the outward ownerships of the human being collapse, and true ownership becomes manifest.

In the world, human beings possess forms of ownership: wealth, rank, government, family, body, speech, choice, and opportunity. But on the Day of Dīn, none of these ownerships remains independent. The human being comes with his deeds, but he does not own the final judgment.

مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ (māliki yawmi al-dīni) means that Day is in the authority of The God: not tribe, not class, not religious scholar, not king, not wealthy person, not political power, and not even the human being’s own image of himself. Final judgment belongs to The God.

This sentence in Al-Fatihah awakens the human being. After recognizing the mercy of The God, the human being learns that mercy is not separated from accountability. The God is The All-Merciful and The Especially Merciful, but He is also the Owner of the Day of Dīn. So conscious servanthood must carry both hope and responsibility.

The Difference Between Mālik and Malik

In the Qur’an, we have two close but different words: مَالِك (mālik) and مَلِك (malik).

مَالِك (mālik) emphasizes ownership, having, authority-holder status, and the right of disposal. مَلِك (malik) emphasizes kingship, rule, sovereignty, and political or governing authority.

These two meanings are close, but they are not identical. Someone may own something without being a king. And someone may be a king without being the true owner of everything. In relation to The God, both meanings are gathered in Him completely: He is both Owner and Sovereign.

For understanding Al-Fatihah, this distinction matters. The expression مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ (māliki yawmi al-dīni) emphasizes that the Day of Accountability is in the complete ownership and authority of The God. That Day is not only under His command; it belongs to Him, is in His hand, and no one stands before Him with independent ownership.

So مَالِك (mālik) and مَلِك (malik) are two different windows into one great truth: true ownership and sovereignty, in the end, belong to The God.

Malik, Mulūk, and Malīk: Kingship and Sovereignty

مَلِك (malik) means king or ruler, and مُلُوك (mulūk) is its plural. The Qur’an speaks of kings and rulers, but the Qur’anic view of sovereignty is always joined with responsibility and limitation.

Human kingship can be an instrument of order and justice, but it can also become tyranny, arrogance, Pharaoh-like domination, and corruption. When a ruler sees himself as the absolute owner of people, his rule turns into oppression.

مَلِيك (malīk) is also a stronger form in this field: a mighty sovereign. When this meaning is attributed to The God, His sovereignty and authority are complete, just, and without need. The God is Sovereign, but His sovereignty is not like human sovereignty built on need, fear, ignorance, or rivalry.

In the Qur’an, human kingship always stands under the kingship of The God. No king is independent. Every sovereignty is either a trust, a test, or the ground of downfall. Whoever has power must know that his power is not final ownership; it is responsibility.

Mulk: Domain of Authority and Sovereignty

مُلْك (mulk) is one of the most important words from this root. It points to rule, sovereignty, power, domain of authority, or broad ownership.

When the Qur’an says لِلَّهِ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ (lillahi mulku al-samāwāti wālarḍi), it is not speaking only of material ownership. It means the sovereignty, governance, law, power, and authority of the heavens and the earth belong to The God.

Human mulk is limited. A king, state, people, or individual may have power within a boundary, but that power is not essential, permanent, or independent. The God gives, takes, raises, lowers, and returns everyone to account.

So مُلْك (mulk) in the Qur’an pulls the human being out of deception by power. Every power that sees itself as independent from The God is false. Every mulk that does not reach justice, trust, and accountability becomes fitnah.

Malak and Malā’ikah: Angels Within the System of Divine Command

مَلَك (malak) and مَلَائِكَة (malāikah) also appear in the Qur’an under this root, but they must be explained carefully. An angel should not be understood as “owner” or “king.” In Qur’anic usage, this word points to beings who are commissioned, obedient, and dependent upon The God within the system of divine command.

In the Qur’an, angels have power, mission, and roles in divine governance, but they are not independent. They do not own the command; they are commissioned by the command. They do not stand in the station of rubūbiyyah, divinity, or independent authority.

This point is very important for tawḥīd. The human being must not place an angel, prophet, jinn, holy person, or any unseen being in the station of independent ownership and sovereignty. Angels too are servants of The God and agents within His system.

So the presence of مَلَك / مَلَائِكَة (malak / malāikah) in the field of the root م ل ک (m l k) reminds us that even powerful unseen beings have no independent ownership before The God.

Malakūt: The Depth of The God’s Sovereignty and Command

مَلَكُوت (malakūt) is one of the very deep words of this root. It points to the deep, hidden, and vast domain of The God’s sovereignty: where the human being does not see only the outward surface of the world, but recognizes the hidden governance, sovereignty, and divine power behind things.

Malakūt can be understood as the deeper inner layer of mulk; not meaning something separate from the world, but meaning seeing the deeper truth of The God’s sovereignty behind the outward appearance of things.

When the human being sees only the outward surface of the world, he may imagine powers, causes, individuals, and states as independent. But looking toward malakūt takes the human being behind the scene: everything is under the command, measure, knowledge, and will of The God.

So مَلَكُوت (malakūt) moves the human being from the surface of appearances toward the depth of truth. The world is not merely a collection of disconnected things; it is a world standing within the domain of The God’s sovereignty.

Mamlūk: One Who Has No Independent Authority

مَمْلُوك (mamlūk) is a passive participle: someone or something placed in the ownership of another. In the Qur’an, this word is used to show limitation of authority and dependence.

Being mamlūk means that a human being or being does not possess independent authority and absolute ownership. This concept may point at the social level to slavery or human dependence, but at a deeper level, all human beings have no independent ownership before The God.

The Qur’an uses the example of the mamlūk to make clear the difference between one who has authority and one who does not. But this example must not be used to justify oppression or break human dignity. The Qur’an speaks from within an existing social reality, but the final principle is that all human beings are servants before The God and responsible before Him.

So مَمْلُوك (mamlūk) in the root page م ل ک (m l k) reminds us that human ownership and authority are limited, and no human being has the right to turn his ownership into a tool of oppression and humiliation.

Divine Ownership and Human Ownership

The ownership of The God and the ownership of the human being share a name, but they are not on the same level.

The ownership of The God is real, complete, independent, and final. He owns existence, life, death, living, time, accountability, and return. Nothing exists outside His ownership.

Human ownership is limited, relative, temporary, and entrusted. The human being may have something, but he did not bring it from himself, its continuation is not in his hand, and in the end he must answer for it.

This distinction creates the ethics of ownership. If the human being knows that his ownership is a trust, he will deal responsibly with wealth, power, body, family, land, and possibilities. But if he sees himself as an absolute owner, the path to oppression opens.

So the Qur’an does not deny human ownership, but places it under the ownership of The God. The human being is an owner, but an answerable owner; a possessor, but a possessor of trust; an authority-holder, but not an absolute authority-holder.

Ownership, Trust, and Accountability

One of the important messages of the root م ل ک (m l k) is that ownership is joined with accountability. The more the human being has in his hand, the greater his responsibility.

Wealth, power, position, knowledge, body, speech, family, and opportunity are all forms of trust. The human being may have control over them in this world, but on the Day of Dīn he must answer for how he used them.

Here the connection of مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ (māliki yawmi al-dīni) with everyday life becomes clear. If The God is the Owner of the Day of Dīn, then the human being’s ownership today is not without account. Every disposal, every oppression, every gift, every betrayal, and every act of trustworthiness is finally connected to that Day.

Ownership without remembrance of accountability intoxicates the human being. But ownership joined with remembrance of The God makes the human being humble, just, and trustworthy.

Ownership Against the Illusion of Human Independence

The greatest danger of ownership is that the human being imagines he has become independent. When he has wealth, he says: my wealth. When he has power, he says: my command. When he has knowledge, he says: my understanding. When he has government, he says: my people. But the Qur’an breaks this illusion.

The human being is not the owner of his beginning, nor the owner of his end. He does not control the time of his death, nor the final result of his deeds. He lives among trusts and is tested through his choices.

The root م ل ک (m l k) reminds the human being that outward ownership must not become imaginary ownership. Whatever the human being has can be taken from him. Every power can collapse. Every sovereignty can end. But the ownership of The God has no end.

So the healthy path is that the human being uses his ownership for servanthood, justice, service, and reform; not for arrogance, domination, and forgetfulness of The God.

Summary

The root م ل ک (m l k) in the Qur’an invites the human being to recognize ownership, sovereignty, authority, power, kingship, and trust. This root shows that ownership is not merely having; responsibility, accountability, and the right of disposal are also present within it.

مَلَكَ (malaka) and يَمْلِكُ (yamliku) speak of having authority and power of disposal. مَالِك (mālik) speaks of ownership and authority-holder status. مَلِك (malik) and مُلُوك (mulūk) speak of human sovereignty. مَلِيك (malīk) speaks of mighty sovereignty. مُلْك (mulk) speaks of the domain of authority. مَلَك (malak) and مَلَائِكَة (malāikah) speak of angels commissioned within the system of divine command. مَلَكُوت (malakūt) speaks of the depth of The God’s sovereignty and command. And مَمْلُوك (mamlūk) speaks of limitation of authority and dependence.

In Surah Al-Fatihah, مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ (māliki yawmi al-dīni) reminds the human being of a Day when all outward ownerships collapse and true ownership becomes manifest. On that Day, final judgment, final accountability, and final authority belong to The God.

So if the root م ل ک (m l k) is understood correctly, it reforms the human being’s view of wealth, power, sovereignty, authority, and even his own life. The human being learns that his ownership is trust, his power is a test, and his accounting is with The God.

True ownership belongs to The God; and whatever the human being has, he finally returns with it toward the Owner of the Day of Dīn:

مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ
