The root ف ر ث — Rumen contents, scattered contents, and the emergence of pure milk

Qur’anic and lexical forms

The triliteral Arabic root ف ر ث, in its principal livestock-related use, denotes transformed food material within an animal’s rumen. فَرْث names the rumen contents: material into which feed is transformed after swallowing and the beginning of digestion, before it is excreted.

Other branches of this root involve splitting, breaking up, and scattering contents, as when a container is split and its contents spill out, or something is shattered by a blow.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَرْثٍ in al-Naḥl 16:66. The verse speaks of pure, agreeable milk emerging within livestock and places farth alongside blood.

Farth should not be equated without explanation with feces already excreted by an animal. Established Arabic definitions locate it inside the rumen; excreted material represents a different stage of digestion.

1. The root in early Arabic

Farth: transformed contents within the rumen

Early dictionaries define فَرْث as follows:

«الْفَرْثُ: السِّرْجِينُ مَا دَامَ فِي الْكَرِشِ»

Al-farthu: al-sirjīnu mā dāma fī al-karish.

That is: “Farth is dung-like material while it remains in the rumen.”

The qualification مَا دَامَ فِي الْكَرِشِ is essential: for as long as the material remains inside the rumen.

The Arabic word كَرِش denotes the rumen, particularly the large digestive compartment of ruminant animals.

Cattle, sheep, and goats are examples of ruminants whose plant feed is transformed within their digestive systems. Microbial activity in the rumen and digestion in subsequent compartments prepare nutrients for absorption.

What Arabic speakers called farth concerned these transformed contents inside the rumen.

Farth is therefore neither grass the animal has yet to eat, nor the animal’s blood, nor necessarily excreted dung.

Suitable equivalents include “partly digested feed in the rumen” and “rumen digesta.”

The historical standing of the definition

This meaning is recorded in early lexicographical material, including reports attributed to al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, and subsequently in later dictionaries. These accounts explicitly define farth by its location in the rumen.

Nevertheless, recording a meaning in an Islamic-period dictionary differs from directly attesting that meaning in pre-Islamic poetry.

This study has not established a secure independent example of farth in poetry whose attribution and pre-Qur’anic date can be verified. No definitive claim is therefore based on such an example.

It does not follow that every semantic detail recorded in dictionaries circulated in precisely the same form and scope throughout the pre-Islamic period.

The word’s occurrence in the Qur’an, however, directly attests Arabic of the revelation period. Early lexical accounts also show that established Arabic usage associated farth with livestock digesta.

2. Splitting and scattering: another branch of the root

Arabic sources also record the verb فَرَثَ for splitting something and scattering its contents.

For example, فَرَثَ الْجُلَّةَ describes splitting a date container or basket and scattering what it holds.

Here جُلَّة means a container or basket used to store dates.

When the container is split and the dates spill out, the verb faratha can describe this action.

Lexical reports attributed to Ibn al-Sikkīt explain the expression through splitting the container and scattering its contents.

Another expression is:

«فَرَثَ كَبِدَهُ»

Faratha kabidahu.

That is: “He crushed and shattered his liver.”

This expression is recorded both for severe physical injury and figuratively for pain or sorrow that seems to tear a person apart inwardly.

For example, فَرَثَ الْحُبُّ كَبِدَهُ means that love or attachment caused someone intense suffering.

It does not mean that affection literally broke the liver into separate pieces. The image of crushing and disintegration conveys the intensity of suffering.

Lexicons also record أَفْرَثَ أَصْحَابَهُ of someone who exposes companions to blame, disgrace, or trouble.

These uses relate to injury, dispersal, and the exposure of what lies inside.

Ibn Fāris likewise emphasizes breaking up and disintegration in his account of this family.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished: these uses are recorded within one lexical family; this does not establish that one historically developed from another.

There may be an imagistic connection between the breakdown of rumen material and the scattering of a container’s contents. That resemblance alone does not establish the root’s derivational history.

Farth in al-Naḥl 16:66 should therefore not be translated as “scattering.” In that verse it names particular material in an animal’s digestive system.

3. Linguistic forms and central meanings

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • فَرْث: rumen digesta.
  • فُرُوث: plural of farth.
  • فُرَاثَة: rumen contents or material removed from it.
  • فَرَثَ: split and scattered the contents; in another use, caused something to break up or shatter.
  • أَفْرَثَ: emptied out contents; in certain social uses, exposed others to trouble or disgrace.
  • فَرَّثَ: crushed or shattered.
  • اِنْفَرَثَ: broke apart or disintegrated.
  • فَرِثَ: in another use, became sated or full of food.

Some sources also record forms concerning nausea during pregnancy under this root. These uses relate to digestion and changes in an internal bodily state, but independent evidence is needed before deriving them all from an animal’s rumen.

Of these forms, the Qur’an uses only the noun فَرْثٍ.

It is therefore useful to retain two levels when defining this family:

The principal Qur’anic sense: transformed, partly digested feed in livestock rumens.

The verbal branch recorded in Arabic: splitting, scattering contents, and crushing or shattering something.

An imagistic relationship between these branches can be explored, but should not be presented as a certain historical origin.

4. Qur’anic usage: pure milk from between digesta and blood

Al-Naḥl 16:66

وَإِنَّ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَنْعَامِ لَعِبْرَةً ۖ نُسْقِيكُمْ مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ

Wa-inna lakum fī al-anʿāmi la-ʿibratan; nusqīkum mimmā fī buṭūnihi min bayni farthin wa-damin labanan khāliṣan sāʾighan li-l-shāribīn.

Translation: “And indeed, in livestock there is a lesson for you. We give you to drink from what is within them, from between digesta and blood: pure milk, agreeable to those who drink.”

The verse addresses a familiar phenomenon: an animal eats feed that is digested and transformed within its body, yet its body also yields milk, differing in appearance, taste, and properties from rumen contents and blood.

The Qur’an places three things alongside one another:

فَرْث — digestive material within the rumen.

دَم — blood.

لَبَن — milk.

These three words do not name one and the same substance.

Farth concerns digestive contents, dam denotes blood, and laban names milk.

The verse draws attention to milk within livestock, distinct from the digesta and blood mentioned alongside it and suitable for drinking. Explaining the bodily processes connecting them is a separate biological question.

Does farth mean feces?

Some translations render فَرْث as “dung” or “feces.”

These renderings relate to lexical accounts insofar as farth is dung-like material produced through the transformation of feed.

In ordinary modern usage, however, “dung” and “feces” commonly suggest material that has already left the animal’s body.

This difference can cause misunderstanding.

The Qur’an says مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ: from what is inside the animal’s body.

Established Arabic definitions likewise emphasize farth’s location in the rumen.

In this context, “rumen digesta” or “partly digested rumen contents” is therefore more precise than “feces.”

Following the feed’s passage clarifies the distinction. Grass eaten by a cow is transformed in its digestive system. Some resulting substances are absorbed to nourish the body, while unabsorbed material is eventually excreted.

In Qur’anic usage, farth refers to material still within this process, rather than merely its final excreted product.

What does مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ mean?

The expression مِنْ بَيْنِ is significant here.

بَيْن expresses a relationship between things, but that relationship need not always place one body at a geometrical midpoint between two others.

Here مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ associates the emergence of milk with digesta and blood. The expression alone does not specify the precise physiological mechanism of milk production.

It should not be read as describing a bodily container with blood on one side and farth on the other, from whose exact intervening space milk flows out.

Nor should milk be imagined as a direct mixture of blood and rumen contents.

The verse itself then calls the milk “pure,” distinguishing it from the other two materials.

The sentence presents distinct, drinkable milk within the animal’s bodily and nutritional system.

Determining the physiological details requires independent knowledge of animal biology.

5. Digesta, blood, and milk in present biological knowledge

In ruminants, plant feed enters the digestive system and undergoes microbial breakdown and fermentation in the rumen.

Some resulting substances are absorbed through the rumen wall. Other portions of the feed are broken down and absorbed later in the digestive tract.

Absorbed nutrients reach different bodily organs through the blood.

In a lactating animal, mammary glands use substances supplied by blood to produce milk components. Some components are synthesized in mammary cells, while others are supplied through material transferred to those cells.

Milk is thus neither a direct mixture of blood and rumen contents nor undigested material turned into milk without transformation.

Digestion, absorption, nutrient transport, and milk production are connected processes within the animal.

Understanding them can help readers consider the verse’s image more precisely.

The word farth itself, however, does not mean microbial fermentation, nutrient absorption, or milk production in the mammary gland.

These are biological explanations relevant to the phenomenon to which the verse draws attention.

Present scientific details should therefore not all be treated as the direct meanings of the verse’s individual words.

Conversely, the image’s significance need not be dismissed because its earliest audience lacked detailed biological knowledge. The differences between feed, rumen contents, blood, and milk were observable; modern knowledge explains their relationships more precisely.

6. لَبَنًا خَالِصًا سَائِغًا: three distinct meanings

After mentioning digesta and blood, the Qur’an describes milk with two adjectives:

خَالِصًا and سَائِغًا.

Neither adjective belongs to ف ر ث; each has an independent meaning.

The purity of milk

Khāliṣ belongs to خ ل ص and here concerns milk distinguished from admixture with the other materials mentioned.

Milk results from internal bodily processes, but normally does not emerge as a visible mixture of blood and rumen contents.

Purity here thus relates to milk’s separation and distinction from those materials.

Khāliṣ should not, however, be taken to mean that raw milk is absolutely free of microbes or contamination.

Milk safety is a health matter involving animal health, milking conditions, storage, and appropriate safety processes.

These questions are not identical to the lexical meaning of khāliṣ in the verse.

The agreeableness of milk

Sāʾigh belongs to س و غ and concerns a drink passing down easily and pleasantly.

سَائِغًا لِلشَّارِبِينَ describes milk agreeable to those who drink it.

This description likewise is not the meaning of farth.

The verse both associates milk with the animal’s internal bodily setting and describes the resulting drink’s qualities.

Someone who has seen an animal’s rumen contents can readily appreciate how different they are from white, agreeable milk.

This difference does not mean that every component of milk is chemically unrelated to feed or blood. Milk’s constituents are supplied through natural bodily activities.

The relationship, together with the evident distinction, invites reflection.

7. Connections with other Qur’anic verses

Al-Muʾminūn 23:21: a lesson in livestock

Al-Muʾminūn 23:21

وَإِنَّ لَكُمْ فِي الْأَنْعَامِ لَعِبْرَةً ۖ نُسْقِيكُمْ مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهَا وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَنَافِعُ كَثِيرَةٌ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

Wa-inna lakum fī al-anʿāmi la-ʿibratan; nusqīkum mimmā fī buṭūnihā wa-lakum fīhā manāfiʿu kathīratun wa-minhā taʾkulūn.

Translation: “And indeed, in livestock there is a lesson for you. We give you to drink from what is within them; in them you have many benefits, and from them you eat.”

This verse shares its opening with al-Naḥl 16:66; both mention a drink obtained from within livestock.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:21, however, does not use farth or include the description involving blood and pure milk.

Instead, it draws attention to other benefits livestock provide for people.

Despite their shared subject, the verses therefore do not have identical emphases.

Al-Naḥl highlights milk emerging in association with distinct bodily materials.

Al-Muʾminūn places the drink alongside livestock’s other benefits.

The difference between بُطُونِهِ and بُطُونِهَا

Al-Naḥl 16:66 says:

«مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهِ»

Mimmā fī buṭūnihi.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:21 instead says:

«مِمَّا فِي بُطُونِهَا»

Mimmā fī buṭūnihā.

In both verses the underlying referent is الْأَنْعَام, livestock.

This variation in pronouns relates to different Arabic ways of referring to collective and generic nouns. A collection may be considered through its generic designation or as a group.

Without further evidence, the masculine and feminine pronouns should not be used to infer that the verses discuss wholly different kinds of animals.

Milk production concerns lactating females, but the general designation الْأَنْعَام refers to livestock collectively; not every member of the collection must produce milk.

Al-Naḥl 16:69: the honeybee’s drink

Al-Naḥl 16:69 likewise speaks of a drink emerging from within honeybees.

This connection is noteworthy in the surah’s context.

Al-Naḥl 16:66 concerns livestock’s drink.

Al-Naḥl 16:69 concerns a drink resulting from honeybees’ activity.

Milk and honey are not produced through identical processes, and farth does not appear in the honeybee verse.

Its meaning should not be transferred to honey production; the shared reference to emergence “from within” does not establish a shared production mechanism.

The main connection is attention to blessings and natural processes within creation.

8. Comparison with nearby roots and words

ف ر ث (فَرْث) and ر و ث (رَوْث)

Rawth in Arabic denotes animal dung or excreted material.

By contrast, the established definition of farth concerns dung-like material still inside the rumen.

The words relate through material and digestion, but do not identify the same stage or location.

“Rumen digesta” is therefore more precise in al-Naḥl 16:66 than “excreted feces.”

ف ر ث (فَرْث) and ب ع ر (بَعْر)

Baʿr is used for certain animals’ dung, particularly recognizable pellets or pieces.

It concerns excreted material.

Farth in the Qur’anic verse instead denotes internal digestive contents.

The form and condition of excreted dung should therefore not be inserted into the verse as farth’s direct definition.

ف ر ث (فَرْث) and ر ف ث (رَفَث)

These roots have similar letters, but the positions of fāʾ and rāʾ differ.

ف ر ث in al-Naḥl 16:66 concerns livestock digesta.

ر ف ث occurs in al-Baqarah 2:187 and al-Baqarah 2:197 in contexts of sexual relations and pilgrimage rites.

Similar letters do not make the roots identical or transfer one root’s meaning to the other.

ف ر ث (فَرْث) and ف ر ت (فُرَات)

These roots may also be confused because they differ by a single letter.

ف ر ت occurs in the Qur’an as furāt, describing fresh, agreeable water.

ف ر ث concerns material inside livestock rumens.

Their appearance in verses about drinks does not establish an etymological connection.

Milk is described as agreeable in al-Naḥl 16:66, and water as furāt in al-Mursalāt 77:27. The milk’s agreeableness, however, comes from sāʾigh, not the root farth.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first challenge is equating farth with feces already outside the body.

Established lexical definitions emphasize its location inside the rumen. Without explanation, “dung” may therefore suggest an inaccurate picture of the process.

The second challenge is imagining digesta and blood mixed directly to make milk.

The verse speaks of pure milk emerging within the animal. Modern biology likewise locates milk production in mammary glands, with the necessary materials supplied through blood.

مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ cannot therefore be used to derive a mechanical recipe for mixing two substances.

The third challenge is making farth a name for all digestive contents of every animal.

Early lexicons particularly associate it with livestock rumens and their contents. Extending it to every digestive compartment or every animal requires independent evidence.

The fourth challenge is constructing a philosophical or ethical definition from farth’s unappealing appearance.

A broader reflection might note that creation’s order yields a valuable blessing such as milk through feed and transformed bodily materials.

Farth itself, however, does not mean moral impurity, sin, corruption, or falsehood.

It is natural material in an animal’s digestive system; human moral qualities should not be defined through it.

Milk’s purity also does not make every other bodily material evil or worthless. Each has its own natural role and place.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for فَرْث in al-Naḥl 16:66 include:

“Rumen digesta,” “partly digested contents of the rumen,” and “transformed feed material within the rumen.”

“Digesta” is shorter for general text, but the first explanation should also clarify the material’s location in the rumen.

“Dung,” given its ordinary modern sense, is insufficient without explanation.

For مِنْ بَيْنِ فَرْثٍ وَدَمٍ, a suitable rendering is:

“From between digesta and blood.”

In the complete translation of al-Naḥl 16:66:

“And indeed, in livestock there is a lesson for you. We give you to drink from what is within them, from between digesta and blood: pure milk, agreeable to those who drink.”

A proposed Forqan word-box formulation would be:

Brief root meaning: rumen contents; in other branches, splitting and scattering contents.

Meaning in this verse: transformed, partly digested feed within livestock rumens, before excretion.

This distinction keeps the root meaning clear without packing the whole discussion of milk production, digestion, and blood circulation into a short word box.

11. Conclusion

In its principal livestock-related use, ف ر ث denotes transformed food material still within the rumen, before excretion.

Early dictionaries also record branches concerning splitting, scattered contents, and disintegration. These uses may share imagery, but their derivational history is not definitively established.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in al-Naḥl 16:66.

The verse places farth alongside blood and speaks of pure, agreeable milk emerging within livestock.

Modern knowledge of digestion and lactation explains how nutrients, after transformation and absorption, reach mammary glands through blood and contribute to milk production. This scientific explanation helps clarify the process without changing farth’s lexical meaning.

The central Qur’anic sense is material within livestock rumens, resulting from the transformation of feed during digestion.

In al-Naḥl 16:66, “rumen digesta” is more precise than “feces” or “excreted dung.”

By recalling the differences between digesta, blood, and milk, the verse invites reflection on a process of creation: how an animal’s complex bodily system, involving feed and blood, provides distinct, pure, agreeable milk for human benefit.
