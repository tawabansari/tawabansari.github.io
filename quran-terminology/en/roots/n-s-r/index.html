From the bird’s name and plucking flesh to Nasr, a deity named in the account of Noah

1. Introducing the root and its semantic branches

The Arabic root ن س ر has several established uses. These include the name of a large flesh-eating bird, plucking or separating something, especially flesh with a beak, and other uses concerning birds’ beaks, parts of a horse’s hoof, and cavalry detachments.

As an animal name, نَسْر denotes a large bird usually called a vulture in English. It uses its powerful beak to tear flesh from a carcass.

Early Arabic distinguishes nasr from عُقَاب, the eagle. The eagle was associated especially with hunting and powerful talons, whereas early descriptions present nasr as a large scavenging bird that relies more on its beak to separate flesh.

Nevertheless, early animal names do not always coincide with modern zoological classifications. Historical uses of nasr should therefore not all be assigned to one particular species without examination.

In its verbal branch, نَسَرَ means to pluck, separate, snatch, or remove something. It is particularly attested of a bird plucking a piece of flesh with its beak.

One etymological explanation treats snatching and separating something as an important center of this word family. The connection between plucking flesh and the bird’s behavior is readily understandable as an image.

Yet this connection alone does not prove that the bird’s name necessarily developed from the verb, or the verb from the bird’s name. Establishing their historical order requires independent evidence.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only once, as the proper name Nasr, one of the deities mentioned in the account of Noah’s people.

The distinction matters: the verse does not discuss the natural behavior of a bird. It records a deity’s name among the gods of Noah’s people.

The Qur’anic form

The root ن س ر occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Nūḥ 71:23, in the following form:

وَنَسْرًا — and Nasr.

Here the word is a proper name, neither a verb nor a description meaning to pluck or snatch.

Other Arabic forms in this family include نَسَرَ for plucking and separating, نَسْر for the bird, نُسُور and أَنْسُر as its plurals, مِنْسَر for a bird’s beak, مَنْسِر for a body of horsemen or soldiers, and اِسْتَنْسَرَ for becoming a nasr or growing strong like one.

None of these other verbs or derivatives occurs in the Qur’an.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing its broader Arabic family from its sole actual Qur’anic use.

2. Nasr in early Arabic: the bird, plucking, and other uses

Nasr: a large scavenging bird

Arabic lexical traditions describe nasr as a familiar bird.

Its plural of paucity is أَنْسُر, and its plural of abundance is نُسُور.

Early zoological descriptions present it as a large bird with a powerful beak that feeds on animal carcasses.

Early lexical descriptions distinguish it from hunting birds with strong talons: unlike the eagle, nasr lacks comparable hunting talons, and its toes are likened more to those of birds such as the crow.

This broadly accords with present knowledge of scavenging vultures, though every detail of the early description should not be treated as a precise scientific classification.

In modern Arabic, nasr is also sometimes used more broadly for large birds of prey. In early usage, however, its distinction from the eagle was significant.

“Vulture” is therefore a suitable equivalent when explaining the common noun. For broader usage or historical naming, “large scavenging bird” can also be used.

Nasr: plucking and separating flesh

In Arabic, نَسَرَ الطَّائِرُ اللَّحْمَ means that the bird plucked or tore off flesh with its beak.

نَسَرَ الشَّيْءَ is also attested for plucking, separating, or removing part of something.

One lexical explanation describes the center of this material as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى اخْتِلَاسٍ وَاسْتِلَابٍ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā ikhtilāsin wa-istilābin

Translation:

“An established root indicating snatching and taking something away.”

Plucking a piece of flesh and taking it away is one example of this meaning.

The explanation connects the bird’s action with the act of separating flesh.

Nevertheless, “snatching” and “plucking” should be distinguished.

Snatching can emphasize taking something quickly or suddenly, whereas plucking flesh concerns separating part of a substance.

Both actions may occur together, but they are not complete synonyms.

Nor does every Arabic use of nasr necessarily involve violence, hunting, or wrongful appropriation.

The bird, for example, bears this name even when no act of plucking or snatching is taking place.

مِنْسَر: a bird’s beak

مِنْسَر is attested for the beak of flesh-eating birds, especially birds such as hawks and eagles.

Here the beak is the organ with which a bird grasps and separates flesh.

The semantic connection between nasr as plucking and minsar as the instrument used for it is clear.

Yet minsar should not be restricted to a vulture’s beak. The word is also attested for the beaks of other predatory birds.

In this use, it names a bird’s natural organ, rather than the bird nasr itself.

مَنْسِر: a body of horsemen or soldiers

In another branch of the family, both مَنْسِر and مِنْسَر are attested for a body of horsemen or part of an army.

Lexical accounts disagree about the number of men involved. Some describe a small cavalry detachment; others give larger numbers.

No fixed number can therefore be made part of the word’s essential meaning.

It generally denotes a group involved in an army’s movement or advance.

One etymological explanation connects this use with snatching or taking something, picturing a force that advances swiftly and obtains something.

The image is understandable, but without historical evidence it cannot establish that the military word necessarily developed from a vulture’s flesh-plucking behavior.

A verse attributed to Labīd

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah describes a military encounter:

سَمَا لَهُمُ ابْنُ الْجَعْدِ حَتَّى أَصَابَهُمْ
بِذِي لَجَبٍ كَالطَّوْدِ لَيْسَ بِمَنْسِرِ

samā lahumu ibnu al-jaʿdi ḥattā aṣābahum / bi-dhī lajabin ka-al-ṭawdi laysa bi-mansiri

Translation:

“Ibn al-Jaʿd advanced against them until he struck them with a clamorous army, mountain-like, rather than a small cavalry detachment.”

Here mansir denotes a limited body of troops, contrasted with a large army.

The verse shows that the Arabic word could have a military meaning.

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, but the attribution of this particular verse to him is not entirely undisputed. Dating its composition requires separate examination; it should not be presented without qualification as certainly pre-Qur’anic.

Minsar or mansir in this verse does not mean a bird’s beak. Despite their overlapping forms, the two uses are readily distinguished by context.

Nasr in the sky: two familiar stellar names

Arabic astronomical tradition recognizes the names النَّسْرُ الطَّائِرُ and النَّسْرُ الْوَاقِعُ.

They denote stars or groups of stars represented through the image of the bird nasr.

The first evokes a nasr in flight; the second, a bird that has alighted or is sitting.

The bird’s name is thus used to identify a celestial phenomenon.

This does not mean that every Arabic occurrence of nasr names a star.

Nor do these astronomical names establish that the Qur’anic deity Nasr was necessarily a star or constellation.

Such a connection would require independent historical evidence.

Nasr in pre-Islamic poetry: an image of long life

Arabic poetry and proverbial tradition also associate nasr with longevity.

One familiar story concerns Luqmān ibn ʿĀd and a succession of these birds. In the story, Luqmān wished to live as long as several nasrs in succession, and his last bird was named Lubad.

The story became a literary image of the desire for long life and, ultimately, life’s impermanence.

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says in a poem:

أَلَمْ تَرَ لُقْمَانَ بْنَ عَادٍ تَتَابَعَتْ
عَلَيْهِ النُّسُورُ ثُمَّ غَابَتْ كَوَاكِبُهْ

a-lam tara luqmāna bna ʿādin tatābaʿat / ʿalayhi al-nusūru thumma ghābat kawākibuh

Translation:

“Have you not seen Luqmān ibn ʿĀd, over whom the vultures passed in succession, before the stars of his life finally set?”

النُّسُور here denotes the birds famed for longevity in Luqmān’s story.

The poet uses the image to recall life’s finitude: even someone measuring his life by several vultures’ lifespans cannot ultimately escape death.

The verse is recorded in Ṭarafah’s collected poetry and clearly illustrates the bird’s name in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet.

The story itself and the combined lifespan of several birds should not, however, be treated as established historical or zoological fact.

The verse’s evidential value concerns linguistic and literary usage, rather than proving a particular lifespan for these birds.

Lubad in the story is also a bird’s name. The poem itself establishes no connection with the deity Nasr in Sūrat Nūḥ.

Nasr as a hard part of a horse’s hoof

Arabic lexical traditions attest نَسْرُ الْحَافِرِ for a hard, projecting part on the underside of a horse’s hoof.

Some lexical descriptions liken it to a hard kernel or pebble.

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā says:

سَوَاهِمُ جُذْعَانُهَا كَالْجِلَامِ
قَدْ أَقْرَحَ الْقَوْدُ مِنْهَا النُّسُورَا

sawāhimu judhʿānuhā ka-al-jilāmi / qad aqraḥa al-qawdu minhā al-nusūrā

A translation conveying the sense:

“Lean horses, whose young are like goat kids; repeated driving has made the parts beneath their hooves sore.”

النُّسُور here does not mean birds. It denotes parts of horses’ hooves.

Even the plural nusūr therefore does not always concern birds in Arabic.

The historical relationship between this name and either the bird nasr or the act of plucking remains unclear. Resemblance in appearance or function may have contributed, but it should not be asserted without evidence.

Other uses under this root concern a rope coming apart or a wound opening. Their connection with the separation of parts is understandable, but they do not occur in the Qur’an.

3. The Qur’anic occurrence: Nūḥ 71:23

وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

wa-qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa-lā tadharunna waddan wa-lā suwāʿan wa-lā yaghūtha wa-yaʿūqa wa-nasran

Translation:

“And they said: Do not abandon your gods; and do not abandon Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, or Nasr.”

Nūḥ 71:23

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of the root ن س ر.

Nasr appears beside four other names: Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, and Yaʿūq.

The sentence shows that these are deities whose worship some of Noah’s people urged others to retain.

A. Does نَسْرًا name a bird here?

As a common Arabic noun, nasr names a familiar bird.

In Nūḥ 71:23, however, it functions as a proper name.

The main contextual indication is its position beside four other names after آلِهَتَكُمْ, “your gods.”

The speakers first mention their gods collectively, then list particular names.

Nasr here therefore names a deity that was worshipped, rather than reporting something about a natural bird.

The deity’s name may be connected with the bird’s name. This possibility alone does not prove that the object of worship was a living bird, a vulture-shaped figure, or something possessing every zoological feature of nasr.

A deity’s name and the form or nature of what is worshipped under that name are different questions.

A deity bearing an animal’s name, for instance, may have a related form or symbol. Establishing this requires historical and archaeological evidence.

The proper name Nasr should therefore be retained in the basic translation.

“Do not abandon the vulture” is unsuitable here, since it may suggest that an actual natural bird is being retained.

B. The grammatical role of وَنَسْرًا

نَسْرًا is accusative in the verse.

The conjunction wāw connects it to the preceding names, within the objects governed by لَا تَذَرُنَّ.

لَا تَذَرُنَّ is an emphatic prohibition from وَذَرَ, meaning “do not leave” or “do not abandon.”

The expression thus means:

“Do not abandon Nasr.”

Nasr is the object of the prohibition against abandonment. That prohibition, however, is quoted from people urging others to retain their gods.

It must not be mistaken for a direct divine command to worship Nasr.

The Qur’an reports a group’s speech and criticizes their conduct in the sūrah’s wider context.

The accusative ending and nunation of نَسْرًا do not themselves reveal the deity’s physical nature. They indicate the noun’s grammatical place in the sentence.

C. Why do five names follow آلِهَتَكُمْ?

The verse first says:

لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ

lā tadharunna ālihatakum

“Do not abandon your gods.”

It then states five names individually.

This may highlight particular deities that held special standing among the people.

Grammatically and rhetorically, naming particular instances after a general category may signal their importance to the speakers.

This construction alone does not establish that Noah’s people had only these five gods and worshipped nothing else.

Nor does their order establish a ranking of power or importance without independent evidence.

The verse lists five names; it does not provide a complete account of the people’s belief system.

D. The preceding verses: following wealth and power

Nūḥ 71:21 says:

قَالَ نُوحٌ رَبِّ إِنَّهُمْ عَصَوْنِي وَاتَّبَعُوا مَنْ لَمْ يَزِدْهُ مَالُهُ وَوَلَدُهُ إِلَّا خَسَارًا

qāla nūḥun rabbi innahum ʿaṣawnī wa-ittabaʿū man lam yazidhu māluhu wa-waladuhu illā khasāran

Translation:

“Noah said: My Lord, they have disobeyed me and followed one whose wealth and children have increased him only in loss.”

The next verse says:

وَمَكَرُوا مَكْرًا كُبَّارًا

wa-makarū makran kubbāran

“And they devised an enormous scheme.”

Nūḥ 71:22

Verse 23 then reports the command not to abandon the gods.

The sequence shows that more is at issue than the mere existence of several names in a society.

Following particular people, wealth and social power, scheming, and urging the retention of the gods appear within a shared narrative setting.

Together, these suggest that mistaken religious beliefs may be associated with social influence and the conduct of leaders or powerful people.

The passage does not explicitly identify the social class associated with each deity, or who first chose their names.

Nor does the preceding mention of scheming establish that every follower knowingly intended to deceive others.

Important differences may exist between people who deliberately promote a belief and those who inherit it from their surroundings.

Those differences require clear evidence; they cannot be extracted from the meaning of nasr itself.

E. وَقَدْ أَضَلُّوا كَثِيرًا: misguidance and responsibility

The following verse says:

وَقَدْ أَضَلُّوا كَثِيرًا وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا ضَلَالًا

wa-qad aḍallū kathīran wa-lā tazidi al-ẓālimīna illā ḍalālan

Translation:

“They have indeed led many astray; and increase the wrongdoers only in straying.”

Nūḥ 71:24

The sentence speaks of many people being led astray.

The plural pronoun may refer to those urging people to retain their gods. Exegetical explanations also discuss attributing the misguidance to the deities themselves, as objects of misleading worship.

In either case, the root ن س ر itself does not mean to mislead.

أَضَلُّوا belongs to ض ل ل and expresses leading astray.

Nasr names one of the deities mentioned.

The deity’s name, the conduct of its worshippers or promoters, and the Qur’an’s judgment concerning misguidance must therefore be distinguished.

This prevents nasr from being defined in a dictionary simply as “misleader” or “false god” without explaining that the judgment comes from the verse’s context.

4. Nasr in South Arabian history and the deity’s form

Historical accounts of Nasr

Islamic historical and exegetical traditions mention a deity named Nasr among groups in southern Arabia, especially in connection with Ḥimyar and Dhū al-Kalāʿ.

One exegetical report associates Nasr with Dhū al-Kalāʿ of Ḥimyar.

The other names in the verse are likewise linked in such reports with different Arab groups and tribes.

These reports matter for understanding historical memory and traditions of naming deities in Islamic times.

They do not independently establish every detail of a transmission from Noah’s time to pre-Islamic Arabia.

Some accounts say that these names originally belonged to righteous people. Images were made after their deaths to commemorate them, and later generations worshipped those images.

These accounts may be examined as part of the history of religious interpretation and narration.

Nūḥ 71:23 itself, however, does not say that these five names originally belonged to righteous people or when their images were made.

Such reports should therefore not be added to the Qur’anic text without independent evidence.

Inscriptional evidence for the name Nasr

South Arabian inscriptions attest a name with letters corresponding to Nasr as the name of a deity.

One South Arabian inscription, for example, records Nasr together with ʿAthtar.

This association places the name Nasr in a religious setting. Other South Arabian divine names, such as Wadd, should not be assumed to appear in that same inscription without evidence.

The evidence matters because it shows that the name Nasr in South Arabian religion was not merely invented in exegetical reports several centuries later.

Its inscriptional occurrence independently supports the historical existence of a deity bearing this name.

Two qualifications must nevertheless be retained.

First, recording Nasr’s name in South Arabia does not prove that the same material object or image survived continuously from Noah’s time.

Second, a shared name between the inscriptional deity and Nasr in Noah’s account does not alone reveal the history of the cult’s transmission.

That would require examination of historical continuity, the inscriptions’ dates and languages, and independent evidence about worship practices.

The historical presence of Nasr’s name in South Arabian religions can thus be affirmed more confidently; details connecting it with Noah’s time must still be assessed separately.

Was Nasr represented as a vulture or eagle?

Some historical and exegetical accounts describe the deity Nasr as having the bird’s form.

The explanation is conceivable as a matter of naming, since the same Arabic word denotes a familiar bird.

Large birds of prey also appear in surviving South Arabian artworks and representations.

A bird’s depiction on an ancient object does not alone identify it as the deity Nasr.

It might symbolize power or a family, serve a decorative purpose, or relate to another deity.

Conversely, an inscription naming a deity does not necessarily reveal its image’s appearance.

A connection between Nasr and bird imagery can therefore be considered, but the deity’s bird form must not be treated as certain merely from the lexical meaning of its name.

The Qur’an gives no description of Nasr’s material, size, color, shape, or the place where an image was kept.

Was Nasr a person’s name or a deity’s name?

In Arabic, nasr can also function as a proper name for a person or something else.

Historical traditions discuss the possibility that some deities’ names originated as personal names.

In Nūḥ 71:23, the context itself places Nasr among the gods the people were urged not to abandon.

This does not establish that the original bearer of the name was human.

Nor does it establish that the name belonged to a living bird that people worshipped.

Its clear contextual use is a deity’s name; the origin of the naming and the historical nature of its referent remain separate questions.

5. Comparison with related roots and interpretive difficulties

ن س ر and ن ص ر: the bird’s name and giving help

These roots are close in sound and writing, but independent in Arabic lexical analysis.

Important branches of ن س ر concern the bird’s name and plucking or separating.

ن ص ر concerns helping, assisting, and supporting.

The latter root occurs frequently in the Qur’an.

For example, Muḥammad 47:7 says:

إِنْ تَنْصُرُوا اللَّهَ يَنْصُرْكُمْ

in tanṣurū allāha yanṣurkum

“If you help The God, He will help you.”

This expression belongs to ن ص ر, rather than ن س ر.

Nasr in Nūḥ 71:23 should therefore not be translated as “helper,” “savior,” or “supporter.”

Resemblance between the name Nasr and the word nuṣrah does not establish their historical linguistic relationship.

Study of N Ṣ R

ن س ر and ع ق ب: vulture and eagle

In early Arabic, nasr and ʿuqāb both name large flesh-eating birds.

Established descriptions associate the eagle more with hunting and grasping prey in powerful talons, whereas nasr is associated with carrion and separating flesh with its beak.

This distinction helps explain the bird names.

Modern zoological boundaries should nevertheless not be imposed on every early name without examination.

In Persian literature too, the names for eagles and vultures may sometimes overlap.

In a lexical study of ن س ر, however, “vulture” accords more closely with the early Arabic descriptions.

This comparison does not establish that the Qur’anic Nasr had either bird’s form. The bird’s name is just one linguistic fact relevant to the proper name.

Study of ʿ Q B

ن س ر and ن ز ع: plucking and separating

ن ز ع concerns pulling, extracting, and separating something from its place.

The verbal branch of ن س ر also denotes plucking or separating part of a substance, especially flesh.

The two roots thus overlap in some uses.

In the Qur’an, ن ز ع is used concerning taking away sovereignty, removing rancor, drawing out a hand, and disputes between people.

In contrast, Qur’anic nasr occurs only as a deity’s proper name.

Their lexical overlap does not justify translating Nasr in Nūḥ 71:23 as “extractor” or “snatcher.”

Study of N Z ʿ

ن س ر and ن ش ر: plucking and spreading

ن ش ر concerns opening, spreading, and disseminating, and in other uses bringing to life.

The established verbal sense of ن س ر instead concerns plucking and separating something.

Some lexical accounts also record forms of ن س ر for a rope coming apart or a wound opening, senses that may approach spreading or opening.

This overlap does not make the two roots identical.

نُشِرَتْ in Al-Takwīr 81:10 belongs to ن ش ر, whereas نَسْرًا in Sūrat Nūḥ is a proper name in the ن س ر family.

Their similar spelling should not lead to confusion.

Nasr and the other divine names

Nasr appears beside Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, and Yaʿūq in Nūḥ 71:23.

Some of these names have linguistic connections with Arabic roots bearing other established meanings.

Wadd, for instance, is linguistically connected with love, and Yaghūth with seeking help or coming to someone’s aid.

All five, however, function as proper names in the verse.

A root’s general meaning should not therefore be turned directly into a description of the deity’s nature.

Just as Wadd here is not merely the abstract concept of love, Nasr is not merely a bird in nature.

Their order likewise provides no independent basis for deriving five historical stages, natural forces, or spiritual ranks.

The verse names five deities in the setting of Noah’s people’s worship. Further conclusions need independent evidence.

6. Translation difficulties and proposals for Forqan

The central difficulty is distinguishing the common noun’s lexical meaning from its Qur’anic function as a proper name.

In Arabic, النَّسْر denotes the familiar bird.

In the Qur’an, نَسْرًا names a deity whose worship Noah’s people were urged to retain.

Replacing Nasr with “vulture” might suggest that people were being told not to abandon a natural bird.

“Vulture-shaped idol” would introduce a detail of form that the Qur’an does not state.

“Snatcher” or “extractor” would replace the proper name with a verbal meaning from its root family.

The basic Qur’an translation should therefore retain the name Nasr.

A proposed rendering of Nūḥ 71:23

وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

wa-qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa-lā tadharunna waddan wa-lā suwāʿan wa-lā yaghūtha wa-yaʿūqa wa-nasran

Proposal:

“And they said: Do not abandon your gods; and do not abandon Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, or Nasr.”

This rendering retains all five names and makes clear that the command is quoted from some of Noah’s people.

A lexical explanation may add:

“Nasr: the name of one of the gods of Noah’s people; the Arabic common noun also names a large scavenging bird, such as a vulture.”

This introduces the lexical sense while preserving the proper name’s role.

The brief root explanation and the meaning in this particular verse should nevertheless keep these levels distinct.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root ن س ر has several established uses.

In an important nominal branch, نَسْر names a large scavenging bird, generally called a vulture in English.

Its verbal branch concerns plucking, separating, or snatching something, especially tearing flesh off with a beak.

Related forms such as minsar or mansir denote birds’ beaks and, in another use, bodies of horsemen or soldiers.

Arabic also uses nasr in certain stellar names, while early poetry and stories associate the bird with long life.

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s verse about Luqmān and his successive birds clearly illustrates the bird’s name in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition. The story of several birds’ lifespans is not thereby certain historical or zoological fact.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as the proper name نَسْرًا in Nūḥ 71:23.

The verse reports people urging others not to abandon their gods, including Nasr.

Here nasr is neither a verb nor a description meaning to snatch or pluck. It names a deity beside four others.

Islamic historical traditions associate Nasr with worship in South Arabia, especially among groups connected with Ḥimyar.

Inscriptional evidence also confirms a South Arabian deity with a corresponding name.

This alone does not prove that the same physical image survived from Noah’s time into later historical periods.

Although the deity and the bird share a name, and some reports describe a bird-shaped image, the Qur’an does not state the deity’s form, material, or appearance.

The name’s common lexical meaning, its function as a proper name, the historical existence of worship under that name, and accounts of its origins and form must therefore be distinguished.

The verse’s central contextual issue is not worshipping a bird, but people’s attachment to deities they refused to abandon in response to Noah’s call.

The surrounding narrative associates this attachment with misguidance and disobedience. Misguidance, however, is not the lexical meaning of nasr itself.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

The Arabic root ن س ر concerns the name of a large scavenging bird, plucking and separating something, especially flesh with a beak, and certain uses involving an organ or cavalry detachments.

As an animal name, نَسْر denotes a bird generally called a vulture. Its connection with plucking and separating is understandable as an image, but the historical order of the meanings remains uncertain.

In the Qur’an, نَسْرًا in Nūḥ 71:23 is the proper name of one of the gods of Noah’s people.

The Qur’anic rendering should therefore remain Nasr. The bird sense belongs in the lexical explanation; the deity’s form and nature cannot be determined without independent evidence.

Plucking and separating are important senses in the verbal branch, and vulture is the established common-noun sense; the family’s only Qur’anic use, however, is the proper name Nasr.
