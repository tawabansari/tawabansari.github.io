Core meaning: this Arabic root has several attested branches, chiefly filling and kindling fire. Other uses concern mingling. Qur’anic occurrences describe the sea, the transformation of seas, and people being burned in fire. Filling an oven with fuel offers a possible connection between filling and kindling, but does not establish a single historical origin for every sense.

1. Introducing the root and its early Arabic meaning

S J R has several distinct Arabic uses.

Sajara al-ināʾa means filling a vessel.

Sajara al-nahra concerns filling a watercourse.

Sajara al-tannūra means stoking or kindling an oven; filling it with fuel may accompany that act.

Al-baḥr al-masjūr can denote a filled sea; in fire-related usage, masjūr concerns being kindled.

One possible semantic link is filling something with fuel and then setting it alight; this is not an independently established derivational history.

Historical study must distinguish independently attested early uses from meanings recorded only in later dictionaries.

An early poetic witness: an abundant spring in Labīd’s Muʿallaqah

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

فَتَوَسَّطَا عُرْضَ السَّرِيِّ وَصَدَّعَا
مَسْجُورَةً مُتَجَاوِرًا قُلَّامُهَا

fa-tawassaṭā ʿurḍa al-sariyyi wa-ṣaddaʿā / masjūratan mutajāwiran qullāmuhā

Translation:

“The two entered the stream’s side and parted the waters of a full spring, whose qullām plants grew close together.”

The poet describes two wild animals reaching water.

Al-sariyy denotes a brook or small watercourse.

Masjūrah describes a spring filled with water.

Qullām names a plant associated with water margins.

ʿAyn, spring, is not explicitly stated, but the feminine adjective describes a full spring understood from context.

The image mentions no fire, heat, or ignition.

Masjūrah here means full of water.

It shows that ancient Arabic masjūr did not necessarily mean burning or aflame.

Something filled with water could be described through this family.

The verse is transmitted in Labīd’s Muʿallaqah and its established commentaries. His life across two periods does not independently date each line, but this transmission supports ancient masjūrah usage.

What the witness establishes

Masjūrah describes the spring’s fullness.

The subject is the amount and abundance of water.

The word does not itself make the water boiling, hot, salty, or violently moving.

Nor does masjūrah mean a destructive flood.

A spring or watercourse may be full without overflowing.

Fullness, overflowing, and flowing must be distinguished.

Labīd’s witness clearly attests fullness, but does not alone establish the kindling sense or its historical relationship to filling.

The kindling branch in Arabic

Lexical records give sajara al-tannūra for kindling and heating an oven.

In that activity, fuel is supplied and the fire kindled.

Sajūr denotes fuel used to kindle an oven.

The family was therefore not restricted to filling with water.

Filling a vessel with liquid and kindling an oven are both actual uses.

Dating a pre-Qur’anic development from filling to kindling would require datable evidence.

Lexical records establish usage, but not alone the chronological order of the branches.

A historically grounded definition

Poetry attributed to early Arabic supports the filling sense.

Classical records also give kindling and mingling as distinct or related branches.

A documented definition should therefore retain several branches:

S J R denotes filling in one main usage; kindling fire, especially in an oven, in another; and mingling or companionship in certain further uses.

They cannot all be restricted to one image without adequate historical evidence.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Sajara yasjuru

The basic verb.

Transitively, it can mean filling something.

سَجَرَ الْإِنَاءَ

sajara al-ināʾa

“He filled the vessel.”

The same verb can mean kindling in fire-related usage.

سَجَرَ التَّنُّورَ

sajara al-tannūra

“He kindled the oven.”

The object helps determine the sense.

A vessel or watercourse can call for filling.

An oven calls for kindling or supplying fuel, according to usage.

Sajr and sujūr

Verbal nouns in this family.

With water, they concern filling.

With fire, sajr denotes kindling.

Sujūr is likewise recorded in uses concerning abundant water or fullness.

No one sense should be transferred to every occurrence without regard to its object.

Sajjara yusajjiru

A Form II verb.

Recorded for filling and kindling.

سَجَّرَ التَّنُّورَ

sajjara al-tannūra

“He kindled the oven.”

In the passive:

سُجِّرَ

sujjira

“It was filled,” or, in a fire context, “it was kindled.”

The feminine passive sujjirat occurs in Al-Takwīr 81:6.

Tasjīr

The Form II verbal noun.

It denotes filling or kindling.

The object and context determine the branch intended.

This verbal noun does not occur in this form in the Qur’an.

Masjūr

The basic verb’s passive participle.

With water, it describes something filled.

With fire, it can describe something kindled.

Al-baḥr al-masjūr in Al-Ṭūr 52:6 uses this form.

Lexical records also give a contrasting sense: empty or emptied.

This opposes fullness and is discussed among words with contrary meanings.

Recording a contrary sense does not suffice to prefer it in every verse.

Sājir

The basic verb’s active participle.

In water usage, it is also recorded for a flood filling a place or the place filled by it.

Some usages thus apply this form to either the filling agent or the filled location.

Insajara

A Form VII verb denoting becoming full.

The state of filling is attributed to the subject itself.

It does not occur in the Qur’an.

Sajūr

Fuel or firewood used to kindle an oven. This is the form verified in the lexical evidence consulted; sijār is not retained here as an established equivalent.

Sajūr belongs to the fire branch, but is not a general name for every kind of fire.

Misjarah

An oven implement, specifically a wooden poker used to move or stir the fuel.

Its subject connects it with kindling.

An implement name does not establish the historical origin of the entire family.

Sajīr

Recorded for a close friend or intimate companion.

This belongs to companionship and mingling.

Sajīr meaning friend should not automatically be understood as a fire-kindler or someone who fills things.

Sajrāʾ

An eye description involving mixed coloration, including redness in its white.

This branch concerns mingling.

Neither kindling an oven nor filling a vessel is involved here.

Forms actually occurring in the Qur’an

The root occurs three times in three forms:

  • Al-masjūri in Al-Ṭūr 52:6: passive participle.
  • Sujjirat in Al-Takwīr 81:6: Form II passive past verb.
  • Yusjarūna in Ghāfir 40:72: basic-verb passive imperfect.

Forms such as sajīr, sajūr, sājir, and insajara do not occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic study should center on these three occurrences.

3. Wa-al-baḥri al-masjūri: a filled or kindled sea?

وَالْبَحْرِ الْمَسْجُورِ

wa-al-baḥri al-masjūr

Al-Ṭūr 52:6

Translation under the kindling reading:

“And by the kindled sea.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:6

Al-masjūri is a passive participle modifying al-baḥri.

The phrase belongs to the opening oaths of Al-Ṭūr.

Preceding verses mention the Mount, a written book, the inhabited house, and the raised canopy.

Then comes al-baḥr al-masjūr.

Al-Ṭūr 52:7 states the oaths’ conclusion: the Lord’s punishment will occur.

First reading: a filled, abundant sea

Filled is an attested Arabic meaning of masjūr.

Labīd’s witness also supplies it.

His poem applies masjūrah to a full spring.

Linguistically, al-baḥr al-masjūr can thus denote a sea filled with water.

This begins with an attested sense and does not require the sea to be burning at that moment.

The sea is known for its extent and abundant water.

Masjūr then emphasizes fullness.

Fullness does not itself mean overflowing all boundaries or covering every landmass.

Overflow or inundation requires a further indication.

Second reading: a kindled sea

Another branch of S J R denotes kindling fire.

Masjūr can accordingly describe something kindled.

Al-baḥr al-masjūr can therefore mean a kindled sea.

This is connected with Al-Takwīr 81:6, where sujjirat describes a transformation of the seas.

Al-Ṭūr 52:7 also follows the oaths with divine punishment’s realization.

The kindling reading is compatible with warning and momentous events.

Contextual compatibility must nevertheless be distinguished from conclusive proof.

Al-Ṭūr 52:6 itself mentions neither fire nor fuel.

Kindling follows from an attested lexical branch and contextual connections, not an explicit fire word in that verse.

Could masjūr mean a dried-up sea?

Some early lexical records give empty or emptied for masjūr.

This underlies a reading concerning a sea whose water is gone.

The recorded meaning should not be denied.

In the poetic evidence examined here, fullness has a clear early witness.

Kindling is also an established Arabic branch supported by another Qur’anic use.

Preferring emptied in Al-Ṭūr 52:6 would require a stronger contextual indication.

Dictionary attestation alone does not give every reading equal evidential weight in a verse.

The oath’s contribution

An oath by the sea can draw attention to a vast created phenomenon.

If masjūr denotes kindling, its connection with future momentous events and punishment can also be examined.

The oath construction alone does not date that kindling.

Nor does it specify a fiery sea’s physical details or location.

Both filled sea and kindled sea have lexical support in Al-Ṭūr 52:6. The latter connects with the chapter’s warning and Al-Takwīr 81:6; the former has a clear early Arabic witness and should not be discarded.

4. Wa-idhā al-biḥāru sujjirat: transformed seas in Al-Takwīr 81:6

وَإِذَا الْبِحَارُ سُجِّرَتْ

wa-idhā al-biḥāru sujjirat

Al-Takwīr 81:6

Translation under the kindling reading:

“And when the seas are set ablaze.”

Al-Takwīr 81:6

Sujjirat is a Form II passive past verb. With idhā here, the past form presents a future event, not an ordinary completed event in the past.

Al-biḥāru is the plural of baḥr. In one established parsing, it is the passive subject of an omitted verb explained by sujjirat; the expressed verb contains an implicit pronoun referring to the seas. Another parsing treats al-biḥāru as a topic with the verbal clause as predicate.

Unlike the adjective masjūr in Al-Ṭūr 52:6, this verb describes an event affecting the seas.

The context of cosmic transformation

Al-Takwīr opens with successive events.

The sun is folded up.

The stars fall or lose their order.

The mountains are set moving.

Wild animals are gathered.

Sujjirat then describes the seas.

Further events concern the transformation of the familiar world and the disclosure of human deeds’ consequences.

This is not an everyday sea condition.

It belongs to a series of immense transformations.

The seas being kindled

The fire-related sense permits sujjirat to denote seas being set ablaze.

This suits the chapter’s transformative setting.

Al-Takwīr 81:12 also speaks of Hell being kindled.

That verse uses suʿʿirat, from S ʿ R.

Two different verbs thus describe the seas and Hell within the same chapter.

The distinction must be retained.

S J R can express kindling.

S ʿ R also expresses blazing in fire-related usage.

Different roots alone do not establish two wholly separate kinds of fire or physical mechanisms.

Filling and overflowing seas

The other branch gives filling and fullness.

Sujjirat can accordingly mean the seas being filled.

Explanations of this reading also mention overflow or seas joining together.

That image relates to abundant water and filling.

Three concepts nevertheless differ:

Filling is reaching fullness.

Overflowing is exceeding prior capacity or boundaries.

Seas joining involves a change in separation and connection.

They can be related within one event without being exact synonyms.

S J R alone does not conclusively specify all those details.

Connection with Al-Infiṭār 82:3

Al-Infiṭār 82:3 describes the seas with fujjirat.

This belongs to F J R, concerning splitting open and opening a path for flow.

Sujjirat and fujjirat resemble each other in sound and formation but have different roots.

Their proximity can assist study of related sea-transformation verses.

Verbal resemblance does not authorize transferring every meaning and detail from one to the other.

Splitting, flowing, filling, and kindling might relate within a greater event, but the two verbs alone do not establish their precise relationship.

Recitation variants

Recognized recitations include sujirat with a single jīm as well as sujjirat with doubled jīm. The base text used here follows the doubled form.

The doubled form is Form II passive.

The undoubled form is the basic verb’s passive.

This real morphological difference does not alone make one necessarily filling and the other necessarily kindling.

Both branches are attested, so context remains necessary.

Does the verse predict a particular scientific phenomenon?

Al-Takwīr 81:6 explicitly describes the seas’ transformation.

The sentence alone does not specify its physical mechanism.

On the kindling reading, it names no particular chemical reaction, energy source, or cosmic conditions.

On the filling reading, it does not independently quantify increased water, identify its source, or map the resulting seas.

This limitation does not deny an event beyond familiar natural conditions.

It distinguishes the Qur’anic report from a particular human scientific theory proposed to explain its mechanism.

Kindling and filling have lexical support in Al-Takwīr 81:6. Cosmic transformation makes kindling pertinent, but the root does not justify dismissing other possibilities without examination.

5. Thumma fī al-nāri yusjarūna: burning in Ghāfir 40:72

فِي الْحَمِيمِ ثُمَّ فِي النَّارِ يُسْجَرُونَ

fī al-ḥamīmi thumma fī al-nāri yusjarūn

Ghāfir 40:72

Translation:

“Into boiling water; then in the fire they are burned.”

Ghāfir 40:72

Yusjarūna is the basic verb’s passive imperfect.

It is masculine plural.

The agent performing the act is not named in the passive construction.

The people concerned are those whose denial and disputation appear in the preceding verses.

Why is kindling clearer here?

Fī al-nār, in the fire, is explicit.

The verb’s setting directly involves fire.

This makes the burning branch clear and appropriate.

Ordinary sajara al-tannūra means kindling an oven.

Ghāfir 40:72 applies the same family to people in fire.

Yusjarūna here can convey their being burned or subjected to blazing fire.

Some lexical and exegetical explanations compare the image with feeding fuel into an oven.

That comparison should not become an unsupported theory about bodies in the Hereafter or fire’s physical constitution.

Connection with Ghāfir 40:71

The preceding verse describes collars and chains about their necks and their being dragged.

Fī al-ḥamīm in Ghāfir 40:72 continues the scene.

Then comes thumma fī al-nāri yusjarūna.

The context connects being dragged through boiling water and then burned in fire.

Al-ḥamīm belongs to another root and here denotes very hot or boiling water.

Al-nār identifies fire itself.

Yusjarūna expresses the fire-related action.

The three words should not be collapsed into one meaning.

Does yusjarūna mean filling the fire?

Some lexical explanations describe fire being filled with those cast into it.

This relates to the filling branch.

The explicit fire setting supports burning, while the passive form presents the people as undergoing the act.

Saying they fill the fire may explain an image, but a natural direct rendering here is “they are burned” or “they are set ablaze in the fire.”

Unlike the sea verses, the explicit word fire considerably reduces ambiguity.

What the three uses establish

Ghāfir 40:72 matters for the fire branch: the root used for the sea in Al-Ṭūr 52:6 and Al-Takwīr 81:6 appears here with fire.

Its entire Qur’anic use therefore cannot be confined to filling with water.

Labīd’s witness likewise prevents restricting every Arabic use to kindling.

Both branches are real; each verse’s construction and context determine the appropriate sense.

6. Filling, kindling, and mingling

How can one root describe both filling a vessel and kindling an oven?

An oven offers one possible explanation.

Fuel is supplied to kindle it.

Filling and kindling are connected within the practical activity.

This makes their semantic proximity understandable.

It does not prove that kindling historically developed from filling.

Kindling is itself an independently recorded Arabic usage.

Mingling, including close companionship and mixed eye colors, also appears under this family.

A single historical origin for every use requires more evidence.

Is filling the parent meaning of the entire family?

Filling has a clear ancient witness.

The antiquity of one use does not prove every other use derives from it.

Nor does the fire branch prove that kindling came first and water filling was secondary.

A careful definition should preserve the actual distinctions between branches.

Filling concerns fullness and abundant contents.

Kindling concerns fire and setting alight.

Mingling appears in certain non-Qur’anic derivatives.

This preserves the evidence and avoids unsupported historical etymology.

7. Distinctions from neighboring roots

S J R and M L ʾ

M L ʾ concerns filling and fullness.

S J R shares that field in one branch.

They converge in masjūrah describing a full spring.

S J R also occurs for fire.

M L ʾ alone does not mean kindling an oven.

The fields overlap without being identical.

S J R and S ʿ R

S ʿ R in fire usage concerns blazing and intensified kindling.

S J R can likewise mean kindling.

Al-Takwīr 81:6 uses sujjirat for the seas.

Al-Takwīr 81:12 uses suʿʿirat for Hell.

They meet in the fire field but have different roots.

S J R additionally has a filling branch.

Not every S J R use can be replaced by S ʿ R without examination.

S J R and F J R

F J R concerns splitting open and opening a course for flow.

Al-Infiṭār 82:3 uses fujjirat for the seas.

Al-Takwīr 81:6 uses sujjirat.

Their forms resemble each other but their roots and fields differ.

Opening a watercourse may relate to filling or flowing without making the verbs synonymous.

Both verses inform sea-transformation study, but one meaning should not be imposed on the other without grounds.

S J R and W Q D

W Q D concerns ignition and burning fire.

S J R likewise concerns kindling in oven usage.

They overlap in this field.

S J R also expresses filling in water usage.

Waqūd denotes fuel, that with which fire is kindled.

Yusjarūna in Ghāfir 40:72 can be connected with fueling fire, but that image should not replace the direct verbal translation.

S J R and Ḥ R Q

Ḥ R Q commonly concerns burning and being burned.

S J R’s fire branch can concern kindling or setting ablaze.

People being burned in Ghāfir 40:72 follows from the fire context.

S J R cannot everywhere be replaced by Ḥ R Q.

Labīd’s spring is masjūrah without having burned.

Subject and context are therefore essential.

S J R and SH J R

The written roots are similar but their first letters differ.

S J R begins with sīn.

SH J R begins with shīn.

SH J R occurs in the Qur’an for trees and, verbally, the arising of disputes.

Visual resemblance does not authorize equating the roots or transferring tree and dispute senses to S J R.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Important meanings in the family

Sajara: filled; in fire usage, kindled.

Sajjara: made full; in a fire context, set ablaze.

Sajr: filling or kindling, according to subject and context.

Sujūr: filling and abundance; a verbal noun in relevant usages.

Masjūr: filled or kindled, according to context.

Sājir: a filling agent or filled place in water-related usage.

Insajara: became full.

Sajūr: oven fuel and firewood.

Misjarah: a wooden implement for moving and stirring oven fuel.

Sajīr: a close friend or companion.

Sajrāʾ: an eye description involving mixed coloration, including redness in its white.

Proposed renderings of Qur’anic forms

In Al-Ṭūr 52:6:

وَالْبَحْرِ الْمَسْجُورِ

wa-al-baḥri al-masjūr

Al-Ṭūr 52:6

“And by the kindled sea.”

This follows the kindling branch.

Filled sea also has independent lexical support and should remain in the analysis.

In Al-Takwīr 81:6:

وَإِذَا الْبِحَارُ سُجِّرَتْ

wa-idhā al-biḥāru sujjirat

Al-Takwīr 81:6

“And when the seas are set ablaze.”

Filling is also a reading here.

The cosmic-transformation context makes kindling pertinent without conclusively excluding filling.

In Ghāfir 40:72:

Ghāfir 40:72

“Into boiling water; then in the fire they are burned.”

Explicit nār clarifies the fire-related meaning.

9. Conclusion

S J R has several Arabic branches, chiefly filling and kindling fire.

Labīd’s transmitted Muʿallaqah applies masjūrah to a full spring.

This attests fullness in early Arabic.

Lexical records also give kindling an oven, its fuel, and some mingling and companionship uses.

Fueling and kindling an oven may explain semantic proximity without establishing every use’s historical origin.

The family occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Masjūr in Al-Ṭūr 52:6 describes the sea within an oath.

Filled sea and kindled sea both have lexical support there.

Sujjirat in Al-Takwīr 81:6 describes the seas’ transformation.

Kindling and filling are proposed readings, but the verb does not conclusively determine the event’s physical mechanism.

Yusjarūna in Ghāfir 40:72 accompanies al-nār.

Burning or being set ablaze is clearer there.

Neither filling alone nor burning alone exhausts the family’s Qur’anic semantic possibilities.

A shared root also does not prove Al-Ṭūr 52:6 and Al-Takwīr 81:6 describe one identical event.

Retain two principal branches: filling something and kindling fire. Mingling is also associated with certain other Arabic uses.

Forqan should consider both branches and the relevant context when translating masjūr and sujjirat.

In Ghāfir 40:72, explicit fire makes “they are burned” a clear, natural rendering.

Fullness and kindling are both actual Arabic senses. Neither should be erased to force uniformity, and no specific physical or eschatological theory should replace the word’s meaning without evidence.
