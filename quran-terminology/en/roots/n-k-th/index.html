Nakth in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from undoing twisted strands and wearing down bonds to covenant-breaking, faithfulness, and responsibility for commitments

The three-consonant root N K TH in Arabic concerns undoing, unraveling, and disrupting something previously tied, twisted, or made firm. Words from this family apply when the strands of a rope are untwisted, the fibers of an old textile are opened for reuse, or the bond of a covenant is broken after being established.

Two important Arabic uses are recognized: unraveling physical things such as rope and thread, and breaking covenants, oaths, and pledges of allegiance. In the latter, what is broken is not a physical connection but a commitment between people or groups.

The root occurs seven times in six Qur’anic verses: six verbs and the noun ankāth once. The Qur’an uses the family for Pharaoh and his people breaking promises, breaking covenantal oaths, violating a pledge of allegiance, and strands unraveled after being twisted and made firm.

A significant feature is that nakth usually concerns disrupting something already formed or established. A covenant not yet made cannot be broken, just as an untwisted strand cannot, in the precise sense of undoing a twisted strand, be untwisted.

This does not mean that every nakth necessarily concerns a formal, written, or sworn agreement. Sometimes the Qur’an explicitly names broken oaths; elsewhere, the object is known from prior conversation and promises.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing unraveling a bond, breaking a commitment, and the moral or legal consequences. The root expresses disruption; the kind of covenant, its conditions, and responsibility for breaking it come from the construction and context.

1. Semantic core and forms

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَكَثَ، يَنْكُثُ (nakatha, yankuthu): to undo or unravel; concerning commitments, to break a covenant or oath.
  • نَكْث (nakth): undoing and disruption; also covenant-breaking.
  • نِكْث (nikth): unraveled thread or fibers, particularly from old textiles prepared for spinning again.
  • أَنْكَاث (ankāth): the plural of nikth; unraveled, untwisted strands or fibers.
  • نَكِيث and مَنْكُوث (nakīth and mankūth): something unraveled or undone, such as a rope whose strands have been untwisted.
  • نَاكِث (nākith): someone who breaks a covenant or commitment.
  • نَكَّاث (nakkāth): someone who unravels old strands or textiles.
  • اِنْتَكَثَ (intakatha): to unravel, come apart, or lose a previously firm state.
  • تَنَاكَثَ (tanākatha): in human relations, to break one another’s covenants.
  • نُكَاثَة (nukāthah): unraveled fibers or portions, such as those at the head of a tooth-cleaning stick or the end of a rope.
  • نَكِيثَة (nakīthah): in different Arabic uses, a difficult undertaking, utmost strength or effort, and in certain expressions a person’s firmness or ability.

Lexical accounts also record al-nukāth for a disease affecting camels’ mouths, and Nikth as a personal name. Without historical evidence, these restricted uses should not be reduced to the principal sense of covenant-breaking.

Only two principal forms of this family occur in the Qur’an:

Nakatha and its inflected forms, six times.

Ankāthan, once.

The Qur’anic verbs are yankuthūna, nakathū, nakatha, and yankuthu. All belong to the basic stem.

Ankāthan is the plural of nikth and concerns unraveled strands. It should not be equated with the basic verb or the verbal noun nakth, although they belong to the same family.

Semantic core: disrupting something made firm

One important lexical core identified for this family is “undoing something.”

This fits its clear uses: a rope is twisted and its strands later undone; a textile is woven and its fibers opened again; a covenant is made and one party breaks it.

In each, a prior bond or structure is disrupted.

The meaning should not extend indiscriminately to every destruction, change of state, or ending.

A rope, for example, may snap through decay. That is not necessarily the same as nakth, in which twisted strands are unraveled.

A covenant may also end when its agreed term expires. Ending according to its own conditions is not necessarily covenant-breaking.

In the commitment branch, nakth is therefore more than a relationship ending: it is disrupting and failing to honor a previously established bond.

2. N K TH in early Arabic: from unraveled strands to difficult undertakings

Undoing rope and thread

Arabic recognizes the following expression:

نَكَثَ الْحَبْلَ

nakatha al-ḥabla

Translation: “He unraveled the rope.”

Here, the rope is already formed from strands twisted and joined together.

When someone untwists them, the firmness produced by twisting is lost.

Also:

اِنْتَكَثَ الْحَبْلُ

intakatha al-ḥablu

Translation: “The rope unraveled.”

The first expression names an action performed by a person or another agent; the second describes the rope itself, whose strands have come apart.

This distinguishes the transitive basic verb from intakatha in expressing agency and result.

Morphology alone, however, does not establish intention or the cause of unraveling. A rope may be deliberately untwisted or come apart through wear and pressure.

Old strands and reuse

In Arab life, old textiles made from wool, hair, and animal underfur were sometimes unraveled so that their fibers could be used for new thread or fabric.

Worn portions were first separated. Their twisted threads were then opened, and the fibers mixed with fresh material and prepared for spinning again.

The unraveled fibers or strands were called nikth, with the plural ankāth.

A person doing this work could be called nakkāth.

This matters because physical nakth is not always wrong or useless.

Unraveling an old textile may allow its materials to be reused and can be beneficial.

By contrast, undoing newly formed, firm strands merely to disrupt the completed work can exemplify wasting earlier effort.

The act’s value thus depends on its purpose and result, rather than the root itself.

This is particularly important for the Qur’anic example of ankāthan. The Qur’an does not prohibit every unraveling; it gives the example of someone undoing yarn after twisting and strengthening it, relating that conduct to covenant-breaking.

Fraying the head of a tooth-cleaning stick

Arabic also records nakatha al-siwāk.

The expression concerns opening and spreading the fibers at the head of a tooth-cleaning stick.

When chewing or working the end of a small cleaning branch separates it into fibers, they emerge from their compact state.

The separated portions can be called nukāthah.

The use shows that nakth is not restricted to a large rope or complete textile: it also describes the separation of fibers in a small object.

The family should nevertheless not be restricted to wood crumbling or fraying.

In covenant-breaking, the object is a social and moral relationship, rather than actual fibers.

Nakīthah: utmost effort and difficulty

Another branch of the early Arabic family is nakīthah.

In some uses, it refers to a difficult undertaking, a situation demanding one’s utmost ability, or a person’s strength itself.

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, the pre-Islamic poet, says:

وَقَرَّبْتُ بِالْقُرْبَى وَجَدِّكَ إِنَّنِي
مَتَى يَكُ أَمْرٌ لِلنَّكِيثَةِ أَشْهَدِ

wa-qarrabtu bi-al-qurbā wa-jaddika innanī / matā yaku amrun li-al-nakīthati ashhadi

Translation: “I sought closeness through kinship; by your fortune, whenever a difficult undertaking demands the utmost strength, I attend it.”

Al-nakīthah here concerns difficult work and the testing of a person’s ability.

The poet declares readiness to face situations requiring effort and endurance.

This is not identical with the direct sense of covenant-breaking.

Lexical explanations also connect nakīthah with strength, the self, and utmost effort. Some explain its relation to exhausted and unraveling powers, as though hardship takes a person’s capacity to its limit.

Such an explanation can make the meanings’ connection understandable, but should not be treated as a definite historical origin.

The transmitted line has variants, including innanī and innahu and differences in the phrase li-al-nakīthah. Its use concerning demanding work and utmost effort is nevertheless clear in early poetic explanations.

The witness shows that the N K TH family in pre-Islamic Arabic was not confined to covenant-breaking.

From unraveling strands to breaking a covenant

Arabic recognizes these expressions:

نَكَثَ الْعَهْدَ

nakatha al-ʿahda

Translation: “He broke the covenant.”

نَكَثَ الْيَمِينَ

nakatha al-yamīna

Translation: “He broke the oath.”

Also:

تَنَاكَثَ الْقَوْمُ عُهُودَهُمْ

tanākatha al-qawmu ʿuhūdahum

Translation: “The people broke one another’s covenants.”

The disrupted bond here is a commitment between people, rather than a physical strand.

Its figurative connection with unraveling rope is clear: just as untwisted strands lose their former firmness, breaking a covenant disrupts a bond created through agreement.

This does not establish that every Arab covenant was literally tied with a rope, or that the expression developed solely from one particular historical custom.

The attested uses are unraveling strands and breaking commitments. Their figurative connection is understandable, but the precise history of a transfer from one to the other is unproven.

Qawlun lā nakīthata fīhi is also recorded: a statement involving no broken promise or reversal of one’s word.

Nakth can thus contrast with keeping one’s word even without a written contract.

3. Ankāthan in the Qur’an: strands unraveled again

وَلَا تَكُونُوا كَالَّتِي نَقَضَتْ غَزْلَهَا مِنْ بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ أَنْكَاثًا تَتَّخِذُونَ أَيْمَانَكُمْ دَخَلًا بَيْنَكُمْ أَنْ تَكُونَ أُمَّةٌ هِيَ أَرْبَىٰ مِنْ أُمَّةٍ

wa-lā takūnū ka-allatī naqaḍat ghazlahā min baʿdi quwwatin ankāthan tattakhidhūna aymānakum dakhalan baynakum an takūna ummatun hiya arbā min ummatin

Translation: “Do not be like the woman who undid her yarn after making it firm, turning it into unraveled strands. You make your oaths a means of deceit among yourselves because one community is greater than another.”

Al-Naḥl 16:92

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of the noun ankāth.

The verse describes a woman undoing yarn after making it firm.

It then relates the image to people making their oaths instruments of deceit and the disruption of covenants.

The meaning of ankāthan

Ankāth is the plural of nikth.

Here it denotes strands or fibers that have lost their previously twisted, firm state.

Ghazlahā denotes what the woman spun, and naqaḍat expresses undoing it.

Ankāthan thus specifies the strands’ condition after unraveling.

A familiar grammatical analysis treats ankāthan as a circumstantial accusative relating to ghazlahā: she undid the yarn so that it became unraveled fibers.

Another takes it as the second object of naqaḍat, explaining that the verb here acquires a sense close to “turning into.”

The grammatical difference does not alter the underlying meaning. Firm strands have been undone in either reading.

Naqaḍat and ankāthan

Two related roots occur together here.

Naqaḍat comes from N Q Ḍ and expresses disrupting and undoing the prior structure.

Ankāthan comes from N K TH and names the unraveled strands.

Their association shows a close semantic connection in unraveling strands.

The connection does not make the roots identical in every Arabic use.

Naqḍ has a broader field, including buildings, covenants, and other structures.

Nakth has a clear physical association with unraveling strands and textiles, and a social use for breaking covenants and oaths.

This is a tendency in usage, rather than an absolute boundary without overlap.

Min baʿdi quwwah: after becoming firm

The verse explicitly says the woman undid the yarn min baʿdi quwwah.

Quwwah here concerns the yarn’s firmness and strength.

The image is therefore more than opening a few loose strands: it concerns disrupting something made firm through work and effort.

This clarifies the example’s connection with covenants and oaths.

A covenant likewise arises through agreement and commitment. Breaking it destroys a bond already established.

Quwwah is nevertheless not an independent component of ankāth’s meaning. Prior firmness is expressed separately by min baʿdi quwwah.

Was the woman a historical person?

Some early interpretive reports describe a woman in Mecca who habitually unraveled what she had spun during the day.

Certain reports give her a name and other identifying details.

The Qur’an, however, specifies neither her name, tribe, location, nor motive.

Other interpreters treat the expression as an example illustrating the futility of covenant-breaking, without requiring identification of a historical woman.

The verse itself clearly depicts a woman undoing her work after making it firm.

Details from outside reports should therefore not be imported as ankāth’s meaning or an essential part of the translation.

Oaths as instruments of deceit

The verse continues:

تَتَّخِذُونَ أَيْمَانَكُمْ دَخَلًا بَيْنَكُمْ

tattakhidhūna aymānakum dakhalan baynakum

Translation: “You make your oaths a means of deceit among yourselves.”

The issue here goes beyond accidentally breaking a promise.

The Qur’an describes people using oaths to deceive in their relationships.

It then says:

أَنْ تَكُونَ أُمَّةٌ هِيَ أَرْبَىٰ مِنْ أُمَّةٍ

an takūna ummatun hiya arbā min ummatin

“Because one community is greater than another.”

The construction shows that one group’s greater numbers or strength may become a pretext for breaking a covenant with another.

A group might, for example, covenant with one community, then abandon its commitment on finding a more numerous or powerful party.

The issue is then more than changing relationships: it is breaking a sworn commitment for greater benefit.

Self-interest and deception are nevertheless not fixed elements of N K TH. They come from tattakhidhūna aymānakum dakhalan baynakum and the continuation.

Connection with the preceding verse

Al-Naḥl 16:91 says:

وَأَوْفُوا بِعَهْدِ اللَّهِ إِذَا عَاهَدْتُمْ وَلَا تَنْقُضُوا الْأَيْمَانَ بَعْدَ تَوْكِيدِهَا

wa-awfū bi-ʿahdi Allāhi idhā ʿāhadtum wa-lā tanquḍū al-aymāna baʿda tawkīdihā

Translation: “Fulfill The God’s covenant when you make a covenant, and do not break oaths after confirming them.”

The verse directly commands fulfilling covenants and keeping confirmed oaths.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 explains the same subject through the image of unraveled strands.

Ankāth thus names more than a few scattered fibers within the Qur’anic example: it provides a physical image of disrupting a commitment previously made firm.

The literal meaning of unraveled strands must nevertheless remain distinct from the example’s moral message.

4. Nakth in Pharaoh’s story: promises in distress and broken pledges after relief

Al-Aʿrāf 7:135: abandoning a promise

فَلَمَّا كَشَفْنَا عَنْهُمُ الرِّجْزَ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ هُمْ بَالِغُوهُ إِذَا هُمْ يَنْكُثُونَ

fa-lammā kashafnā ʿanhumu al-rijza ilā ajalin hum bālighūhu idhā hum yankuthūna

Translation: “When We lifted the affliction from them until a term they would reach, they suddenly broke their pledge.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:135

Yankuthūna is a plural imperfect verb of the basic stem.

Its object requires reading the preceding verse.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:134, Pharaoh and his people, afflicted by punishment, ask Moses to call upon his Lord to remove it.

They promise:

لَئِنْ كَشَفْتَ عَنَّا الرِّجْزَ لَنُؤْمِنَنَّ لَكَ وَلَنُرْسِلَنَّ مَعَكَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ

la-in kashafta ʿannā al-rijza la-nuʾminanna laka wa-la-nursilanna maʿaka banī isrāʾīla

Translation: “If you remove the affliction from us, we will certainly believe you and send the Children of Israel with you.”

The promise has two clear elements: believing Moses and allowing the Children of Israel to accompany him.

Once the affliction is removed, however, they abandon their promise.

Yankuthūna in Al-Aʿrāf 7:135 therefore concerns breaking that prior commitment.

Nakth and the response after danger is removed

The promise is made in distress; faithfulness is tested after relief.

An individual or group may promise something to escape danger in difficult conditions. Once the pressure disappears, the same commitment may be ignored.

The conduct is clear in the account of Pharaoh and his people.

It does not establish that every promise made in danger is necessarily deceptive.

Nakth here is recognized by comparing the prior promise with subsequent behavior. N K TH itself does not mean fear, deception, or requesting help in distress.

Nor can the verb determine each person’s intention when promising. The Qur’an describes their collective conduct as pledge-breaking.

Al-Zukhruf 43:50: the same pattern

In Al-Zukhruf 43:49, Pharaoh’s people say to Moses:

يَا أَيُّهَ السَّاحِرُ ادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ بِمَا عَهِدَ عِنْدَكَ إِنَّنَا لَمُهْتَدُونَ

yā ayyuha al-sāḥiru udʿu lanā rabbaka bi-mā ʿahida ʿindaka innanā la-muhtadūna

Translation: “Magician, call upon your Lord for us by what He has pledged with you; we will indeed follow guidance.”

The next verse, Al-Zukhruf 43:50, says:

فَلَمَّا كَشَفْنَا عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابَ إِذَا هُمْ يَنْكُثُونَ

fa-lammā kashafnā ʿanhumu al-ʿadhāba idhā hum yankuthūna

Translation: “When We removed the punishment from them, they suddenly broke their pledge.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:50

The construction and subject of these verses are close to Al-Aʿrāf 7:134 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:135.

In both, Pharaoh’s people speak of returning or accepting guidance while distressed, but fail to honor their promise after punishment is lifted.

Al-Aʿrāf gives more detail about the promise; Al-Zukhruf says innanā la-muhtadūn.

The difference shows why the preceding sentences must be examined to identify what is broken.

The role of idhā hum yankuthūn

Both verses use idhā hum yankuthūn.

Idhā here highlights the sudden appearance of conduct after the preceding event.

The verses thus do more than predict some future breach: they describe a prompt or apparent return to pledge-breaking after relief.

Suddenness or the speed of the response, however, is not an independent meaning of nakth. It comes from idhā hum and the sequence of events.

The verb’s core in both instances remains breaking a promise or commitment.

5. Breaking covenantal oaths in Al-Tawbah

Al-Tawbah 9:12: breaking oaths after a covenant

وَإِن نَّكَثُوا أَيْمَانَهُم مِّن بَعْدِ عَهْدِهِمْ وَطَعَنُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ فَقَاتِلُوا أَئِمَّةَ الْكُفْرِ إِنَّهُمْ لَا أَيْمَانَ لَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَنْتَهُونَ

wa-in nakathū aymānahum min baʿdi ʿahdihim wa-ṭaʿanū fī dīnikum fa-qātilū aʾimmata al-kufri innahum lā aymāna lahum laʿallahum yantahūna

Translation: “If they break their oaths after their covenant and attack your religion, fight the leaders of disbelief; they have no binding oaths, so that they may desist.”

The object of nakatha is explicitly named here:

أَيْمَانَهُم

aymānahum

It means “their oaths.”

Aymān is the plural of yamīn, “oath,” in this construction. It should not be confused with īmān, “faith.”

Min baʿdi ʿahdihim also places the oaths in relation to a prior covenant.

The verse therefore concerns breaking an already established commitment.

Does nakth here simply mean changing belief?

No.

The verse concerns a covenant and oaths.

The preceding verses also discuss groups with whom covenants had been made.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 says:

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ عَاهَدتُّم مِّنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَنقُصُوكُمْ شَيْئًا وَلَمْ يُظَاهِرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ أَحَدًا فَأَتِمُّوا إِلَيْهِمْ عَهْدَهُمْ إِلَىٰ مُدَّتِهِمْ

illā alladhīna ʿāhadtum mina al-mushrikīna thumma lam yanquṣūkum shayʾan wa-lam yuẓāhirū ʿalaykum aḥadan fa-atimmū ilayhim ʿahdahum ilā muddatihim

Translation: “Except those polytheists with whom you made a covenant, who then diminished nothing of your rights and supported no one against you: fulfill their covenant until its term.”

This shows that covenants must be honored even with groups differing from Muslims in religion.

Al-Tawbah 9:7 also says:

فَمَا اسْتَقَامُوا لَكُمْ فَاسْتَقِيمُوا لَهُمْ

fa-mā istaqāmū lakum fa-istaqīmū lahum

Translation: “As long as they remain faithful toward you, remain faithful toward them.”

This continuity is decisive for understanding Al-Tawbah 9:12.

Breaking the covenant in this context should therefore not be defined simply as differing in belief or refusing Islam.

The subject is a commitment between two parties that one party has disrupted.

Wa-ṭaʿanū fī dīnikum

Alongside breaking oaths, Al-Tawbah 9:12 also mentions attacking the religion.

The expression comes from Ṭ ʿ N and concerns attacking and finding fault with the religion.

The two descriptions must nevertheless be distinguished.

Nakathū aymānahum concerns breaking covenantal oaths.

Ṭaʿanū fī dīnikum states another behavior within the same condition.

Al-Tawbah 9:13 also says:

أَلَا تُقَاتِلُونَ قَوْمًا نَّكَثُوا أَيْمَانَهُمْ وَهَمُّوا بِإِخْرَاجِ الرَّسُولِ وَهُم بَدَءُوكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ

a-lā tuqātilūna qawman nakathū aymānahum wa-hammū bi-ikhrāji al-rasūli wa-hum badaʾūkum awwala marratin

Translation: “Will you not fight a people who broke their oaths, intended to expel the messenger, and themselves began against you the first time?”

The verse clarifies the hostile setting: breaking oaths, intending to expel the messenger, and initiating confrontation.

Mere verbal criticism or disagreement in belief should therefore not, without examining this background, be equated with the conduct addressed by the fighting ruling in these verses.

Nor should covenant-breaking extend to every small disagreement over interpreting an agreement’s clauses. Whether a covenant was actually breached requires judgment according to its terms, the parties’ conduct, and the circumstances.

Innahum lā aymāna lahum

The end of Al-Tawbah 9:12 says:

إِنَّهُمْ لَا أَيْمَانَ لَهُمْ

innahum lā aymāna lahum

“For they have no binding oaths.”

In the familiar reading, aymān begins with a fatḥah and is the plural of “oath.”

Ibn ʿĀmir’s reading has īmān, with an initial kasrah. It has been explained both through affirmation and faith and through a sense involving granting security. This reading should neither be treated as an unidentified transmission nor merged with aymān in the familiar reading.

The difference should not blend two meanings in the basic translation.

In the familiar reading and the context of covenants and oaths, the expression concerns their oaths’ lack of practical credibility or their failure to keep them.

Lā aymāna lahum does not mean they never verbally swore an oath: the verse itself discusses breaking their oaths.

The point is the lack of practical reliability and faithfulness in the conduct under discussion.

Does nakth always authorize fighting?

No such ruling comes from N K TH itself.

Al-Tawbah discusses nakth in the context of particular covenants, hostile behavior, and aggression against the other party.

Other verses use it for Pharaoh’s broken promises or violation of allegiance without issuing an identical command to fight in every instance.

The meaning of covenant-breaking must therefore be distinguished from the legal or practical ruling for each event.

A legitimate end to an agreement, amendment by mutual consent, or openly announced termination under conditions allowed by the Qur’an is also not necessarily identical with secret, deceptive covenant-breaking.

Al-Anfāl 8:58 commands an openly and equally understood declaration of the covenant’s status when betrayal by the other party is feared.

Faithfulness thus involves more than an agreement’s existence: how it is handled also matters.

All the conditions for legitimately ending a covenant belong to a separate study of covenants, pledges, and relations between groups.

6. Breaking allegiance: the responsibility of the person who breaks the pledge

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُبَايِعُونَكَ إِنَّمَا يُبَايِعُونَ اللَّهَ يَدُ اللَّهِ فَوْقَ أَيْدِيهِمْ فَمَن نَّكَثَ فَإِنَّمَا يَنْكُثُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ وَمَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِمَا عَاهَدَ عَلَيْهُ اللَّهَ فَسَيُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

inna alladhīna yubāyiʿūnaka innamā yubāyiʿūna Allāha yadu Allāhi fawqa aydīhim fa-man nakatha fa-innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsihi wa-man awfā bi-mā ʿāhada ʿalayhu Allāha fa-sa-yuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīman

Translation: “Those who pledge allegiance to you are in fact pledging allegiance to The God. The God’s hand is above their hands. Whoever breaks the pledge does so only to his own detriment; whoever fulfills what he covenanted with The God will soon receive a great reward from Him.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:10

N K TH occurs twice here:

فَمَن نَّكَثَ

fa-man nakatha

“Whoever breaks the pledge.”

فَإِنَّمَا يَنْكُثُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ

fa-innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsihi

“He breaks it only to his own detriment.”

Both verbs belong to the basic stem, but one is perfect and the other imperfect.

Allegiance and breaking a covenant

The opening concerns people pledging allegiance to the messenger.

Bayʿah comes from B Y ʿ and here concerns a commitment and pledge of loyalty.

The Qur’an relates the allegiance to The God, then describes the consequences of breaking it.

The preceding sentence thus clarifies the object of nakatha, although fa-man nakatha has no separate object following the verb.

Nakth concerns disrupting the commitment established through that allegiance.

ʿAlā nafsihi: the consequence of covenant-breaking

ʿAlā nafsihi shows that the consequence returns to the person who breaks the pledge.

This matters in the verse’s context.

Someone breaking a covenant may think the act frees him from commitment or gains him an advantage. The Qur’an reminds him that breaking it carries responsibility and consequences.

The expression does not establish that covenant-breaking never harms anyone else.

In society, breaking a covenant may also violate other people’s rights. The verse places responsibility and spiritual detriment on the pledge-breaker, rather than necessarily restricting every effect to him alone.

Nor is ʿalā nafsihi part of nakth’s independent meaning. It specifies where the consequences fall in this verse.

Nakth contrasted with fulfillment

The verse continues:

وَمَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِمَا عَاهَدَ عَلَيْهُ اللَّهَ

wa-man awfā bi-mā ʿāhada ʿalayhu Allāha

“Whoever fulfills what he covenanted with The God.”

Nakth and fulfillment are contrasted here.

Nakth is breaking a commitment.

Fulfillment is keeping and completing it.

The same contrast appears in other covenant verses.

Al-Naḥl 16:91, for example, commands fulfillment and prohibits breaking confirmed oaths.

The contrast does not make every change in a decision or personal undertaking necessarily nakth.

Some decisions are corrected, contracts amended by consent, and commitments ended according to their own terms.

Covenant-breaking concerns disrupting a commitment still valid and binding, rather than merely a change of state.

Yadu Allāhi fawqa aydīhim

The verse says, “The God’s hand is above their hands.”

The expression matters for allegiance’s standing and relation to The God.

It should not establish a bodily form for The God’s hand like a human hand.

Nor does it by itself give unlimited divine authority to every later person who receives allegiance.

The Qur’an concerns a particular pledge to the messenger; extending that relationship to others or to institutions requires independent grounds.

For N K TH, the central point is that breaking such a pledge is called nakth, with responsibility returning to the pledge-breaker.

Does nākithīn occur in the Qur’an?

In political and religious history after the messenger, nākithīn was applied to groups accused of breaking allegiance.

Linguistically, it is connected with nākith, a covenant-breaker.

Nākithīn itself, however, does not occur in the Qur’an.

Nor do the N K TH verses name later people or events by this title.

The term’s historical usage should therefore not replace the root’s Qur’anic meaning.

If needed, the subject belongs in a separate study of allegiance and political disagreements. A lexical article should not become a defense of a historical or religious faction.

7. Comparison with related roots

N K TH (nakth) and N Q Ḍ (naqḍ)

The roots are close in breaking covenants and disrupting a prior structure.

Both families occur in Al-Naḥl 16:92: naqaḍat ghazlahā and ankāthan.

Arabic uses naqḍ for buildings, covenants, strands, and other structures.

Physical nakth has a clear connection with unraveling strands and textiles; socially, it is used for breaking covenants and oaths.

Nakth thus has a particular connection with undoing something twisted or tied, whereas naqḍ has a broader field of dismantling and disrupting structures.

The difference is not absolute.

Both roots can refer to breaking a covenant, and lexical explanations sometimes use one to explain the other.

It would therefore be wrong to say naqḍ always concerns physical objects and nakth always covenants.

Nor does nakth necessarily mean deliberate breaking and naqḍ accidental breaking. Intention and awareness require context and evidence from conduct.

Study of N Q Ḍ

N K TH (nakth) and Ḥ N TH (ḥinth)

Ḥinth concerns breaking an oath and, in some uses, sin and violating a commitment.

This is close to nakth, particularly when an oath is not kept.

Qur’anic nakth, however, also concerns broken promises and allegiance, beyond explicit oaths.

Ḥinth also occurs in al-ḥinth al-ʿaẓīm in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, where its semantic field includes grave sin.

Every use of ḥinth should therefore not be equated precisely with breaking allegiance, nor every nakth with ḥinth.

Breaking oaths under different conditions may also have different rulings. Expiation and an oath’s validity require the relevant verses and cannot be established from nakth alone.

Study of Ḥ N TH

N K TH (nakth) and KH L F (khalf)

In promise-related usage, khalf concerns failing to fulfill something and acting contrary to what was said.

Breaking a promise can be close to nakth in outcome.

Nakth in its clear uses, however, highlights disrupting a commitment already established.

KH L F has a broader field in other constructions, including succession, coming behind, and differences between states.

Only part of its field therefore overlaps with nakth in covenant-breaking.

Nor is every failure to keep a promise necessarily deliberate deception. Inability or unforeseen circumstances may prevent someone from doing something.

That situation requires separate examination from conscious, deceptive violation of a commitment.

Study of KH L F

N K TH (nakth) and N B DH (nabdh)

Nabdh concerns casting aside or discarding something and, in certain Qur’anic uses, applies to a covenant.

Al-Anfāl 8:58 concerns openly declaring a covenant’s status when betrayal is feared.

Discarding or ending an agreement is therefore not necessarily identical with secret covenant-breaking: its conditions and manner matter.

Nakth in oath and allegiance verses concerns disrupting an established commitment.

The roots should therefore not be treated as exact synonyms in every use.

Study of N B DH

N K TH (nakth) and N K Ṣ (nukūṣ)

Nukūṣ concerns retreating and withdrawing from a position or action.

Al-Anfāl 8:48 says:

نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ

nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi

“He retreated on his heels.”

The expression concerns retreat.

Nakth, however, concerns breaking a covenant or unraveling a prior bond.

Someone may retreat after undertaking an action and thereby also break a pledge, but retreat and covenant-breaking remain independent concepts.

Similar letters and overlapping behavior thus do not make the roots identical.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For physical nakatha, Persian equivalents meaning “unraveled,” “undid,” and, in certain constructions, “untwisted the strands” are appropriate.

For intakatha describing a rope, “unraveled” or “its strands came apart” is natural.

For nikth and ankāth, “unraveled thread” and “untwisted strands” are suitable.

For covenants, oaths, and allegiance, “broke the covenant” or “broke the oath” is natural.

Persian has a clear, familiar term for covenant-breaking; cumbersome phrases such as “unraveled the covenant’s strand” need not replace it merely to preserve the physical image.

The twisted-strand image belongs in the lexical explanation; the basic translation should be natural for the reader.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:135 and Al-Zukhruf 43:50

إِذَا هُمْ يَنْكُثُونَ

idhā hum yankuthūna

“They suddenly broke their pledge.”

The rendering fits both contexts.

In both, the object of nakth is known from Pharaoh’s people’s earlier promises.

Al-Tawbah 9:12 and Al-Tawbah 9:13

نَكَثُوا أَيْمَانَهُمْ

nakathū aymānahum

“They broke their oaths.”

Aymān is explicit and should remain in translation.

“They lost their faith” is wrong here, since the word in this construction is the plural of “oath.”

Al-Naḥl 16:92

أَنْكَاثًا

ankāthan

“Unraveled strands” or “untwisted fibers.”

Within the complete construction, a natural rendering is:

“Do not be like the woman who undid her yarn after making it firm, turning it into unraveled strands.”

This preserves the image of disrupting yarn made firm.

The explanation of ankāth can describe fibers and strands that have lost their twisted state, without making complete tearing and severing each individual strand necessary elements of the meaning.

Al-Fatḥ 48:10

فَمَن نَّكَثَ فَإِنَّمَا يَنْكُثُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ

fa-man nakatha fa-innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsihi

“Whoever breaks the pledge does so only to his own detriment.”

The pledge is understood from allegiance in the context; “to his own detriment” comes from ʿalā nafsihi.

Harm should not be made part of nakth’s independent meaning: physically unraveling old strands may even be beneficial.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: unraveling and disrupting something previously tied or made firm; socially, breaking a covenant, oath, or pledge of allegiance.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:135: breaking the promises Pharaoh’s people made to Moses during distress.

In Al-Tawbah 9:12: breaking covenantal oaths after the covenant was established.

In Al-Tawbah 9:13: breaking those oaths amid an intention to expel the messenger and confrontation initiated by the pledge-breakers.

In Al-Naḥl 16:92: unraveled strands and fibers undone after being twisted and made firm.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:50: abandoning a commitment after punishment is removed.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:10: breaking allegiance and commitment, with responsibility returning to the pledge-breaker.

9. Conclusion

N K TH in Arabic concerns unraveling and disrupting something previously tied, twisted, or made firm.

The meaning appears in undoing rope, unraveling old textiles, and separating fibers. The social use concerns breaking covenants, oaths, and allegiance.

Lexical witnesses support the connection without establishing a definite historical origin for every derivative. Forms such as nakīthah, concerning difficult work and utmost effort, have independent uses requiring their own evidence.

The Qur’an uses the root in two principal settings.

In Al-Naḥl 16:92, ankāth names unraveled strands. The image depicts undoing something previously made firm, connected with breaking confirmed oaths and using covenants deceptively.

Elsewhere, nakatha concerns broken promises, covenantal oaths, and allegiance.

In Pharaoh’s story, nakth follows relief from distress: people who promised during danger abandon the commitment once rescued.

In Al-Tawbah, nakth concerns covenantal oaths amid hostile conduct, intending to expel the messenger, and initiating confrontation. N K TH itself therefore does not mean war or a change of belief.

In Al-Fatḥ, breaking allegiance is called nakth, and responsibility returns to the pledge-breaker. Fulfillment is contrasted with it and accompanied by a promise of reward.

The uses show that Qur’anic nakth concerns disrupting a real commitment. The word itself does not establish the kind of covenant, the person’s intention, a method of remedy, or every case’s legal ruling.

Ending an agreement according to its conditions or amending it by the parties’ consent is not necessarily nakth. Judging a breach requires establishing the commitment, whether it remained valid, and what the obligated party did.

Final definition: N K TH principally denotes undoing and unraveling something previously tied, twisted, or made firm. In commitments, it means breaking a covenant, oath, or allegiance: disrupting a bond established through a promise and undertaking. Qur’anic ankāth preserves the physical image of unraveled strands, while the verbs concern covenant-breaking. The uses share the disruption of a prior bond, but the moral and legal consequences require the particular covenant’s context and conditions.
