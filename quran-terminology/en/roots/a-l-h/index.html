Qur’anic Roots

The Qur’anic Root A L H

Worship, turning toward, placing something in the station of a god, and recognizing The One God

Ilāh denotes a deity or object of worship, while Allāh is the proper-name designation rendered here as “The God.” The distinction between common noun and proper name, together with singular, dual, and plural uses, is central to this family.

Dual and plural: two gods, gods

Meaning and background

General meaning of the root A L H

The word Allah in the Qur’an is not merely a verbal name. It is an entryway into understanding tawḥīd, faith, worship, Lordship, mercy, guidance, messengership, the Hereafter, and the true meaning of being a believer.

If the meaning of Allah is not understood correctly, religion can be reduced to habits, words, sectarian attachments, and inherited historical loyalties. But when this word is understood in its Qur’anic depth, a person recognizes that all prophets, all divine books, and all truthful calls were originally directed toward the same single reality: The One God, Creator of the worlds.

In English, we explain Allah with the expression “The One God” or “The God”. This is not merely a stylistic choice; it is an attempt to carry the Qur’anic meaning of the word into English. That meaning can, in shāʾ Allāh, gather believers again around the shared truth: belief in The One God, the Day of Accountability, and righteous action.

The root A L H in Arabic is connected with the semantic field of worship, turning toward, seeking refuge, awe before greatness, and placing something in the station of a god. From this root comes the word ilāh: a god, or something a person places in the position of worship, final command, absolute reliance, ultimate hope, or the center of surrender.

This page is not a short explanation of one isolated word. It is the reference page for the root A L H. Whenever the reader meets a Qur’anic word from this root, they can return to this page and find that word’s usage in its proper place.

Arabic before and around the Qur’an

Early Arabic inscriptions provide evidence independent of transmitted poetry. Al-Jallad and Sidky’s study of Rīʿ al-Zallālah reports al-ilāh and Allāh in the Paleo-Arabic corpus. The ʿAbd-Shams inscription includes, in normalized modern spelling, “باسمك اللهم”: in your name, O The God.

The inscription carries no explicit date; its dating rests on script. It attests the name in prayer, without settling the writer’s precise religious affiliation or the name’s ultimate etymology.

Ilāh and the name Allāh

Ilāh: a god, a center of worship and final command

Being an object of worship is distinct from ordinary obedience or trust. The discussion of power and desire applies the concept of worship interpretively to the passages; ilāh does not mean every authority or every frightening thing.

Ilāh is originally a general word. It means “a god,” “an object of worship,” or anything placed in the position of worship, final command, or surrender. It can be used for a true object of worship or a false object of worship.

From the Qur’anic view, an ilāh is not only a stone idol or an outward form of ritual worship. Anything to which a person gives final status can function as an ilāh in practice: political power, inherited tradition, a religious scholar, tribe, ego, fear, benefit, or any structure that occupies the station of final command.

Therefore, understanding the word ilāh is essential for understanding shirk. Shirk is not only denying the name of God. Shirk can occur whenever something other than The God is placed in the station of absolute obedience, final judgment, ultimate fear, or existential reliance.

Allah: The One God

The Qur’an distinguishes between ilāh and Allah. Ilāh can refer generally to any god or object of worship, whether true or false. But Allah points to the unique station that has no partner, equal, likeness, replacement, or rival.

So Allah in the Qur’an does not merely mean “God” in a vague or generic sense. Allah means The One God: the reality toward whom a person must direct all worship, ultimate trust, existential obedience, and return.

Allah is not one god among other gods, not a tribal god, not an unknown power, and not a name separated from meaning. Allah is the unique station of divinity: the station to which all true praise, all Lordship, all final command, all return, and all beautiful names belong.

The “al” in Allah and its role in Arabic

Ilāh is a noun for a deity or object of worship; Allāh functions in the Qur’an as the proper name of The God. A widely accepted linguistic analysis connects Allāh with al-ilāh through loss of the hamzah and assimilation. Other analyses occur in the lexical tradition, so the derivation should not be presented as an undisputed historical fact.

Al- is a means of making a noun definite and may, according to context, refer to something known or to a class. It does not by itself establish uniqueness, infinity, or divinity. The same article accompanies words for created things.

Qur’anic monotheism is expressed by statements such as “وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ” and by the rejection of partners. The rendering “The God” is this site’s translation convention; the unity explained in the article comes from Qur’anic statements, not from the article al- alone.

The difference between ilāh and Allah

Ilāh is a general word. A person may make something their ilāh even if that thing has no real power. A stone idol, a tyrant, an inherited tradition, a religious authority, a political system, a desire, a fear, a benefit, or even a mental image of God can become an ilāh for a person; that is, something before which the person surrenders and to which the person gives final status.

But Allah is not like this. Allah points to the true and unique station in which nothing else can share. He is not merely one “god” among claimed gods; He is The One God. Whatever is placed in the station of divinity besides Him is either illusion, human construction, or deviation from truth.

From here, the foundation of tawḥīd becomes clear. Tawḥīd is not merely saying that God exists. Many mushrikūn also believed in God’s existence. Tawḥīd means placing nothing in His station: not in worship, not in final command, not in absolute judgment, not in ultimate fear, not in ultimate hope, and not in ownership over religion.

Unity and the rejection of other gods

Ilāhukum: your God

One important form of this root in the Qur’an is ilāhukum, meaning “your God.” This expression takes the reader out of abstract theory and connects the matter directly to a person’s existential orientation: Who is your God? Where does your final command come from? Toward whom is your return?

The Qur’an says:

وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ ۖ لَّا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ الرَّحِيمُ wa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid; lā ilāha illā huwa al-Raḥmānu al-Raḥīm

This verse uses ilāhukum and explains it with ilāh wāḥid. Your God is One God. Then, through negation and affirmation, it states: there is no god except Him, Al-Raḥmān, Al-Raḥīm.

Ilāhunā and ilāhukum: our God and your God

In dialogue with the People of the Book, the Qur’an draws attention to the shared ground of tawḥīd. The expression ilāhunā wa ilāhukum means “our God and your God.” It shows that the Qur’anic call is not the ethnic or sectarian ownership of God; it is a return to The One God.

The Qur’an says:

وَإِلَٰهُنَا وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ وَاحِدٌ وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ wa-ilāhunā wa-ilāhukum wāḥid, wa-naḥnu lahu muslimūn

This verse is foundational for the Forqan project. Instead of imprisoning believers inside sectarian identities, the Qur’an calls them to the shared truth: our God and your God is One, and we submit to Him.

Ilāh wāḥid: One God

The expression ilāh wāḥid is one of the clearest Qur’anic forms of tawḥīd. In this expression, the Qur’an pairs the general word ilāh with wāḥid so it becomes clear that the station of divinity is neither dual nor multiple.

The Qur’an says:

إِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid

The meaning of this phrase is not only that “one God exists.” Its deeper meaning is that the station of worship, final command, absolute reliance, and existential return is only one. Anything placed beside Him in that station is outside tawḥīd.

Lā ilāha: the negation of every god

The phrase lā ilāha begins with negation. This negation is the foundation of tawḥīd’s purification. Before a person reaches affirmation, the false stations of divinity must be demolished.

Lā ilāha means no god has the right to sit in the final station. Not an idol, not power, not tribe, not tradition, not a religious scholar, not government, not ego, not fear, and not benefit. This negation frees the human being from servitude to other than The God.

In the Qur’an, this negation appears repeatedly in different structures so that the human being knows tawḥīd is not only a positive claim. Tawḥīd begins with rejecting false gods.

Lā ilāha illā Allah: negating all false gods and affirming The One God

The phrase lā ilāha illā Allah is the foundation of tawḥīd. It begins with negation: there is no god. Then it becomes complete with affirmation: except Allah, meaning The One God.

The Qur’an says:

فَاعْلَمْ أَنَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ faʿlam annahu lā ilāha illā Allāh

Before this phrase is a spoken slogan, it is a revolution at the center of the human being. A person negates all false gods and reserves the final station for Allah alone.

This is true human freedom. A person becomes free when they do not serve any power except the Absolute Truth.

Ilāhayn: two gods and the rejection of duality in divinity

The Qur’an rejects not only multiplicity in divinity, but even duality. The expression ilāhayn means “two gods.” This usage shows that, in Qur’anic logic, divinity is not divisible.

The Qur’an says:

لَا تَتَّخِذُوا إِلَٰهَيْنِ اثْنَيْنِ ۖ إِنَّمَا هُوَ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ lā tattakhidhū ilāhayni ithnayn; innamā huwa ilāhun wāḥid

This verse negates “two gods” and affirms “One God.” Qur’anic tawḥīd is not merely preferring one god over several gods; it negates the very division of the station of divinity.

Ālihah: gods and associated powers

Ālihah is the plural of ilāh: gods. The Qur’an usually uses this word in critique of constructed gods, associated gods, or powers to which people give the station of divinity beside The God.

The Qur’an says:

لَوْ كَانَ فِيهِمَا آلِهَةٌ إِلَّا اللَّهُ لَفَسَدَتَا law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illā Allāhu lafasadatā

This verse shows that if there were gods in the heavens and the earth besides Allah, their order would be corrupted. Multiple divinity is not compatible with the Qur’an’s vision of order, unity, and Lordship.

Al-ālihah: the accepted or constructed gods

Al-ālihah means “the gods”: gods that have become known, accepted, or constructed in a society or collective mind. This form shows that shirk is not only an individual belief; sometimes a cultural, political, or religious system forms around constructed gods.

The Qur’an reports the objection of those who rejected tawḥīd:

أَجَعَلَ الْآلِهَةَ إِلَٰهًا وَاحِدًا ۖ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عُجَابٌ ajaʿala al-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidan; inna hādhā lashayʾun ʿujāb

Their objection shows that the message of tawḥīd is not merely a verbal disagreement for a culture shaped by shirk. Tawḥīd collapses the structure of accepted gods and returns the human being to one final station.

Taking as a god and following desire

Ittakhadha ilāh: taking something as a god

One important Qur’anic expression is ittakhadha ilāh: to take something as a god. This expression shows that shirk is not only a theoretical belief; it is an act that shapes direction. A person chooses something, elevates it, and places it in the station of obedience, surrender, or final dependence.

The Qur’an critiques this behavior:

وَلَا تَدْعُ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ ۘ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ wa-lā tadʿu maʿa Allāhi ilāhan ākhara; lā ilāha illā huwa

“Another god” is not necessarily only an outward idol. Anything that gains final status beside Allah falls within the field of this warning. The Qur’an prevents the human being from constructing any parallel center of worship and final command.

Desire as ilāh: when the ego’s desire sits in the place of a god

One of the deepest usages of the root A L H in the Qur’an appears where desire is described as an ilāh. This shows that an ilāh in the Qur’an is not only an external idol; sometimes a person’s god is inside them.

The Qur’an says:

أَفَرَأَيْتَ مَنِ اتَّخَذَ إِلَٰهَهُ هَوَاهُ afaraʾayta mani ittakhadha ilāhahu hawāhu

That is: have you seen the one who took his desire as his god? This verse shows that a person may speak of God with the tongue, while in practice making desire the final criterion. In that state, desire sits in the place of ilāh.

This usage makes the definition of ilāh broad and precise. Ilāh means anything before which a person finally surrenders, even if that thing does not outwardly carry a religious name.

Names and descriptions

Allah and the Most Beautiful Names

The Qur’an states that the Most Beautiful Names belong to Him. These names are not separate essences or independent powers. Al-Raḥmān, Al-Raḥīm, Al-ʿAlīm, Al-Qadīr, Al-Malik, Al-Ḥakīm, and the other beautiful names are each a doorway through which our limited understanding approaches the same single reality.

The Qur’an says:

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ لَهُ الْأَسْمَاءُ الْحُسْنَىٰ Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa; lahu al-asmāʾu al-ḥusnā

The plurality of names does not mean a plurality of God. The names are many, but the station is one. Through these names we understand aspects of His Lordship, mercy, knowledge, power, justice, and wisdom; but all these names return to the same station of Allah.

Allah and Lordship: the link between divinity and nurturing sovereignty

In the Qur’an, Allah is not only the object of worship; He is also Rabb. That is, the One worthy of worship is also the Creator, nurturer, disposer, and the One who brings beings toward their growth and destination.

In Al-Fātiḥah, it says:

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ al-ḥamdu lillāhi Rabbi al-ʿālamīn

This verse links Allah with Rabb al-ʿālamīn. The One God is the Lord of all worlds. Worship of Him is not separate from His Lordship; because He is the Lord, He is worthy of praise and servitude.

Allah and Al-Raḥmān: names, not a prison of wording

One common error in surface-level religiosity is to imagine that the sanctity of truth is preserved by imprisoning it inside one specific word. The Qur’an itself does not confirm this narrow view.

The Qur’an says:

قُلِ ادْعُوا اللَّهَ أَوِ ادْعُوا الرَّحْمَٰنَ ۖ أَيًّا مَا تَدْعُوا فَلَهُ الْأَسْمَاءُ الْحُسْنَىٰ quli idʿū Allāha awi idʿū al-Raḥmān; ayyan mā tadʿū fa-lahu al-asmāʾu al-ḥusnā

This verse shows that the issue is not being trapped inside one word; the issue is correctly pointing toward the same unique station. If a name truly points to Him and does not mix Him with created things, partners, lords, or defective concepts, that name can become a means of turning toward Him.

When we speak of The God, we must always be careful not to make His attributes identical to the attributes of created beings. The Qur’an teaches us that He is pure and exalted beyond every likeness and comparison.

لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ laysa kamithlihi shayʾ

وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ wa-lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad

This logic is consistent with Qur’anic transcendence. We use words, but we know that His essence is not confined inside any word. We call upon Him, but we know He is beyond every name, every image, every comparison, and every mental picture.

Connections within the Semitic languages

Arabic, Hebrew, and Aramaic are Semitic languages. Arabic ilāh belongs beside Hebrew forms such as ʾēl and ʾĕlōah and Aramaic ʾĕlāhā in the vocabulary for a deity. Comparing them is useful for lexical history.

Related words do not mean that all their speakers shared the same beliefs, and do not by themselves establish a text’s or community’s monotheism. Each form must be read in its own language and context. Qur’anic Arabic likewise distinguishes ilāh as a common noun from Allāh as a proper-name designation.

Forqan’s recommended translation

This site renders Allāh as “The God.” English also conventionally uses capitalized “God” as a proper-name designation. This rendering emphasizes the connection with ilāh; it does not imply that every theological attribute is encoded in the word’s morphology.

Render ilāh as “a god,” “deity,” or “object of worship” according to the sentence, ilāhayn as “two gods,” and ālihah as “gods” or “deities.” A word may name what others claim as a god without affirming that it deserves worship.

Keep the proper name distinct from the common noun. Likewise, obedience or trust in an ordinary relationship is not automatically worship; discussions of desire and power require attention to the passage and the relationship it describes.

Summary

The root A L H in the Qur’an confronts us with the principle of tawḥīd. Ilāh means a god or object of worship: something a person places in the station of worship, final command, absolute reliance, or the center of surrender.

But Allah means The One God: not one god among other gods, but the unique station of divinity with no partner, equal, likeness, replacement, or rival.

Lā ilāha illā Allah means that all false gods are negated and the final station remains only for The One God. This negation frees the human being from servitude to idols, powers, fears, blind traditions, desire, and constructed lords.

The Qur’an repeatedly declares God pure and exalted above what people associate with Him. Shirk is not only outward idol worship. Shirk can also occur at the level of thought, law, obedience, fear, love, tradition, and religious authority.

Whenever something other than The God sits in the station of final command, the danger of shirk begins. Whenever human speech is placed above the Book of God, the danger of shirk begins. Whenever a group claims ownership over salvation, faith, or people’s religion, the danger of shirk begins. Whenever the name of God becomes a tool of domination, takfīr, religious trade, or the separation of believers, the human being has moved away from the true meaning of Allah.

For this reason, we say:

Pure and exalted is The God above what they associate with Him.

Pure and exalted is The God above what they attribute to Him.

Pure and exalted is The God above what they ascribe to Him.

Therefore, our aim is not to defend one word against another word. Our aim is to return hearts to the truth toward which all prophets called: The One God, Creator of the worlds, Lord of the Day of Accountability, and owner of all beautiful names.

قُلِ ادْعُوا اللَّهَ أَوِ ادْعُوا الرَّحْمَٰنَ ۖ أَيًّا مَا تَدْعُوا فَلَهُ الْأَسْمَاءُ الْحُسْنَىٰ quli idʿū Allāha awi idʿū al-Raḥmān; ayyan mā tadʿū fa-lahu al-asmāʾu al-ḥusnā
