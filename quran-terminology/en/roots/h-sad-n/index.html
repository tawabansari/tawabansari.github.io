From fortresses and protection to chastity and marriage

The Arabic root ح ص ن concerns protection, safeguarding, and making something secure against access or violation. حِصْن denotes a fortress or a place whose interior is difficult to reach. Within the same family, حَصِين describes a place or thing that is strong and protected, while تَحْصِين refers to the act of protecting and fortifying.

This semantic field is not limited to buildings and military equipment. In Arabic, a person’s safeguarding of chastity is also expressed through this root. Marriage and the social condition of being free have likewise been counted among the forms of iḥṣān in some usages. These meanings are distinct: a person may be chaste without being married, or married while violating marital commitments. The designation muḥṣan alone therefore does not establish all these qualities.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs 18 times in 12 verses. Its Qur’anic forms are حُصُونُهُمْ, مُحَصَّنَةٍ, تُحْصِنُونَ, لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ, أَحْصَنَتْ, أُحْصِنَّ, مُحْصِنِينَ, مُحْصَنَات in definite and indefinite forms, and تَحَصُّنًا.

These usages show that the root concerns fortresses, armor, and food reserves, as well as safeguarding chastity, marriage, and human dignity against false accusation.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one basic principle for ح ص ن: preservation, safeguarding, and keeping something secure.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: preservation, safeguarding, and security

In Al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad likewise defines حِصْن as a place whose contents are not easily accessible. This definition clarifies both sides of the relationship: something lies within a boundary, and that boundary prevents outside access or violation.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: ḥiṣn and the inaccessibility of its interior

From this meaning, حَصُنَ is used for a place becoming strong and inaccessible, حَصَّنَ for protecting and fortifying it, and تَحَصَّنَ for taking refuge and placing oneself within protection.

Protection in this family does not always mean building walls. Its means may be a fortress, armor, a storage place, a person’s own conduct, or a social and legal condition.

Fortresses, armor, and protective equipment

In its familiar usage, حِصْن denotes a fortress or defensive building. حُصُون is its plural. حَصَّنَ الْمَدِينَةَ means to strengthen and secure a city against danger.

When describing military equipment, دِرْعٌ حَصِينَةٌ denotes armor whose strong construction protects the body from injury.

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā reads:

وَكُلُّ دِلَاصٍ كَالْأَضَاةِ حَصِينَةٍ
تَرَى فَضْلَهَا عَنْ رَبِّهَا يَتَذَبْذَبُ

wa-kullu dilāṣin ka-l-aḍāti ḥaṣīnatin / tarā faḍlahā ʿan rabbihā yatadhabdhabu

Meaning: And every protective coat of smooth, gleaming mail, like a pool; you see its excess hanging away from its wearer and swaying.

In this verse, دِلَاص describes smooth, gleaming armor, while حَصِينَة emphasizes its strength and protective capacity. The armor does not prevent its wearer from moving, but interposes a barrier between the body and an enemy’s weapon.

The witness is recorded in Al-Aʿshā’s Dīwān and in dictionaries. Although Al-Aʿshā is known as a poet of the pre-Islamic period, the precise date of this particular verse should not be treated as certain without independent evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ḥ Ṣ N: Al-Aʿshā’s armor witness

Protection does not always depend on walls

A verse attributed to Al-Asʿar al-Juʿfī reads:

وَلَقَدْ عَلِمْتُ عَلَى تَجَشُّمِيَ الرَّدَى
أَنَّ الْحُصُونَ الْخَيْلُ لَا مَدَرُ الْقُرَى

wa-la-qad ʿalimtu ʿalā tajashshumiya al-radā / anna al-ḥuṣūna al-khaylu lā madaru al-qurā

Meaning: Despite undertaking the peril of death, I have learned that the true fortresses are horses, not the clay buildings of settlements.

In this image, the poet favors the defensive strength and mobility of horses over shelter behind clay walls.

The verse is transmitted in early literary sources, including Al-Aṣmaʿiyyāt. Its importance for this root study is that ḥiṣn is not only the name of a building: something that serves the function of defense and protection may also be described by the same word.

Al-Aṣmaʿiyyāt: Al-Asʿar al-Juʿfī’s witness and its transmitted wording

This literary usage is not, however, the direct meaning of ḥiṣn in every sentence. When the Qur’an speaks of a people’s حُصُون, actual fortresses should be considered first, unless the context provides another indication.

Ḥaṣān, ḥāṣin, and muḥṣan

In Arabic, حَصَان and حَاصِن are recorded for a chaste woman. حَصَانَة likewise concerns strength and security and, in human usage, chastity.

Within the same family, حِصَان is also a name for a stallion. Lexicographers offer explanations connecting this name with protection, strength, or the horse’s characteristics. Without sufficient historical evidence, however, this usage cannot be made the foundation of every meaning of the root.

When applied to people, إِحْصَان can express safeguarding chastity. In some usages, marriage is also considered a means of iḥṣān. In early lexical and legal sources, free status and being Muslim are sometimes counted among its aspects as well.

This range must not turn all these qualities into the root’s basic meaning. The lexical core is protection and security; whether a person is called muḥṣan in a particular verse because of chastity, marriage, or social status must be determined from context.

Al-Muḥīṭ and Lisān al-ʿArab: chastity, marriage, and free status distinguished

2. Material protection in the Qur’an: fortresses, armor, and reserves

Al-Ḥashr 59:2 and Al-Ḥashr 59:14: fortresses and fortified settlements

In Al-Ḥashr 59:2, the Qur’an speaks of a group among the People of the Book who thought their fortresses would protect them against what was coming. حُصُونُهُمْ in this verse is the plural of حِصْن with the pronoun هُمْ attached.

Here the fortresses are actual places of protection. The verse shows that their occupants relied on the strength of these refuges, but events unfolded contrary to their calculations.

The direct meaning of the word must be distinguished from the story’s outcome. حُصُون does not mean “imaginary security”: these are real fortresses whose occupants trusted their defensive capacity. The inadequacy of that confidence emerges from the events described in the verse, not from the word itself.

Al-Ḥashr 59:14 states:

لَا يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ جَمِيعًا إِلَّا فِي قُرًى مُحَصَّنَةٍ أَوْ مِنْ وَرَاءِ جُدُرٍ بَأْسُهُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ شَدِيدٌ تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

lā yuqātilūnakum jamīʿan illā fī quran muḥaṣṣanatin aw min warāʾi judurin baʾsuhum baynahum shadīdun taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yaʿqilūna

Translation: They do not fight you all together except in fortified settlements or from behind walls. Their conflict among themselves is fierce. You think them united, while their hearts are divided. That is because they are a people who do not reason.

مُحَصَّنَة in this verse is a form II passive participle. It describes settlements strengthened by defensive means.

The distinction between حُصُون and قُرًى مُحَصَّنَة is also clear. The first refers to fortresses themselves; the second to settlements protected by defensive structures.

This usage does not imply that every fortified place is safe from every danger. Taḥṣīn expresses a protective condition and measure, not a guarantee of invincibility.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80: protection through armor

وَعَلَّمْنَاهُ صَنْعَةَ لَبُوسٍ لَكُمْ لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ مِنْ بَأْسِكُمْ فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ شَاكِرُونَ

wa-ʿallamnāhu ṣanʿata labūsin lakum li-tuḥṣinakum min baʾsikum fa-hal antum shākirūna

Translation: And We taught him the making of battle clothing for you, to protect you from the harm of your warfare. Will you, then, be thankful?

The verse concerns David and his being taught to make protective battle clothing.

لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ is a form IV verb. Together with the particle لِـ, it expresses the purpose of making that clothing. Protection here is entirely tangible: defensive clothing interposes a barrier between the human body and injury in battle. In the reading of Ḥafṣ, the verb begins with tāʾ and may refer back to صَنْعَة, or to the armor through the feminine noun دِرْع. Readings with yāʾ and nūn are also recorded in sources on Qur’anic readings.

Al-Baghawī, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80: readings with tāʾ, yāʾ, and nūn

The particle مِنْ in مِنْ بَأْسِكُمْ is also significant: it specifies what the person is protected against.

This is clear evidence that iḥṣān in the Qur’an does not inherently have a sexual or marital meaning. The same root used elsewhere for chastity and marriage here concerns physical protection by armor.

Yūsuf 12:48: preserving reserves

ثُمَّ يَأْتِي مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ سَبْعٌ شِدَادٌ يَأْكُلْنَ مَا قَدَّمْتُمْ لَهُنَّ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِمَّا تُحْصِنُونَ

thumma yaʾtī min baʿdi dhālika sabʿun shidādun yaʾkulna mā qaddamtum lahunna illā qalīlan mimmā tuḥṣinūna

Translation: Then, after that, seven hard years will come, consuming what you have put forward for them, except a little of what you preserve.

In Yūsuf 12:47, Joseph speaks of cultivation and leaving the harvest in its ears. This verse turns to the seven difficult years that follow.

In this context, تُحْصِنُونَ refers to keeping a portion of the food reserve secure. What is preserved must be protected against consumption and loss.

Neither a fortress nor armor is involved here, but the basic protective relationship remains. Food is kept in a condition that allows it to be used when needed.

This usage shows that protection in ح ص ن is not confined to preventing an enemy’s attack. Keeping something from being lost or consumed can also be expressed through this family.

On the other hand, تُحْصِنُونَ alone does not prescribe a particular storage method. Leaving grain in its ears comes from Yūsuf 12:47, not from the verb’s morphological meaning.

3. Human iḥṣān: chastity and the will to protect oneself

Mary and safeguarding chastity

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91 states:

وَالَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهَا مِنْ رُوحِنَا وَجَعَلْنَاهَا وَابْنَهَا آيَةً لِلْعَالَمِينَ

wa-llatī aḥṣanat farjahā fa-nafakhnā fīhā min rūḥinā wa-jaʿalnāhā wa-bnahā āyatan li-l-ʿālamīna

Translation: And the woman who guarded her chastity: We breathed into her of Our spirit, and made her and her son a sign for all worlds.

In this verse, أَحْصَنَتْ is an active verb and فَرْجَهَا is its object. Mary herself is presented as the agent who safeguards her chastity.

The same expression is used of Mary in Al-Taḥrīm 66:12.

In these two verses, iḥṣān does not mean marrying. In this context, Mary is characterized by safeguarding her chastity, not by having a husband.

This is important evidence against restricting the root to marital status. أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا expresses guarding sexual privacy. Further details of Mary’s life and Jesus’s birth appear elsewhere, but should not all be added to the lexical meaning of أَحْصَنَتْ.

Al-Nūr 24:33: the will to remain chaste

In Al-Nūr 24:33, تَحَصُّنًا appears in the discussion of enslaved women and the prohibition against forcing them into prostitution.

تَحَصُّن is a form V verbal noun. In this context, it means safeguarding chastity and avoiding forced or illicit sexual relations.

It differs from أَحْصَنَتْ in that taḥaṣṣun emphasizes a person’s action or inclination toward keeping herself protected.

The phrase إِنْ أَرَدْنَ تَحَصُّنًا also shows that the women’s will is present in the text. They are human beings with wishes and a right to safeguard their own privacy, not objects others may exploit for financial benefit.

This qualification must not be converted into permission for sexual coercion when such a wish is absent or unexpressed. The verse addresses a situation in which the women desire chastity; its wording does not establish permission for coercion in a different situation.

Here iḥṣān does not mean free status either, since the same women living in slavery may desire taḥaṣṣun and the safeguarding of their chastity.

From a lexical perspective, therefore, chastity is a form of conduct and an ethical condition that does not depend on whether a person is free or enslaved.

4. Muḥṣanāt in the marriage verses

In verses concerning marriage, iḥṣān and muḥṣanāt acquire a range broader than a simple ethical description. Here the distinction between chastity, marriage, and social status is particularly important.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:24: married women

At the beginning of Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, الْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ continues a list of women with whom marriage is prohibited.

Given the context of marriage provisions, the appropriate meaning of الْمُحْصَنَاتُ here is married women.

This sense cannot be generalized to every occurrence. If الْمُحْصَنَاتُ here were translated simply as “chaste women,” it might suggest that marriage to chaste women is forbidden, whereas Al-Māʾidah 5:5 expressly discusses the possibility of marriage to chaste women.

An existing marriage and an established marital bond are therefore decisive contextual indications in Al-Nisāʾ 4:24.

The same verse uses مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ of men. Here muḥṣinīn does not mean men who already have spouses or have previously married. It concerns those seeking a lawful and chaste relationship, rather than sexual relations outside that framework.

Two forms of one root thus occur in the same verse with different functions: first for women in an existing marriage, then for men who are to seek a lawful relationship.

The exception إِلَّا مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ near the beginning of the verse is also a separate legal question, not resolved solely by defining the root. The lexical meaning of muḥṣanāt cannot by itself establish the conditions, limits, and legal effects of that exception.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25: three different positions of muḥṣanāt

This verse is among the most important witnesses to the root’s semantic range. Muḥṣanāt occurs three times, and the verb أُحْصِنَّ once.

At the beginning, الْمُحْصَنَاتِ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ is contrasted with believing women living in slavery. This contrast shows that muḥṣanāt in this position concerns the status of free women, alongside the general field of protection and chastity.

“Free believing women” is appropriate in context. Freedom is not, however, the fixed meaning of every derivative of ح ص ن: it emerges from the contrast between the two groups in this verse.

Later in the verse, after the instruction to marry and pay the women their financial dues, the phrase مُحْصَنَاتٍ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ appears.

Here muḥṣanāt refers to chastity and keeping the relationship within a lawful framework. This description applies to the very women living in slavery.

Consequently, a definition of muḥṣanāt as always meaning free women encounters a limiting counterexample in the second occurrence within this same verse.

The third part contains فَإِذَا أُحْصِنَّ. In the widely used reading of Ḥafṣ, the verb is passive. Given the instruction to marry in the preceding passage, “when they have married” or “have entered a marriage” is a strong reading suited to the context.

The traditions of reading and interpretation nevertheless also record the active reading أَحْصَنَّ, along with explanations such as becoming Muslim or chaste. This difference should not be concealed. Choosing marriage in this context rests on the connection between the clauses and the verse’s structure, not on a claim that أَحْصَنَ always means only marriage.

The verse continues by saying that if these women commit indecency after iḥṣān, they incur half the punishment prescribed for الْمُحْصَنَاتِ. At this point, the comparison is with free women.

This shows that differences in legal status have a role in the verse’s provision. All details of punishment and jurisprudence should not, however, be placed within the root’s lexical definition. The divisibility of the punishment is an important question for a separate study of Qur’anic provisions, to be examined with Al-Nūr 24:2 and other relevant verses.

With four occurrences of this root in one verse, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 makes clear that a derivative’s meaning cannot be established without examining what it concerns, its grammatical position, and the contextual contrasts.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25: senses of iḥṣān and variant readings

Al-Māʾidah 5:5: chastity within marriage

Al-Māʾidah 5:5 places الْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ and الْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ alongside one another.

The verse discusses the possibility of marriage to chaste women among the believers and the People of the Book, and also addresses payment of their financial dues.

In this context, muḥṣanāt cannot mean women who already have husbands: the verse concerns women being considered for marriage, not women simultaneously in another existing marriage.

“Chaste women” is therefore a more appropriate equivalent here. Early commentaries also transmit “free women” for this position, however; Al-Ṭabarī prefers that interpretation. “Chaste” is thus this study’s contextual choice, not the sole, undisputed meaning in the interpretive tradition.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Māʾidah 5:5: chastity and freedom, and Al-Ṭabarī’s preference

The same verse also describes men through مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ وَلَا مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ. This construction contrasts lawful marriage with sexual relationships outside it.

This usage makes clear that iḥṣān is not only a description of women. Men too are to exercise chastity and responsibility in sexual conduct and marriage.

5. Muḥṣanāt and protecting human reputation

Al-Nūr 24:4: accusing chaste women

وَالَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَأْتُوا بِأَرْبَعَةِ شُهَدَاءَ فَاجْلِدُوهُمْ ثَمَانِينَ جَلْدَةً وَلَا تَقْبَلُوا لَهُمْ شَهَادَةً أَبَدًا وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْفَاسِقُونَ

wa-lladhīna yarmūna al-muḥṣanāti thumma lam yaʾtū bi-arbaʿati shuhadāʾa fa-jlidūhum thamānīna jaldatan wa-lā taqbalū lahum shahādatan abadan wa-ulāʾika humu al-fāsiqūna

Translation: And those who accuse chaste women and then do not produce four witnesses: give them eighty lashes, and never accept their testimony; those are the defiantly disobedient.

Here الْمُحْصَنَاتِ refers to women accused of illicit sexual conduct. The issue is protecting people’s reputation and dignity against an unproven allegation.

“Chaste women” is suitable in this context. The verse does not stipulate that these women must be married. Restricting the word’s application to married women therefore does not follow from the expression الْمُحْصَنَاتِ itself.

Another important point is that protecting someone’s reputation is not the same as proving innocence through knowledge of every part of her private life. The verse places responsibility for proving the claim upon the accuser and makes the absence of the required witnesses the subject of its provision.

The lexical meaning of muḥṣanāt is not itself the four-witness rule. That rule is expressed in the verse’s following clauses and should not be added to the root’s definition.

Al-Nūr 24:23: muḥṣanāt, ghāfilāt, and muʾmināt

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَرْمُونَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ الْغَافِلَاتِ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ لُعِنُوا فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

inna alladhīna yarmūna al-muḥṣanāti al-ghāfilāti al-muʾmināti luʿinū fī al-dunyā wa-l-ākhirati wa-lahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

Translation: Those who accuse chaste, unsuspecting, believing women are cursed in this world and the Hereafter, and a great punishment awaits them.

Three descriptions appear side by side here: الْمُحْصَنَاتِ, الْغَافِلَاتِ, and الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ.

Their presence shows that they should not be treated as synonyms. Muḥṣanāt concerns chastity; ghāfilāt in this context refers to women unsuspecting of the accusation and remote from the illicit conduct; muʾmināt describes their faith.

Rendering الْمُحْصَنَاتِ broadly as “good, believing women” would erase the distinction between the verse’s descriptions.

Chastity here also bears on a person’s social standing, since false accusation violates her privacy and credibility. Muḥṣanāt does not, by itself, mean “having social prestige,” however. That implication comes from the word’s use in the context of accusation and the relevant provision.

Neither of these verses should be turned into a claim that people who have erred in the past have no dignity or rights against false accusation. Determining the full scope of the provisions on accusation and testimony requires a separate study of the relevant verses; all those provisions cannot be extracted from the meaning of one adjective.

6. Morphological distinctions, related roots, and translation proposals

The difference between muḥṣin and muḥṣan

In studying this root, the word’s vowel distinctions matter.

مُحْصِن, with kasrah on ṣād, has the regular structure of a form IV active participle and refers to someone who practices iḥṣān or is engaged in safeguarding himself.

مُحْصَن, with fatḥah on ṣād, has the regular structure of a passive participle and refers to someone placed in a condition of iḥṣān or protected by something.

In usages concerning women, the meanings of these two forms may approach each other. Lexical sources also discuss the difference between chastity arising from a person’s own conduct and iḥṣān resulting from marriage or an external factor.

The vowel on ṣād alone must not, however, be used to decide that someone is necessarily married or unmarried. In Qur’anic readings and Arabic usage, the range of these forms is broader, and identifying the referent requires context.

Likewise, the form II مُحَصَّنَة, as in قُرًى مُحَصَّنَة in Al-Ḥashr 59:14, differs morphologically from the form IV مُحْصَنَات. Both may convey protection, but their morphological forms are distinct.

Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥiṣn) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥifẓ)

Ḥifẓ has a broad semantic field: keeping, caring for, remembering, and preventing something from being lost.

In its tangible usage, ḥiṣn emphasizes security against access or violation and being in a protected condition.

Fortresses and armor, for example, are clear instances of taḥṣīn, whereas ḥifẓ can take place without such a defensive structure.

This is a tendency, not an absolute separation between the two roots. Yūsuf 12:48 shows that iḥṣān can also concern preserving food reserves.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥiṣn) and ʿ F F (ʿafāf)

ʿIffah concerns refraining from improper conduct and keeping oneself within the bounds of chastity.

In sexual usage, iḥṣān can express this condition, but its semantic field is broader, also including marriage, fortresses, armor, and reserves.

In Al-Nūr 24:33, taḥaṣṣun approaches chastity. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80, the same family concerns protection through armor and has no connection with the sexual meaning of ʿiffah.

Chastity is therefore one contextual meaning of iḥṣān, not the definition of the entire root.

Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥiṣn) and Ḥ Ṣ R (ḥaṣr)

Ḥaṣr concerns restriction, placing in straits, and preventing expansion or movement.

Ḥiṣn also creates a restriction on access, but its familiar purpose is to protect what lies within the secure boundary.

A fortress, for example, may make an enemy’s entry difficult; yet not every restriction or siege amounts to protecting the person being confined.

حَصُور in Āl ʿImrān 3:39 is also from ح ص ر, not ح ص ن. Its thematic proximity to self-restraint does not justify identifying the two roots with each other.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ R

Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥiṣn) and Ḥ S N (ḥusn)

These roots differ only in their middle letter. ح ص ن is written with ṣād and concerns protection, while ح س ن is written with sīn and concerns goodness and beauty.

مُحْصِن and مُحْسِن are likewise different words: the first concerns iḥṣān and protection, the second iḥsān and doing good.

Their visual or phonetic resemblance should not lead to transferring the characteristics of one to the other.

Study of Ḥ S N

Translation proposals for Forqan

For verses about material protection, the equivalents are clear. حُصُونُهُمْ in Al-Ḥashr 59:2 means “their fortresses,” and مُحَصَّنَة in Al-Ḥashr 59:14 means “fortified” or “provided with defensive fortifications.” لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80 means “to protect you,” while تُحْصِنُونَ in Yūsuf 12:48 means “you preserve,” given the context of storing grain.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:91 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:12, أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا is rendered “she guarded her chastity.” تَحَصُّنًا in Al-Nūr 24:33 likewise refers to “chastity” or “safeguarding chastity.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, الْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ means “married women” in context, while مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ in the same verse describes men seeking a lawful and chaste relationship.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, the three occurrences of muḥṣanāt must be examined separately: at the beginning, the context points to free believing women; in the second occurrence, chastity is contrasted with illicit intercourse and secret relationships; in the third, free women provide the standard for a legal comparison. أُحْصِنَّ in the same verse, given the sequence of clauses, concerns entering marriage.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:5, Al-Nūr 24:4, and Al-Nūr 24:23, “chaste women” is a suitable equivalent for muḥṣanāt, since these contexts do not generally call for the meaning “married women.”

Uniformly translating muḥṣanāt as “married women” in every verse is therefore mistaken; translating it as “chaste women” everywhere likewise erases significant contextual differences.

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: In Arabic, ح ص ن denotes protection, safeguarding, and making something secure against access or violation. حِصْن is a place that protects what is inside it; taḥṣīn is the act of strengthening and securing; iḥṣān can apply in material, behavioral, and social contexts.

In the Qur’an, the root extends from actual fortresses and fortified settlements to armor and food reserves. In human usage, safeguarding chastity, entering marriage, and certain social conditions are also expressed through this family.

These usages do not mean that marriage, chastity, and free status are one concept. Mary is described through أَحْصَنَتْ without reference to marriage. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, enslaved women are also called muḥṣanāt with regard to chastity. In Al-Māʾidah 5:5, chaste women being considered for marriage are called muḥṣanāt, whereas at the beginning of Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, the same family refers to women in an existing marital bond.

Thus, the shared semantic core is protection and security, but the means of protection and the resulting condition differ in each usage. A fortress protects through the strength of its structure; armor protects the body; preservation protects food reserves; ethical iḥṣān safeguards a person’s chastity. Marriage and legal status are also counted among the aspects of iḥṣān in some contexts, without limiting every derivative to them.

To understand and translate this root, first identify the morphological form and what the word concerns. The verse’s context then clarifies whether material protection, chastity, marriage, or legal status is intended. This approach preserves the family’s shared meaning while preventing one limited instance from being imposed on every Qur’anic occurrence.
