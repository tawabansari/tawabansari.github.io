Qur'anic Root

Qur'anic Root DH L L

Being tamed, made accessible, humbled, or lowered from honor.

1. Short Introduction to the Root

The root ذ ل ل is one of the roots whose semantic field in the Qur'an is far broader than the common translation “humiliation.” The Qur'an uses derivatives of this root for livestock, the earth, paths, the fruits of the Garden, the relationship of a child with father and mother, the conduct of believers with one another, and also the breaking of honor and the descent of a person or society from a previous position. For this reason, all of its uses cannot be rendered with one fixed equivalent such as “humiliation,” “lowliness,” or “humility.” What joins these diverse uses together is not a fixed moral judgment, but a kind of reduction or removal of resistance, rebelliousness, hardness, inaccessibility, or superiority. The result of this change is determined by the subject and context of the verse: sometimes it is being tamed and prepared, sometimes accessibility, sometimes conscious humility, and sometimes the loss of honor and being caught in humiliation. Therefore, “humiliation” is one branch of this root, not its comprehensive definition.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

Among the most important Qur'anic forms of this root are these derivatives: ذُلّ: a state of humility, lowering oneself, or setting aside superiority; its direction is determined by context. ذِلّة: loss of honor, falling from a position, or being caught in a lower condition. ذَلِيل: lower, broken, or powerless before a dominant force. ذَلُول: tamed, prepared, controllable, or available for use; its precise meaning becomes clear from the subject of speech. ذُلُل: plural of ذلول; in the context of paths or beings, it denotes being prepared, passable, or compliant. ذَلَّلْنَا: We tamed and prepared; a transitive verb that expresses bringing this state into being. ذُلِّلَتْ: was made tame or accessible. أَذِلَّة: plural of أذلّ or ذليل, which may mean humble and gentle in conduct, or low and powerless, depending on context. الأَذَلّ / الأَذَلِّين: lower, more humiliated, or those who have been placed in a lower position. تُذِلّ: to bring someone into a state of humiliation or lowness. Shared root membership does not mean that all these forms have exactly one meaning or moral value. Morphological form, grammatical role, subject, object, preposition, and the context of each verse all contribute to determining the final meaning.

4. The Semantic Core of the Root

When the Qur'anic uses are placed together, the semantic core of this root can be formulated as follows:

The removal or reduction of a state of resistance, rebelliousness, hardness, superiority, or inaccessibility, such that a being or person enters a new relation of being tamed, accessible, humble, compliant, or lowered.

This definition deliberately does not assign a fixed moral value to the root. The root itself does not say whether this change is good or bad. When livestock is made tame for human beings, the result is beneficial and morally neutral. When fruit is placed within reach, the verse speaks of ease and blessing. When a child humbles himself toward parents out of mercy, that state is a virtue. But when a person falls from honor as a result of arrogance, wrongdoing, or defeat, the same semantic field reaches humiliation and disgrace. So it is better to distinguish between the core of the root and the contextual result of each derivative.

5. The Material Image of the Root

One of the clearest images of this root is a powerful animal that has been tamed and made controllable. Being tamed does not mean the destruction of strength. The animal may still be stronger than the human being, but its strength no longer resists human direction and use. This image is helpful for understanding some uses, but it should not be turned into the certain origin of all meanings of the root. The Qur'an itself uses this root in various settings, and each use must be examined separately. The image of the tamed animal can clarify the link between some branches, but the final definition of the root is not built from one material image alone.

6. Reported Lexical Distinctions

Lexical sources report distinctions between forms such as ذُلّ and ذِلّة. Some explanations place ذُلّ more in the field of softness and compliance, and ذِلّة in contrast with honor. These distinctions are useful for understanding semantic tendencies, but they should not be turned into an absolute rule. Vowel pattern, morphological form, and sentence structure can affect use, but none of them alone produces a complete moral or interpretive meaning. In every verse, we must see what the derivative is used for, who the subject is, what relation is being built, and what result the continuation of the verse states.

7. Qur'anic Uses of the Root

Taming Livestock

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا خَلَقْنَا لَهُم مِّمَّا عَمِلَتْ أَيْدِينَا أَنْعَامًا فَهُمْ لَهَا مَالِكُونَ ۝ وَذَلَّلْنَاهَا لَهُمْ فَمِنْهَا رَكُوبُهُمْ وَمِنْهَا يَأْكُلُونَ

Have they not seen that We created for them, from what Our hands made, livestock, and they are owners over them? And We tamed them and placed them at their disposal; so some of them are their mounts, and from some they eat.

Ya-Sin 36:71-72

The verb ذَلَّلْنَا speaks of bringing about a state that makes human benefit from livestock possible. The continuation of the verse explains the result: riding and eating. Therefore, “We tamed and placed at disposal” is more natural than “We humiliated.” Too much meaning should not be extracted from the morphological form by itself. What is clear in this verse is transitivity and the creation of a state of being tamed; the benefit is understood from the continuation of the verse.

A Cow That Was Not Dhalul

قَالَ إِنَّهُ يَقُولُ إِنَّهَا بَقَرَةٌ لَّا ذَلُولٌ تُثِيرُ الْأَرْضَ وَلَا تَسْقِي الْحَرْثَ مُسَلَّمَةٌ لَّا شِيَةَ فِيهَا

He said: He says it is a cow that is not tamed and trained for work, to plow the earth or water the field; sound, with no mark in it.

Al-Baqarah 2:71

In this verse, ذلول describes a cow that is tame and prepared for work. The phrase “to plow the earth and water the field” is the result or example of the function of such an animal, not the entire definition of the word. The verse does not say that every ذلول necessarily plows; rather, this cow is not from the tamed and trained animals used in these tasks.

The Earth as Dhalul

هُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ ذَلُولًا فَامْشُوا فِي مَنَاكِبِهَا وَكُلُوا مِن رِّزْقِهِ وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ

He is the One who made the earth prepared and available for your benefit; so walk through its expanses and eat from His provision, and to Him is the rising.

Al-Mulk 67:15

Here the translation “humiliated” makes no sense. The earth is not a moral being that is humiliated. The description ذلول is clarified by the two commands that follow: movement on the earth and benefiting from its provision. This description is not limited to complete flatness or physical softness of the entire earth; mountains, valleys, rock, and sea are also part of the earth. The point is that the earth as a whole has been made suitable for human life, movement, and benefit. “Prepared and available for benefit” conveys the contextual result more naturally in English, while the image of “tamed” can be preserved in root-level explanation.

Fruits Within Reach

وَدَانِيَةً عَلَيْهِمْ ظِلَالُهَا وَذُلِّلَتْ قُطُوفُهَا تَذْلِيلًا

Its shades are near over them, and its fruits have been fully placed within reach.

Al-Insan 76:14

In this verse, ذُلِّلَتْ is used of clusters and fruits. Neither is the value of the fruit reduced, nor is humiliation being discussed; the obstacle to access has been removed. The verbal noun تذليلاً emphasizes the act of making accessible, but its physical quality should not be made more certain than what the verse says. The verse reports ease of access, not the exact shape of the branches or the material mechanism of it.

The Paths of the Lord

ثُمَّ كُلِي مِن كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا

Then eat from all the fruits and follow the paths of your Lord, while they are made smooth and possible for you.

An-Nahl 16:69

In the grammatical connection of ذُلُلًا, more than one possibility has been raised: it may describe the paths, meaning paths prepared and passable; or it may be understood as a state of the addressee, the bee: follow the paths of your Lord obediently. Both possibilities fit the field of the root, but neither should be presented without decisive evidence as the only analysis. What is clear from the context is the availability of the way to perform the task assigned to the bee. Turning this expression into a definite theory about flight networks, navigation, or a specific scientific mechanism would go beyond the text of the verse.

8. Human and Ethical Uses

The Wing of Humility From Mercy

وَاخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ وَقُل رَّبِّ ارْحَمْهُمَا كَمَا رَبَّيَانِي صَغِيرًا

And lower for them the wing of humility from mercy, and say: My Lord, have mercy on them, just as they raised me when I was small.

Al-Isra 17:24

In this verse, ذُلّ cannot be translated as “humiliation.” The phrase مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ clarifies the source and direction of this state: humility from mercy, not from fear, compulsion, or degradation. The main image of the verse is built from the combination of “lowering the wing” and “ذل.” The result is setting aside self-superiority and behaving gently and mercifully with parents. This verse does not speak of destroying the dignity of the child; it speaks of consciously restraining superiority before those who raised him in childhood.

أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَن يَرْتَدَّ مِنكُمْ عَن دِينِهِ فَسَوْفَ يَأْتِي اللَّهُ بِقَوْمٍ يُحِبُّهُمْ وَيُحِبُّونَهُ أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَعِزَّةٍ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ يُجَاهِدُونَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَخَافُونَ لَوْمَةَ لَائِمٍ

O you who have faith, whoever among you turns back from his path, The God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him; humble and gentle with the believers, firm and honored against those who cover truth; they strive in the path of The God and do not fear the blame of any blamer.

Al-Maidah 5:54

Translating أذلة as “humiliated” does not fit the continuation of the verse. This group is the very people who strive in the path of The God and do not fear blame. So the verse is not describing broken, will-less personalities. The contrast between أذلة على المؤمنين and أعزة على الكافرين shows two modes of relation: within the community of believers, they are not people of superiority and domination; but before the front of kufr and hostility, they do not lose their honor and firmness. “Humble and gentle with the believers” is a more suitable rendering. The pairing of أذلة with the preposition على is grammatically discussed. One may speak of implication or rhetorical differences, but the preposition alone does not produce a new and certain moral meaning. The main direction is understood from the contrast between the two descriptions and from the full context of the verse.

9. Dhillah as the Collapse of Honor

ضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ وَالْمَسْكَنَةُ

وَضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ وَالْمَسْكَنَةُ وَبَاءُوا بِغَضَبٍ مِّنَ اللَّهِ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَكْفُرُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ النَّبِيِّينَ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ ذَٰلِكَ بِمَا عَصَوا وَّكَانُوا يَعْتَدُونَ

And humiliation and miskana were placed upon them, and they returned with anger from The God. That was because they used to cover the signs of The God and kill the prophets without right; that was because they disobeyed and used to transgress.

Al-Baqarah 2:61

The structure ضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِم speaks of a state placed upon them, not of an innate or racial trait. The verse itself ties the cause to specific behaviors. Therefore, the verse is not a license to ascribe a fixed defect to every member of a people in every age. ذلة and مسكنة appear together and should not be treated as one thing. ذلة is close to falling from honor and position; مسكنة is close to a state of need, helplessness, and lack of refuge. The word مسكنة belongs to the family س ك ن, and its form and semantic development should be examined in the study of that root; one cannot invent an independent artificial root in order to separate its meaning. The pairing of the two words shows that the Qur'an brings together two related but non-identical dimensions: the loss of honor and being caught in a state of miskana.

The Exception in Al Imran

ضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ أَيْنَ مَا ثُقِفُوا إِلَّا بِحَبْلٍ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَحَبْلٍ مِّنَ النَّاسِ

Humiliation has been placed upon them wherever they are found, except by a bond from The God and a bond from the people.

Al Imran 3:112

The existence of an exception shows that the verse is not speaking about an unrestricted, innate, and unchangeable condition. The continuation of the verse again names behaviors such as covering the signs, killing the prophets, disobedience, and transgression as the cause. At the same time, this point should not be immediately turned into a universal historical law for all nations. What the verse itself clarifies is the connection between the stated condition and the behaviors that are mentioned.

Humiliation in Face and Gaze

In verses such as:

خَاشِعَةً أَبْصَارُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ

Their eyes are lowered, and humiliation overwhelms them.

Al-Qalam 68:43; Al-Ma'arij 70:44

Dhillah is a state that overwhelms them, and its effects appear in face and gaze. “Lowered eyes” and “humiliation” come together, but they are not complete synonyms: the first describes the state of the gaze, and the second describes their encompassing condition.

الأَعَزّ and الأَذَلّ

يَقُولُونَ لَئِن رَّجَعْنَا إِلَى الْمَدِينَةِ لَيُخْرِجَنَّ الْأَعَزُّ مِنْهَا الْأَذَلَّ وَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ وَلِرَسُولِهِ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

They say: If we return to Madinah, the more honored will surely expel from it the lower one; while honor belongs to The God, His messenger, and the believers, but the hypocrites do not know.

Al-Munafiqun 63:8

In this verse, الأعز and الأذل stand in direct contrast. The speaker sees the measure of honor in his own social power and influence, but the continuation of the verse rejects that claim and places the source of honor elsewhere. From this contrast one can understand that أذل here is not merely “physically weaker”; it is a lower position set against honor. Still, not every political or military defeat should automatically be treated as moral humiliation. The verse rejects the claimed measure of the hypocrites; it does not give a simple formula for measuring every historical event.

تُعِزُّ مَن تَشَاءُ وَتُذِلُّ مَن تَشَاءُ

قُلِ اللَّهُمَّ مَالِكَ الْمُلْكِ تُؤْتِي الْمُلْكَ مَن تَشَاءُ وَتَنزِعُ الْمُلْكَ مِمَّن تَشَاءُ وَتُعِزُّ مَن تَشَاءُ وَتُذِلُّ مَن تَشَاءُ بِيَدِكَ الْخَيْرُ إِنَّكَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Say: O The God, Owner of sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will and take sovereignty away from whom You will; You give honor to whom You will and bring into humiliation whom You will. Good is in Your hand; indeed, You are powerful over every thing.

Al Imran 3:26

The verse states “giving and taking sovereignty” separately from “giving honor and bringing into humiliation.” This separation shows that sovereignty and honor are not one concept; but from the mere grammatical separation one should not build a complete and certain system about the relation between politics and ethics. The verb تُذِلّ conveys transitivity: the subject places another in a state of humiliation. The manner and wisdom of this act must be understood within the wider network of verses concerning will, justice, human conduct, and the consequences of deeds. This morphological form by itself does not explain by what path and on what basis this humiliation is realized.

10. Independent Examination of Important Derivatives

ذَلُول

The dominant tendency of ذلول in the Qur'an is being prepared for control, movement, or use. In the cow, it is a tame and work-trained animal; in the earth, an environment prepared for movement and provision; and in its plural, paths that can be traversed or a state of compliance is seen. This word should not be limited in every case to “soft” or “smooth.” Softness and smoothness may be results in some instances, but the core of the use is reduction of obstacle and preparedness for a new relation or function.

ذُلّ

In the verse of parents, ذل is joined with the image of the “wing” and the qualification “from mercy.” Therefore, its suitable translation is mercy-rooted humility, not humiliation. This result comes from the full composition of the verse, not from the root alone.

ذِلّة

This form usually carries a negative weight and appears in contrast with honor or in contexts of punishment, collapse of position, and being overwhelmed. Even so, all details of cause and manner should not be inserted into the lexical definition. Wrongdoing, kufr, transgression, or denial are obtained from the context of the verses, not from the word ذلة itself.

أَذِلّة and أَذَلّ

أذلة in Al-Maidah 5:54 has a positive and relational meaning: humble and gentle with the believers. In other contexts, forms such as أذل and أذلين may denote lowness and humiliation. This difference shows that even a close structure does not have a fixed translation without context.

Tadhil and Idhlal

In the Qur'anic use of Form II, forms such as ذلّلنا and ذُلّلت are used for taming and preparing or making accessible. In Form IV, forms such as تذلّ appear in the field of human honor and humiliation. This is an important tendency in Qur'anic usage, but it should not be turned into an absolute rule for the entire Arabic language. The morphological form gives the capacity for transitivity or creating a state; the precise meaning of “taming,” “making accessible,” or “humiliating” becomes known from the object and context.

11. Grammatical and Structural Notes

Grammatical role is very important in this root, but it should not produce more meaning than it can carry. In جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ, the annexation of “wing” to “ذل” and the qualification “from mercy” together build the image and ethical direction. In أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ, the preposition “على” must be examined beside the contrast with أعزة على الكافرين. One cannot build a certain theory of domination, encompassing, or intensity from the preposition alone. In سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا, the connection of “ذللاً” with the paths or with the addressee is one of the cases where valid grammatical possibilities exist. Translation should either show the chosen possibility with caution or mention the other possibility in explanation. In ضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ, the passive structure and the preposition “على” report the placement or occurrence of a state upon them; but whether it is acquired or historical is understood from the whole structure and the causes stated later in the verse, not from “على” alone.

12. Comparison With Nearby Roots

ذ ل ل Compared with ع ز ز

These two roots stand opposite one another in many verses. ع ز ز tends toward firmness, dominance, inaccessibility, and honored position; ذ ل ل tends toward the removal of resistance or descent from a position. However, this contrast is not absolute. Mercy-rooted humility before parents is not blameworthy humiliation and can coexist with moral honor. On the other side, apparent arrogance is not necessarily true honor.

ذ ل ل Compared with خ ض ع

خ ض ع tends more toward lowering and submission before power or awe. ذ ل ل is broader and is also used of animals, the earth, and fruit. Therefore, not every ذل is خضوع, and not every خضوع is necessarily ذل. This boundary is a tendency, not an absolute separation; the meaning of each case must be examined from the real uses of that root.

ذ ل ل Compared with خ ش ع

خ ش ع often describes a subdued and receptive state of heart, voice, gaze, or even earth. ذ ل ل tends more toward a change in relation, resistance, or position. The effects of the two may appear together in one scene, as “lowered eyes” and “humiliation that overwhelms them” are gathered in one verse. This co-occurrence shows their nearness, but it does not prove that they are identical.

ذ ل ل Compared with ل ي ن

ل ي ن focuses on softness as opposed to hardness. Softness can be one result of ذل, but it is not its whole meaning. A tamed animal may be very powerful; what makes it ذلول is not mere softness, but acceptance of control and readiness for a specific function.

ذ ل ل Compared with ه و ن

ه و ن in some contexts tends toward ease, calmness, lightness, or unpretentious conduct. In the description of the servants of Ar-Rahman:

وَعِبَادُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الَّذِينَ يَمْشُونَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ هَوْنًا

The servants of Ar-Rahman are those who walk upon the earth with calm ease.

Al-Furqan 25:63

The speech is about a manner of walking marked by calmness and dignity. This is not identical with “the wing of ذل”; the verse of parents emphasizes setting aside superiority from mercy, not merely calm behavior.

13. Challenges of Understanding and Translation

The main challenge of this root is making all derivatives uniform with “humiliation” and “disgrace.” Such a translation becomes wrong or unintelligible in verses about the earth, livestock, paths, and fruits, and in the verses about parents and believers it changes the moral weight of the verse. The opposite excess is also wrong: one should not render all negative uses as “humility” or “softness” in order to avoid “humiliation.” The Qur'an truly speaks of humiliation, lowness, and loss of honor. The suitable way is that:

the base translation should be natural and concise;

the image of the root should be clarified in separate explanation;

the ethical or historical result should not be placed inside the lexical meaning;

every derivative should be translated according to its own structure and context.

14. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For ذَلَّلْنَا in Ya-Sin 72: “We tamed and placed at disposal.” For لَا ذَلُولٌ in Al-Baqarah 71: “not tamed and trained for work.” For الْأَرْضَ ذَلُولًا in Al-Mulk 15: “He made the earth prepared and available for your benefit.” For ذُلِّلَتْ قُطُوفُهَا in Al-Insan 14: “its fruits have been fully placed within reach.” For سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا in An-Nahl 69: depending on grammatical analysis, “follow the paths of your Lord, which are made smooth and possible for you,” or “follow the paths of your Lord obediently.” The other possibility is better mentioned in explanation. For جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ in Al-Isra 24: “the wing of humility from mercy.” For أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ in Al-Maidah 54: “humble and gentle with the believers.” For الذِّلَّة in negative contexts: depending on the verse, “humiliation,” “disgrace,” “lowness,” or “loss of honor.” For تُذِلّ in Al Imran 26: “You bring into humiliation” or “You lower,” according to the tone of the full verse translation. None of these equivalents alone is the translation of every derivative of the root.

15. Conclusion

The root ذ ل ل cannot be limited to “humiliation.” The Qur'an uses this root across a range of applications: the taming of livestock, the prepared state of the earth, the accessibility of fruits, the passability of paths, mercy-rooted humility toward parents, gentleness among believers, and also the collapse of honor and being caught in humiliation. The shared core of these uses is the removal or reduction of some kind of resistance, rebelliousness, hardness, superiority, or inaccessibility. But the final result is not obtained from the root alone. The subject of speech, the derivative used, the grammatical structure, and the context of the verse specify whether this change leads to being tamed, prepared, accessible, humble, or humiliated. Therefore, the final definition of the root can be stated as follows:

ذ ل ل expresses a change from a state of resistance, rebelliousness, superiority, hardness, or inaccessibility into a state in which a being or person becomes tamed, prepared, accessible, humble, or lowered from a previous position. The moral value and precise meaning of this change are determined by context.

This definition preserves the semantic unity of the root without reducing all derivatives to one fixed translation or placing the result of each verse in the root in advance.
