The Arabic root Ḥ B L concerns rope and something extending between two points to connect them. In its material sense, حَبْل (ḥabl) is a rope used to tie, pull, hold, or connect something. Arabic also uses the word for a bond, covenant, pledge, protection, or means of maintaining a relationship or security.

Other recorded uses of ḥabl concern bodily cords and vessels, and elongated formations of land. حِبَالَة (ḥibālah) concerns a snare or means of trapping. حَبَل (ḥabal) and حُبْلَى (ḥublā), concerning pregnancy, also belong to this lexical family, although their historical connection with rope should not be assumed without firm evidence.

Ḥ B L occurs seven times in six Qur’anic verses. All occurrences are singular or plural forms of the noun ḥabl. No verb or other derivative from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

These seven uses appear in different settings: The God’s rope, which believers are called to hold fast to; covenant and protection from The God or people; the ropes thrown by the magicians before Moses; ḥabl al-warīd in describing divine nearness to the human being; and the rope described around the neck of Abū Lahab’s wife.

These uses require distinguishing actual rope, an intangible bond, and figurative application. The same root can occur in all three settings, while the construction and context determine each expression’s meaning.

1. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • حَبْل (ḥabl): rope or cord used to tie, pull, hold, or connect; in other uses, covenant, pledge, and protection.
  • حِبَال (ḥibāl): a plural of ḥabl: ropes; figuratively, bonds and covenants.
  • أَحْبَال، أَحْبُل، حُبُول (aḥbāl, aḥbul, ḥubūl): other recorded plurals of ḥabl.
  • حَبَلَ ـ يَحْبُلُ (ḥabala–yaḥbulu): tied something with rope; in another use, caught game in a snare.
  • حَابِل (ḥābil): someone tying a rope or setting a snare; also a hunter catching game in a trap.
  • حِبَالَة (ḥibālah): a snare or device set to catch game.
  • حَبَائِل (ḥabāʾil): plural of ḥibālah: snares and trapping devices.
  • اِحْتَبَلَ (iḥtabala): hunted with a snare or trapped something.
  • مَحْبُول (maḥbūl): bound with rope or caught in a snare.
  • حَابُول (ḥābūl): a rope or device used to climb palm trees.
  • حَبْلُ الْعَاتِق (ḥabl al-ʿātiq): a cord or sinew in the shoulder and neck region.
  • حَبْلُ الْوَرِيد (ḥabl al-warīd): an established expression for a vessel in the neck.
  • حَبِلَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ (ḥabilati al-marʾatu): the woman became pregnant.
  • حَبَل (ḥabal): pregnancy in the relevant usage.
  • حُبْلَى (ḥublā): a pregnant woman, with plurals including حَبَالَى (ḥabālā).

The presence of these forms in dictionaries does not mean that they all occur in the Qur’an.

The actual Qur’anic forms are بِحَبْلِ (bi-ḥabli) in Āl ʿImrān 3:103; بِحَبْلٍ (bi-ḥablin) and وَحَبْلٍ (wa-ḥablin) in Āl ʿImrān 3:112; حِبَالُهُمْ (ḥibāluhum) in Ṭā Hā 20:66; حِبَالَهُمْ (ḥibālahum) in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44; حَبْلِ (ḥabli) in Qāf 50:16; and حَبْلٌ (ḥablun) in Al-Masad 111:5.

Morphologically, all return to the noun ḥabl. The differences concern singular or plural number, case, prepositions, and attached pronouns; they do not constitute seven independent derivatives.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Rope and continuous extension

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s core as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى امْتِدَادِ الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā imtidādi al-shayʾi

That is: “A single core indicating the extension of something.”

He then examines ḥabl as rope, حَبْلُ الْعَاتِق (ḥabl al-ʿātiq), and elongated stretches of sand within this family.

Extension matters here. A rope is normally a strand continuing along its length that can connect two things or tie one thing to another.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, ḥabl is explained through رِبَاط (ribāṭ): something used for tying.

Extension and tying nevertheless differ. Not everything elongated is a rope, nor is every bond established through a material rope.

A rope also serves more purposes than tying two objects together. It can pull a load, keep someone from falling, restrain an animal, or lift something off the ground.

The material sense of ḥabl thus combines an extended strand with possible uses in connection, grasping, restraint, and pulling.

These features also provide a basis for understanding figurative applications.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: extension, rope, and covenant

Rope in early Arab life

Rope was a familiar tool in Arab travel and pastoral life.

It was used to tether camels, hold their reins, secure loads, and fasten travel equipment together.

Lexicons use ḥabl and رَسَن (rasan) in closely related explanations of rope and animal restraint.

Rasan, however, more particularly concerns an animal’s halter, whereas ḥabl is a broader word for rope.

حَابُول (ḥābūl) is also recorded for a rope or device used to climb a palm tree.

In that use, the rope connects and supports. It helps a person reach somewhere that would otherwise be more difficult or dangerous to reach.

This image helps explain figurative ḥabl: a rope can maintain connection, access, and safety.

It does not establish that all the root’s metaphors necessarily arose from climbing palms or being rescued by rope. These are concrete examples of rope use, rather than a proven history of every other meaning.

Ḥabl as covenant or pledge

Covenant, pledge, safe conduct, and protection form an important branch of this family.

In this setting, Lisān al-ʿArab explains ḥabl through الْعَهْد (al-ʿahd), الذِّمَّة (al-dhimmah), and الْأَمَان (al-amān).

Ibn al-Sikkīt also associates one use of ḥabl with الْوِصَال (al-wiṣāl): connection or relationship.

This differs from a material rope. A covenant does not normally stretch an actual rope between two people; it establishes a commitment maintaining their relationship and rights.

Its semantic relationship can be illustrated by a rope holding two things together.

In social usage, however, commitment and protection maintain the relationship, rather than a material strand.

Arabic could accordingly use ḥabl for an agreement securing a traveler’s safety within a group’s territory.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetic witness to covenant and protection

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris quotes a line attributed to Al-Aʿshā:

وَإِذَا تُجَوِّزُهَا حِبَالُ قَبِيلَةٍ
أَخَذَتْ مِنَ الْأُخْرَى إِلَيْكَ حِبَالَهَا

wa-idhā tujawwizu-hā ḥibālu qabīlatin / akhadhat mina al-ukhrā ilayka ḥibālahā

That is: “When one tribe’s pledges of protection conduct her through, she obtains the next tribe’s pledges on her way to you.” The feminine pronouns concern the she-camel with her rider in the poetic setting.

Ḥibāl here is the plural of ḥabl, but actual ropes are not intended.

The poet describes protective pledges needed by the traveler passing through different tribal territories.

Ibn Fāris explains these ḥibāl as al-amān and عُهُودُ الْخَفَارَة (ʿuhūd al-khafārah): safe conduct and protective agreements.

Here the rope becomes an image of a relationship preserving someone’s safety.

The line matters lexically because it illustrates a nonmaterial use of ḥabl in Arabic poetry.

Al-Aʿshā belonged to the pre-Islamic period near Islam’s beginnings, but a lexical quotation alone does not establish this line’s exact date. Sources also transmit minor textual variants. The witness should therefore be understood within the limits of its attribution and wording.

Ḥabl as connection or relationship

Beyond formal covenants, ḥabl also denotes connection or relationship.

In lexicons, وَصَلَ حَبْلَهُ (waṣala ḥablahu) can concern establishing or maintaining a relationship.

In such usage, قَطَعَ حَبْلَهُ (qaṭaʿa ḥablahu) concerns severing a bond.

The rope is no longer a means of restraining or pulling, but an image of a relationship that may remain intact or be broken.

The image is familiar in Persian too, in expressions equivalent to “the thread of friendship,” “kinship bond,” and “severing the thread of communication.”

Persian–Arabic resemblance supplies no independent etymological proof; it merely illustrates the semantic connection between rope and bond.

Snares and trapping with rope

Arabic ḥibālah denotes a snare or device that catches game.

Lisān al-ʿArab records حِبَالَةُ الصَّيْد (ḥibālat al-ṣayd) for a hunting snare, and اِحْتَبَلَ الصَّيْدَ (iḥtabala al-ṣayda) for catching game in a trap.

Here rope serves trapping rather than protection or rescue.

The distinction matters.

The same material tool may serve rescue, connection, restraint, or capture, depending on its use.

Rope itself is therefore neither morally good nor bad. The purpose and action determine the value and result of its use.

Arabic literature also uses حَبَائِل (ḥabāʾil) figuratively for the snares of deception, danger, and death.

This branch illustrates rope’s range of applications. Neither ḥibālah nor the verb iḥtabala occurs in the Qur’an.

Rope in the body and nature

Lexicons also record ḥabl for certain bodily cords, sinews, and vessels.

Ḥabl al-ʿātiq concerns a cord in the neck and shoulder region, while حَبْلُ الْوَرِيد (ḥabl al-warīd) denotes a vessel in the neck.

These uses depend on the visual resemblance of extended bodily cords and vessels to rope.

Ibn Fāris also records ḥabl for an elongated stretch of sand.

Here again the referent is not a rope made of fibers; the word describes an extended natural formation.

Such uses show extension and cord-like shape within the root’s semantic range, without establishing that Arabic calls every long, thin phenomenon ḥabl.

The pregnancy branch: ḥabal and ḥublā

In this family, حَبِلَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ (ḥabilati al-marʾatu) means that a woman became pregnant, while ḥublā denotes a pregnant woman.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn Sīdah explains this branch through the womb becoming full. Other recorded uses concern the stomach or body becoming filled with drink.

This explanation shows that pregnancy was an established meaning in Arabic lexicography.

It does not justify claiming that ḥublā necessarily arose from ḥabl, “rope,” because an umbilical cord connects the fetus.

That image may seem appealing, but without historical evidence it is not an established origin of the word.

Ḥ B L also differs from Ḥ M L.

Qur’anic pregnancy words such as حَمْل (ḥaml) and أَحْمَال (aḥmāl) belong to Ḥ M L. Semantic proximity does not make them derivatives of Ḥ B L.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: rope, snares, bodily cords, and pregnancy

3. Qur’anic ḥabl as bond and covenant

Āl ʿImrān 3:103: holding fast together to The God’s rope

Verse text:

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا وَاذْكُرُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذْ كُنْتُمْ أَعْدَاءً فَأَلَّفَ بَيْنَ قُلُوبِكُمْ فَأَصْبَحْتُمْ بِنِعْمَتِهِ إِخْوَانًا وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

wa-iʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli Allāhi jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū wa-udhkurū niʿmata Allāhi ʿalaykum idh kuntum aʿdāʾan fa-allafa bayna qulūbikum fa-aṣbaḥtum bi-niʿmatihi ikhwānan wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tahtadūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Hold fast together to The God’s rope, and do not become divided. Remember The God’s favor upon you: when you were enemies, He brought your hearts together, and by His favor you became brothers. You were on the brink of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it. Thus The God makes His signs clear to you, so that you may be guided.”

Here حَبْلِ اللَّهِ (ḥabl Allāh) accompanies the verb اعْتَصِمُوا (iʿtaṣimū).

Iʿtaṣimū belongs to ʿ Ṣ M and concerns holding fast, seeking refuge, and maintaining a protective hold.

In its material sense, ḥabl is a rope that can be grasped to establish a firm connection.

Together the words form a clear image: people grasp a rope serving to connect and support them.

The verse, however, is not describing a rope manufactured from fibers.

In this context, ḥabl Allāh expresses a bond, covenant, or means of connection and commitment to The God.

Is ḥabl Allāh the Qur’an?

Early commentaries offer various explanations of ḥabl Allāh, including the Qur’an, The God’s covenant, His religion, divine command, and a community gathered upon truth.

These explanations should be distinguished from lexical meaning.

Ḥabl itself does not mean “book” in Arabic. The book enters the interpretation through what is identified as a means of connection to The God.

The Qur’an can provide knowledge of divine covenant, command, and guidance; identifying ḥabl Allāh with it is therefore defensible within the religious context.

The expression bi-ḥabli Allāh itself nevertheless does not name al-Qurʾān.

The direct translation should therefore preserve “The God’s rope.” Analysis can explain the covenant and bond, and the Qur’an as a clear means of knowing that covenant and guidance.

Interpreting ḥabl Allāh as community should likewise not make every community or social power The God’s rope merely because it is united.

Ḥabl Allāh is attributed to The God. That attribution establishes the bond’s orientation and criterion.

Al-Ṭabarī: covenant, Qur’an, and community as explanations of ḥabl Allāh

Jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū

جَمِيعًا (jamīʿan) and وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا (wa-lā tafarraqū) matter in understanding the image.

Jamīʿan shows that the command to hold fast to The God’s rope addresses the audience collectively.

Wa-lā tafarraqū prohibits division and separation.

The rope thus portrays more than an individual’s relationship with The God: it also represents a shared bond capable of bringing people together.

The verse continues with their former hostility and the affection established between their hearts.

This continuity connects holding fast to The God’s rope with hostility ending and brotherhood arising.

The unity described is nevertheless not detached from a divine criterion.

Wa-lā tafarraqū therefore does not establish that preserving outward unity always outweighs everything else, even truth and justice.

The rope is attributed to The God, and the verse also mentions guidance and His signs being made clear.

The intended bond should accordingly be understood in relation to that covenant and guidance.

Āl ʿImrān 3:112: covenant and protection from The God and people

Verse text:

ضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ أَيْنَ مَا ثُقِفُوا إِلَّا بِحَبْلٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَحَبْلٍ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَبَاءُوا بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الْمَسْكَنَةُ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَكْفُرُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ الْأَنْبِيَاءَ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ ذَٰلِكَ بِمَا عَصَوْا وَكَانُوا يَعْتَدُونَ

ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-dhillatu ayna mā thuqifū illā bi-ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nāsi wa-bāʾū bi-ghaḍabin mina Allāhi wa-ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-maskanatu dhālika bi-annahum kānū yakfurūna bi-āyāti Allāhi wa-yaqtulūna al-anbiyāʾa bi-ghayri ḥaqqin dhālika bi-mā ʿaṣaw wa-kānū yaʿtadūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Humiliation has been imposed upon them wherever they are found, except under a covenant from The God and a covenant from people. They incurred The God’s anger, and destitution was imposed upon them. That was because they rejected The God’s signs and killed the prophets without right; and because they disobeyed and transgressed.”

Ḥabl occurs twice here: حَبْلٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ (ḥablin mina Allāhi) and حَبْلٍ مِنَ النَّاسِ (ḥablin mina al-nāsi).

Unlike Āl ʿImrān 3:103, the verse contains no iʿtaṣimū.

The context nevertheless concerns a group whose security and protection relate to a covenant from The God and a covenant from people.

Covenant, safe conduct, and protection are therefore appropriate senses of ḥabl here.

Early Arabic independently records this meaning, as in Al-Aʿshā’s use of ḥibāl for tribal protective pledges.

Mina Allāhi and mina al-nāsi

The verse identifies two sources for ḥabl.

One is mina Allāhi, “from The God”; the other is mina al-nāsi, “from people.”

The distinction is real and should remain visible in translation.

Ḥablin mina Allāhi concerns a covenant or protection from The God.

Ḥablin mina al-nāsi concerns a covenant or protection established by people.

In Arab social usage, protection could arise through a pledge, refuge, or binding relationship. This sense therefore fits both constructions.

Without clear evidence, however, neither should be restricted to a particular political agreement or legal institution.

Some later commentaries, for example, apply ḥablin mina al-nāsi to specific agreements within Islamic political systems. That historical application may warrant examination, but ḥabl does not lexically name that particular agreement.

The role of illā bi-ḥablin

Lexicographers and commentators discuss the syntax of إِلَّا بِحَبْلٍ (illā bi-ḥablin).

Some understand an exception separating protection and security from the preceding statement. Others differ over whether the exception is connected or disconnected, and precisely what is excepted.

This disagreement shows why the verse’s entire legal and historical relationship cannot be extracted from the meaning of ḥabl alone.

Ḥabl concerns covenant and protection here, while the exception’s operation and scope are matters of syntax and context.

The verse also explains the conduct underlying the situation: rejection of The God’s signs, killing prophets without right, disobedience, and transgression.

It should therefore not become a judgment about an ethnicity’s inherent nature or the unchangeable fate of everyone associated with it.

Nearby, particularly in Āl ʿImrān 3:113, Āl ʿImrān 3:114, and Āl ʿImrān 3:115, the Qur’an explicitly distinguishes among the People of the Book and describes a righteous group among them.

The meaning of ḥabl should remain distinct from judgments about particular conduct. The word itself is neither an ethnic name nor a permanent judgment on every generation.

Al-Ṭabarī: covenant, safe conduct, and the syntax of the exception

The relationship between the two uses in Āl ʿImrān

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, believers are commanded to hold fast together to ḥabl Allāh.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:112, ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nāsi concerns covenant and protection.

The two uses are close in their underlying imagery.

Both concern something maintaining a relationship and capable of providing support and protection.

Their complete meanings nevertheless differ.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 concerns a shared bond with The God and avoidance of division.

Āl ʿImrān 3:112 concerns covenant and protection affecting the group under discussion.

The shared word therefore does not make ḥablin mina al-nāsi in Āl ʿImrān 3:112 identical to what believers are commanded to hold fast to in Āl ʿImrān 3:103.

4. Material and figurative Qur’anic uses of ḥabl

Ṭā Hā 20:66: the magicians’ ropes

Verse text:

قَالَ بَلْ أَلْقُوا فَإِذَا حِبَالُهُمْ وَعِصِيُّهُمْ يُخَيَّلُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ سِحْرِهِمْ أَنَّهَا تَسْعَىٰ

qāla bal alqū fa-idhā ḥibāluhum wa-ʿiṣiyyuhum yukhayyalu ilayhi min siḥrihim annahā tasʿā

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“He said: Rather, you throw. Then suddenly their ropes and staffs appeared to him, through their magic, to be moving.”

Ḥibāluhum here is the plural of ḥabl.

It means the actual ropes possessed by the magicians.

عِصِيُّهُمْ (ʿiṣiyyuhum) belongs to another family and denotes their staffs.

Two kinds of objects appear together: ropes and staffs.

Their juxtaposition identifies ḥibāl as a material implement here, rather than covenant, pledge, or social relationship.

The role of yukhayyalu ilayhi

The verse continues by saying that their magic made the ropes and staffs seem to move.

This matters because the verse distinguishes the material objects from the impression produced in Moses’ perception.

The ropes and staffs are objects used by the magicians, while their movement is described through يُخَيَّلُ (yukhayyalu).

Ḥibāl itself therefore does not establish that the ropes actually became living beings.

Nor does the verse explain the magic’s precise mechanism.

Some early reports and commentaries propose particular devices or substances to explain the objects’ apparent movement. The Qur’an itself names none of these materials or devices in this verse.

For a root study, ḥibāluhum simply means their ropes.

How they appeared to move concerns magic and perception, rather than the lexical meaning of ḥabl.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44: throwing ropes and staffs

Verse text:

فَأَلْقَوْا حِبَالَهُمْ وَعِصِيَّهُمْ وَقَالُوا بِعِزَّةِ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّا لَنَحْنُ الْغَالِبُونَ

fa-alqaw ḥibālahum wa-ʿiṣiyyahum wa-qālū bi-ʿizzati firʿawna innā la-naḥnu al-ghālibūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They threw their ropes and staffs and said: By Pharaoh’s might, we shall certainly be the victors.”

Ḥibālahum is again the plural of ḥabl.

Here, however, it is the object of أَلْقَوْا (alqaw), “they threw.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:66, ḥibāluhum is nominative; in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44, ḥibālahum is accusative.

This difference in case does not produce two distinct derivatives.

Both verses concern the same kind of material implement: rope.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44 describes throwing the ropes, then quotes the magicians’ claim to victory.

Ṭā Hā 20:66 describes the action’s apparent effect through yukhayyalu ilayhi.

The verses thus concern one event from two angles: one emphasizes throwing the objects, the other their appearance of movement.

Qāf 50:16: ḥabl al-warīd

Verse text:

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ وَنَعْلَمُ مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِ نَفْسُهُ وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ حَبْلِ الْوَرِيدِ

wa-la-qad khalaqnā al-insāna wa-naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihi nafsuhu wa-naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi min ḥabli al-warīdi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We have indeed created the human being, and We know what his own self whispers within him; and We are nearer to him than his neck vein.”

Ḥabl al-warīd here is a genitive construction.

Ḥabl does not mean a rope manufactured from fibers here. It describes a bodily vessel.

الْوَرِيد (al-warīd) belongs to W R D and is recorded in Arabic as a term for a vessel.

Lexicons explain ḥabl al-warīd as a vessel in the neck.

It is associated with the jugular vein in modern anatomical terminology, but early Arabic naming should not be assumed to follow today’s precise classification of the circulatory system.

The familiar referent suffices for understanding the verse: a vessel in the neck belonging to the human body and intimately close to the person.

The role of ḥabl in this construction

Ḥabl preserves the image of an extended cord here.

A vessel too is an elongated structure extending through the body.

Applying ḥabl to it is therefore understandable in Arabic.

Not every association of rope—tying loads, pulling animals, or knotting objects—should be transferred to this use.

Ḥabl al-warīd is an established expression for a vessel, rather than a statement that a separate rope is tied to it.

In natural Persian, “neck vein” conveys the construction’s meaning.

A lexical explanation can preserve the imagery by noting that ḥabl concerns the vessel’s cord-like extension.

Divine nearness and bodily imagery

The verse begins by saying:

“وَنَعْلَمُ مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِ نَفْسُهُ” (wa-naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihi nafsuhu): “We know what his own self whispers within him.”

The God knows what passes within the human self.

Then it says:

“وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ حَبْلِ الْوَرِيدِ” (wa-naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi min ḥabli al-warīdi): “We are nearer to him than his neck vein.”

In this context, being nearer than the neck vein depicts a profound and encompassing nearness.

The verse alone nevertheless does not locate The God physically within the human body.

Nor does the expression establish that The God is part of the circulatory system or bodily vessels.

The verse’s clear imagery should also not be removed without reason. The Qur’an itself uses an intimately close bodily part to express this relationship.

The translation should preserve that image, while reflection examines nearness in light of divine knowledge of the human interior.

A material word thus occurs within a bodily expression, while the complete sentence concerns more than anatomy alone.

Al-Masad 111:5: a rope around the neck

Verse text:

فِي جِيدِهَا حَبْلٌ مِنْ مَسَدٍ

fī jīdihā ḥablun min masadin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Around her neck is a twisted rope.”

Ḥablun here is an indefinite noun in the nominative.

فِي جِيدِهَا (fī jīdihā) identifies its location: around her neck.

مِنْ مَسَدٍ (min masadin) describes its material or characteristic.

مَسَد (masad) belongs to the separate root M S D, whose lexical uses concern twisting strands together.

Early sources record different materials for masad, including plant fibers, leather, and twisted iron. The word should therefore not be restricted to plant fibers without further evidence.

Ḥabl names the rope here, while masad describes its characteristic.

The relationship with ḥammālat al-ḥaṭab

The preceding verse, Al-Masad 111:4, describes Abū Lahab’s wife as حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ (ḥammālata al-ḥaṭabi).

The next verse mentions a rope around her neck.

This juxtaposition brings the images of carrying firewood and rope together.

Historical and exegetical explanations sometimes identify the rope as a means of binding firewood, and sometimes interpret it as an image of punishment in the hereafter.

Al-Masad 111:5 itself mentions only a rope of masad around the neck.

Ḥabl alone does not establish the rope’s length, color, exact material, or method of use.

Nor does the single word establish that the image is entirely physical or every element purely figurative.

That question requires examining the entire surah’s context.

What is clear for this root study is the familiar material sense of ḥabl as rope.

5. Comparison with related roots

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and R B Ṭ (rabaṭa): rope and connecting

In its primary sense, ḥabl names an implement used to tie or connect.

Rabaṭa concerns the action of tying and joining.

The two families are therefore close in meaning but distinct.

A rope can enable rabṭ, but not every connection is made with rope.

Figurative uses can likewise describe human relationships or inner states through connection and firmness.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, ḥabl concerns the bond or connecting means, while iʿtaṣimū expresses holding fast.

These roles should not all be merged into ḥabl itself.

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and ʿ Ṣ M (ʿaṣama): rope and holding fast for protection

Iʿtaṣimū and ḥabl occur together in Āl ʿImrān 3:103.

Iʿtiṣām concerns holding fast, taking refuge, and protecting oneself against harm.

Ḥabl is what the person grasps or uses to maintain a bond.

Iʿtiṣām thus expresses the person’s action, while ḥabl names the supporting means or bond.

Translating ḥabl as “immunity” does not preserve the whole expression, since protection is more closely associated with the result of holding fast.

“The God’s rope” better preserves the original image.

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and S B B (sabab): rope and a means of reaching

Arabic سَبَب (sabab) has a broad semantic range encompassing means, route, and what enables someone to reach a destination.

Some early uses also record sabab for rope.

Ḥabl and sabab therefore approach one another in connection and access.

Sabab is nevertheless not confined to rope. In a suitable context, it may denote a means or relationship enabling an intended outcome.

Material ḥabl conveys a more specific rope image; figurative ḥabl expresses covenant and bond through that image.

The two Qur’anic roots require separate study. Semantic proximity does not make every sabab ḥabl Allāh, or every ḥabl the means called sabab elsewhere.

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and ʿ H D (ʿahd): rope and covenant

ʿAhd concerns covenant and commitment.

Some Arabic uses of ḥabl likewise denote covenant and protection.

This overlap matters in Āl ʿImrān 3:112.

Not every ḥabl is a covenant, however. Ṭā Hā 20:66 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44 concern actual ropes.

Conversely, ordinary ʿahd concerns commitment and does not name a material rope.

Ḥabl thus has a wider range and acquires the covenant sense in an appropriate context.

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and W TH Q (wathiqa): a bond and its firmness

Study of W TH Q

Wathiqa and its derivatives concern firmness, confidence, and a connection or covenant becoming secure.

Ḥabl can provide a means of tying and holding firmly.

The word itself, however, does not necessarily mean strong or unbreakable.

A rope may be strong, weak, or liable to break.

A covenant too may be maintained or broken.

Its firmness and trustworthiness therefore require description and context, rather than being assumed in every use of ḥabl.

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and J W R (jiwār): covenant and providing refuge

Study of J W R

Arabic جِوَار (jiwār) concerns neighborhood and, in social usage, refuge and protection.

Ḥabl can likewise denote a protective pledge maintaining someone’s safety.

These fields were related in Arab tribal usage.

Ḥabl, however, emphasizes the relationship or connecting means itself, while jiwār more particularly concerns being someone’s neighbor or under their protection.

The distinction helps explain ḥablin mina al-nāsi in Āl ʿImrān 3:112.

Ḥ B L (ḥabl) and KH Y Ṭ (khayṭ): rope and thread

Arabic خَيْط (khayṭ) concerns thread or a thin strand.

Ḥabl usually denotes a rope or cord used to tie, pull, or hold.

Both may be made of fibers or strands in material uses, but ḥabl is usually thicker or better suited to pulling and tying.

The distinction should not be absolute: actual strands vary in size and material.

Qur’anic khayṭ occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:187, while ḥabl occurs in other verses.

Despite semantic proximity, the two families do not derive from one root.

Ḥ B L (ḥabal) and Ḥ M L (ḥaml): pregnancy and carrying

Ḥabal in its pregnancy use and ḥaml in Ḥ M L are close in meaning.

Their roots nevertheless differ.

Ḥamala concerns carrying something upon oneself or within oneself, with a wide Qur’anic range.

Ḥabilati al-marʾatu means that a woman became pregnant.

Their shared pregnancy sense should not cause Qur’anic ḥaml, حَامِل (ḥāmil), or aḥmāl to be assigned to Ḥ B L.

6. Interpretive challenges and semantic development

Is ḥabl Allāh a material object?

Ḥabl primarily means rope.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, however, ḥabl Allāh accompanies holding fast, gathering together, and avoiding division.

The context therefore concerns a divine bond and source of support.

Preserving the root’s sense does not require imagining a material rope between earth and heaven.

Nor should translation erase the rope image altogether, since the Qur’an itself chooses it.

A more precise approach preserves “The God’s rope” in translation and explains covenant, guidance, and shared connection in reflection.

Can ḥabl Allāh name a person or institution?

Ḥabl Allāh in Āl ʿImrān 3:103 does not itself name a particular person.

The verse commands holding fast to The God’s rope without naming a family, leader, or human institution.

Ḥabl alone therefore does not establish a particular person’s right to rule or absolute authority.

An exclusive identification with a particular person or institution requires independent Qur’anic evidence.

Conversely, the absence of a personal name from this expression does not invalidate every form of human leadership, teaching, or cooperation.

The essential distinction is between lexical covenant-and-bond imagery and specific claims about authority.

Does ḥabl always symbolize rescue and protection?

No.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, the rope relates to holding fast and unity.

Āl ʿImrān 3:112 concerns covenant and protection.

In the magicians’ story, however, ḥibāl are actual ropes used in their display.

Al-Masad 111:5 likewise describes a rope around a woman’s neck.

Other lexical uses even make rope a means of trapping.

The root’s meaning is therefore not confined to one fixed moral value.

Rope can serve rescue, connection, protection, restraint, or capture. Context and application establish its particular role.

Is ḥabl al-warīd a precise scientific term?

Modern language identifies bodily vessels using precise anatomical terminology.

Lexicons also explain ḥabl al-warīd as a vessel in the neck, associated with the jugular vein.

The Qur’anic expression should nevertheless not become a technical account of all vessels, circulation, or the nervous system.

The verse describes divine nearness to the human being, using the neck vein in a comparison.

Scientific knowledge can help identify the referent without restricting the sentence’s meaning and purpose to anatomy.

Do ḥabl and ḥublā have a proven single historical origin?

Lexicons place both within Ḥ B L.

Shared letters alone, however, do not explain the historical relationship between rope and pregnancy.

Lexicographic tradition explains pregnancy through the womb becoming full.

That explanation should be reported as recorded, rather than supplemented without evidence by a story about rope, the umbilical cord, or fetal attachment.

Morphological family membership and semantic explanation differ from proving a precise historical origin.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For bi-ḥabli Allāh in Āl ʿImrān 3:103, “to The God’s rope” is appropriate.

In the complete sentence, “Hold fast together to The God’s rope” preserves both ḥabl’s image and iʿtaṣimū’s action.

Reflection can explain the covenant and divine bond in this context. The direct translation should not silently replace “rope” with one exegetical identification.

For illā bi-ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nāsi in Āl ʿImrān 3:112, “except under a covenant from The God and a covenant from people” is appropriate.

Covenant and protection accord better here with context and attested Arabic usage than a material-rope image.

For ḥibāluhum in Ṭā Hā 20:66 and ḥibālahum in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44, “their ropes” is appropriate.

For ḥabl al-warīd in Qāf 50:16, “neck vein” is natural. Lexical explanation can clarify its extended, cord-like imagery.

For ḥablun min masadin in Al-Masad 111:5, “a twisted rope” is appropriate. Its exact material should not be restricted to plant fibers without sufficient evidence; early sources also record leather and iron.

Forqan’s word boxes should separate the brief root meaning from each verse’s particular sense.

Brief root meaning: “Rope and an extended cord; bond, covenant, and a means of connection and protection.”

Meaning in the verse: Depending on context: “divine bond and covenant,” “protective agreement,” “actual rope,” “neck vein,” or “twisted rope.”

These distinctions must remain in each word’s data: one identical explanation for all seven occurrences would obscure some verses’ actual meanings.

8. Conclusion

Arabic Ḥ B L concerns rope and a cord-like extension used to tie, hold, or connect two points.

Covenant, pledge, protection, and relationship are established applications associated with this material field.

In early Arabic, ḥabl denotes protective pledges as well as rope. The line attributed to Al-Aʿshā about passage through tribal territories is an important witness to this nonmaterial use.

The root occurs seven times in the Qur’an.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, ḥabl Allāh portrays the divine bond and covenant that believers are called to hold fast to together.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:112, ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nāsi concerns covenant and protection.

In Ṭā Hā 20:66 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44, ḥibāl are the magicians’ actual ropes.

In Qāf 50:16, ḥabl al-warīd denotes a neck vessel used in expressing divine nearness to the human being.

In Al-Masad 111:5, ḥablun min masadin is a rope whose material or characteristic is described by masad.

Thus connection and extension are important features of this root’s semantic field, while ḥabl does not have the same referent in every verse.

It may denote actual rope, covenant and protection, or a bodily cord.

Forqan’s translations must preserve these differences. Ḥabl cannot be translated only as material rope throughout, nor can every use become a spiritual covenant or bond.

Root study clarifies the shared imagery, while each sentence’s construction and context establish its final meaning.
