Closing a breach, joining, and being closed; from sewing and mending to كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا in the Qur’an

In Arabic, R T Q concerns closing a gap, joining separated parts, and being in a closed or joined condition. In tangible uses, رَتَقَ describes sewing a tear in a garment or closing a breach so that its two sides meet.

The same field includes mending a breach, joining parts, and repairing divisions between people. Ratq can therefore concern physical objects or, figuratively, restoring relationships and resolving disputes.

Its closest opposite in early Arabic is فَتْق: ratq concerns closure, joining, or closing a breach, while fatq concerns opening or splitting something closed or joined.

A brief explanation expresses this opposition:

الرَّتْقُ ضِدُّ الْفَتْقِ

al-ratqu ḍiddu al-fatq

“Ratq is the opposite of fatq.”

R T Q occurs only once in the Qur’an, as رَتْقًا in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30. It is an indefinite accusative noun; the same verse contrasts it with فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا, from the separate root F T Q.

The essential distinction in this study is between the lexical sense of being closed or joined and what exactly was closed in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30. How the heavens and earth were ratq, and what process their opening describes, must be assessed from context and related verses.

A single word cannot establish a specific cosmological theory. Nor should disagreement about the nature of ratq and fatq erase the clear lexical sense of closure and joining.

1. Basic meaning in early Arabic

Closing a breach and joining separated parts

One early explanation of the root reads:

الرَّتْقُ إِلْحَامُ الْفَتْقِ وَإِصْلَاحُهُ

al-ratqu ilḥāmu al-fatqi wa-iṣlāḥuhu

That is: “Ratq is joining and repairing a breach.”

The definition distinguishes two conditions: first there is a gap between parts of something; then the gap closes and the separated parts join.

For example, sewing a tear in cloth can be called ratq. This involves more than concealing the surface of a hole: the cloth’s parts are rejoined.

However, ratq does not necessarily mean sewing with needle and thread. A gap may close by another means, or something may join and heal without human intervention.

Accordingly, اِرْتَتَقَ is also used for a breach healing or closing.

Another explanation clarifies the scope of this joining:

الرَّتْقُ: الضَّمُّ وَالِالْتِحَامُ، خِلْقَةً كَانَ أَمْ صَنْعَةً

al-ratqu: al-ḍammu wa-al-iltiḥāmu, khilqatan kāna am ṣanʿah

That is: “Ratq is joining and coalescence, whether naturally formed or produced by workmanship.”

This matters because the root is not confined to an action performed with tools. Something whose parts are closed or joined from the outset can also be described by this family.

Does ratq always restore a previous condition?

No.

When cloth tears and someone sews it, ratq repairs an existing breach.

But sewing two pieces of cloth together from the outset can also produce a joined condition.

Likewise, describing a bodily opening as naturally closed or joined does not require an earlier gap that subsequently closed.

Two concepts should therefore be distinguished: closing a gap and being in a closed or joined condition.

This is particularly important in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30. The heavens and earth are described as رَتْقًا; this alone does not say that they had first been separate and were then sewn together.

The noun ratq does not supply such a history on its own.

The opposition between ratq and fatq

In Arabic usage, فَتْق stands opposite رَتْق.

فَتَقَ can describe opening, splitting, or separating something closed.

For example, opening a closed seam in cloth or splitting a part of something continuous can be described as fatq.

But ratq and fatq do not always mean joining two independent bodies and subsequently separating them.

Sometimes a single body without a gap is subsequently split open.

In other cases, two adjacent parts are joined and then separated.

Each verse must therefore be assessed to determine what is closed and what part is opened.

2. Important forms and derivatives

رَتَقَ يَرْتُقُ and يَرْتِقُ

رَتَقَ is a Form I verb concerning closing a breach, mending a tear, and joining separated parts.

Both imperfect forms يَرْتُقُ and يَرْتِقُ are attested in early Arabic.

In رَتَقَ الْفَتْقَ, someone closes the breach.

Here, fatq is the object: the gap being repaired.

The principal verbal noun is رَتْق. رُتُوق is also recorded as a verbal noun or related nominal form.

رَتْق: the act of closing or something closed

رَتْق can be a verbal noun naming the act of closing or joining.

In some uses, the verbal noun also stands in place of a passive participle: ratq has the sense of مَرْتُوق, something closed or joined.

This matters particularly for the grammatical analysis of the verse in Al-Anbiyāʾ.

In كَانَتَا رَتْقًا, ratq can describe a closed or joined condition without itself identifying an agent or instrument of closure.

اِرْتَتَقَ: it closed and healed

اِرْتَتَقَ is a Form VIII verb.

In اِرْتَتَقَ الْفَتْقُ, the breach closes and heals.

Its difference from رَتَقَ فُلَانٌ الْفَتْقَ is clear.

The first expression describes the closure of the breach itself.

In the second, the person closing the gap is the subject.

This difference follows from the verb’s structure; all forms should not simply be translated as “sew.”

رَاتِق: joined or coalesced

رَاتِق is the active-participle form of رَتَقَ.

Some Arabic uses apply رَاتِق to joined or continuous clouds.

This does not require someone to have tied or sewn clouds together. It describes a cloud mass appearing joined and continuous.

The use is also attested in Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī’s poetry.

مَرْتُوق: something closed or joined

مَرْتُوق is the root’s passive participle.

It can describe a breach that has closed or something whose parts have been joined.

The verbal noun رَتْق itself is also sometimes used with this sense.

رِتَاق: a garment sewn together

رِتَاق is used for two pieces of cloth, or two garment sections, sewn together along their edges.

The joining of the cloth’s parts is prominent here.

This suits the family’s basic image of closing a breach and joining.

أَرْتَق and رَتْقَاء: closure of a bodily opening

In early Arabic, أَرْتَق is also recorded for an organ whose opening or cleft is closed or joined.

In older descriptive and medical usage, رَتْقَاء denotes a woman whose genital opening is unusually closed or constricted.

رَتَق, with an open t, is also recorded in this bodily use.

This shows that R T Q can describe natural or bodily closure, rather than only sewing cloth.

The specialized usage should nevertheless not be imported into the Qur’anic description of the heavens and earth. The verse in Al-Anbiyāʾ does not concern human organs.

Ratq in repairing human relationships

A figurative expression concerning restored relationships reads:

رَتَقَ فُلَانٌ فَتْقَ الْقَوْمِ

rataqa fulānun fatqa al-qawm

That is: “So-and-so repaired the breach among the people.”

This is figurative usage.

There is no physical gap between pieces of cloth. Instead, people have become divided, and someone works to resolve the rupture.

The Persian expression “repairing a relationship” belongs to a similar semantic field.

However, this does not make ratq in the Qur’an intrinsically mean social reform, reconciliation, or political unity.

Such senses depend on the subject and context.

Rare uses and disputed wordings

A sense concerning the spaces between fingers is also transmitted for الرُّتْق and الرَّتَق.

At first sight, this differs from closure and joining.

The wording of this entry is disputed, however, and possible textual confusion between forms such as رَتَقَة and رَتَبَة has been raised.

The report therefore cannot be used uncritically to add “an open space between fingers” to the root’s essential meaning.

رُتُوق is also recorded for strength, honor, and impregnability. A connection with closure and firmness can be considered, but these uncommon senses should not replace the clear Qur’anic meaning.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Joined clouds in Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī’s poetry

A verse by Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī reads:

يُضِيءُ سَنَاهُ رَاتِقٌ مُتَكَشِّفٌ
أَغَرُّ كَمِصْبَاحِ الْيَهُودِ أَجُوجُ

yuḍīʾu sanāhu rātiqun mutakashshifun / agharru ka-miṣbāḥi al-yahūdi ajūj

Approximate meaning: “Its radiance shines: a joined yet parting cloud, bright like the Jews’ blazing lamp.”

One explanation of this verse glosses رَاتِق as joined or continuous cloud.

This witness matters because it takes the root beyond sewing and repairing cloth.

In the cloud description, the visible feature is coalescence and continuity, rather than sewing.

Nevertheless, explanations differ about the precise relationship between رَاتِق, مُتَكَشِّف, and سَنَاهُ. Both أَجُوج and دَلُوج are also transmitted at the verse’s ending.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This particular verse is not independently dated, so it cannot confidently be presented as evidence preceding Qur’anic revelation.

A plant still enclosed in its covering

The following verse is attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar, and in other accounts to ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ:

فَأَصْبَحَ الرَّوْضُ وَالْقِيعَانُ مُمْرِعَةً
مِنْ بَيْنِ مُرْتَتِقٍ مِنْهَا وَمُنْصَاحِ

fa-aṣbaḥa al-rawḍu wa-al-qīʿānu mumriʿatan / min bayni murtatiqin minhā wa-munṣāḥ

The image is of meadows and level ground becoming lush after rain: some plants remain closed within their coverings, while others have opened and emerged.

One lexical explanation takes مُرْتَتِق here as a plant whose flower or blossom has not yet emerged from its covering.

By contrast, مُنْصَاح describes a plant whose blossom has appeared.

The witness matters because closure can describe a natural structure, such as a blossom still enclosed in its covering.

The variant wording must nevertheless be retained. Some versions have مُرْتَفِق rather than مُرْتَتِق, with an explanation involving water and abundant rain.

The attribution also varies between Aws ibn Ḥajar and ʿAbīd ibn al-Abraṣ. Both are recognized as pre-Islamic poets, but uncertainty over attribution and wording prevents treating every detail as settled.

رِتَاق in an anonymous rajaz verse

An anonymous rajaz verse reads:

جَارِيَةٌ بَيْضَاءُ فِي رِتَاقِ
تُدِيرُ طَرْفًا أَكْحَلَ الْمَآقِي

jāriyatun bayḍāʾu fī ritāqi / tudīru ṭarfan akḥala al-maʾāqī

“A fair girl in a garment sewn together, turning a dark-eyed gaze, its corners kohl-like.”

Here, رِتَاق denotes a garment made by joining and sewing pieces of cloth.

The use illustrates the root’s tangible, direct sense.

The examined accounts do not establish the poet’s name or the verse’s precise date. It should therefore be treated as a witness preserved in early Arabic usage, rather than a securely dated pre-Islamic poem.

Strength and refuge in a verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt

A witness attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt reads:

إِنَّ وَجًّا وَمَا يَلِي بَطْنَ وَجٍّ
دَارُ قَوْمِي بِرَبْوَةٍ وَرُتُوقِ

inna wajjan wa-mā yalī baṭna wajjin / dāru qawmī bi-rabwatin wa-rutūq

Approximate sense: “Wajj and the country adjoining its valley are my people’s home, in an elevated, secure place.” The final word’s precise sense depends on the differing explanations.

Some explanations connect رُتُوق with strongholds and secure, inaccessible places; elevation and honor are also transmitted explanations. It should therefore not be treated simply as a name for castles in every account.

On that analysis, joining and closure extend to impregnability and strength.

The witness is attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt, but the present study has not independently established its date or every textual detail.

It can therefore be included among literary uses, but the verse alone cannot justify explaining ratq in Al-Anbiyāʾ as “fortress” or “castle.”

Findings from the witnesses

The poetry reveals three important uses: joined clouds, plants still enclosed in their coverings, and garments whose parts are sewn together.

Other uses also raise strength and impregnability.

These witnesses confirm that ratq need not describe a gap sewn by a person with thread; it can also describe the closed or joined condition of natural things.

None of them, however, determines exactly which tangible condition resembles that of the heavens and earth in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30.

4. Qur’anic رَتْقًا in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30

أَوَلَمْ يَرَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَنَّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ أَفَلَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

a-wa-lam yara alladhīna kafarū anna al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa kānatā ratqan fa-fataqnāhumā wa-jaʿalnā mina al-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayyin a-fa-lā yuʾminūn

A rendering close to the wording:

“Have those who disbelieved not seen that the heavens and earth were closed and joined, then We opened the two, and We made every living thing from water? Will they not believe?”

“Closed and joined” reflects two related aspects of ratq’s meaning.

This phrase alone should not, however, be taken to establish that the heavens and earth necessarily formed a single physical body.

The morphology of رَتْقًا

رَتْقًا is an indefinite accusative noun; the familiar reading has a still t.

In the common syntactic analysis, رَتْقًا is the predicate of كَانَتَا.

It is the verbal noun of رَتَقَ, used here to describe the condition of two subjects.

One grammatical analysis explains كَانَتَا رَتْقًا by كَانَتَا ذَوَاتَيْ رَتْقٍ: both possessed a closed or joined condition.

The fact that رَتْق is a verbal noun also explains its singular form in the sentence.

Why is كَانَتَا dual while رَتْقًا is singular?

This is an important grammatical issue in the verse.

السَّمَاوَاتِ is plural and الْأَرْضَ singular, but they are treated as two groups or two sides of the description.

كَانَتَا therefore takes a dual subject pronoun.

رَتْقًا, however, is a verbal noun. Arabic can apply a verbal noun to a singular, dual, or plural subject without the usual numerical agreement of an adjective.

The absence of رَتْقَيْنِ consequently does not, on its own, establish that the heavens and earth were one body.

This matters because the singular رَتْقًا is sometimes taken as proving that the Qur’an necessarily describes a single material mass.

That conclusion is not grammatically required.

The readings رَتْقًا and رَتَقًا

The familiar reading is رَتْقًا, with a still t.

Readings attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Zayd ibn ʿAlī, Abū Ḥaywah, and ʿĪsā also transmit رَتَقًا, with an open t.

This form has been analyzed both as a verbal noun and as a description close to “something closed.”

The difference concerns form and vocalization, while both remain within the field of closure and joining.

5. The central question: how were the heavens and earth ratq?

Early explanations record several important readings of the verse. They should be examined separately, since their linguistic and contextual support is not identical.

First reading: the heavens and earth were joined to one another

On this reading, the heavens and earth were joined, and The God separated them.

This explanation is transmitted in early interpretive accounts.

Another account describes the two as together, with The God separating them by placing an interval between them.

The reading suits familiar meanings of ratq and fatq.

When two parts are joined, separating them can be described as fatq.

The verse alone nevertheless does not specify the form of that union, the material involved, its precise date, or the mechanism of separation.

The reading is therefore lexically possible and has an early interpretive history, but specific scientific details should not be added to it.

Second reading: the heavens were closed and the earth was also closed

On this reading, the heavens and earth were each in a closed condition, and The God opened both.

In this explanation, the heavens were closed and rain did not descend from them; the earth was closed and no plants emerged from it.

The sky was then opened through rain, and the earth through vegetation.

This reading also occurs in early lexical and interpretive explanations.

Fatq here does not mean separating the sky from the earth. It describes the opening of each in relation to its own particular phenomenon.

The sky opens for water to descend, and the earth for plants to emerge.

This too suits the semantic field of ratq and fatq: closure of an outlet and its subsequent opening can be expressed by these words.

Third reading: multiple heavens and earths emerged from a closed whole

Some interpretive accounts describe the heavens as one joined group that was then separated into seven heavens.

A similar explanation is given for the earth.

This reading has been connected with verses concerning seven heavens, such as Al-Mulk 67:3 and Fuṣṣilat 41:12.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 itself, however, does not mention seven.

The number of layers and how they separated therefore cannot be established from this root or sentence alone.

The explanation should be recorded as an interpretation rather than a direct definition of رَتْقًا.

Fourth reading: ratq as darkness, fatq as the emergence of light

An interpretive account connects ratq with darkness and speaks of the emergence of day after night.

This reading is recorded in early explanations.

In ordinary usage, however, R T Q does not mean “darkness.”

Defending this explanation requires showing how closure and opening relate in this context to concealed light and its appearance.

The verse itself does not contain the words night, day, light, or darkness.

This reading consequently has weaker direct lexical and contextual support than the first two.

6. Assessing the two main readings

The principal difference is between the union of the heavens and earth and their closure to rain and vegetation.

The linguistic case for union

Ratq in Arabic concerns joining and closure.

Two things being joined and then separated is therefore one clear application of the root’s field.

فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا can also suit this meaning.

However, as the grammatical examination showed, dual هُمَا and singular رَتْقًا do not necessarily establish that the heavens and earth formed a single material body.

The contextual case for rain and vegetation

Immediately after فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا, the verse says:

وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ

wa-jaʿalnā mina al-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy

“And We made every living thing from water.”

The phrase brings water and life to the foreground.

If the heavens’ closure concerns the outlet for rain and the earth’s closure concerns the emergence of plants, the continuation connects directly with the same topic.

One early interpretive preference also considers the second reading more appropriate for this reason.

In this argument, the suitability of rain and vegetation follows from the placement of وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ after ratq and fatq.

Does the continuation exclude the first reading?

Not conclusively.

The verse might first describe a condition involving the heavens and earth, then present water and life as another sign of creation.

The conjunction وَ does not linguistically rule out that possibility.

The second reading nevertheless has a significant contextual advantage in its direct connection with water and vegetation.

Research finding

Both principal readings are lexically defensible. Union and separation suit familiar uses of ratq and fatq; the opening of the heavens to rain and earth to vegetation also draws support from the continuation about water and life.

At the contextual level, the second reading therefore has an important advantage, but there is insufficient evidence to exclude the first conclusively.

The root study should make the disagreement visible rather than present one reading in advance as the word’s certain meaning.

7. Connections between Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 and other verses

The sky associated with rain and the splitting earth

Al-Ṭāriq 86:11 and Al-Ṭāriq 86:12 read:

وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الرَّجْعِ
وَالْأَرْضِ ذَاتِ الصَّدْعِ

wa-al-samāʾi dhāti al-rajʿ / wa-al-arḍi dhāti al-ṣadʿ

“By the sky possessing return, and the earth possessing a cleft.”

The imagery of these two verses connects with the second reading of Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30.

Early explanations connect رَجْع here with rain, and صَدْع with the earth splitting for plants to emerge.

These two verses are also placed beside Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 in the early explanation involving rain and vegetation.

However, R J ʿAYN and Ṣ D ʿAYN are separate roots. They should not be treated as direct synonyms of ratq and fatq.

Rain and the emergence of plants

ʿAbasa 80:25, ʿAbasa 80:26, and ʿAbasa 80:27 read:

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا
ثُمَّ شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا
فَأَنْبَتْنَا فِيهَا حَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban / thumma shaqaqnā al-arḍa shaqqan / fa-anbatnā fīhā ḥabbā

“We poured water abundantly, then split the earth open, and caused grain to grow in it.”

These verses explicitly place descending water and the splitting of the earth for growth within one process.

This provides conceptual support for the rain-and-vegetation reading of Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30.

But ʿAbasa uses شَقَقْنَا, from SH Q Q, rather than fatq.

The resemblance should be recognized without merging all roots connected with splitting.

The creation of the heaven while it was smoke

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 reads:

ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ إِلَى السَّمَاءِ وَهِيَ دُخَانٌ

thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾi wa-hiya dukhān

“Then He turned to the heaven while it was smoke.”

This verse describes another condition of the heaven.

Its relationship with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 can be examined in a broader study of creation.

دُخَان does not, however, establish that ratq itself means smoke.

Nor should the verses be turned into a single cosmological model before their sequence and subjects have been established.

Are ratq and fatq identical with the Big Bang theory?

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 is sometimes correlated with the Big Bang theory.

Such explanations connect ratq with a very dense initial condition and fatq with the universe’s expansion.

The correlation is not the lexical meaning of ratq.

The verse itself does not specify temperature, density, the starting time of expansion, spacetime structure, or stages in Earth’s formation.

In scientific cosmology, Earth formed much later than the universe’s earliest stages. The heavens and earth therefore cannot simply be equated with two parts of a primordial body without further explanation.

Conversely, differences between Qur’anic language and modern scientific terminology do not justify prohibiting all inquiry into possible relationships between creation verses and scientific findings.

The appropriate boundary is that a scientific theory may be compared and studied, but should not replace lexical meaning or become the verse’s certain interpretation by assertion.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 speaks of ratq and fatq of the heavens and earth. Identifying the natural reality to which it refers requires more evidence than the word ratq alone.

8. أَوَلَمْ يَرَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا: seeing or recognizing?

The verse begins with this question:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

a-wa-lam yara alladhīna kafarū

“Have those who disbelieved not seen?”

In Arabic and the Qur’an, رَأَى can concern eyesight or recognition and understanding.

If this verse concerns an initial condition of creation, its ordinary audience plainly did not observe it with their own eyes.

Seeing can therefore also be examined in the sense of attending to and recognizing signs.

On the rain-and-vegetation reading, the topic is closer to phenomena whose effects people can observe: rain, the earth splitting, vegetation, and the emergence of life.

This may offer the second reading another contextual advantage.

R ʾ Y must nevertheless be studied independently; the mere presence of يَرَ does not conclusively establish either interpretation.

The verse also ends:

أَفَلَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

a-fa-lā yuʾminūn

“Will they not believe?”

It thus presents phenomena of creation as signs calling for belief.

That message is not the meaning of ratq itself; it follows from the verse as a whole.

9. Distinctions from related roots

R T Q and F T Q: closing and opening

The two roots are opposites in their basic uses.

R T Q concerns closure, joining, and mending.

F T Q concerns splitting, opening, and separating.

The Qur’an itself places both in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30, providing the clearest witness to their opposition.

Opposition between the roots does not mean that every instance involves the same physical situation.

Something may be split without previously having been sewn with thread; something may be closed without a previous gap that someone later repaired.

R T Q and L Ḥ M: joining and coalescence

لَحَمَ and forms related to الْتِحَام concern parts joining or coming together.

إِلْحَامُ الْفَتْقِ, joining a breach, is one explanation of ratq.

The resemblance does not make ratq refer to flesh or living tissue in every use.

In the garment example the subject is cloth; in the cloud example, a cloud mass; in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30, the heavens and earth.

Study of L Ḥ M

R T Q and S D D: closing and blocking

سَدَّ belongs to S D D and concerns closing a passage or creating a barrier.

رَتَقَ has sometimes been explained through سَدَّ.

Their usages are not completely identical, however.

Sadd can describe constructing a barrier in a route; clear uses of ratq emphasize joining, closing a breach, and mending.

“Blocked” may therefore suit some uses of ratq, but does not exhaust the root’s meaning in every sentence.

R T Q and SH Q Q: mending and splitting

شَقَّ belongs to SH Q Q and concerns splitting.

In ʿAbasa 80:26, the earth is split; subsequent verses describe plant growth.

The theme connects with the reading of the earth opening in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30.

However, shaqq and fatq are themselves separate roots; their features should not be equated wholesale without examination.

Study of SH Q Q

R T Q and R T L: joining and order

R T L concerns order, coordination, and clearly distinct parts within a whole.

R T Q concerns closure and joining.

A collection may be orderly while its parts remain separate.

Something may also be joined yet appear irregular in shape and structure.

The resemblance of their letters should therefore not lead to translating ratq as tartīl or arranging in order.

Study of R T L

R T Q and R T B: closure and arrangement

R T B is another independent root, with Arabic uses concerning stability, position, and arrangement.

Ratq, by contrast, primarily concerns closure or the mending of a gap.

Particularly in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30, translating رَتْقًا as “orderly and arranged” without evidence weakens its clear opposition to fatq.

10. Translation proposal and lexical data for Forqan

For the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence, the Persian equivalent should convey closure and joining as far as possible.

For the phrase:

أَنَّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا

anna al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa kānatā ratqan fa-fataqnāhumā

Proposed rendering:

“That the heavens and earth were closed and joined, then We opened the two.”

This rendering is more cautious than “the heavens and earth were a single body,” since it does not draw a conclusion beyond the grammar from the singular رَتْقًا.

Conversely, “the heavens gave no rain and the earth grew no plants” should not replace the direct translation. It is a contextual interpretation to be examined separately.

Nor are “Big Bang,” “initial explosion,” “cosmic expansion,” “gaseous mass,” “black hole,” or “separation of seven heavens” lexical equivalents of ratq.

In a root study, these topics are addressed only far enough to identify which conclusions the word alone does not establish.

11. Concluding findings

In early Arabic, R T Q concerns closure, closing breaches, and joining parts.

رَتَقَ describes closing and repairing a breach; اِرْتَتَقَ, healing or joining; رِتَاق, cloth sewn together; and رَاتِق, something joined or coalesced.

Beyond physical objects, the family also has uses concerning the resolution of divisions between people.

Poetic witnesses, including joined clouds and blossoms still enclosed in their coverings, show that ratq is not confined to sewing cloth. Uncertainties in the wording and attribution of some poems must nevertheless remain explicit.

The sole Qur’anic form is رَتْقًا in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30.

Alongside its opposite فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا, it describes the closed or joined condition of the heavens and earth in contrast to their opening.

Early explanations offer two principal readings: the heavens and earth were joined and then separated, or the heavens were closed to rain and the earth to vegetation before both were opened.

The first suits a clear use of ratq and fatq. The second also receives significant contextual support from the continuation about water and life; the same connection is cited in an early preference for that reading.

The lexical meaning of ratq nevertheless does not, on its own, determine the mechanism of creation.

A final definition proposed for Forqan:

R T Q concerns closing a gap, joining parts, and being in a closed or joined condition. It can describe cloth, organs, clouds, and plants, or figuratively the repair of relationships. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30, رَتْقًا describes the closed or joined condition of the heavens and earth, and فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا their opening. The nature of this closure and opening is not determined by the word alone. The two main readings—union and separation, or the sky opening to rain and earth to vegetation—must be assessed separately through contextual study.
