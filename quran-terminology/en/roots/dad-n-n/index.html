Withholding, holding tightly, and cherishing: from possessions and friendship to the trust of revelation

In early Arabic, the root ḍ n n concerns withholding, holding something tightly, and refusing to make it available. Ḍanna is used when someone keeps something and refrains from giving, transferring, or sharing it.

This usually concerns something the person values, such as wealth, possessions, knowledge, time, or a relationship they do not wish to lose or share.

Within this family, ḍanīn describes someone who withholds something or is reluctant to give or share it. Ḍinn can denote something precious that a person is unwilling to relinquish because of its importance.

Two important aspects are therefore keeping and withholding something, and the value it holds for its possessor.

Nevertheless, something's value and the refusal to give it are distinct matters. Not every valuable thing must be given away, nor is every act of keeping something blameworthy stinginess. The ethical judgment depends on the thing involved, others' rights to it, and the circumstances of giving or withholding.

In the Quran's reading used by Forqan, the root occurs once, as بِضَنِينٍ in Al-Takwīr 81:24. The verse describes someone who does not withhold what has reached him from the unseen.

This usage shows that the semantic field extends beyond material possessions to knowledge and a message.

1. The root in early Arabic

Generosity and hospitality were recognized social values in pre-Islamic Arab society. Poets used various expressions to praise generous people and criticize those who withheld assistance or gifts.

Ḍann was one word used in this semantic field.

A poetic witness from al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, the renowned pre-Islamic Arab poet, praises a man named al-Muḥallaq. The following wording is recorded in Lisān al-ʿArab:

يَدَاكَ يَدَا صِدْقٍ فَكَفٌّ مُفِيدَةٌ
وَأُخْرَى إِذَا مَا ضُنَّ بِالْمَالِ تُنْفِقُ

yadāka yadā ṣidqin fa-kaffun mufīdatun / wa-ukhrā idhā mā ḍunna bi-l-māli tunfiqu

Translation: “Your two hands are hands of genuine goodness: one bestows benefit, and the other spends when wealth is withheld.”

In this line, ضُنَّ بِالْمَالِ is contrasted with تُنْفِقُ.

This contrast helps establish the meaning. The poet praises someone who remains generous even when others keep their wealth and refuse to give.

Here, ḍann means withholding wealth, in contrast to spending it or making it available to others.

This is close to the familiar meaning of bukhl, stinginess. What matters in the line, however, is the act of withholding, rather than merely possessing or ordinarily retaining wealth.

Literary accounts attribute the poem to al-Aʿshā and connect its composition with al-Muḥallaq. Although al-Aʿshā is a pre-Islamic poet and the poem is well known in the literary tradition, its surviving wording comes through later compilations. It is historically valuable, but the earliest form and date of every word cannot be independently established with certainty.

Later witnesses and continuity of meaning

A line attributed to Qaʿnab ibn Umm Ṣāḥib, an Umayyad poet, reads:

مَهْلًا أَعَاذِلُ قَدْ جَرَّبْتِ مِنْ خُلُقِي
أَنِّي أَجُودُ لِأَقْوَامٍ وَإِنْ ضَنِنُوا

mahlan aʿādhilu qad jarrabti min khuluqī / annī ajūdu li-aqwāmin wa-in ḍaninū

Translation: “Gently, reproacher! You have tested my character: I am generous to people even when they withhold.”

Here too, أَجُودُ and ضَنِنُوا stand in contrast.

Jūd is generosity, while ḍann is withholding what could be made available to another.

The form ضَنِنُوا is also morphologically significant. The two nūns normally merge in this conjugated form, but the verse separates them to accommodate its metre.

This is an Umayyad-period witness and should not be presented as pre-Islamic. It nevertheless demonstrates the continuing use of the meaning “withhold” or “be stingy.”

Together, literary witnesses and lexical records show that withholding gifts or refusing to relinquish something has an early history in this root family; it is not merely a later technical meaning.

2. Central meaning and forms

Ḍ n n is a doubled Arabic root: its second and third consonants are identical.

In ضَنَّ, the two nūns are written as one consonant bearing a shaddah. In certain morphological constructions or poetic witnesses, they may appear separately.

Arabic sources record several imperfect and verbal-noun forms, including يَضَنُّ, يَضِنُّ, ضَنّ, ضِنّ, ضِنَّة, and ضَنانَة.

The principal derivatives and usages include:

ضَنَّ: withheld something, was reluctant to give it, or refused to relinquish it.

ضَنِين: someone who withholds; stingy, especially regarding something they value.

ضِنَّة: withholding or reluctance to give or make something available.

ضَنانَة: holding tightly or withholding; certain expressions also concern remaining in an unchanged condition.

مَضَنَّة and مَضِنَّة: in expressions such as عِلْقُ مَضَنَّةٍ, convey something's preciousness and desirability, making it difficult to relinquish and an object of competition.

مَضْنُون: something withheld or not relinquished.

ضِنّ: a person or thing cherished for its special value, whom or which someone does not wish to lose.

ضَنِينَة: in one usage, a person or thing cherished for its value and special position.

أَضِنَّاء and ضَنَائِن: recorded plural forms of ḍanīn and ḍanīnah, according to usage.

Of these forms, only ضَنِين occurs in the Quranic reading used here, with the attached bāʾ in بِضَنِينٍ.

From withholding to cherishing

One noteworthy expression is عِلْقُ مَضَنَّةٍ.

ʿIlq denotes a precious thing to which someone is attached or attentive; maḍannah in this construction reinforces its value and desirability.

The expression may describe a precious object its owner is unwilling to lose easily.

Someone may also describe a close friend as فُلَانٌ ضِنِّي: “That person is dear to me and holds a special place with me.”

Such a sentence is not simply about the vice of stinginess; it concerns the importance attached to a relationship.

For example, someone may value a friendship deeply and wish to preserve it. This is not blameworthy in itself.

If, however, selfishness leads that person to obstruct a friend's legitimate rights and freedoms, a separate ethical issue arises.

A positive usage concerning a cherished relationship does not make every use of the root positive. Equally, blameworthy financial stinginess does not make every instance of keeping or caring expressed by this family objectionable.

Less common usages

Some lexicons also record ضَنَّ بِالْمَكَانِ for not leaving a place and remaining there.

Its connection with holding tightly and not letting go is intelligible, but its subject is not giving wealth or knowledge.

In certain expressions, ضَنانَة also concerns something remaining fresh or unchanged.

Lexicons record the differently vocalized ضَنَن for a brave person. This rare usage cannot be derived from stinginess or cherishing without adequate historical evidence.

The clear central field concerns keeping and withholding, but every recorded form should not be forced into a single explanation.

3. The Quranic use of بِضَنِينٍ

Al-Takwīr 81:24

وَمَا هُوَ عَلَى الْغَيْبِ بِضَنِينٍ

wa-mā huwa ʿalā al-ghaybi bi-ḍanīn

Al-Takwīr 81:24

Translation: “And he is not withholding concerning the unseen.”

This is the root's only occurrence in the reading underlying Forqan's text.

Here, ضَنِين describes someone who withholds or is reluctant to make something available.

The attached bāʾ reinforces the negation. In the common analysis treating mā as the Hijazi negative particle that governs like laysa, هُوَ is its subject and بِضَنِينٍ its predicate, genitive in visible form but accusative in syntactic position. If mā is treated as non-governing, the clause is analyzed as a nominal subject and predicate.

The phrase عَلَى الْغَيْبِ identifies the subject matter in relation to which withholding is denied.

The verb ضَنَّ also commonly takes bāʾ, as in ضَنَّ بِمَالِهِ, “he withheld his wealth.” The verse uses ʿalā, and this syntactic difference should not be overlooked.

The contextual meaning is clear: the person who speaks about the unseen and has received a message from it does not withhold its communication.

Identifying the referent of هُوَ and the meaning of “the unseen” here requires examining the surrounding verses.

The referent of هُوَ

Al-Takwīr 81:19 begins by speaking of a noble Messenger.

Al-Takwīr 81:20 and Al-Takwīr 81:21 describe that Messenger as possessing strength, holding an established position with the Lord of the Throne, being obeyed there, and being trustworthy.

Al-Takwīr 81:22 then states that the audience's companion, Muhammad, is not mad.

Al-Takwīr 81:23 speaks of seeing that Messenger on the clear horizon.

In this context, Muhammad is the appropriate referent of هُوَ in Al-Takwīr 81:24: he receives revelation and conveys it to people.

The connection with the Messenger bearing revelation earlier in the passage must also be preserved. The surah first describes the heavenly Messenger's trustworthiness and then Muhammad's condition, emphasizing fidelity throughout the transmission of the message.

This connection does not make the two Messengers one person: one brings the message from God, and the other conveys it among people.

What الْغَيْبِ means here

Ghayb concerns what is hidden from ordinary human sight or knowledge.

Here it concerns what reached Muhammad through revelation and lay beyond people's direct access to knowledge.

Without supporting evidence, “the unseen” should therefore not be extended to every hidden secret of the universe or all of God's knowledge.

Al-Jinn 72:26 and Al-Jinn 72:27 speak of God's knowledge of the unseen and His disclosure to a Messenger whom He chooses.

Al-Anʿām 6:50 also instructs Muhammad to disclaim possession of God's treasuries or independent knowledge of the unseen.

These verses show that receiving and conveying revelation is not identical with independently knowing every unseen matter.

Al-Takwīr 81:24 therefore does not say that Muhammad possesses all the universe's secrets or knows and tells everyone every hidden thing.

Its subject is his not withholding what has reached him in his role as Messenger.

Not withholding revelation

Here, ḍanīn is not about stinginess with money.

The subject is knowledge and a message that must be communicated to its audience.

Someone who receives knowledge and is commissioned to convey it, but conceals it to preserve personal advantage, may be described as withholding that knowledge.

The verse denies such conduct of the Messenger.

In his role as Messenger, Muhammad does not make the divine message his private property, retaining whatever parts he chooses and communicating only others.

This meaning accords with the principle of clear communication elsewhere in the Quran.

Al-Māʾidah 5:67 commands the Messenger to convey what has been sent down to him from his Lord.

Al-Nūr 24:54 likewise associates the Messenger's duty with clear communication.

Al-Takwīr 81:27 describes this message as a reminder to all the worlds.

These connections show that revelation was not sent down to remain concealed with one person. The Messenger is commissioned to convey it.

This principle does not authorize anyone claiming an unseen secret to impose it on others as revelation or exclusive divine knowledge. The validity of an attribution to God is a separate question from the lexical meaning of ḍanīn.

4. The readings بِضَنِينٍ and بِظَنِينٍ

A central issue is the difference of reading in Al-Takwīr 81:24.

In Ḥafṣ's transmission from ʿĀṣim, used in the current Forqan text, the word is read with ḍād:

بِضَنِينٍ — from the root ḍ n n

In readings including those of Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, and al-Kisāʾī, it has ẓāʾ:

بِظَنِينٍ — from the root ẓ n n

This is not merely a different pronunciation of one letter: it changes the root and the word's meaning.

The reading بِضَنِينٍ

In this reading, the verse means:

“And he is not withholding concerning the unseen.”

He does not withhold from people what has reached him from God, nor fail to convey it through stinginess or a desire to keep it to himself.

The central point is not withholding revelation.

The reading بِظَنِينٍ

Ẓanīn belongs to ẓ n n and here means someone accused or suspected concerning their report or trustworthiness.

Under this reading, the verse means:

“And he is not under suspicion concerning the unseen.”

He is not presented as an accused, untrustworthy reporter of the unseen.

The central point is denying suspicion concerning the Messenger's trustworthiness and report.

These words must not be assigned to one root simply because their sounds and written forms resemble each other.

Ḍanīn concerns withholding; ẓanīn concerns accusation and suspicion.

Both readings are recorded in the recognized Quranic reading tradition. Their existence does not justify merging the two meanings without explanation in a base translation.

For the study of ḍ n n, بِضَنِينٍ is the primary form. بِظَنِينٍ must be considered as a reading belonging to another root.

The readings do not conceptually contradict each other: someone can both communicate a trust without withholding it and report it faithfully. This compatibility does not erase the lexical difference.

5. Comparison with related roots

Ḍ N N (ḍanna) and B Kh L (bakhila)

Bukhl in Arabic and the Quran concerns reluctance to give or make something available.

Āl ʿImrān 3:180 censures those who withhold what God has given them of His bounty.

This clearly overlaps with ḍann.

The ḍ n n family also emphasizes keeping something because of its special value. It can therefore concern knowledge, friendship, or a precious object.

Abū Hilāl al-ʿAskarī distinguishes ḍann in lending from bukhl in giving: sharing knowledge does not remove it from its possessor, making it resemble a loan more than a gift. This is his proposed distinction, not a complete definition of every usage.

Such a distinction may illuminate tendencies in some usages, but should not become an absolute rule for all Arabic.

The evidence shows that both roots can concern money and withholding gifts.

We cannot therefore say that ḍann never means financial stinginess or that bukhl never concerns knowledge and intangible matters.

Al-Takwīr 81:24 uses ḍanīn to express not withholding the message and knowledge received through revelation.

Ḍ N N (ḍanna) and Sh Ḥ Ḥ (shuḥḥ)

Shuḥḥ concerns stinginess and an intense desire to retain or acquire something.

Al-Ḥashr 59:9 describes those protected from their own shuḥḥ as successful.

Al-Taghābun 64:16 expresses the same theme.

Quranic uses of shuḥḥ emphasize an inward disposition and an inclination to withhold.

Ḍann can likewise refer to withholding, but the root alone does not entail every feature of greed, acquisitiveness, or insatiability.

Someone may withhold something precious without seeking to accumulate more of it.

Thus, shuḥḥ and ḍann overlap in withholding and stinginess but are not identical.

Ḍ N N (ḍanna) and M S K (amsaka)

Imsāk concerns holding or restraining something.

In the Quran, holding something can occur in different circumstances and is not necessarily negative.

Keeping something to fulfil a duty or prevent harm is not the same as unjustly withholding another person's right.

In human conduct, ḍann usually emphasizes reluctance to relinquish or share, whereas imsāk can simply denote keeping something.

Ḍann thus involves a kind of withholding, but not every act of imsāk is stinginess or ḍann.

Ḍ N N (ḍanna) and K T M (katama)

Kitmān concerns concealing something one knows or possesses.

Al-Baqarah 2:159 censures concealing the clear signs and guidance sent down.

Ḍann and concealment may occur in the same action, but their meanings differ.

Someone may selfishly refuse to share knowledge. If they also deliberately conceal it, kitmān is involved as well.

Nevertheless, refusing to give something does not necessarily mean concealing its existence. One may openly acknowledge having it while refusing to relinquish it.

Conversely, someone may conceal a truth out of concern for status, fear of consequences, or another advantage, rather than stinginess.

Motive, the object involved, and the kind of conduct must therefore be examined separately.

In Al-Takwīr 81:24, the negation of ḍann emphasizes not withholding revelation. Its connection with communication and concealment arises from the context and other verses, not from identical roots.

Ḍ N N (ḍanna) and Ẓ N N (ẓanna)

Ẓ n n is an independent root concerning supposition, belief, and, in certain constructions, accusation.

Ẓanīn in the alternative reading of Al-Takwīr 81:24 means being suspected or accused.

Ḍanīn, by contrast, belongs to ḍ n n and concerns withholding.

Their resemblance must not transfer the meaning “supposition” to ḍanīn or “stinginess” to ẓanīn.

This is a clear illustration of how changing one consonant can change an Arabic word's root and meaning.

Ḍ N N (ḍanna) and Ḍ N Y (ḍaniya)

Ḍ n y is another root, concerning prolonged illness, weakness, and bodily wasting.

Ḍanā concerns illness and infirmity, whereas ḍann from ḍ n n concerns withholding.

The families resemble one another in letters and sound, but this alone does not establish historical or semantic kinship.

Al-Takwīr 81:24 has no reference to illness or bodily weakness.

6. Withholding knowledge and responsibility for a trust

One important use of this root concerns withholding something that sharing would not remove from its possessor.

For example, teaching someone ordinarily does not cause the teacher to lose that knowledge.

This differs from giving money, which reduces one's possessions.

Being ḍanīn with knowledge can therefore mean refusing to teach or share information regarded as an exclusive advantage.

Not every refusal to convey knowledge is blameworthy, however. Information may be confidential, concern another person's privacy, or have harmful consequences. Whether it should be shared depends on responsibilities and circumstances.

The situation in Al-Takwīr 81:24 is specific.

It concerns a Messenger commissioned to convey the divine message. Revelation entrusted to a Messenger is to be communicated, rather than treated as private property to distribute according to personal advantage.

Within this framework, not withholding the message is significant.

This also connects with the general responsibility of intellectual honesty. Someone responsible for teaching, informing people, or communicating a necessary truth should not conceal it to preserve personal privilege or gain.

This ethical conclusion is not the direct meaning of ḍann, however. The root means withholding; responsibilities of trust arise from the kind of knowledge, the audience's rights, and its context.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ordinary Arabic uses of ضَنَّ, equivalents include “withhold,” “be stingy,” “hold tightly,” and, where appropriate, “refuse to relinquish.”

“Withhold” is broad enough to cover money, knowledge, time, assistance, and other valued things.

“Be stingy” can also be appropriate, but it usually emphasizes blameworthy reluctance to give.

For Al-Takwīr 81:24, the existing Persian Forqan translation expresses the following meaning:

وَمَا هُوَ عَلَى الْغَيْبِ بِضَنِينٍ

wa-mā huwa ʿalā al-ghaybi bi-ḍanīn

Al-Takwīr 81:24

“And he is not withholding concerning the unseen.”

This rendering is defensible in relation to the principal meaning of ḍanīn.

An explanatory note can clarify its contextual use:

“He does not withhold from people what he has received from the unseen through revelation.”

“He knows all the secrets of the unseen” is not a valid translation: neither ḍanīn nor the sentence establishes knowledge of everything unseen.

“He is not under suspicion” belongs to the reading بِظَنِينٍ with ẓāʾ, not the direct meaning of بِضَنِينٍ with ḍād. “He is not a liar” is not a precise direct rendering of either word; in the ẓāʾ reading, the lexical sense is not being accused or suspected.

For other derivatives, مَضْنُون can be rendered “withheld” or “something one refuses to relinquish,” while مَضَنَّة in an appropriate construction conveys something precious and sought after.

Ḍinnī, said of a cherished person, can be rendered “my dear one” or “someone very precious to me.”

In every case, the equivalent must fit the sentence's subject. Translating every derivative as “stinginess” is not always accurate.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ḍ n n concerns withholding, holding tightly, and refusing to give or make something available.

It usually concerns something valuable to the person, such as wealth, knowledge, a relationship, or something they do not wish to lose.

Early poetic witnesses contrast ḍann with generosity. Lexicons also record ḍanīn, ḍinnah, maḍannah, and maḍnūn within this field.

The family should not be restricted entirely to blameworthy stinginess. Sometimes it expresses something's special value and a wish to preserve it, without the word itself establishing that this is wrong.

The Quran has بِضَنِينٍ in Al-Takwīr 81:24. The verse denies that the Messenger withholds the message received from the unseen.

The subject is revelation and the responsibility to communicate it, rather than wealth and possessions.

The alternative readings بِضَنِينٍ and بِظَنِينٍ are significant: the first concerns withholding, the second suspicion. Both are intelligible within the surrounding account of the Messenger's trustworthiness, but their roots and lexical meanings differ.

A defensible final definition is:

Ḍ N N: withholding and holding something tightly, especially something valued, and refusing to give, relinquish, or make it available to others.

This definition explains both the financial use and the use concerning knowledge and a message.

In Al-Takwīr 81:24, the prominent meaning is that the Messenger does not keep the received revelation to himself or withhold it from people. His trustworthiness is evident in its communication as well as its reception.
