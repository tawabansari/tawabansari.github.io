Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root Ṣ L Ḥ

Salāh, rightness, soundness, harmony, reform, and returning something to its proper state

Morphological forms of this root

The root ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) stands opposite fasād. If fasād means corruption, ruin, departure from soundness, and the breakdown of beneficial order, then salāh means becoming sound, being proper, being fit, and placing things back into their right condition.

General meaning of the root Ṣ L Ḥ

The root ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) points to a state in which something is right, sound, beneficial, harmonious, and trustworthy. If something has become damaged, salāh means coming out of that damage. If a relationship has broken, salāh means returning it toward harmony. If an action has become wrong, iṣlāḥ means setting it right.

Salāh is not merely the absence of corruption. It is the presence of a sound and beneficial state. Something may not be visibly ruined, yet still not be truly ṣāliḥ. To be ṣāliḥ means that something can perform its proper function, incline toward good and benefit, and occupy a healthy place within the larger order of life.

For this reason, the root applies to the human being, action, relationship, society, land, covenant, and systems of life.

Usage of the root in Arabic

In classical Arabic, الصَّلَاح (al-ṣalāḥ) is the opposite of الفَسَاد (al-fasād). One says صَلَحَ الشيءُ (ṣalaḥa al-shayʾ), meaning that the thing became right, came into salāh, or left corruption. One also says أَصْلَحَهُ (aṣlaḥahu), meaning that he repaired it, set it right, or restored it to a sound state.

This root has a broad field in Arabic. صَلَحَ الطعامُ (ṣalaḥa al-ṭaʿām) means the food became sound, fit, and usable. صَلَحَ الماءُ (ṣalaḥa al-māʾ) means the water became sound and drinkable. صَلَحَ الأمرُ (ṣalaḥa al-amr) means the matter became set right and ordered. أَصْلَحَ بين القوم (aṣlaḥa bayna al-qawm) means he brought reconciliation, harmony, and order among the people.

In Arabic, الصُّلْح (al-ṣulḥ) often refers to reconciliation, settlement, and ending conflict. But this settlement should move toward salāh, not merely cover the problem temporarily.

The material image of salāh in Arabic life

To understand this root, it helps to notice the material and daily background of Arabic. Salāh was not first only a moral or religious idea. Something was ṣāliḥ when it was usable, sound, free of harmful defect, fit for its function, and ready to serve its purpose.

Land ready for cultivation could be called ṣāliḥ. Drinkable water was ṣāliḥ. Healthy beneficial food was ṣāliḥ. A tool that performed its work properly was ṣāliḥ. Even armor whose rings sat together correctly and had no dangerous opening could be understood in this field, because it protected properly.

This material image clarifies ʿamal ṣāliḥ, a righteous deed. A righteous deed is not only an act with a religious name. It is an act whose intention, form, effect, and direction fit together and stand within truth, justice, mercy, trust, and benefit.

Examples outside the Qur’an

In ordinary and literary Arabic, one may say صَلَحَ الزرعُ (ṣalaḥa al-zarʿ): the crop became good, sound, or ready for benefit. This shows that salāh is connected with benefit, growth, readiness, and health.

One may say صَلَحَتِ الأرضُ للزراعة (ṣalaḥat al-arḍ lil-zirāʿah): the land became suitable for agriculture. That is, the land found its beneficial capacity and became ready for the intended work.

In human relationships, one may say صَلَحَ ما بينهما (ṣalaḥa mā baynahumā): what was between the two became right. The conflict, break, or distance between them moved toward harmony and calm.

In social and legal Arabic, الصُّلْح (al-ṣulḥ) is used for ending conflict and reaching an agreement. Its value, however, depends on whether it moves the conflict toward justice and sound order rather than hiding oppression.

Morphological and semantic forms

The simple verbal form is صَلَحَ / يَصْلُحُ (ṣalaḥa / yaṣluḥu): it became right, became ṣāliḥ, or became fit and suitable for a purpose. This form mainly describes the state of the thing or person itself.

The Form IV verb is أَصْلَحَ / يُصْلِحُ (aṣlaḥa / yuṣliḥu): he reformed, set right, or moved something toward salāh. This form usually highlights the agent of reform.

صُلْح (ṣulḥ) points to reconciliation and settlement that calms conflict and moves a relationship toward peace and harmony. إِصْلَاح (iṣlāḥ) points to the process of setting right, ordering, and repairing.

صَالِح (ṣāliḥ) is used for a human being, action, child, society, or any fitting matter that stands in a sound and beneficial state. In the Qur’an, الصَّالِحَات (al-ṣāliḥāt) often refers to deeds aligned with faith, truth, justice, and good.

Salāh against fasād

The key to this root is its contrast with fasād. Fasād means departure from soundness, balance, and beneficial order. Salāh means return to soundness, balance, and right condition.

In this contrast, salāh is not merely a moral word. It is a real state. If land is sound, cultivable, and beneficial, it is in a state of salāh. If a human relationship stands on trust, justice, and respect, it is near salāh. If a society protects truth and reduces oppression, salāh is strengthened in it.

So salāh is living beneficial order: an order that brings things nearer to their proper place.

Ṣulḥ, iṣlāḥ, and repairing bonds

One of the major fields of ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) is the repair of bonds. Relationships break, covenants are harmed, conflict appears among people, and society leaves balance. In that condition, iṣlāḥ means returning the bonds toward truth, justice, and harmony.

Ṣulḥ means reconciliation, but in the root sense it is not merely making both sides silent. True reconciliation should treat a wound, avoid wasting a right, and move the relationship toward a healthier state.

For this reason, reform may be in the family, in society, among groups, or between a human being and his own path. Wherever something has been torn apart and must return to its proper state, the semantic field of ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) is present.

Usage of the root in the Qur’an

In the Qur’an, the root ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) appears widely. It stands against fasād, but it does not mean only the negative absence of corruption. It points to the positive state of fitness, rightness, benefit, and alignment with truth.

The Qur’an calls human beings to ʿamal ṣāliḥ, righteous action. This means an action that is sound, beneficial, just, and aligned with faith and truth. A righteous deed is not merely an outwardly religious act; it is any act that stands within truth, justice, trust, mercy, and reform.

The Qur’an also speaks of the ṣāliḥīn, those who have entered the path of salāh. In this sense, being righteous is not only belief. It must be visible in conduct, responsibility, trust, and the effect one has on others.

On the other side, the Qur’an speaks of iṣlāḥ and the muṣliḥīn: those who are not only sound in themselves, but who work to set relationships right, remove corruption, and return matters to a healthy state.

Righteous deed and righteous person

The expression عَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَات (ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt) is one of the most important uses of this root in the Qur’an. It shows that Qur’anic faith must have practical effect. If faith does not appear in action, justice, truthfulness, mercy, giving, trust, and responsibility, it remains at the level of claim.

Ṣāliḥ means fit, proper, and righteous. But in the Qur’an, fitness is measured by the direction of truth. A righteous deed is not only socially approved; it agrees with truth, justice, and the pleasure of The One God.

A righteous person is someone whose being moves toward benefit and soundness for himself, his family, society, the earth, and his relation with his Lord. To be ṣāliḥ means not becoming an agent of ruin, but inclining toward repair, trust, and good.

The difference between ṣāliḥ and muṣliḥ

In the Qur’an, an important difference can be seen between being ṣāliḥ and being muṣliḥ. A ṣāliḥ person stands in a state of rightness, fitness, and benefit. A muṣliḥ person works to set a situation, relationship, society, or structure right.

This difference becomes clear in Sūrah Hūd. Verse 11:117 says: وَمَا كَانَ رَبُّكَ لِيُهْلِكَ الْقُرَىٰ بِظُلْمٍ وَأَهْلُهَا مُصْلِحُونَ (wamā kāna rabbuka liyuhlika al-qurā biẓulmin wahluhā muṣliḥūna). The verse does not speak of ṣāliḥūn, but of muṣliḥūn.

This means that the survival of a society does not depend only on private goodness. It depends on the presence of people who reduce corruption, repair relationships, keep justice alive, and move the structure of life toward salāh.

Reform against the claim of reform

In the Qur’an, reform is a great value, but claiming reform is not enough. As seen in the root F S D, people may say: إِنَّمَا نَحْنُ مُصْلِحُونَ (innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn), we are only reformers, while the Qur’an calls them corrupters. This warning appears in 2:11.

This shows that reform is not proven by slogan. The standard of reform is its real effect on truth, justice, trust, the earth, relationships, and the health of society. If an action is done under the name of reform but produces corruption, lying, oppression, division, or ruin, the name of reform does not change its reality.

True reform means reducing fasād and moving matters toward salāh. Therefore every reform must be measured by its result, not only by the claim of the one who performs it.

Reconciliation and reform upon justice

Ṣulḥ has value in the Qur’an, but not every settlement necessarily produces salāh. Reconciliation remains faithful to the root ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) when it moves conflict toward truth, justice, and the health of the relationship.

The Qur’an says in 4:128: وَالصُّلْحُ خَيْرٌ (wālṣṣulḥu khayrun), reconciliation is better. But in social and group conflict, 49:9 commands: فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا بِالْعَدْلِ (faʾaṣliḥūā baynahumā biālʿadli), reform between them with justice.

This shows that sound reconciliation is not merely an outward end to conflict. If a settlement wastes the right of the wronged, covers oppression, or leaves rooted corruption untouched, it may bring temporary calm, but it is not true reform.

صَالِح (ṣāliḥ): righteous; fit; proper; sound; standing in salāh.

Summary

The root ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) stands opposite fasād in Arabic. Salāh means becoming right, being sound, being beneficial, becoming harmonious, and standing in one’s proper state. Iṣlāḥ means moving something from corruption, damage, conflict, or disorder toward rightness, soundness, and order.

In the Qur’an, this root is one of the foundations of ethics and human responsibility. Faith is joined with righteous action. A ṣāliḥ person stands in the path of truth and benefit. A muṣliḥ person does not only save himself, but works to repair relationships, reduce corruption, and return matters to a sound condition.

Against fasād, the Qur’an does not call the human being only to avoid ruin. It calls him to reform. The human being should not merely avoid being a corrupter; within his capacity, he should become part of the world’s return toward salāh.
