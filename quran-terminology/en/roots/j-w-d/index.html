Qur’anic Root J W D

Generosity, abundant giving, excellence of quality, and outstanding ability

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Arabic ج و د has several established branches: generosity and abundant giving, the goodness and quality of something, and outstanding ability and agility in an activity. Jawād describes a generous person; of a horse, it describes quality, ability, and swiftness. Jayyid means good or of desirable quality; jūd means generosity and openhanded giving.

Only الْجِيَادُ from this family occurs in the Qur’an, once in Ṣād 38:31. It describes the horses presented to Solomon. Here it concerns excellent, swift horses rather than human financial generosity.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one adjectival occurrence

A root’s semantic breadth does not place all its branches in every occurrence. Understanding al-jiyād requires first examining Arabic usage and then its role in the sentence and Solomon’s story.

1. Forms and derivatives

The family’s principal Arabic forms include:

  • جَادَ ـ يَجُودُ: acted generously and gave something; in another use, became good or excellent in quality.
  • جُود: generosity, openhandedness, and abundant giving.
  • جَوَاد: a generous person; also an excellent, agile, swift horse.
  • أَجْوَاد: a plural of jawād, especially for generous people.
  • جِيَاد: a plural used for excellent, swift horses; also a plural of jayyid for things of desirable quality.
  • جَيِّد: good, desirable in quality.
  • جَوْدَة / جُودَة: goodness, excellence, and desirable quality.
  • أَجَادَ ـ يُجِيدُ: performed something well and correctly, brought it to desirable quality.
  • إِجَادَة: performing work well and demonstrating skill.
  • جَوَّدَ ـ يُجَوِّدُ: improved something’s quality or precision.
  • تَجْوِيد: improving quality and performing something correctly and precisely.
  • اِسْتَجَادَ: found or judged something good, or sought its better quality.
  • مِجْوَاد: someone repeatedly demonstrating skill and excellence in work or speech.

Most do not occur in the Qur’an. Even familiar forms such as جُود, جَوَاد, جَيِّد, and تَجْوِيد do not appear there in these forms.

The sole Qur’anic form is الْجِيَادُ. The other branches help establish the family’s background and range, but its Qur’anic use must not be confined to generosity or later technical terminology.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Generosity and abundant giving

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris relates the root’s core to openhandedness and abundant giving. He mentions رَجُلٌ جَوَادٌ for a generous person and al-jawd for plentiful rain.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: openhandedness, rain, and swift horses

In جَادَ الرَّجُلُ بِمَالِهِ, someone gives from their wealth. Jūd here conveys more than transferring money: openhandedness and abundant giving are also present.

Jawād therefore describes someone known for generosity. It can describe a woman too, without invariably requiring a feminine tāʾ ending.

Bukhl, by contrast, concerns withholding and being restrictive in giving. Generosity and stinginess thus represent opposite ethical directions.

Jūd alone does not settle whether every gift is right. Someone may give generously for a wrongful purpose or with harmful consequences. Giving must also be assessed through justice, rights, and avoiding waste.

جَوْد: plentiful rain

One material use of the family is jawd, copious, abundant rain.

Arabic uses it for rain falling in great quantity. Some sources describe an especially high degree of abundance.

This concerns neither human openhandedness nor financial giving: the rain’s abundance and intensity are prominent.

Abundant giving and abundant rainfall share a conceivable semantic relationship: something is given or pours down in quantity. This does not establish which use arose first historically.

Neither the claim that Arabs first called rain generous and then extended this to people, nor its reverse, is established without independent historical evidence.

جَيِّد: good and desirable quality

Another important branch concerns goodness and quality.

جَادَ الشَّيْءُ can mean that something became good or excellent in quality. Jayyid describes desirable quality and contrasts with radīʾ, poor or inferior.

This extends beyond generous people. It can describe a good sword, quality merchandise, well considered speech, or skillfully performed work.

In أَجَادَ فِي عَمَلِهِ, someone performs their work well. The work’s quality, rather than the amount of money given away, is central.

Tajwīd belongs to this field, concerning doing something better and more accurately.

In technical usage, tajwīd also names the discipline of correctly articulating letters and applying rules in Qur’anic recitation. This specialized sense must not become the root’s sole original meaning. The general sense of performing well and improving quality remains alongside the technical term; one should not assume that specialization ended the wider use.

جَوَاد: an excellent, swift horse

Early Arabic uses jawād for an excellent, swift horse as well as a generous person.

The horse’s agility, movement, running speed, and high quality can be prominent.

Jiyād is an established plural of jawād describing horses.

Dictionaries record جَادَ الْفَرَسُ for a horse outstanding and agile in running, with related expressions concerning the quality and ability of its movement.

This must not be treated as literal financial generosity: a horse does not give away its property. Some classical lexicographers, including Al-Rāghib, nevertheless explain the metaphor by the horse’s yielding its stored running power. That is an attested lexical explanation of the relationship, not a requirement to insert financial giving into the Qur’anic word’s meaning.

Al-Rāghib: the metaphor of yielding stored running power

Excellence and ability in movement remain the clearer meaning here. A shared root does not alone establish the precise historical relationship to the generosity branch.

Poetry and early language

Literary sources transmit a verse about excellent horses standing:

لَهُ قُبَّةٌ مَضْرُوبَةٌ بِفِنَائِهَا
عِتَاقُ الْمَهَارَى وَالْجِيَادُ الصَّوَافِنُ

lahu qubbatun maḍrūbatun bi-fināʾihā / ʿitāqu al-mahārā wa-al-jiyādu al-ṣawāfinu

“He has a pavilion, before which stand noble camels and excellent horses.”

Sources vary in attribution and wording: Al-Manāqib al-Mazyadiyyah attributes the verse to Al-Aʿshā, whereas Al-Qurṭubī transmits it under Al-Nābighah with lanā, “we have,” at its opening. Attribution and textual variants should therefore remain qualified.

Al-Manāqib al-Mazyadiyyah: lahu qubbatun attributed to Al-Aʿshā

Its linguistic significance is the pairing of al-jiyād and al-ṣawāfin, the same two descriptions associated with Solomon’s horses, though in reverse order.

Al-Qurṭubī: lanā qubbatun attributed to Al-Nābighah

Another witness transmitted by Ibn al-Aʿrābī describes swords as جِيَادَات. The word is a plural of a plural: jiyādāt from jiyād, in the branch of jayyid. Here it means quality swords rather than swift horses.

Lisān: jiyādāt as a plural of a plural and the swords witness

The family of jiyād can thus describe desirable quality in objects. The thing described and the surrounding expression determine the appropriate meaning.

Applied to horses, excellence concerns their quality and ability; applied to swords, it concerns the swords’ quality.

3. Qur’anic use

Ṣād 38:31: Solomon’s excellent, swift horses

Verse text:

إِذْ عُرِضَ عَلَيْهِ بِالْعَشِيِّ الصَّافِنَاتُ الْجِيَادُ

idh ʿuriḍa ʿalayhi bi-al-ʿashiyyi al-ṣāfinātu al-jiyādu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When, in the later part of the day, the poised, swift horses were presented to him.”

Al-jiyād describes horses presented to Solomon.

Al-ṣāfināt belongs to ص ف ن and concerns a way of standing. Ṣāfin can describe a horse standing in a particular posture, including resting part of one hoof upon the ground while standing calmly.

Al-jiyād belongs to ج و د and expresses the horses’ excellence. Agility and swiftness are important in its established equine use.

Two descriptions can thus express two different features: their stance and the quality of their movement.

When standing, a horse is calm and steady; when moving, agile and swift.

This pairing fits Arabic uses of al-ṣāfināt and al-jiyād and explains their joint presence.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣāfināt and the presentation of horses

Does الْجِيَاد mean swift or excellent in quality?

Lexical sources offer two important analyses of jiyād.

First, it is the plural of jawād, describing an excellent, swift horse.

Second, it can be the plural of jayyid: good things of desirable quality.

Both belong to ج و د. In this verse, the established sense concerning swift horses is more precise, since horses receive two commendatory descriptions.

Rendering al-jiyād only as “good” is not necessarily wrong, but makes the established equine feature less explicit.

“Swift” should not imply speed without any relation to the horse’s quality and ability. Jawād describes an excellent horse whose swiftness is prominent.

“Excellent, swift horses” preserves both aspects in lexical explanation; “swift” can suffice in a concise verse translation.

The grammatical role of الْجِيَاد

Al-jiyādu qualifies al-ṣāfinātu, both nominative.

Al-ṣāfinātu is the grammatical subject of the passive عُرِضَ عَلَيْهِ; al-jiyādu describes it.

The verse therefore does not introduce two groups, one called ṣāfināt and another jiyād. Both descriptions concern the same horses presented.

Bi-al-ʿashiyyi specifies the presentation’s time and is not part of the root ج و د.

In Ṣād 38:32, Solomon speaks of his love of al-khayr. Its connection to valuable horses can be considered in the immediate context. Khayr and jiyād nevertheless remain different words, and one root’s meaning must not be transferred to the other.

Ṣād 38:33 mentions bringing them back and masḥ involving their legs and necks. The precise event belongs in the interpretation of those verses. Al-jiyād alone decides neither the horses’ fate nor Solomon’s motivation.

4. Important distinctions

ج و د (جَوَاد) and ج ي د (جِيد): quality and the neck

Study of J Y D

Their written and spoken similarity can lead to conflating their meanings.

Jayyid, good or of desirable quality, belongs to ج و د. Its formation involves a sound change affecting wāw, yielding jayyid.

Al-Miṣbāḥ and Lisān: the wāw sound change in jayyid

Jīd, neck, is assigned to ج ي د in the Qur’anic morphological index.

It occurs in Al-Masad 111:5 and means neck.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: jīd under J Y D

Jayyid, good, and jīd, neck, must not be identified merely through similar spelling.

Some commentaries explain al-jiyād as horses with long necks, connecting the description with jīd, neck.

The established plural jiyād from jawād for swift horses nevertheless has more direct lexical support. A long neck may be a desirable equine feature, but without adequate morphological evidence it should not replace the principal sense in this verse.

الْجُودِيّ: a place name, without a securely established derivation from ج و د

Hūd 11:44 mentions al-Jūdiyy, the place on which Noah’s vessel came to rest.

Verse text:

وَقِيلَ يَا أَرْضُ ابْلَعِي مَاءَكِ وَيَا سَمَاءُ أَقْلِعِي وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ وَقُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ وَقِيلَ بُعْدًا لِلْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-qīla yā arḍu iblaʿī māʾaki wa-yā samāʾu aqliʿī wa-ghīḍa al-māʾu wa-quḍiya al-amru wa-istawat ʿalā al-jūdiyyi wa-qīla buʿdan li-al-qawmi al-ẓālimīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“It was said: Earth, swallow your water; sky, desist. The water subsided, the matter was concluded, and the vessel came to rest upon al-Jūdiyy. It was said: Away with the wrongdoing people.”

Al-Jūdiyy is a proper place name here. Geographical and lexical sources describe a mountain or mountainous location.

Yāqūt, Muʿjam al-Buldān: al-Jūdiyy’s location

Yāqūt locates al-Jūdiyy near Jazīrat Ibn ʿUmar and also mentions Qardā among nearby places. Such geographical reports concerning northern Mesopotamia must remain distinct from establishing the name’s linguistic origin; they do not independently prove its precise derivation.

Yāqūt, Muʿjam al-Buldān: Qardā and places near al-Jūdiyy

Al-Rāghib does propose deriving al-Jūdiyy from jūd. That lexical proposal should be reported, while distinguishing it from a demonstrated historical etymology. Similar letters alone do not establish derivation from generosity, excellence, or abundant rain.

Al-Rāghib: a lexical proposal relating al-Jūdiyy to jūd

The verse uses al-Jūdiyy as a place name, not as an adjective declaring the place generous. Without independent etymological evidence, it should not be counted beside al-jiyād as another established Qur’anic derivative of ج و د.

ج و د (جَوَاد) and ك ر م (كَرَم): generosity and nobility

Jūd and karam can overlap ethically, but are not complete synonyms.

Jūd particularly emphasizes generosity and abundant giving.

Karam has a broader range, including nobility, dignity, honor, and worth.

A generous person may also be karīm, but not every instance of karīm concerns giving money.

Positive ethical descriptions cannot therefore all be reduced to one shared meaning.

ج و د (جَيِّد) and ح س ن (حَسَن): quality and goodness

Study of Ḥ S N

Jayyid and ḥasan can both describe something good.

Jayyid often emphasizes desirable quality, whereas ḥasan can express beauty, good conduct, approval, and other positive aspects.

This distinction is not absolute; the fields sometimes overlap.

In Solomon’s verse, al-jiyād concerns the horses’ quality and ability. It does not independently supply an ethical judgment about them.

ج و د (جَوَاد) and و ه ب (وَهَبَ): generosity and the act of giving

Wahaba concerns giving or granting something. Jawād as a human description expresses a disposition of generosity and openhandedness.

Giving something once does not necessarily make someone known for lasting generosity. Someone called jawād, by contrast, is known for abundance and openhanded giving.

Wahaba thus focuses more on the act of granting, while jūd in ethical usage emphasizes giving’s generous quality and abundance.

This distinction helps establish the root’s lexical background, although Qur’anic al-jiyād concerns horses rather than directly referring to financial giving.

5. Questions of understanding and translation

One difficulty is forcing a single mandatory meaning upon every derivative.

Reducing jūd to generosity makes jayyid, desirable quality, and jawād, a swift horse, harder to explain. Reducing the family to good quality weakens the openhandedness and abundant giving of its human uses.

The more precise approach recognizes attested branches and explains their relationships only as far as evidence supports.

Abundant giving matters in generosity; desirable quality and well performed work in the quality branch; ability and swiftness in the equine branch.

A kind of excellence or abundance in displaying a feature can be perceived across these branches. This is not a certain account of every derivative’s historical origin.

Another difficulty is reducing al-jiyād to generic “good.” This preserves part of its meaning, but inadequately expresses the ability and speed associated with a jawād horse.

“Racehorses,” however, may import modern organized competition and breeding for that purpose. The verse supplies no such details.

The place name al-Jūdiyy likewise must not be assigned to ج و د with certainty merely through similar letters.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For al-jiyādu in Ṣād 38:31, “swift” is the most appropriate concise rendering here: horses are the subject, and agility and ability in movement are established senses.

In the complete verse, “poised, swift horses” can naturally express both al-ṣāfināt and al-jiyād.

Lexical explanation should add that al-jiyād describes excellent, agile horses, expressing high quality alongside swiftness.

For non-Qur’anic derivatives: jūd means generosity; jawād of people, generous and openhanded; jayyid, good or of desirable quality; ijādah, performing well and skillfully; tajwīd in its general sense, improving quality and performing correctly.

These equivalents are not interchangeable without context. Rendering al-jiyād as “generous people” in Solomon’s verse fails to fit its equine use.

7. Conclusion

Arabic ج و د has several main branches: generosity and abundant giving, goodness and desirable quality, and outstanding ability and swiftness in a horse. Brief root meaning: Generosity and abundant giving; goodness and desirable quality; jawād of a horse means excellent and swift.

Jawād can describe a generous person or an excellent, agile horse. Jayyid expresses desirable quality; ethical jūd emphasizes generosity and abundant giving.

Only al-jiyādu from the family occurs in the Qur’an. In Ṣād 38:31 it describes horses presented to Solomon. Its pairing with al-ṣāfinātu produces the image of poised, swift horses.

The appropriate meaning here is therefore excellent, swift horses, rather than generous people or anything good in general.

Al-Jūdiyy in Hūd 11:44 is a place name. Similar letters do not establish a historical shared root with ج و د.

A word family may contain several branches, while form and context establish which meaning is present. Solomon’s verse emphasizes the horses’ quality and ability, expressed in Arabic through jawād and its plural jiyād.
