Qur’anic Root w-h-n (wahn)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَهَنَ
(wahana)
Became weak; perfect verb

تَهِنُوا
(tahinū)
Falter; in a prohibition

وَهْنًا
(wahnan)
Weakness; accusative noun

أَوْهَن
(awhan)
Weaker or weakest; comparative/superlative

مُوهِن
(mūhin)
One who weakens; active participle

Weakness, diminished strength, reduced firmness, and faltering steadfastness

The Arabic root w-h-n denotes weakness in strength, firmness, or the ability to act. وَهَنَ means to become weak; وَهْن means weakness; وَاهِن describes someone or something lacking sufficient strength or firmness; and أَوْهَنَ means to weaken something.

This weakness may affect the body, bones, strength, and ability, or a person’s resolve, steadfastness, and capacity to continue. A house or shelter may likewise be weak in structure or protective capacity.

The Qur’an uses this family for Zechariah’s weakened bones, a mother’s weakness and hardship during pregnancy, the frailty of the spider’s house, the weakening of hostile schemes, and warnings to believers against losing steadfastness.

Diminished strength or firmness is common to these uses, but what becomes weak and the consequences differ in each verse.

Bodily weakness, faltering in responsibility, and an inadequate support are distinct conditions. The root can describe all three without itself determining their moral value.

1. Meaning in early Arabic and transmitted poetry

In early Arabic, وَهَنَ الشَّيْءُ describes something whose strength or firmness has diminished.

وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ describes weakened bones, وَهَنَ الْبَدَنُ a weakened body, and وَهَنَ فِي الْأَمْرِ weakness in undertaking or pursuing a matter.

Weakness here need not mean total incapacity. Something may remain functional despite weakening. Illness, age, or severe pressure may reduce someone’s strength without making every activity impossible.

A verse by the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

وَإِذَا تَلْسُنُنِي أَلْسُنُهَا
إِنَّنِي لَسْتُ بِمَوْهُونٍ فَقِرْ

wa-idhā talsununī alsunuhā / innanī lastu bi-mawhūnin faqir

Translation: “When she assails me with her tongue, I answer her in kind; I am not weak and broken-backed.”

The poet describes confronting harsh reproach and answering it, insisting that he is not broken or helpless.

مَوْهُون describes someone afflicted by weakness or injury. Lexical explanations connect فَقِر here with a broken or injured backbone.

Together the words picture incapacity and collapse. Denying that condition, the poet presents himself as able to respond to others’ verbal attacks.

The line is transmitted under Ṭarafah’s name. Some lexical accounts have غُمُر in place of فَقِر; the latter is preserved in several lexical sources.

Ṭarafah was a pre-Islamic poet, and the attribution is established in Arabic literary tradition. Nevertheless, the text comes through later copies, and this particular verse’s date is not independently verifiable.

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā also reads:

وَمَا إِنْ عَلَى قَلْبِهِ غَمْرَةٌ
وَمَا إِنْ بِعَظْمٍ لَهُ مِنْ وَهَنْ

wa-mā in ʿalā qalbihi ghamratun / wa-mā in bi-ʿaẓmin lahu min wahan

Translation: “No obscuring darkness lies upon his heart, and no weakness is in his bones.”

The poet describes a strong, steadfast person.

The first hemistich concerns the heart; the second concerns the strength of the bones.

وَهَن denotes weakened bones. The poet is praising physical vigor, not reporting a medical assessment.

This witness shows that applying wahn to bones and the body was familiar in early poetry.

Al-Aʿshā lived before Islam and into its beginnings. The verse occurs in a poem attributed to him, but its date is not independently clear. Further historical evidence would be needed to establish that it predates revelation.

This uncertainty does not obscure the word’s meaning. The poetry, Qur’anic usages, and lexical accounts together establish weakness as a settled meaning of this family.

Other classical Arabic uses

رَجُلٌ وَاهِنٌ describes a man lacking sufficient strength.

مَوْهُونٌ describes someone or something weakened, including by an external cause.

Both وَهَّنَهُ and أَوْهَنَهُ are recorded for weakening another.

الْوَاهِنَة also named a pain in the shoulder, arm, or other bodily region. It could designate certain bones or areas near an animal’s neck and chest; lexicographers differ over the precise location.

Such names show the family’s use for bodily weakness and injury, but do not establish the historical origin of every derivative.

A different branch is also recorded: الْوَهْن and الْمَوْهِن for a time of night, near midnight or some time afterward.

This temporal usage is known lexically, but its historical connection to weakness is uncertain. Without evidence, one should not assert that the hour was named for human tiredness.

This temporal branch does not occur in the Qur’anic uses discussed here, which concern weakness.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • وَهَنَ and وَهِنَ: became weak.
  • يَهِنُ: becomes weak or loses strength or firmness.
  • وَهْن and وَهَن: weakness or diminished strength.
  • وَاهِن: weak; lacking adequate strength or firmness.
  • مَوْهُون: weakened or afflicted by injury and weakness.
  • أَوْهَنَ: weakened something.
  • إِيهَان: weakening or reducing strength or firmness.
  • مُوهِن: one who weakens another’s strength or firmness.
  • وَهَّنَ: made weak.
  • تَوْهِين: weakening or reducing firmness or strength; not necessarily the sense of insult familiar in Persian.
  • تَوَهَّنَ: became weak; in some uses, could no longer continue moving under weight or pressure.
  • أَوْهَن: weaker or weakest, according to the comparison.
  • وَهْنَانَة: a woman described as slow or weak in rising or acting.
  • وَاهِنَة: a name for certain bodily regions or pains in early Arabic.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The root occurs nine times in eight verses, in four morphological groups:

1. Basic verbs: وَهَنَ, وَهَنُوا, and تَهِنُوا.

2. The noun وَهْن, occurring twice in Luqmān 31:14.

3. The comparative/superlative أَوْهَن in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41.

4. The Form IV active participle مُوهِن in Al-Anfāl 8:18.

Intransitive وَهَنَ means to become weak; some Arabic constructions also use the basic verb transitively.

Form IV أَوْهَنَ expresses causing another to weaken.

The Qur’an illustrates the distinction: Zechariah’s bones have weakened in Maryam 19:4, while The God weakens hostile schemes in Al-Anfāl 8:18.

3. Qur’anic uses: bodily weakness and an inadequate support

1. وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ: Zechariah’s weakened bones

Maryam 19:4

قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا وَلَمْ أَكُن بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

qāla rabbi innī wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:4

Translation: “He said: My Lord, my bones have weakened and my head has flared with white hair; and, my Lord, I have never been disappointed in calling upon You.”

This verse forms part of Zechariah’s prayer.

He speaks to his Lord of his physical condition and old age.

وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي means that his bones have weakened.

وَهَنَ is a past-tense verb, with الْعَظْمُ as its subject. مِنِّي identifies the bones as his own.

الْعَظْمُ is singular, but can refer generically to bone and the body’s skeletal structure rather than one particular bone.

The verse should therefore not be restricted to a single weakened bone.

Nor does wahn alone establish a specific medical diagnosis, such as osteoporosis.

Osteoporosis is a recognized medical condition that can occur in old age, but the verse supplies neither test results nor a specialist diagnosis of Zechariah.

What is clear is diminished bodily strength and firmness.

Zechariah then mentions his whitening hair. وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا vividly pictures white hair spreading over his head.

The two descriptions stand together: weakened bones and white hair.

One concerns bodily weakness; the other presents a visible sign of age.

Zechariah goes on to state his request. Maryam 19:5 mentions his wife’s condition and his request for a successor.

His physical weakness therefore does not imply losing faith, hope, or his relationship with The God.

Despite age and diminished strength, he continues praying and hoping for his Lord’s mercy.

This usage shows that bodily weakness and weak faith are not identical.

Someone can become physically frail yet remain firm in understanding, hope, and steadfastness.

Age and physical weakness are not themselves moral faults; they can be part of ordinary human life.

2. وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ: a mother’s successive weakness in pregnancy

Luqmān 31:14

وَوَصَّيْنَا الْإِنسَانَ بِوَالِدَيْهِ حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ وَفِصَالُهُ فِي عَامَيْنِ أَنِ اشْكُرْ لِي وَلِوَالِدَيْكَ إِلَيَّ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-waṣṣaynā al-insāna bi-wālidayhi ḥamalathu ummuhu wahnan ʿalā wahnin wa-fiṣāluhu fī ʿāmayni ani ushkur lī wa-li-wālidayka ilayya al-maṣīr

Luqmān 31:14

Translation: “We enjoined the human being concerning his parents: his mother carried him in weakness upon weakness, and his weaning is in two years. Give thanks to Me and to your parents; to Me is the return.”

وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ concerns the mother’s condition while carrying her child.

وَهْن occurs twice, joined by the preposition عَلَى.

The expression pictures successive weakness, accumulating strain, and difficulty through pregnancy.

Pregnancy brings new bodily changes and demands. Carrying a child can involve fatigue, reduced strength, physical pressure, and various difficulties.

The experience is not uniform throughout pregnancy: one stage may bring greater fatigue, another a different strain.

وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ expresses this cumulative experience concisely.

It does not establish that every mother must become progressively weaker at every stage, or that every pregnancy involves serious illness or injury.

The verse describes the strain of carrying a child, not one medical pattern applying to all women.

Lexical and exegetical discussions also offer another possibility: the mother’s weakness together with that of the child growing within her.

That reading can be considered within the broader image of pregnancy. In the immediate syntax, however, حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ makes the mother the subject, making the weakness associated with her carrying the child the closer reading.

Successive maternal weakness is therefore preferable in the basic translation; the child’s condition may be discussed in a broader explanation.

The verse then mentions weaning in two years.

It calls upon people to give thanks to The God and their parents.

This connection matters.

The Qur’an recalls the hardship of carrying and raising a child so that parental effort, especially the mother’s, is not disregarded.

Gratitude does not, however, transfer the child’s moral and religious judgment to the parents.

Luqmān 31:15 says that parents must not be obeyed if they press their child into associating partners with The God, while still requiring good companionship in worldly life.

Respect for parents and personal responsibility toward truth both appear in this connected passage.

Wahn in Luqmān 31:14 concerns the mother’s bodily hardship. It does not give her unlimited authority over the child’s beliefs or decisions.

3. أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ: the frail house of the spider

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَوْلِيَاءَ كَمَثَلِ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ اتَّخَذَتْ بَيْتًا وَإِنَّ أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ لَبَيْتُ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ

mathalu alladhīna ittakhadhū min dūni Allāhi awliyāʾa ka-mathali al-ʿankabūti ittakhadhat baytan wa-inna awhana al-buyūti la-baytu al-ʿankabūti law kānū yaʿlamūn

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

Translation: “The likeness of those who take protectors apart from The God is that of the spider taking a house; and the frailest of houses is the spider’s house, if only they knew.”

أَوْهَنَ is a comparative/superlative from w-h-n.

أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ means “the frailest of houses.”

This differs from the preceding examples: Maryam describes weakened bones, Luqmān maternal strain, and Al-ʿAnkabūt a frail house.

بَيْتُ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ is the dwelling the spider makes for itself.

The central point is the comparison.

The Qur’an compares those taking protectors apart from The God to a spider making a house.

For humans, a house ordinarily offers shelter, rest, and protection from danger.

A structure of fine threads, however, does not offer the protection against many external harms that a firm, lasting house does.

Spiders may use silk to hunt, move, or protect eggs. These actual functions should not be denied.

Something can be useful for one task without providing dependable shelter against major threats.

That distinction helps explain the verse.

An individual silk thread may possess strong material properties; the strength of a strand is nevertheless distinct from the protective capacity of the whole dwelling.

Nor does the verse pronounce a scientific judgment on every feature of spider biology. Different spiders use different methods of shelter, hunting, and protecting offspring.

The comparison directly concerns the inadequacy of a trusted support.

Those taking protectors apart from The God have established a refuge that possesses no independent power against divine authority.

أَوْهَنَ describes that support’s weakness.

Its lexical meaning should not be replaced by “association,” “falsehood,” or “deception.”

Those concepts arise from the verse’s subject and its relation to مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَوْلِيَاءَ.

Nor does the verse make every instance of trust, cooperation, or human support an act of associating partners with The God.

People help one another socially, and the Qur’an recognizes cooperation in goodness.

The problem arises when a limited, dependent being is assigned authority and protection independent of The God.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:42 refers to divine knowledge of what people call upon besides Him. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:43 describes the parables offered to people and understood by those with knowledge.

The spider’s house is thus more than natural description: it tests the firmness of whatever one treats as an ultimate support.

4. Faltering before hardship and responsibility

Other Qur’anic uses concern human steadfastness under harm, pressure, and danger, beyond bodies or buildings.

4. وَلَا تَهِنُوا: do not falter

Āl ʿImrān 3:139

وَلَا تَهِنُوا وَلَا تَحْزَنُوا وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-lā tahinū wa-lā taḥzanū wa-antumu al-aʿlawna in kuntum muʾminīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:139

Translation: “Do not falter or grieve; you are uppermost, if you are believers.”

تَهِنُوا is a second-person plural imperfect form of وَهَنَ.

Here it follows prohibitive لَا and addresses a group of believers.

وَلَا تَهِنُوا tells them not to lose steadfastness under hardship and harm.

وَلَا تَحْزَنُوا follows it.

The expressions are not synonymous.

Wahn concerns weakened strength or resolve; ḥuzn concerns grief.

Someone may grieve over an event while continuing to improve matters and fulfill responsibilities.

Conversely, a person may falter psychologically and practically without displaying obvious grief.

The paired commands address related but distinct conditions.

Āl ʿImrān 3:140 mentions the harm suffered by believers and recalls comparable harm suffered by their opponents.

It then speaks of days alternating among people.

“Do not falter” therefore follows an experience of difficulty and harm.

The Qur’an does not deny the harm or demand that believers pretend no defeat, wound, or grief occurred.

It warns against letting injury turn into the loss of steadfastness.

وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ is qualified by إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ.

The verse does not promise permanent, unconditional superiority to any group calling itself believing. Faith is explicitly a condition, while the following context discusses alternating success and defeat.

The command calls for steadfastness in truth, rather than claiming immunity from harm or defeat.

5. فَمَا وَهَنُوا: steadfastness under injury

Āl ʿImrān 3:146

وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ نَبِيٍّ قَاتَلَ مَعَهُ رِبِّيُّونَ كَثِيرٌ فَمَا وَهَنُوا لِمَا أَصَابَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَمَا ضَعُفُوا وَمَا اسْتَكَانُوا وَاللَّهُ يُحِبُّ الصَّابِرِينَ

wa-ka-ayyin min nabiyyin qātala maʿahu ribbiyyūna kathīrun fa-mā wahanū li-mā aṣābahum fī sabīli Allāhi wa-mā ḍaʿufū wa-mā istakānū wa-Allāhu yuḥibbu al-ṣābirīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:146

Translation: “How many a Prophet fought, with many devoted worshippers beside him! They did not falter because of what afflicted them in the way of The God, nor weaken, nor submit; and The God loves the steadfast.”

Three expressions appear together:

“فَمَا وَهَنُوا”

“وَمَا ضَعُفُوا”

“وَمَا اسْتَكَانُوا”

They belong to different roots and should not be rendered identically without examination.

وَهَنُوا concerns faltering and diminished steadfastness.

ضَعُفُوا, from ḍ-ʿ-f, concerns weakness and reduced strength.

اسْتَكَانُوا concerns submission or yielding under pressure in this context.

Their combination describes steadfastness from several perspectives.

They suffered harm but did not abandon resolve, allow their capacity and readiness to collapse, or surrender to the enemy.

This sequence should not become a fixed three-stage model of behavior. Bodily weakness, psychological faltering, and surrender may coincide or occur in a different order.

The verse denies these conditions of a group of devoted worshippers.

لِمَا أَصَابَهُمْ matters: they encountered harm, but its severity alone did not determine their response.

Someone wounded while defending a right may lose physical capacity yet remain firm in resolve.

This distinguishes bodily weakness from moral or psychological surrender.

The verse ends with divine love for the steadfast.

Here ṣabr means steadfastness through difficulty, rather than merely enduring suffering silently and without purpose.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:147, the same group asks for forgiveness, firm steps, and help.

Their steadfastness does not imply claiming independence from The God or denying the possibility of error.

They do not disregard potential weaknesses and mistakes despite their resolve.

6. وَلَا تَهِنُوا فِي ابْتِغَاءِ الْقَوْمِ: persistence despite pain

Al-Nisāʾ 4:104

وَلَا تَهِنُوا فِي ابْتِغَاءِ الْقَوْمِ إِنْ تَكُونُوا تَأْلَمُونَ فَإِنَّهُمْ يَأْلَمُونَ كَمَا تَأْلَمُونَ وَتَرْجُونَ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَرْجُونَ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

wa-lā tahinū fī ibtighāʾi al-qawmi in takūnū taʾlamūna fa-innahum yaʾlamūna ka-mā taʾlamūna wa-tarjūna mina Allāhi mā lā yarjūna wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalīman ḥakīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:104

Translation: “Do not falter in seeking that people. If you suffer pain, they suffer as you suffer; and you hope from The God for what they do not hope. The God is Knowing, Wise.”

تَهِنُوا again occurs in a prohibition.

Its object differs from Āl ʿImrān 3:139.

فِي ابْتِغَاءِ الْقَوْمِ concerns not faltering in pursuing that group.

The preceding context concerns warfare, precautions, prayer in danger, and continuing responsibility in difficult conditions.

The command should not be extended to pursuing every opposing group at every time without regard to context.

The verse continues with a clear observation:

“إِنْ تَكُونُوا تَأْلَمُونَ فَإِنَّهُمْ يَأْلَمُونَ كَمَا تَأْلَمُونَ”

If you suffer pain, they suffer just as you do.

This recognizes pain and injury on both sides.

Believers are not portrayed as physically immune through faith; nor are opponents a different kind of being incapable of suffering.

Another distinction follows:

“وَتَرْجُونَ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَرْجُونَ”

Believers have hope in The God that the opposing group lacks.

The command therefore connects persistence with hope, purpose, and resolve, without denying pain or injury.

This clarifies the root: wahn here does not mean feeling pain, but faltering in responsibility despite pain.

A person may suffer and remain steadfast in doing right. Physical pain and weakened resolve are distinct.

The verse does not require everyone to endure every danger regardless of actual capacity or bodily condition. Its scope follows the wartime context; other Qur’anic verses also relate responsibility to capacity.

7. فَلَا تَهِنُوا وَتَدْعُوا إِلَى السَّلْمِ: faltering and calling for peace while uppermost

Muḥammad 47:35

فَلَا تَهِنُوا وَتَدْعُوا إِلَى السَّلْمِ وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ وَاللَّهُ مَعَكُمْ وَلَنْ يَتِرَكُمْ أَعْمَالَكُمْ

fa-lā tahinū wa-tadʿū ilā al-salmi wa-antumu al-aʿlawna wa-Allāhu maʿakum wa-lan yatirakum aʿmālakum

Muḥammad 47:35

Translation: “Do not falter and call for peace while you are uppermost. The God is with you and will not diminish your deeds.”

This also addresses believers in confrontation.

فَلَا تَهِنُوا commands them not to falter.

The continuation links that faltering with calling for peace while the audience is described as uppermost.

This does not establish that peace is always condemned or prohibited.

Al-Anfāl 8:61 directs Muhammad to incline toward peace if the other side does, and to trust in The God.

The two verses must therefore be read together.

Muḥammad 47:35 warns against faltering and seeking peace while uppermost, when the appeal may arise from lost resolve.

Al-Anfāl 8:61 concerns responding positively to the other side’s inclination toward peace.

The situations differ.

Peace can prevent bloodshed and protect life. Relinquishing others’ rights and safety solely through fear or weakness raises a different issue.

The root w-h-n itself does not mean seeking peace.

The verse mentions two actions linked by this context: faltering and calling for peace.

Nor should resistance to faltering become an unlimited order to continue every war, including unjust or purposeless ones. The context identifies the particular audience and circumstances.

Wahn here concerns weakened resolve. Right action must be assessed alongside Qur’anic directions concerning justice, covenants, defense, and peace.

5. مُوهِنُ كَيْدِ الْكَافِرِينَ: weakening a scheme

Al-Anfāl 8:18

ذَٰلِكُمْ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ مُوهِنُ كَيْدِ الْكَافِرِينَ

dhālikum wa-anna Allāha mūhinu kaydi al-kāfirīn

Al-Anfāl 8:18

Translation: “Thus it is; and The God weakens the scheme of the disbelievers.”

This is a different use of w-h-n.

مُوهِن is a Form IV active participle.

In basic وَهَنَ something becomes weak, while أَوْهَنَ concerns weakening it or reducing its strength or firmness.

The God is the agent, and كَيْدِ الْكَافِرِينَ is what is weakened.

كَيْد, from k-y-d, concerns planning, devising, or a scheme that may be used to harm or overcome another.

The expression concerns reducing the strength and effectiveness of the scheme of those confronting believers in this context.

Something nonphysical can thus be described through w-h-n.

A plan has neither bones nor muscles, but its effectiveness can weaken.

A group may plan harm, for instance, only for changed circumstances to deprive its scheme of its former effectiveness.

Weakening the plan means reducing or removing its efficacy, not breaking a physical object.

Al-Anfāl 8:17 relates the encounter to divine action and aid. Al-Anfāl 8:18 continues that context with the weakening of hostile schemes.

It does not establish that every group labeled unbelieving must lose every conflict at every time.

Elsewhere the Qur’an discusses victory and defeat, alternating days among people, and believers being tested.

مُوهِن describes divine action here; without evidence, it should not be converted into an independent, fixed divine name in later lists.

The specific object is a scheme, not the physical strength of everyone who disbelieves.

The family thus encompasses weakness in a person, body, structure, or plan’s effectiveness.

6. Distinctions from related roots

w-h-n and ḍ-ʿ-f: faltering and weakness

Wahn and ḍaʿf are closely related in Arabic.

Both can express reduced strength or incapacity.

In some uses wahn emphasizes weakened firmness or resolve, while ḍaʿf covers a broader lack of strength, ability, or power.

The verbs appear together in Āl ʿImrān 3:146:

“فَمَا وَهَنُوا”

“وَمَا ضَعُفُوا”

Their juxtaposition invites examination of the difference.

Here wahn can be understood in terms of maintaining resolve, and ḍaʿf in terms of retaining the capacity to continue the confrontation.

This is not an absolute rule making wahn always psychological and ḍaʿf always physical.

Maryam 19:4 explicitly applies wahn to bones, demonstrating a fully physical use.

Likewise, Qur’anic ḍaʿf is not confined to the body.

Study of ḍ-ʿ-f

w-h-n and w-n-y: weakness and slackening effort

W-n-y concerns diminished effort or the ability to continue an activity.

In Ṭā Hā 20:42, The God tells Moses and Aaron not to slacken in remembering Him.

Wahn can likewise concern faltering in duty, but extends to bones, a house, and schemes.

In work contexts, wany tends toward diminished pursuit or activity; wahn can express diminished capacity or firmness itself.

This is a tendency, not an absolute division.

Someone may stop through bodily weakness or neglect a duty despite adequate ability. These conditions are related but differ in cause and responsibility.

Study of w-n-y

w-h-n and f-t-r: weakening and subsiding intensity

Fatr concerns subsiding intensity or activity and stillness following strength or movement.

Wahn can likewise denote reduced strength.

They overlap when human activity diminishes over time.

Wahn can also describe structural weakness without requiring a preceding period of intense activity.

Subsiding activity and weakened capacity are therefore related but not identical.

w-h-n and w-h-y: weakness and structural frailty

W-h-y is an independent root concerning frailty and loss of structural firmness.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:16 describes the sky as وَاهِيَة.

It concerns the sky’s weakened, unstable condition in that day’s scene.

Wahn also covers structural weakness, but additionally describes bodies, human resolve, and schemes’ effectiveness.

The roots’ resemblance should not make them interchangeable.

وَاهِيَة belongs to w-h-y, not w-h-n; وَاهِن belongs to w-h-n.

w-h-n and h-w-n: weakness versus ease or humiliation

These roots contain the same consonants in a different order.

Qur’anic h-w-n concerns meanings including ease, gentleness, and humiliation.

For example, هَوْنًا describes how the servants of the Entirely Merciful walk in Al-Furqān 25:63; مُهِين elsewhere means humiliating.

W-h-n, however, concerns weakness.

مُهِين and مُوهِن therefore differ in both root and meaning.

مُهِين means humiliating; مُوهِن means weakening.

Al-Anfāl 8:18 actually reads مُوهِن. Its visual resemblance to muhīn must not obscure the distinction.

Study of h-w-n

w-h-n and k-s-l: weakness and laziness

Kasal concerns laziness, reluctance, or sluggishness in acting.

Wahn can reduce activity without arising from laziness or reluctance.

Zechariah’s bones weaken with age. The mother’s pregnancy strain in Luqmān 31:14 is likewise physical, not moral negligence.

Translating these uses as laziness would be wrong.

Someone who can act but neglects the task through indifference may, conversely, be behaviorally slack.

Responsibility requires considering the cause of weakness and the task involved.

Being weak is not itself a sin; knowingly yielding to falsehood or abandoning responsibility despite ability is a different moral matter.

w-h-n and ṣ-b-r: faltering and steadfastness

Qur’anic ṣabr concerns steadfastness and holding oneself to a course or condition.

Āl ʿImrān 3:146 ends by praising as steadfast those who neither falter under injury nor submit.

Ṣabr here thus involves maintaining resolve.

It does not mean never feeling weakness, pain, or fatigue.

A physically frail person may persist in the right course within their capacity.

Persistence has positive moral value only when directed rightly. Obstinacy in injustice is not the steadfastness praised in the Qur’an.

Faltering and persistence must therefore be assessed in relation to their object, purpose, and the person’s actual ability.

Study of ṣ-b-r

7. Suggested renderings for Forqan

For bodily وَهَنَ, use “became weak,” “grew frail,” or “lost firmness.”

For وَهْن, “weakness” and “frailty” are primary equivalents.

For وَاهِن, use “weak,” “frail,” or “lacking strength,” according to context.

For مَوْهُون, use “weakened” or “debilitated.”

For أَوْهَنَ, use “weakened” or “made frail”; for a plan, “reduced its effectiveness.”

For مُوهِن, use “one who weakens” or “weakening.”

For comparative أَوْهَن, choose “weaker” or “weakest” according to the sentence.

Suggested Qur’anic renderings follow:

Maryam 19:4 — وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي

“My bones have weakened.”

Luqmān 31:14 — وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ

“Weakness upon weakness.”

“With successive weakness and hardship” is natural explanatory prose, but “hardship” explains the pregnancy context rather than directly translating every use of wahn.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41 — أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ

“The frailest of houses.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:139 — وَلَا تَهِنُوا

“Do not falter.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:146 — فَمَا وَهَنُوا

“They did not falter.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:104 — وَلَا تَهِنُوا فِي ابْتِغَاءِ الْقَوْمِ

“Do not falter in seeking that people.”

Muḥammad 47:35 — فَلَا تَهِنُوا

“So do not falter.”

Al-Anfāl 8:18 — مُوهِنُ كَيْدِ الْكَافِرِينَ

“Weakening the scheme of the disbelievers.”

These equivalents preserve the basic meaning while matching each use to its object and context.

Wahn should not always be translated “being afraid”: weakness can arise from age, bodily strain, or inadequate firmness.

Nor should every use become “laziness,” only one possible association in certain behavioral contexts.

For the spider’s house, “frailest” is more accurate than “most worthless.” Weakness and worthlessness are different meanings.

8. Conclusion

Arabic w-h-n concerns weakness in capacity, firmness, or resolve.

It applies to bodies, bones, the ability to continue, structural firmness, and a plan’s effectiveness.

Poetry attributed to Ṭarafah and al-Aʿshā shows early bodily and debilitating senses. Other classical branches name bodily regions and a time of night, but cannot all be traced to one original historical image without sufficient evidence.

The Qur’an contains nine occurrences in eight verses.

Maryam 19:4 describes Zechariah’s bones; Luqmān 31:14, successive maternal strain; and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41, the spider’s house as an inadequate support illustrating reliance on protectors independent of The God.

Āl ʿImrān 3:139, Āl ʿImrān 3:146, Al-Nisāʾ 4:104, and Muḥammad 47:35 concern faltering amid difficulty and responsibility.

Al-Anfāl 8:18 describes The God weakening disbelievers’ schemes.

This range shows that wahn has no fixed moral value in itself.

Zechariah’s frailty and maternal pregnancy strain are natural conditions, not sin or worthlessness. Faltering in responsibility or yielding to falsehood can instead be morally censured in a specific context.

The verses of steadfastness do not deny injury or pain. People may suffer bodily weakness yet remain firm in faith, decision, and right effort.

Wahn is related in meaning to ḍaʿf, wany, fatr, and wahy, but these roots are distinct. It should be restricted neither to psychological weakness nor to bodily conditions alone.

W-h-n denotes diminished strength or firmness and weakness in a person, body, structure, or effectiveness. Behaviorally, it can describe faltering resolve or the ability to continue a responsibility. The cause, moral value, and possibility of overcoming weakness are not fixed by the root alone, but require attention to each verse’s context.
