The root ف ر ط — Going ahead, exceeding bounds, and neglecting responsibility

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَرَّطَ / تَفْرِيط
(farraṭa / tafrīṭ)
Neglect and omission

يَفْرُطَ / فُرُطًا
(yafruṭa / furuṭan)
Hasty action and excess

مُفْرَطُونَ
(mufraṭūn)
Those sent ahead or abandoned

The triliteral root ف ر ط in Arabic is associated with going ahead, advancing, hastening, and exceeding a limit. Two apparently opposed concepts also belong to this family: إِفْرَاط, excess and going beyond due measure, and تَفْرِيط, neglect, abandoning responsibility, and losing something through inattention.

In early Arabic, فَارِط designated someone who went ahead of a caravan to water to prepare for the others’ arrival. Going ahead could therefore be useful and necessary. In another situation, however, a hasty preemptive act or exceeding due measure could have harmful consequences.

The root ف ر ط occurs eight times in the Qur’an. Its uses range from human neglect of responsibility, nothing being omitted from the Book, and divine Messengers not neglecting their duty, to Pharaoh’s haste, disorder in someone’s affairs, and being sent ahead toward punishment.

The central point is that tafrīṭ and ifrāṭ should not be treated simply as unrelated opposites. Nor should an invented image reduce every use of the root to a single meaning.

1. The root in early Arabic and evidence attributed to the pre-Qur’anic period

Going ahead and advancing

One clear early meaning of فَرَطَ is to go ahead of others.

فَرَطَ الْقَوْمَ was used of someone who set out before his people or caravan.

In early Arab life, this use was especially associated with reaching water. Someone went ahead to a well or watering place to prepare vessels, ropes, and equipment for drawing water.

Such a person was called فَارِط, with the plural فُرَّاط.

This work mattered in desert travel: bringing people and animals to water, particularly on long journeys, required preparation and planning.

A فَارِط in this use was therefore not merely a hurried person. He went ahead to perform a service for others.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim, a pre-Islamic poet, says in describing the swift movement of horses:

يُبَارِينَ الْأَسِنَّةَ مُصْغِيَاتٍ
كَمَا يَتَفَارَطُ الثَّمَدَ الْحَمَامُ

Yubārīna al-asinnata muṣghiyātin / kamā yatafāraṭu al-thamada al-ḥamāmu.

Translation: “With ears inclined, they race alongside the spears, like pigeons vying to reach the scant water first.”

In this verse, يَتَفَارَطُ concerns going ahead and competing to arrive first.

الثَّمَد is a small quantity of water collected in a place.

The poet pictures pigeons outstripping one another to reach water.

This evidence matters for the root’s historical meaning: advancing and competing to arrive first were recognized uses of this family in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The verse is transmitted in poetry attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim and in early lexicons. Its wording varies, including يُبَارِينَ الْأَسِنَّةَ versus يُنَازِعْنَ الْأَعِنَّةَ and الْحَمَامُ versus الْحِيَامُ. These variants do not decisively alter the basic meaning of يَتَفَارَطُ as going ahead of others.

Leaving and neglecting something

Another branch concerns abandoning, losing, or failing to preserve or carry out something.

A poem attributed to the early Hudhalī poet Ṣakhr al-Ghayy says:

ذَلِكَ بَزِّي فَلَنْ أُفَرِّطَهُ
أَخَافُ أَنْ يُنْجِزُوا الَّذِي وَعَدُوا

Dhālika bazzī fa-lan ufarriṭahu / akhāfu an yunjizū alladhī waʿadū.

Translation: “This is my battle equipment, and I will not abandon it; I fear they will carry out what they have threatened.”

بَزِّي here refers to the poet’s weapons and battle equipment.

فَلَنْ أُفَرِّطَهُ means that he will not abandon or lose it, or neglect its safekeeping.

Lexical explanations transmit both “he does not leave his weapons behind and advance beyond them” and “he does not send his weapons ahead and remain behind them.” The more direct explanation of not wasting or abandoning his weapons is also given.

This difference shows that the imagery connecting advancement and abandonment can be explored. Neither explanation, however, should be presented as the established history of how the sense of neglect arose.

The poem is attributed to Ṣakhr al-Ghayy, an early poet of Hudhayl. Lexicons preserve the verse, but no independent contemporary evidence establishes exactly when it was composed.

Its use clearly conveys not abandoning something whose preservation matters to the speaker.

Haste, exceeding limits, and neglect

Three further uses of this family are established in Arabic.

فَرَطَ عَلَيْهِ can refer to hasty action against someone, taken before that person has a proper opportunity.

أَفْرَطَ فِي الْأَمْرِ means exceeding a limit or going too far in an affair.

فَرَّطَ فِي الْأَمْرِ means neglecting or wasting a task that should have been performed.

To understand the distinction, consider a responsibility with a defined scope and time.

Someone who exceeds the necessary limit commits ifrāṭ.

Someone who fails to discharge the responsibility, or neglects it until the opportunity is lost, commits tafrīṭ.

This example only clarifies the relationship between the concepts. Without evidence, it cannot establish historically that every meaning arose from a single original image of going ahead or falling behind.

2. Linguistic forms and the semantic core

The main forms of this family in Arabic include:

  • فَرَطَ: went ahead or advanced; in some constructions, acted hastily.
  • فَرَط: a person or thing that goes ahead, including someone advancing to water.
  • فَارِط: one who goes ahead, especially to water before a caravan.
  • فُرَّاط: the plural of فَارِط.
  • فُرُط: an affair that has exceeded due measure or has been abandoned and ruined.
  • فَرَّطَ: neglected, abandoned, or wasted.
  • تَفْرِيط: neglect and abandonment of responsibility, allowing a task or opportunity to be lost.
  • أَفْرَطَ: exceeded limits or went too far; in some constructions, sent someone ahead.
  • إِفْرَاط: excess and exceeding due measure.
  • مُفْرِط: someone who goes too far.
  • مُفَرِّط: someone who neglects and wastes a responsibility.
  • مُفْرَط: someone or something sent ahead, abandoned, or left behind.
  • مَفَارِط: in مَفَارِطُ الْبَلَد, the edges or outlying parts of a land.

Lexicons also record فَرَط for a child who dies before the parents, as though preceding them toward life’s end. This use should not be transferred to every Qur’anic derivative.

In some Arabic uses, فَرْط also denotes elevated or projecting ground. This physical branch does not occur in the Qur’an.

Among this family’s forms, the Qur’an uses the simple verb يَفْرُطَ, various inflections of the form II verb فَرَّطَ, the noun فُرُطًا, and the description مُفْرَطُونَ.

Three semantic directions should be kept distinct in Qur’anic usage:

Going ahead and hastening: especially in يَفْرُطَ.

Neglect and omission: especially in فَرَّطَ and يُفَرِّطُونَ.

Exceeding limits or being sent ahead: in فُرُطًا and مُفْرَطُونَ, according to form and context.

3. فَرَّطَ: neglecting responsibility and losing an opportunity

A. Al-Anʿām 6:31 — Regret over a lost opportunity

Surah Al-Anʿām 6:31

قَدْ خَسِرَ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِلِقَاءِ اللَّهِ ۖ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً قَالُوا يَا حَسْرَتَنَا عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِيهَا وَهُمْ يَحْمِلُونَ أَوْزَارَهُمْ عَلَىٰ ظُهُورِهِمْ ۚ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَزِرُونَ

Qad khasira alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-liqāʾi Allāhi ḥattā idhā jāʾathumu al-sāʿatu baghtatan qālū yā ḥasratanā ʿalā mā farraṭnā fīhā wa-hum yaḥmilūna awzārahum ʿalā ẓuhūrihim; alā sāʾa mā yazirūna.

Translation: “Those who denied the meeting with The God have indeed lost. When the Hour comes upon them suddenly, they say, ‘Alas for our neglect in this matter!’ while carrying their burdens on their backs. Indeed, evil is what they bear.”

Here فَرَّطْنَا means “we neglected” and let the opportunity slip away.

The speakers recognize their neglect when the Hour has arrived unexpectedly.

يَا حَسْرَتَنَا expresses their sorrow and regret, but tafrīṭ itself does not mean regret.

Tafrīṭ was their earlier conduct; regret is the response that follows when its consequences become apparent.

The referent of فِيهَا must also be considered in context. The nearest feminine noun is السَّاعَة, but human neglect occurs during life before its arrival. A translation may therefore use “in this matter” or refer to the final outcome and meeting with God, without constructing a theory of the time and place of neglect from the pronoun alone.

Neglect here is more than failing to do a small task: it is losing an opportunity to recognize the truth and reform one’s conduct.

B. Al-Anʿām 6:38 — Nothing omitted from the Book

Surah Al-Anʿām 6:38

وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ إِلَّا أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ ۚ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِي الْكِتَابِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ۚ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يُحْشَرُونَ

Wa-mā min dābbatin fī al-arḍi wa-lā ṭāʾirin yaṭīru bi-janāḥayhi illā umamun amthālukum; mā farraṭnā fī al-kitābi min shayʾin; thumma ilā rabbihim yuḥsharūna.

Translation: “There is no creature on earth, nor any bird flying with its two wings, but they are communities like you. We have omitted nothing from the Book. Then they will be gathered to their Lord.”

This is one of the clearest uses of the root to deny neglect.

مَا فَرَّطْنَا means “We have not neglected,” “We have not failed,” or “We have omitted nothing.”

فِي الْكِتَابِ specifies the domain to which the denial of neglect applies.

مِنْ شَيْءٍ makes the negation comprehensive: nothing within the scope of this statement has been omitted.

The construction is emphatic and should not be reduced to “We have not forgotten some important things.”

Does “the Book” mean the Qur’an?

The root ف ر ط alone cannot answer this question.

فَرَّطْنَا establishes that nothing has been omitted. Precisely which Book الْكِتَاب denotes must be determined from the immediate context and other Qur’anic uses.

Al-Anʿām 6:59 speaks of a clear Book excluding nothing moist or dry. That verse concerns the recording and encompassing of created things.

By contrast, Al-Anʿām 6:114 mentions the Book God has sent down in detail. Al-Naḥl 16:89 likewise describes the revealed Book as تِبْيَانًا لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ, a clarification of everything.

The Qur’an thus speaks both of a Book associated with divine recording and encompassing knowledge and of the Book revealed to guide humanity.

Al-Anʿām 6:38 concerns animal communities and their gathering before their Lord. This context matters when examining the referent of الْكِتَاب.

Nevertheless, debate over that referent does not change the meaning of مَا فَرَّطْنَا itself: nothing has been omitted.

The Qur’an’s completeness and sufficiency for religious guidance should also be examined through independent verses on the Book’s clarification, detail, and sufficiency. The entire argument should not rest on a single possible referent of الْكِتَاب in Al-Anʿām 6:38.

C. Al-Anʿām 6:61 — Divine Messengers do not neglect their duty

Surah Al-Anʿām 6:61

وَهُوَ الْقَاهِرُ فَوْقَ عِبَادِهِ ۖ وَيُرْسِلُ عَلَيْكُمْ حَفَظَةً حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ تَوَفَّتْهُ رُسُلُنَا وَهُمْ لَا يُفَرِّطُونَ

Wa-huwa al-qāhiru fawqa ʿibādihi wa-yursilu ʿalaykum ḥafaẓatan ḥattā idhā jāʾa aḥadakumu al-mawtu tawaffathu rusulunā wa-hum lā yufarriṭūna.

Translation: “He is the Subduer above His servants, and He sends guardians over you. When death comes to one of you, Our Messengers take him in full, and they do not neglect their duty.”

لَا يُفَرِّطُونَ is used here of divine Messengers.

The verb is a plural imperfect of form II.

لَا negates neglect and omission.

The Messengers mentioned therefore do not neglect their assigned task.

Tafrīṭ itself does not mean delay. Neglect may involve delay, but it can also take the form of abandonment, leaving work unfinished, or wasting it.

In this context, لَا يُفَرِّطُونَ means that they do not neglect their mission. The word alone does not supply every detail about the timing or manner of death.

Since divine Messengers are the subject, their lack of neglect should not be turned into a claim that every human or official with a duty is inherently immune to error.

The verse concerns the particular agents and situation it describes.

D. Yūsuf 12:80 — Failing in responsibility toward Joseph

Surah Yūsuf 12:80

فَلَمَّا اسْتَيْأَسُوا مِنْهُ خَلَصُوا نَجِيًّا ۖ قَالَ كَبِيرُهُمْ أَلَمْ تَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ أَبَاكُمْ قَدْ أَخَذَ عَلَيْكُمْ مَوْثِقًا مِنَ اللَّهِ وَمِنْ قَبْلُ مَا فَرَّطْتُمْ فِي يُوسُفَ ۖ فَلَنْ أَبْرَحَ الْأَرْضَ حَتَّىٰ يَأْذَنَ لِي أَبِي أَوْ يَحْكُمَ اللَّهُ لِي ۖ وَهُوَ خَيْرُ الْحَاكِمِينَ

Fa-lammā istayʾasū minhu khalaṣū najiyyan; qāla kabīruhum a-lam taʿlamū anna abākum qad akhadha ʿalaykum mawthiqan mina Allāhi wa-min qablu mā farraṭtum fī Yūsufa; fa-lan abraḥa al-arḍa ḥattā yaʾdhana lī abī aw yaḥkuma Allāhu lī; wa-huwa khayru al-ḥākimīna.

Translation: “When they despaired of him, they withdrew to confer privately. Their eldest said, ‘Do you not know that your father took from you a solemn pledge in the name of The God, and that previously you failed in your responsibility toward Joseph? I will not leave this land until my father permits me or The God judges for me; He is the best of judges.’”

فَرَّطْتُمْ here describes the conduct of Joseph’s brothers.

They had previously failed in their responsibility toward Joseph and now faced the question of safeguarding their other brother.

Their failure toward Joseph was not merely an unintended mistake or a forgotten duty. The narrative shows that their own decisions and actions separated Joseph from his father.

Tafrīṭ can thus describe deliberate neglect and harmful conduct.

The root itself, however, does not mean plotting, lying, or throwing someone into a well. Those actions are known from other verses in the narrative.

A grammatical point about مَا فَرَّطْتُمْ

In وَمِنْ قَبْلُ مَا فَرَّطْتُمْ فِي يُوسُفَ, مَا should not simply be taken as a negative particle.

Here مَا can be a nominalizing particle, yielding the sense “your failure in responsibility toward Joseph.”

The meaning is therefore not “you did not previously fail in responsibility toward Joseph.”

On the contrary, the statement concerns their earlier failure.

This example shows how recognizing particles and sentence structure is essential to translating a root correctly.

E. Al-Zumar 39:56 — Regret over neglect toward God

Surah Al-Zumar 39:56

أَنْ تَقُولَ نَفْسٌ يَا حَسْرَتَىٰ عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطْتُ فِي جَنْبِ اللَّهِ وَإِنْ كُنْتُ لَمِنَ السَّاخِرِينَ

An taqūla nafsun yā ḥasratā ʿalā mā farraṭtu fī janbi Allāhi wa-in kuntu la-mina al-sākhirīna.

Translation: “Lest a soul say, ‘Alas for me, for what I neglected in my duty toward The God, while I was indeed among the mockers.’”

فَرَّطْتُ is first-person singular here.

The person speaks of their own neglect, not that of others.

فِي جَنْبِ اللَّهِ concerns God’s due, His command, and what pertains to Him.

The physical meaning of جَنْب concerns a side or flank, but this expression should not be understood to give God a bodily flank.

The context concerns neglect of what a person should have taken seriously and observed from God.

The verse also ends with:

«وَإِنْ كُنْتُ لَمِنَ السَّاخِرِينَ»

Wa-in kuntu la-mina al-sākhirīna.

This means that the person was among the mockers.

The issue is therefore not simply forgetting to perform an act; their mocking conduct also contributes to the final regret.

This outcome should not be separated from the preceding verses.

In Al-Zumar 39:53, God tells His servants not to despair of His mercy.

Al-Zumar 39:54 and Al-Zumar 39:55 command return, submission, and following the best of what has been revealed.

Al-Zumar 39:56 therefore warns against losing the opportunity to reform and reaching a state in which only regret remains.

Tafrīṭ here is not merely “doing too little”; it can mean losing an opportunity for return and reform.

4. يَفْرُطَ: hasty, harmful preemptive action

Surah Ṭā Hā 20:45

قَالَا رَبَّنَا إِنَّنَا نَخَافُ أَنْ يَفْرُطَ عَلَيْنَا أَوْ أَنْ يَطْغَىٰ

Qālā rabbanā innanā nakhāfu an yafruṭa ʿalaynā aw an yaṭghā.

Translation: “They both said, ‘Our Lord, we fear that he may act hastily against us or transgress.’”

These are the words of Moses and Aaron.

They have been instructed to go to Pharaoh and speak with him.

Ṭā Hā 20:44 commands them to speak gently to Pharaoh, so that he might take heed or become fearful.

Moses and Aaron, however, fear his response.

يَفْرُطَ عَلَيْنَا here uses the root’s simple verb, not form II.

It means taking preemptive or hasty action against them, possibly before they have an opportunity to speak and convey the message.

عَلَيْنَا indicates that the feared action would be directed against Moses and Aaron.

The distinction between يَفْرُطَ and يَطْغَى

In this verse, يَفْرُطَ and يَطْغَىٰ are joined by أَوْ, “or.”

يَفْرُطَ here concerns hasty and harmful preemptive action.

يَطْغَىٰ derives from ط غ ي and refers to rebellion and overstepping limits.

The two behaviors can coexist. An oppressive ruler may hasten to harm opponents while also exceeding the bounds of authority and justice.

By using two different verbs, the verse allows two aspects of his conduct to be examined.

Both should therefore not simply be translated “he oppresses,” erasing the distinction between haste in acting and transgression.

In Ṭā Hā 20:46, God tells them not to fear and speaks of being with them, hearing and seeing.

This response does not render their real concern meaningless; it assures them that they have not been abandoned in carrying out their mission.

5. فُرُطًا: an affair beyond due bounds and in ruin

Surah Al-Kahf 18:28

وَاصْبِرْ نَفْسَكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُمْ بِالْغَدَاةِ وَالْعَشِيِّ يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُ ۖ وَلَا تَعْدُ عَيْنَاكَ عَنْهُمْ تُرِيدُ زِينَةَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا ۖ وَلَا تُطِعْ مَنْ أَغْفَلْنَا قَلْبَهُ عَنْ ذِكْرِنَا وَاتَّبَعَ هَوَاهُ وَكَانَ أَمْرُهُ فُرُطًا

Wa-ṣbir nafsaka maʿa alladhīna yadʿūna rabbahum bi-al-ghadāti wa-al-ʿashiyyi yurīdūna wajhahu; wa-lā taʿdu ʿaynāka ʿanhum turīdu zīnata al-ḥayāti al-dunyā; wa-lā tuṭiʿ man aghfalnā qalbahu ʿan dhikrinā wa-ittabaʿa hawāhu wa-kāna amruhu furuṭan.

Translation: “Keep yourself steadfast with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His face. Do not let your eyes pass beyond them, seeking the adornment of worldly life. Do not obey one whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his desire, and whose affair has gone beyond due measure and fallen into ruin.”

فُرُطًا is a noun here, not a verb.

أَمْرُهُ refers to the person’s affair or condition.

فُرُطًا describes the state of that affair.

Lexical accounts record أَمْرٌ فُرُطٌ both for an affair that has exceeded bounds and for one that has been wasted or neglected.

Both senses can be considered in this verse’s context.

The person follows personal desire while remaining heedless of God’s remembrance. Such conduct can lead to excess, departure from proper measure, and the ruin of one’s affairs and life.

فُرُطًا should not, however, be translated directly as “worship of desire,” since following desire is already expressed in the preceding phrase.

وَاتَّبَعَ هَوَاهُ describes the person’s behavior, while وَكَانَ أَمْرُهُ فُرُطًا describes the state of their affair.

Grammatically, أَمْرُهُ is the subject of كَانَ and فُرُطًا its predicate.

The distinction between heedlessness and neglect

This verse also contains أَغْفَلْنَا, from غ ف ل.

Heedlessness concerns inattention and being removed from remembrance and awareness.

Neglect can result from this condition, but the two are not identical.

A person may know their duty yet abandon it through self-interest or carelessness.

They may also lose an opportunity to act through heedlessness and inattention.

In Al-Kahf 18:28, heedlessness, following desire, and the ruin of one’s affairs occur in a shared context, but each retains its own meaning.

6. مُفْرَطُونَ: differences of form and reading

Surah Al-Naḥl 16:62

وَيَجْعَلُونَ لِلَّهِ مَا يَكْرَهُونَ وَتَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُهُمُ الْكَذِبَ أَنَّ لَهُمُ الْحُسْنَىٰ ۖ لَا جَرَمَ أَنَّ لَهُمُ النَّارَ وَأَنَّهُمْ مُفْرَطُونَ

Wa-yajʿalūna li-llāhi mā yakrahūna wa-taṣifu alsinatuhumu al-kadhiba anna lahumu al-ḥusnā; lā jarama anna lahumu al-nāra wa-annahum mufraṭūna.

Translation: “They assign to The God what they themselves dislike, and their tongues falsely claim that the finest good is theirs. Without doubt, the Fire is theirs, and they will be sent ahead to it.”

مُفْرَطُونَ is especially important here morphologically and semantically.

Unlike the preceding forms, whose contextual meanings were relatively clear, this word is pronounced differently in recognized readings.

The reading مُفْرَطُونَ

In the widely used reading of Ḥafṣ, مُفْرَطُونَ has a sukūn on the fāʾ and a fatḥah on the rāʾ.

This form is a passive participle of form IV.

Two recognized explanations of its meaning exist.

The first is those sent ahead or hastened toward the Fire.

The second is those abandoned or left behind.

The first relates to the early use of the root for going ahead and sending ahead.

The second relates to أَفْرَطَهُ in the sense of abandoning or leaving someone behind.

Under either interpretation, the word remains a passive participle: it describes a condition imposed upon them.

Consequently, translating this particular form as “they are excessive” without regard to the reading is inaccurate.

The reading مُفْرِطُونَ

In Nāfiʿ’s reading, مُفْرِطُونَ has a kasrah on the rāʾ.

It is an active participle of form IV, meaning those who go to excess or exceed limits.

This reading describes their own behavior.

The reading مُفَرِّطُونَ

In Abū Jaʿfar’s reading, the word is مُفَرِّطُونَ, with a doubled rāʾ carrying a kasrah.

It is an active participle of form II.

It means those who have neglected and wasted their responsibility.

This is the same branch found in فَرَّطْنَا and فَرَّطْتُ.

How the readings differ in meaning

These three forms should not be treated as morphologically identical:

مُفْرَطُونَ — those sent ahead or abandoned.

مُفْرِطُونَ — those who go to excess.

مُفَرِّطُونَ — those who neglect their duty.

The latter two primarily describe human behavior in worldly life. The first, especially when understood as being sent ahead to the Fire, describes the outcome.

All three relate to the verse’s broader context, but their grammatical differences are real.

For a base text following Ḥafṣ’s reading, “they will be sent ahead to the Fire” is a defensible rendering.

The alternative “those abandoned” may be mentioned in the explanation. It should not imply that God becomes unaware of their existence: abandonment in punishment differs from forgetfulness arising from ignorance.

7. The distinction between tafrīṭ and ifrāṭ

In contemporary Persian, ifrāṭ and tafrīṭ are commonly paired.

Ifrāṭ is called excess, and tafrīṭ neglect or falling short.

Both senses are recognized in Arabic, but their relation to ف ر ط requires care.

In its familiar use, إِفْرَاط means exceeding measure and limits.

تَفْرِيط means neglecting or abandoning something that should be done.

For example, someone entrusted with safeguarding people’s property may commit excess by handling it beyond the limits of their authority.

If that same person neglects its protection and causes it to be lost, their conduct is tafrīṭ.

The two are not always entirely separate in practice.

Someone may go too far in pursuing personal desires and, precisely for that reason, abandon an important responsibility.

In such a case, excess in one behavior can accompany neglect in another.

This relationship should not become the lexical definition of every derivative. يَفْرُطَ in Ṭā Hā 20:45 refers to hasty preemptive action, while مُفْرَطُون in Al-Naḥl 16:62 is a passive participle in the common reading.

Not every form of the root can therefore be confined to the two categories of excess and neglect.

8. Comparison with related roots

ف ر ط and ق ص ر

In some constructions, ق ص ر concerns becoming short, remaining limited, or falling short.

Tafrīṭ can also refer to falling short in a responsibility.

In tafrīṭ, however, losing something or abandoning a duty is prominent.

Shortfall may concern size, duration, or ability without necessarily involving a neglected responsibility.

Taqṣīr and tafrīṭ are therefore close in some uses, but not absolute synonyms.

ف ر ط and س ر ف

Isrāf concerns exceeding measure and going to excess.

Ifrāṭ belongs to the same semantic field.

In ethical contexts, both may describe behavior beyond a proper limit.

Yet ف ر ط also has branches involving advancement, haste, and neglect; these cannot all be transferred to س ر ف.

Nor is Qur’anic isrāf confined to spending money: it also describes conduct and transgression in other matters.

ف ر ط and ع ج ل

ع ج ل concerns haste.

In Ṭā Hā 20:45, يَفْرُطَ عَلَيْنَا can refer to hasty action against Moses and Aaron.

Not every instance of haste is neglect or excess. A person may act quickly and appropriately to save someone’s life.

In فَرَطَ عَلَيْهِ, preemption and haste in acting against another are prominent; ع ج ل has a broader field of speed and haste.

ف ر ط and ط غ ي

These two roots occur together in Ṭā Hā 20:45.

Ṭughyān concerns rebellion and exceeding limits.

يَفْرُطَ in this context concerns hasty and harmful preemptive action.

A ruler may both act hastily against someone and exceed the bounds of justice and authority.

The verbs’ juxtaposition invites examination of their differences; it does not establish their identity.

ف ر ط and ن س ي

ن س ي concerns forgetting and losing something from memory.

Tafrīṭ concerns neglecting and wasting a duty.

Someone may forget a duty and consequently fail to carry it out.

They may also be fully aware of it and nevertheless leave it undone.

Tafrīṭ is therefore not always the result of forgetfulness.

This distinction matters in Yūsuf 12:80 and Al-Zumar 39:56, where neglect concerns people’s actual conduct and choices.

ف ر ط and س ب ق

س ب ق concerns going ahead and arriving before others.

This is very close to the early advancement branch of ف ر ط.

Yet a فَارِط in the water-related use is more than someone ahead of others: he may have gone ahead to prepare the watering place.

س ب ق expresses the general sense of preceding, while some historical uses of ف ر ط are associated with advancing to make preparations.

This difference is observable in particular uses, but should not become an absolute boundary between all forms of the two roots.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

One major error is to assume that ف ر ط always means neglect.

In Ṭā Hā 20:45, the simple verb refers to hasty preemptive action.

In Al-Naḥl 16:62, the common form مُفْرَطُونَ is a passive participle and may concern being sent ahead or abandoned.

Neglect should therefore not be imposed on every derivative.

A second error is equating tafrīṭ with forgetfulness.

Neglect can arise from ignorance or forgetting, but in many uses a person abandons necessary work despite having the opportunity and responsibility.

A third error is making tafrīṭ equivalent to every minor mistake.

A person may err despite sincere effort without having abandoned their responsibility.

In responsibility-related uses, tafrīṭ emphasizes neglect, carelessness, or wasting something. An unsuccessful outcome alone therefore does not always prove neglect.

A fourth error is treating excess and neglect as identical.

They may be connected in a particular course of conduct, but semantically one exceeds due measure and the other abandons or fails to carry out what is required.

A fifth error is using مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِي الْكِتَابِ to establish details the verse itself does not state.

The expression stresses the absence of omission. Identifying the Book and the scope of the things intended requires syntax, context, and other Qur’anic verses.

Conversely, the comprehensive مِنْ شَيْءٍ should not be weakened without grounds merely to restrict the verse’s implications as one wishes.

A sixth error is conflating مُفْرَطُونَ, مُفْرِطُونَ, and مُفَرِّطُونَ.

Their vowels and consonant doubling change the type of derivative and the direction of meaning. A translation of Al-Naḥl 16:62 must therefore remain faithful to the reading underlying the text.

10. Translation recommendations for Forqan

A single fixed Persian equivalent cannot cover this root’s uses.

In its early advancement sense, فَرَطَ can be rendered “went ahead” or “went in advance.”

فَرَطَ عَلَيْهِ in the context of Ṭā Hā 20:45 can be translated “acted hastily against him.”

فَرَّطَ فِي الْأَمْرِ means “neglected the task,” “abandoned it,” or “wasted it.”

إِفْرَاط in its familiar use means excess and exceeding limits.

تَفْرِيط means neglect, abandonment, or failure in responsibility.

The following renderings suit the Qur’anic forms:

In Al-Anʿām 6:31:

“Alas for our neglect in this matter!”

In Al-Anʿām 6:38:

“We have omitted nothing from the Book.”

In Al-Anʿām 6:61:

“And they do not neglect their duty.”

In Yūsuf 12:80:

“And previously you failed in your responsibility toward Joseph.”

In Al-Naḥl 16:62, following Ḥafṣ’s reading:

“And they will be sent ahead to the Fire.”

In Al-Kahf 18:28:

“And his affair has gone beyond due measure and fallen into ruin.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:45:

“We fear that he may act hastily against us or transgress.”

In Al-Zumar 39:56:

“Alas for me, for what I neglected in my duty toward The God.”

A brief lexical explanation should also distinguish the root’s general meaning from a derivative’s contextual meaning.

Brief root meaning: going ahead and exceeding due measure; in some constructions, neglect and omission.

Meaning in this verse: according to usage, neglecting a responsibility, failing to perform necessary work, hasty preemptive action, or being sent ahead.

Each verse’s contextual explanation should give only the meaning relevant to that form and context, rather than every branch of the family.

11. Synthesis

In early Arabic, ف ر ط concerns advancing, going ahead, and hastening. A فَارِط went before others, sometimes to prepare water and equipment for a caravan.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry also concerns outstripping others and not abandoning important possessions.

The family also developed the recognized branches of ifrāṭ, excess and exceeding limits, and tafrīṭ, neglect and failure in responsibility.

The root occurs eight times in the Qur’an, and its varied uses show why “neglect” cannot translate every derivative.

فَرَّطَ in verses about human responsibility concerns neglect and omission. لَا يُفَرِّطُونَ describes divine Messengers not neglecting their duty. يَفْرُطَ in the story of Moses and Aaron concerns Pharaoh’s hasty preemptive action. فُرُطًا describes an affair that has exceeded bounds and fallen into ruin; مُفْرَطُونَ in the common reading of Al-Naḥl 16:62 concerns being sent ahead or abandoned.

The family’s semantic core concerns going ahead and passing a prescribed limit or position; its tafrīṭ branch concerns abandoning work that should have been done properly and on time.

This semantic connection is useful, but does not by itself establish the history of every derivative.

Qur’anic uses also reveal an important difference between making a mistake and neglecting responsibility. Someone may err despite proper effort; neglecting a right, a trust, or an opportunity entrusted to them is another matter.

Accordingly, in verses about regret, tafrīṭ becomes associated with a lost opportunity: the chance to recognize truth and reform one’s conduct before the final outcome arrives.
