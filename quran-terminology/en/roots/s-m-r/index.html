Nighttime conversation, dark coloration, and the name al-Samiri: from evening gatherings to turning away from Quranic signs

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root S M R has several established semantic branches, chiefly nighttime conversation, a color between white and black, and certain names of plants, objects, and substances.

In the conversational branch, سَمَرَ يَسْمُرُ سَمَرًا means spending the night awake in conversation with others. السَّمَر denotes nighttime conversation; السَّامِر is someone participating in it.

Here samar is more specific than speaking: it is conversation at night. People may sit in a house, around a fire, outdoors, or at a gathering place and discuss various matters.

The root does not specify the subject. Such gatherings may involve friendly conversation, news, consultation, poetry, everyday affairs, or idle and objectionable speech.

Consequently, samar does not itself mean lying, backbiting, mocking, or discussing a particular subject. These features require contextual evidence.

In another branch, سُمْرَة denotes a color between white and black. أَسْمَر and سَمْرَاء describe something with that coloration.

The description can apply to human skin, animals, wood, plants, and other objects. Depending on the subject, Persian equivalents suggesting a wheat-colored, brown, or dusky appearance may approximate this range; no single equivalent fits every use.

In certain early uses, السَّمَر also denotes the darkness or dimness of night.

Early lexicographers connected the color of night, gentle moonlight, and nighttime gatherings. In their explanation, people gathered and talked in darkness or moonlight, linking the name for the nighttime setting with conversation.

This is a comprehensible semantic connection, but a semantic relationship differs from a securely established derivational history. Conversation occurring at night does not itself prove that the verb samar arose from a color term or the name for darkness at a particular historical stage.

Historical meaning and early evidence

A familiar Arabic expression reads:

لَا آتِيكَ السَّمَرَ وَالْقَمَرَ

lā ātīka al-samara wa-al-qamara

Meaning: I shall not come to you as long as night and the moon endure; that is, I shall never come.

Here samar accompanies qamar, relating to nighttime and the passage of time. The word was thus not exclusively a name for conversation: it also concerned nighttime darkness and duration.

The expression is recorded in early dictionaries, but its inclusion in Islamic-period works alone does not establish precisely when it circulated before Islam.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Shanfara reads:

هُنَالِكَ لَا أَرْجُو حَيَاةً تَسُرُّنِي
سَمِيرَ اللَّيَالِي مُبْسَلًا بِالْجَرَائِرِ

hunālika lā arjū ḥayātan tasurrunī / samīra al-layālī mubsalan bi-al-jarāʾiri

Meaning: In that condition, as long as the nights continue, I hope for no life that brings me joy, held captive by the consequences of my deeds.

In this verse, سَمِيرَ اللَّيَالِي concerns continuing time and passing nights, connecting with expressions for the endurance of night and day.

Early literary works attribute the verse to al-Shanfara. His pre-Islamic affiliation is well known, but the exact date of this verse and every detail of its subsequent transmission are not independently certain.

Moreover, the verse does not directly depict a gathering for nighttime conversation; it illustrates the temporal branch. It should not replace evidence for samar meaning nighttime speech.

Dictionaries clearly record سَمَرَ for conversation at night. The Quranic سَامِرًا also attests that meaning in the language of revelation.

A defensible account is therefore that early Arabic S M R concerned darkness and the nighttime setting, nighttime conversation, and a color between white and black. The branches have explicable semantic connections, but available evidence does not establish which historically preceded the others.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important recorded forms include:

  • سَمَرَ / يَسْمُرُ: to spend the night awake in conversation.
  • سَمَر: nighttime conversation; also, in certain uses, darkness or the nighttime setting.
  • سَامِر: a participant in nighttime conversation; sometimes a group or its nighttime meeting place.
  • سُمَّار and سُمَّر: people conversing at night.
  • سَمِير: a nighttime companion in conversation.
  • مُسَامَرَة: nighttime conversation and companionship.
  • سَمِرَ / سَمُرَ: to become brown or dusky in color.
  • سُمْرَة: a color between white and black.
  • أَسْمَر and سَمْرَاء: brown or dusky in color.
  • السَّمُر: a kind of thorny tree of arid regions.
  • سَمَار: thin milk or milk mixed with water.

Other uses are also recorded. For example, سَمَرَتِ الماشيةُ النباتَ describes livestock grazing on vegetation at night. The time of the action matters here; the animals are not conversing.

سَمَّرَ الخشب also means fastening wood with nails, and مِسْمَار names a nail.

This use should not be derived from nighttime gatherings or dark coloration without evidence. Grouping words under one dictionary entry does not independently establish their derivational history or a shared origin for every word.

Sumra: coloration and the naming of objects

In his account of S M R, Ibn Faris emphasizes coloration differing from whiteness and connects many branches with dark coloration.

Within this account, السَّمْرَاء is recorded for wheat, a designation associated with the color of its grains.

الأَسْمَر also describes a spear or wood in some uses. The dark coloration and appearance of wood can help explain such naming.

The samur tree was familiar in Arabia. A thorny tree of arid regions, it mattered in desert life for shade, wood, and livestock use.

Nevertheless, its historical naming cannot be established solely from a resemblance in color to other things called asmar.

Dictionaries also record سَمَار for thin milk. Lexicographers sometimes explain the name through the change in color when milk is mixed with water. Again, this is a possible semantic connection, not proof of the word's historical origin.

Color and darkness are therefore established branches, but every member of the family should not be derived from a single physical image without adequate evidence.

3. Quranic occurrences

Some morphological Quran concordances index four occurrences under S M R:

  • السَّامِرِيُّ in Ta Ha 20:85.
  • السَّامِرِيُّ in Ta Ha 20:87.
  • يَا سَامِرِيُّ in Ta Ha 20:95.
  • سَامِرًا in Al-Mu'minun 23:67.

These four occurrences are not identical in word type or linguistic meaning.

سَامِرًا is a descriptive word in the nighttime-conversation family.

By contrast, السَّامِرِيّ is a name or identifying designation for a person in the story of Moses. Its placement under these letters in a concordance does not establish the historical origin of the name.

The Quranic investigation should therefore distinguish the clear meaning of samir from the role of al-Samiri as a proper name.

4. سَامِرًا: nighttime conversation in Al-Mu'minun

Al-Mu'minun 23:67 states:

مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ سَامِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ

mustakbirīna bihi sāmiran tahjurūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:67

While behaving arrogantly toward it, you gathered for nighttime conversation and spoke idly or offensively.

The preceding verse is essential to understanding the expression.

Al-Mu'minun 23:66 states:

قَدْ كَانَتْ آيَاتِي تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنْكِصُونَ

qad kānat āyātī tutlā ʿalaykum fa-kuntum ʿalā aʿqābikum tankiṣūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66

My signs were recited to you, but you kept retreating on your heels.

The next verse describes their condition and conduct: arrogance, nighttime gatherings, and speech expressed through تَهْجُرُونَ.

The meaning of سَامِرًا in this construction

سَامِرًا follows the pattern faʿil and is accusative, describing the condition of the people addressed.

Samir can designate an individual nighttime conversationalist, but Arabic also uses this singular form for a group gathering to converse at night.

Thus, although the people addressed are plural, as are مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ and تَهْجُرُونَ, سَامِرًا appears in singular form.

Here it can describe a collective: people assembled at night and talking together.

Lexicographers also record samir for a nighttime meeting place, but the group or state of nighttime conversation fits this verse's construction more directly.

Consequently, سَامِرًا does not independently mean idle speakers or mockers here. It conveys the nighttime conversational setting.

The conduct's negative character emerges from the whole verse, particularly مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ and تَهْجُرُونَ.

Does the Quran condemn nighttime gatherings?

Samar itself carries no fixed moral judgment in Arabic.

Nighttime conversation can serve learning, consultation, reconciliation, good works, or friendly companionship. It can also become an occasion for humiliation, falsehood, mockery, or idle speech.

In Al-Mu'minun 23:67, merely staying awake at night is not the object of criticism.

The verse concerns people who heard the signs recited but turned away, engaging arrogantly in conversations associated with an objectionable response to the truth.

This distinction matters. Translating samir itself as a slanderer or an idle speaker transfers the context's negative quality into the root's fixed meaning.

Conversely, mentioning nighttime gatherings while ignoring tahjurun would leave the criticized conduct unclear.

تَهْجُرُونَ and its distinction from سَامِرًا

تَهْجُرُونَ belongs to H J R, not S M R.

That Arabic family concerns abandonment and separation, and in some forms and uses, idle speech, delirious utterance, or offensive language.

For the reading تَهْجُرُونَ, including Hafs, two semantic directions have been discussed: turning away from the truth, and speaking objectionably or idly.

Another reading, تُهْجِرُونَ, transmitted from Nafi, is form IV and more explicitly foregrounds offensive language.

The distinction matters for the verse but does not change samiran. In either reading, samir concerns nighttime conversation; its character comes from the accompanying verb and context.

The following verse, Al-Mu'minun 23:68, asks:

أَفَلَمْ يَدَّبَّرُوا الْقَوْلَ أَمْ جَاءَهُمْ مَا لَمْ يَأْتِ آبَاءَهُمُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

a-fa-lam yaddabbarū al-qawla am jāʾahum mā lam yaʾti ābāʾahumu al-awwalīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:68

Have they not reflected on the speech, or has something come to them that did not come to their earliest ancestors?

Its connection with the preceding verses shows that their response to the signs and truthful speech is at issue.

Al-Mu'minun 23:70 also states:

أَمْ يَقُولُونَ بِهِ جِنَّةٌ بَلْ جَاءَهُمْ بِالْحَقِّ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لِلْحَقِّ كَارِهُونَ

am yaqūlūna bihi jinnatun bal jāʾahum bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-aktharuhum li-al-ḥaqqi kārihūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:70

Or do they say that he is possessed? Rather, he has brought them the truth, but most of them dislike the truth.

Speaking against the bearer of truth and turning away from the signs thus have a clear place in this sequence.

Each verb and description nevertheless has its own role: arrogance, nighttime conversation, objectionable speech, and rejecting the truth are connected concepts, not synonyms.

The referent of بِهِ

Several interpretations have been proposed for the pronoun in مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ.

Some relate it to the Quran or the speech being recited to the audience.

Another reading relates it to the sacred House or sanctuary: people boasting over others because of their association with it.

That interpretation relies on the audience's social setting and familiarity with the sanctuary, although the preceding verse does not explicitly name it.

A connection with the messenger conveying the signs has also been discussed.

For root research, resolving the pronoun's referent need not determine the meaning of samiran. Whichever interpretation is adopted, samir still concerns nighttime conversation.

The disagreement about bihi therefore belongs to syntactic and contextual analysis, rather than alteration of the root's meaning.

5. Nighttime gatherings and social responses to truth

Al-Mu'minun 23:66, Al-Mu'minun 23:67, Al-Mu'minun 23:68, Al-Mu'minun 23:69, and Al-Mu'minun 23:70 depict a group's response to truthful speech.

The signs were recited to them, but instead of reflection and examination they responded with arrogance and withdrawal. Their nighttime conversations continued that conduct.

Conversation itself is not condemned in this picture; the response to truth is.

People may discuss a statement, request evidence, raise objections, and even disagree. Disagreement alone does not make their discussion an instance of the behavior criticized here.

But when arrogance, mockery, false allegations, and refusal to reflect replace fair examination, the exchange departs from honest inquiry.

This distinction comes from the interconnected verses, not from samar itself.

Today, group conversations, social gatherings, and online exchanges may likewise share knowledge and experience or become settings for humiliation, rumor, and repeated unsupported claims.

This is a contemporary application of the verse's message, not a claim that samir lexically names every kind of online or group conversation.

A gathering's value depends on more than its time or attendance: its content, conduct, and relationship to truth and justice matter.

6. السَّامِرِيّ: a name in the story of Moses

Al-Samiri occurs three times in Ta Ha.

Ta Ha 20:85 states:

قَالَ فَإِنَّا قَدْ فَتَنَّا قَوْمَكَ مِنْ بَعْدِكَ وَأَضَلَّهُمُ السَّامِرِيُّ

qāla fa-innā qad fatannā qawmaka min baʿdika wa-aḍallahumu al-sāmirī

Ṭā Hā 20:85

He said: We have tested your people after you, and al-Samiri has led them astray.

Ta Ha 20:87 also states:

فَقَذَفْنَاهَا فَكَذَٰلِكَ أَلْقَى السَّامِرِيُّ

fa-qadhafnāhā fa-kadhālika alqā al-sāmirī

Ṭā Hā 20:87

So we cast them away; al-Samiri likewise cast.

In Ta Ha 20:95, Moses addresses him:

قَالَ فَمَا خَطْبُكَ يَا سَامِرِيُّ

qāla fa-mā khaṭbuka yā sāmirī

Ṭā Hā 20:95

He said: What, then, is your affair, O Samiri?

In all three instances, al-Samiri is a person involved in the calf episode and the misguidance of a group of Israelites.

Is al-Samiri derived from samar, nighttime conversation?

Concordances place al-Samiri under S M R. This classification alone does not prove that his name or designation derives from the Arabic verb for nighttime conversation.

The word outwardly resembles a relational designation. Such a name could relate to a people, place, group, or another linguistic form.

Historical and exegetical sources propose differing origins, sometimes associating it with Samaria or Samaritans, and sometimes suggesting other affiliations.

The Quran itself does not explain al-Samiri's territory, people, or linguistic background in these verses.

Without independent historical evidence, one cannot establish that he necessarily came from a particular region, belonged to a particular people, or bore a name meaning nighttime conversationalist.

Nor does the name itself establish a semantic connection between his conduct in the calf episode and nighttime conversation, dark coloration, or another branch of S M R.

A person's name and a root's meaning

The distinction between a proper name and a descriptive word is decisive here.

سَامِرًا in Al-Mu'minun 23:67 is descriptive, and its lexical meaning contributes to the sentence.

By contrast, السَّامِرِيّ identifies a particular person in Ta Ha.

His conduct is expressed through verbs such as أَضَلَّهُمُ and أَلْقَى and other narrative phrases, rather than an unsupported meaning assigned to his name.

Although these forms appear together in concordances, historical and semantic research must distinguish them.

The root definition should therefore not begin with the calf episode and invent a meaning from nighttime conversation or dark coloration to explain al-Samiri's behavior.

The story provides Quranic uses of a proper name; establishing its historical derivation from an Arabic root requires independent evidence.

7. Distinctions from neighboring roots

S M R and S H R

Sahar concerns staying awake, especially at night.

In its conversational branch, samar concerns gathering and talking at night.

The two can coincide. A participant in nighttime conversation is normally awake, but not every instance of nighttime wakefulness involves conversation.

Someone may stay awake for work, guarding, study, nursing a patient, or insomnia without talking to anyone.

Sahar thus emphasizes wakefulness, while this familiar use of samar emphasizes nighttime conversation.

Both can occur in favorable or unfavorable contexts. Neither independently supplies a fixed moral judgment about wakefulness at night.

S M R and H D TH

Hadith concerns speech, discourse, and news in Arabic.

The conversational branch of samar specifically concerns speech at night.

In this branch, every samar is conversation, but not every discourse or utterance is samar.

Speech can occur at any time and need not belong to a nighttime gathering.

In Al-Mu'minun 23:67, samiran supplies the nighttime setting; the content and response to the signs come from other parts of the verse.

S M R and N J W

Najwa concerns secret speech or private conversation.

Two or more people may participate, with a commendable or objectionable purpose.

Samar, by contrast, need not be secret. People may converse at night in public or at an open gathering.

Najwa is also not restricted to nighttime.

Nighttime occurrence and secrecy are distinct features. A conversation can have both without making the roots synonymous.

S M R and L GH W

Laghw concerns speech or conduct that is idle, useless, or without standing in relation to its meaning, purpose, or relevant value.

In its conversational branch, samar specifies the nighttime setting.

Nighttime conversation may be idle or useful.

In Al-Mu'minun 23:67, its objectionable quality comes from context and tahjurun; this should not become the fixed meaning of samir.

Samiran therefore cannot always be translated as idle speech, even though this verse associates nighttime conversation with criticized conduct.

S M R and H J R

One branch of H J R concerns abandonment and separation; another concerns idle speech, delirious utterance, or offensive language.

In Al-Mu'minun 23:67, tahjurun introduces this issue alongside nighttime conversation.

If the sentence describes offensive or idle speech, that meaning comes from tahjurun and context, not samiran alone.

If abandonment and withdrawal are preferred, samir still retains nighttime conversation; only its connection with the accompanying behavior changes.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

In its conversational use, سَمَرَ يَسْمُرُ can be rendered as spending the night in conversation, conversing at night, or talking with others at night.

سَمَر can be rendered as nighttime conversation or a nighttime conversational gathering.

Individual سَامِر means a nighttime conversationalist or companion. Collectively, a group conversing at night is more suitable.

سُمَّار naturally means nighttime conversationalists or companions.

سَمِير may be a nighttime conversation partner or companion.

In the color branch, سُمْرَة may suggest a brown or gently dusky coloration, depending on the subject. أَسْمَر likewise requires attention to context and the object described.

Translating Al-Mu'minun 23:67

The Arabic expression is:

مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ سَامِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ

mustakbirīna bihi sāmiran tahjurūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:67

The current Persian Forqan translation can be represented in English as follows:

“Arrogant toward it, you engaged in idle talk at nighttime gatherings.”

This conveys the overall conduct being criticized.

For lexical precision, however, nighttime gatherings corresponds to samiran, while idle talk represents an interpretation of tahjurun.

To follow the Arabic structure more explicitly, one could say:

“While behaving arrogantly toward it, you gathered for nighttime conversation and spoke idly.”

This makes the three elements more distinct: arrogance, nighttime conversation, and the kind of speech.

Nevertheless, legitimate disagreement over the referent of bihi and the precise meaning of tahjurun requires reflection on the verse and cannot be settled from S M R alone. These suggestions do not change the installed verse translations.

Translating السَّامِرِيّ

In Ta Ha 20:85, Ta Ha 20:87, and Ta Ha 20:95, retain al-Samiri as the person's name or designation.

Rendering it as nighttime conversationalist, dark-colored person, native of Samaria, or another description is inappropriate without independent evidence for the name's origin.

Al-Samiri suffices to identify the person; the verbs and constructions describe his actions.

Conclusion

S M R has several established semantic branches in Arabic.

In its conversational branch, samar denotes gathering and speaking at night. Samir names a participant and can collectively designate the group.

In the color branch, sumra denotes a color between white and black, alongside asmar and samra. Early lexicographers connect this with nighttime darkness, but the historical derivation of one branch from the other is not certain.

Arabic also records plant and object names, nighttime grazing, and fastening with nails within this lexical family. These branches should not be reduced to an invented historical image without sufficient evidence.

Quranic samiran in Al-Mu'minun 23:67 concerns nighttime conversation. It describes people who heard the signs but arrogantly turned away, engaging in objectionable talk at their gatherings.

The negative judgment comes from the whole verse. Samir itself does not mean liar, mocker, or abusive speaker.

Al-Samiri appears three times in the story of Moses as a person's name or identifying designation. Concordance placement under S M R does not establish its historical derivation from nighttime conversation or dark coloration.

Final definition: One major branch of S M R denotes nighttime conversation and companionship; another denotes a color between white and black. Quranic samir describes a nighttime conversationalist or group. The conversation's content and moral character come from context, not the word itself.

In Al-Mu'minun 23:67, the word belongs to a picture of arrogance and turning away from the signs. The message is not that every nighttime gathering is objectionable, but that people distance themselves from truth when they make conversation an instrument of arrogance, humiliation, and rejection instead of listening, thinking, and examining what is said.
