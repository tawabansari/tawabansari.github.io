Returning, bringing back, and repeatedly turning toward a point of reference

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The three-letter Arabic root A W B denotes returning: coming back from somewhere, returning to an origin or reference point, and, in some uses, repeating a movement that comes back to its previous point or state.

Related uses include a traveler’s return home, the recurrent movement of limbs, bringing back a sound, a person’s repeated turning toward God, and the ultimate destination or place of return a person reaches.

Qur’anic derivatives appear prominently in three settings: servants turning back toward God, mountains and birds accompanying David, and people’s final return and outcome.

These uses are connected but distinct. A traveler’s return, a bird’s repeated sound, and a person’s turning toward the Lord differ in application. Returning itself means neither repentance from sin nor a guaranteed good outcome. Its direction, subject, and moral value emerge from the sentence and context.

2. Qur’anic forms and the root in early Arabic

A W B occurs 17 times in the Qur’an, in 17 verses and four principal forms:

  • أَوِّبِي: a Form II imperative, occurring once in Sabaʾ 34:10.
  • أَوَّاب: an intensive form on the pattern faʿʿāl, with differing inflections, occurring six times: Al-Isrāʾ 17:25, Ṣād 38:17, Ṣād 38:19, Ṣād 38:30, Ṣād 38:44, and Qāf 50:32.
  • إِيَاب: a verbal noun meaning return, occurring once in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:25.
  • مَآب: a noun that can denote returning, a point of reference, or a place of return, occurring nine times: Āl ʿImrān 3:14, Al-Raʿd 13:29, Al-Raʿd 13:36, Ṣād 38:25, Ṣād 38:40, Ṣād 38:49, Ṣād 38:55, Al-Nabaʾ 78:22, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:39.

The family’s Form I verb is آبَ يَؤُوبُ, but that verb itself does not occur in the Qur’an. Āʾib, awbah, taʾwīb, and taʾawwub are also known in Arabic but do not appear in these forms in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 17 occurrences in four groups

Semantic core: returning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one fundamental principle for the root: al-rujūʿ, returning.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records آبَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ as returning to something. آبَ الْغَائِبُ describes an absent person returning, with iyāb and awbah naming that return.

Three elements can be distinguished: movement or departure, a reference point or destination, and the occurrence of coming back.

These should not always be imagined as a physical journey. A person may turn away from misconduct, return to an earlier commitment, or renew attention to the Lord after heedlessness. Returning then has a behavioral or relational sense.

Returning can involve repetition, but not every return is repeated. Repetition is more prominent in certain derivatives, such as awwāb.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: returning and its derivatives

Tangible imagery in early Arabic

One tangible use concerns the movement of an animal’s limbs when walking or running.

Dictionaries record أَوْبُ الْأَيْدِي وَالْقَوَائِمِ for bringing the limbs back during movement: an animal advances a limb and brings it back for the next motion.

This illustrates the relationship between returning and repetition. A movement can include a return that prepares for another movement.

Another use is آبَتِ الشَّمْسُ for sunset. The expression presents sunset as the sun returning to its place of disappearance.

This is evidence of ordinary Arabic expression, rather than a scientific explanation of the sun’s motion or proof that it returns to a fixed location every night.

Early sources also record al-awb as a collective name for bees. They explain it through bees returning to their home after dispersing to forage. This relates to coming back, but does not establish that bee behavior originated every meaning of the root.

Arabic can thus attribute returning to humans, animals, limb movements, and natural phenomena, although its manner differs in each case.

Lisān al-ʿArab: limb movement, sunset, and bees

3. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

An absent person returning

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ contains:

وَكُلُّ ذِي غَيْبَةٍ يَؤُوبُ
وَغَائِبُ الْمَوْتِ لَا يَؤُوبُ

wa-kullu dhī ghaybatin yaʾūbu / wa-ghāʾibu al-mawti lā yaʾūbu

“Everyone who is absent returns, but one whom death has taken away does not return.”

Yaʾūbu here concerns an absent person coming back. Its meaning is direct: someone who has gone away from those close to them may return.

The second hemistich contrasts a traveler’s or absent person’s return with death. The return envisaged is to ordinary worldly life and relationships.

This does not establish a comprehensive theory of life after death on the poet’s part, nor show that awb cannot describe a return in the Hereafter. It belongs to a poetic image of absence and death.

The witness clearly illustrates returning in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet. Attribution and the precise date of this particular verse remain separate questions; its quotation in a dictionary does not conclusively establish when it was composed.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the witness attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ

Taʾwīb in movement and travel

The pre-Islamic poet Salāmah ibn Jandal describes the days of his life and activities in a verse:

يَوْمَانِ يَوْمُ مَقَامَاتٍ وَأَنْدِيَةٍ
وَيَوْمُ سَيْرٍ إِلَى الْأَعْدَاءِ تَأْوِيبِ

yawmāni yawmu maqāmātin wa-andiyatin / wa-yawmu sayrin ilā al-aʿdāʾi taʾwībi

“Two kinds of day: a day for attending gatherings and assemblies, and a day of sustained daytime travel toward the enemies, until evening.”

Taʾwīb is associated here with travel. Early lexical explanations also record the word for journeying throughout the day until evening.

Continuity and sustained progress matter alongside movement. Yet not every occurrence of taʾwīb must mean daytime travel. This is one particular branch of a broader family.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah also records expressions concerning the recurring movement of limbs, driving camels toward their place of return, and coming back at night.

These witnesses show that returning was associated with sustained and recurring movement in Arabic.

Al-Ṭabarī and the Abū ʿUbaydah transmission: Salāmah ibn Jandal and taʾwīb

Bringing back a sound

Arabic taʾwīb can also concern repeating or returning a sound.

A sound can be repeated, answered, or produced again. Returning need not then involve a body moving from one place to another.

Repetition, a natural echo, and joining in singing or glorification are not identical phenomena. The particular sentence’s evidence must determine which is intended.

This distinction matters especially for the Qur’anic command to the mountains.

4. Awwibī and awwāb: bringing back and repeatedly returning

The mountains and David: Sabaʾ 34:10

In Sabaʾ 34:10, God mentions the favor bestowed on David and addresses the mountains with أَوِّبِي مَعَهُ.

Awwibī is Form II and can express bringing back, repeating, or joining in a repeated utterance.

Ṣād 38:18 describes the mountains glorifying alongside David. In light of this parallel, awwibī in Sabaʾ 34:10 concerns joining in and repeating glorification.

“Join him in glorification” is therefore an appropriate contextual rendering.

A W B itself does not mean glorification. Glorification is the activity associated here with a verb of repetition and accompaniment.

The verb does not establish that an ordinary mountain echo must be intended. An echo offers a conceivable natural explanation for returning sound. Sabaʾ 34:10, however, speaks of the command to the mountains and their accompanying David, while Ṣād 38:18 describes their being subjected. Their manner of participation is not defined as an acoustic mechanism.

The verse’s image should be retained, and a hypothetical natural model should not replace its established meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: the command to the mountains and glorification

David and the birds: Ṣād 38:19

Ṣād 38:19

وَالطَّيْرَ مَحْشُورَةً كُلٌّ لَهُ أَوَّابٌ

wa-al-ṭayra maḥshūratan kullun lahu awwābun

“And the birds gathered together; all were returning to him.”

Here, awwāb concerns a group that includes mountains and birds in the surrounding context.

The intensive pattern usually highlights the strength or repetition of returning. Its precise form here depends on context.

In Ṣād 38:18, the mountains glorify alongside David. The following verse describes gathered birds and then states كُلٌّ لَهُ أَوَّابٌ.

Two readings of lahu are transmitted: reference to David, with mountains and birds responding to his glorification; and reference to God, in which case kull can include David, the mountains, and the birds. In the second reading, all turn toward God in glorification. “All were returning to him” should therefore not silently present one referent as certain.

The pronoun alone cannot establish the birds’ precise physical movement or the manner of their glorification.

One lexical point is nevertheless clear: awwāb in the Qur’an is not restricted to a human repenting of sin.

Its application to birds shows that the basic meaning extends beyond human confession of wrongdoing. Repeating, returning, and recurrently responding to a particular referent can belong to this semantic field.

Al-Rāzī: the two referents of lahu and responsive glorification

David: Ṣād 38:17

Ṣād 38:17

اصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَاذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا دَاوُودَ ذَا الْأَيْدِ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

iṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wa-udhkur ʿabdanā dāwūda dhā al-aydi innahu awwābun

“Be steadfast in the face of what they say, and remember Our servant David, possessed of strength. He repeatedly turned back.”

Awwāb describes David here.

The context presents him as capable and devoted to God. It continues with wisdom, judgment, trial, and his turning back.

In Ṣād 38:24, after recognizing his trial, David seeks his Lord’s forgiveness and turns back. This illustrates returning in connection with recognition of error or a test.

Awwāb in Ṣād 38:17 should not be restricted to that single episode. Its pattern suits a prominent or recurring personal characteristic.

“One who repeatedly turns to God” is therefore more suitable than “a sinner who repents repeatedly.” The latter adds numerous sins, whose number the word itself does not establish.

Turning toward God can occur after error or heedlessness, during a trial or prayer, or through sustained devotion.

Al-Ṭabarī: senses of awwāb in describing David

Solomon: Ṣād 38:30

Ṣād 38:30

وَوَهَبْنَا لِدَاوُودَ سُلَيْمَانَ نِعْمَ الْعَبْدُ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

wa-wahabnā li-dāwūda sulaymāna niʿma al-ʿabdu innahu awwābun

“We granted Solomon to David. What an excellent servant! He repeatedly turned back.”

Here too, awwāb accompanies the description of servanthood.

Subsequent verses describe Solomon through power, possessions, trial, and prayer. After mentioning his trial, Ṣād 38:34 uses anāba of him: he returned or turned toward.

Anāba belongs to N W B, rather than A W B. These semantic families are close but distinct.

Their occurrence within the same group of verses shows that Solomon’s turning toward God fits the orientation described by awwāb.

Awwāb should not, however, be reduced to a particular verb from N W B. It describes the broader characteristic of repeated turning in this context.

Job: Ṣād 38:44

Ṣād 38:44 describes Job with two important qualities: ṣābir and awwāb.

Steadfastness and returning are related but distinct characteristics.

Ṣābir concerns Job’s steadfastness; awwāb highlights his returning and the direction of his turning.

In Ṣād 38:41, Job presents his suffering to his Lord. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:83, he likewise speaks of the affliction that has reached him and appeals to God’s mercy.

Turning toward God does not necessarily mean confessing sin. People may turn to the Lord while suffering, facing hardship, or seeking help.

Rendering awwāb everywhere as “one who repeatedly repents of sin” narrows the word and, for Job, introduces an additional claim about his sinfulness.

“One who repeatedly turns toward God” is more precise here.

Those who return in Al-Isrāʾ 17:25

Al-Isrāʾ 17:25

رَبُّكُمْ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا فِي نُفُوسِكُمْ إِنْ تَكُونُوا صَالِحِينَ فَإِنَّهُ كَانَ لِلْأَوَّابِينَ غَفُورًا

rabbukum aʿlamu bi-mā fī nufūsikum in takūnū ṣāliḥīna fa-innahu kāna li-al-awwābīna ghafūran

“Your Lord knows best what is within you. If you are righteous, He is forgiving toward those who return.”

This follows the commands of kindness and humility toward parents.

The subjects are God’s knowledge of people’s inner selves, righteousness, and forgiveness for those who return.

In this context, al-awwābīn are those who return to what is right rather than persisting in misconduct.

The connection with God’s knowledge of inward intentions matters. Someone may err in conduct while differing in intention and orientation from a person who knowingly persists in injustice and ill treatment.

Awwāb does not itself mean “having a good intention”: returning remains its core meaning. God’s knowledge of inward states and the condition of righteousness come from other parts of the verse.

Nor does divine forgiveness make parents’ rights or the harm caused by one’s conduct unimportant. The verse speaks of returning and forgiveness, rather than authorizing misconduct.

Awwābin ḥafīẓ: Qāf 50:32

Qāf 50:32

هَٰذَا مَا تُوعَدُونَ لِكُلِّ أَوَّابٍ حَفِيظٍ

hādhā mā tūʿadūna li-kulli awwābin ḥafīẓin

“This is what you were promised, for everyone who repeatedly returns and keeps watch.”

Awwāb appears alongside ḥafīẓ here.

The first quality highlights returning and turning toward; the second concerns guarding and preserving. “Keeping one’s covenant” is one contextual explanation of ḥafīẓ; covenant is not a separate word in this verse.

Qāf 50:33 describes someone who fears Al-Raḥmān unseen and comes with a munīb heart.

Together, these verses connect the praised return with inward attentiveness, reverent fear, and preserving commitment.

These qualities should not all be absorbed into the lexical meaning of awwāb. Ḥafīẓ, khashiya, and munīb each contribute independently.

Their combination depicts a person repeatedly turning toward God and remaining attentive to responsibility.

5. Iyāb and maʾāb: returning and final outcome

Iyāb: the act of returning

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:25

إِنَّ إِلَيْنَا إِيَابَهُمْ

inna ilaynā iyābahum

“Their return is surely to Us.”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:26

ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا حِسَابَهُمْ

thumma inna ʿalaynā ḥisābahum

“Then their reckoning is surely Our responsibility.”

Iyāb in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:25 names the return itself.

The preceding context, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:21 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:22, states the Prophet’s duty to remind and the limits of his authority over others.

The verses then discuss turning away, disbelief, return to God, and reckoning.

Iyābahum does not mean their repentance here. This return includes people who turned away in worldly life and must ultimately answer for their actions.

Final return to God is a comprehensive reality in the context of reckoning; consciously turning toward God morally is a person’s choice and conduct during life.

Translating iyāb here as repentance would therefore narrow its meaning incorrectly.

Ilaynā should likewise not be taken as a bodily return to a particular location occupied by God. The verse identifies the final direction of return and authority of reckoning. The word iyāb does not determine the return’s existential or spatial nature.

Maʾāb: returning, a reference point, and a destination

Maʾāb belongs to the same family but differs morphologically from iyāb.

Dictionaries allow it to function as a mīm-prefixed verbal noun meaning returning, or as a noun of place denoting a destination or point of return.

These uses are close but not always interchangeable.

In ḥusn maʾāb or sharr maʾāb, destination and outcome become prominent. Where the action and direction of returning are central, “return” may be more natural.

Morphological form alone therefore does not require “place of return” in every verse.

A good return

After mentioning worldly attachments and enjoyments, Āl ʿImrān 3:14 speaks of ḥusn al-maʾāb with God.

Al-Raʿd 13:29 describes a good destination for those who believe and do righteous deeds.

Ṣād 38:25 and Ṣād 38:40 associate David and Solomon with nearness and ḥusn maʾāb.

Ṣād 38:49 likewise describes a good destination for the mindful, followed by verses about gardens of lasting dwelling.

Maʾāb itself denotes returning or a place of return; ḥusn specifies its goodness.

Maʾāb alone does not mean Paradise or reward. Those senses come from accompanying descriptions and context.

An evil destination and the return of transgressors

Ṣād 38:55 gives transgressors sharr maʾāb.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:22 identifies Hell as their maʾāb.

These uses provide an important test of the root’s meaning.

A definition making maʾāb invariably a good return, mercy, or divine nearness would fail to account for its application to transgressors.

Maʾāb itself is neither good nor bad. The accompanying description and context establish whether the outcome is desirable.

Just as ḥusn maʾāb expresses a good destination, sharr maʾāb expresses an evil one.

The family’s meaning should therefore not be narrowed through examples of righteous servants until its application to transgressors becomes inexplicable.

Ilayhi maʾāb: Al-Raʿd 13:36

In Al-Raʿd 13:36, the Prophet is commanded to speak of worshipping God, rejecting association, calling toward Him, and returning to Him.

Ilayhi maʾāb means “my return is to Him” in this context.

Maʾāb emphasizes the direction and reality of returning more than a specified physical location. Its final kasrah relates to an omitted first-person possessive yāʾ, hence “my return,” rather than simply “return.”

Even where maʾāb can formally be a noun of place, “returning” may therefore be the appropriate sense in the sentence.

Ittakhadha ilā rabbihi maʾābā: Al-Nabaʾ 78:39

Al-Nabaʾ 78:39

ذَٰلِكَ الْيَوْمُ الْحَقُّ فَمَنْ شَاءَ اتَّخَذَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِ مَآبًا

dhālika al-yawmu al-ḥaqqu fa-man shāʾa ittakhadha ilā rabbihi maʾāban

“That is the true Day. Whoever wills, then, may take a way of return to his Lord.”

Here, maʾāb accompanies ittakhadha and the preposition ilā.

The construction concerns a person’s choice to undertake a return toward the Lord.

“Way of return” naturally explains the sentence in Persian, but “way” is not independently present in the Arabic expression.

This should not be reduced to going to a particular material location. The direction is the Lord, and the context concerns the true Day and human responsibility.

A notable contrast appears with Al-Ghāshiyah 88:25: that verse speaks of people’s final return, while Al-Nabaʾ 78:39 presents the choice to undertake a return to the Lord.

This distinction comes from the two sentences, particularly fa-man shāʾa ittakhadha, rather than making choice part of maʾāb’s lexical meaning.

6. Distinctions from related roots

A W B (āba) and R J ʿ (rajaʿa): return and coming back

R J ʿ is one of the closest semantic families to awb.

Both can express coming back from somewhere or returning to a point of reference.

R J ʿ has a broad Qur’anic range, applying to people, speech, responses, events, and different situations.

Awb also denotes returning, with repeated and persistent turning particularly prominent in some derivatives, especially awwāb.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute rule making every rujuʿ a single occurrence and every awb repeated.

Both families can express returning to God without becoming identical in every semantic detail.

Study of R J ʿ

A W B (āba) and T W B (tāba): returning and repentance

The roots overlap in returning, and some dictionaries explain awwāb through tawwāb.

Qur’anic usage nevertheless shows that they should not be treated as complete synonyms everywhere.

Tawbah often concerns turning away from error, reform, and divine acceptance. The verb is also attributed to God, whose turning toward a servant differs in meaning and direction from a servant turning toward God.

A W B, meanwhile, applies to a traveler returning in Arabic, mountains and birds responding or joining in the Qur’an, and people’s final return to God.

The awb family therefore extends beyond repentance understood as abandoning sin.

In human moral conduct, turning toward God can include repentance. Yet an awwāb need not be someone who has committed many sins and repeatedly repented of them.

This distinction matters when translating the descriptions of David, Solomon, and Job.

Study of T W B

A W B (āba) and N W B (nāba): returning and inābah

Qur’anic N W B appears in forms including anāba and munīb.

Ṣād 38:24 uses anāba of David, and Ṣād 38:34 uses it of Solomon.

Both had earlier been described as awwāb.

Their association places returning and inābah in nearby semantic fields.

Their co-occurrence alone does not establish an absolute boundary—for example, that awb always means the initial stage and inābah the final stage of returning.

Both concern turning and returning, directed toward God in a spiritual context. Their precise meanings require examining each root’s full range of uses.

A W B (āba) and Ṣ Y R (ṣāra): return and outcome

Ṣ Y R concerns becoming, changing state, and reaching an outcome.

Qur’anic expressions involving maṣīr can concern people’s end or final outcome.

Maʾāb sometimes occupies that field too, but its basic image is returning to a point of reference.

“Outcome” may therefore appropriately render maʾāb in some verses, without making the roots historically or semantically identical.

Not every outcome is explicitly expressed as a return, just as not every return is a final ending.

Study of Ṣ Y R

A W B (āba) and A W Y (awā): returning and taking refuge

A W Y concerns taking refuge, seeking shelter, and turning toward a protecting place.

Maʾwā belongs to that family.

Maʾwā and maʾāb may refer to the same reality in some passages about the Hereafter, but remain different words.

Maʾāb approaches the subject through returning and final reference; maʾwā through lodging, refuge, or a place of settlement.

This matters in translation: a destination does not necessarily contain “refuge” in its lexical meaning.

Nor should the words’ relative resemblance in unvowelled Persian cause their roots to be confused.

Study of A W Y

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Persian equivalents for A W B should follow morphological form and context.

Awwibī in Sabaʾ 34:10:

“Join him in a responding utterance” or “join him in glorification.”

The latter is clearer contextually, because related verses explain the mountains accompanying David through glorification. Glorification should nevertheless not be made the root’s independent meaning.

Awwāb as a human description:

“One who repeatedly returns,” “one who continually turns toward God,” or “one who repeatedly returns to his Lord.”

Where context establishes the direction, “one who repeatedly returns” suffices in Persian. Where readers may otherwise miss the referent, the sentence can clarify the destination.

“One who often repents” is understandable in some contexts, but should not become a fixed equivalent for every occurrence, as it can restrict the word to abandoning sin.

Awwāb in Ṣād 38:19:

In light of the mountains and birds accompanying David, equivalents involving responding, returning, or recurrent participation in glorification may be considered, according to the contextual reading.

“Repenting” should not be used here.

Iyāb in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:25:

“Return.”

This is concise, natural, and precise. Repentance, reckoning, and resurrection are distinct concepts that should not replace the word itself.

Maʾāb in expressions concerning outcome:

“Return,” “place of return,” or, in some contexts, “outcome.”

For ḥusn maʾāb and sharr maʾāb, “a good place of return” and “an evil place of return” better preserve the root’s image.

In Al-Raʿd 13:36, “my return is to Him” is more natural than “my place of return is toward Him.”

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:39, “taking a way of return toward the Lord” can convey the sentence fluently. “Way” is not an independent component of maʾāb itself.

Repentance from sin, divine nearness, reward, punishment, Paradise, and Hell should not be inserted into the root’s translation unless separate wording or the verse’s clear context identifies such a sense.

8. Conclusion

Arabic A W B denotes returning. This can be physical, behavioral, or related to a final reference point and outcome.

Early Arabic uses include an absent person coming back, recurrent limb movements, returning during travel, and repeating a sound.

Qur’anic awwibī concerns the mountains joining David in glorification. Awwāb describes distinguished servants such as David, Solomon, and Job repeatedly turning toward God. Its application to birds also shows why the root should not be restricted to human repentance.

Iyāb names the act of returning. Al-Ghāshiyah 88:25 uses it for people’s final return toward God.

Maʾāb can denote returning, a point of reference, or a destination, depending on the sentence. Its application to both the mindful and transgressors shows that the word itself is neither morally positive nor negative. The description and context establish whether the destination is good or evil.

In moral use, awwāb is not simply someone who repeatedly sins and then repents. Turning toward God can take the form of correction after error, renewed attention, prayer, sustained devotion, and persistent turning toward the Lord.

Proposed final definition: A W B denotes returning to a point of reference or bringing something back. This may appear in movement and repetition, a person’s persistent turning toward God, and final return or destination. Context, rather than the root alone, determines the direction and value of returning. Brief root meaning: Returning and bringing back; awwāb: one who repeatedly returns or turns toward; maʾāb: return or a place of return.
