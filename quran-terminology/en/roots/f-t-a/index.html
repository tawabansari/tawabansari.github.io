The Root ف ت أ — Ceasing, Desisting, and Continuity in Negative Constructions

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَفْتَؤُا
(taftaʾu)
With understood negation: you continue

مَا فَتِئَ
(mā fatiʾa)
Lexical form: did not cease

The three-consonant root ف ت أ in early Arabic is associated with ceasing an activity, desisting, and bringing its continuation to an end. Its best-known uses occur in negative constructions such as مَا فَتِئَ and مَا فَتَأَ, which convey “continuing,” “not ceasing,” and “not desisting.”

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Yusuf 12:85, in the form تَفْتَؤُا. Its distinctive feature is that the negative particle is not explicitly written in the verse, although its meaning remains present in the sentence.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Early Arabic

In established Arabic usage, فَتِئَ is associated with ceasing, abandoning, and interrupting an activity. When the verb is negated, the construction expresses continuation:

مَا فَتِئْتُ أَذْكُرُهُ means “I continued to remember him” or “I did not stop remembering him.”

In this construction, negation applies to ceasing; the resulting meaning is therefore that the activity continues.

A verse attributed to Aws ibn Hajar, a pre-Islamic poet, reads:

فَمَا فَتِئَتْ خَيْلٌ تَثُوبُ وَتَدَّعِي
وَيَلْحَقُ مِنْهَا لَاحِقٌ وَتَقَطَّعُ

Fa-mā fatiʾat khaylun tathūbu wa-taddaʿī / wa-yalḥaqu minhā lāḥiqun wa-taqaṭṭaʿu.

Translation: “Horsemen kept returning and calling to one another; some joined them, while others broke away.”

The verse describes the movement and engagement of horsemen. فَمَا فَتِئَتْ indicates that their movement and activity did not cease but continued.

This verse is preserved in the collection attributed to Aws ibn Hajar and in early lexical citations. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet makes it important evidence for the language before the Qur’an, although the text has reached us through later manuscripts and transmissions rather than a document contemporary with the poet.

Early dictionaries also record فَتِئْتُ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ in the sense of desisting from something or forgetting it. This usage is consistent with breaking off an engagement and ending an activity’s continuation. Establishing that this particular construction was current in the pre-Islamic period, however, requires independently datable evidence.

Some lexical accounts also give فَتَأَ the meaning of quenching or extinguishing. The reading of this usage has nevertheless been disputed in relation to the root ف ث أ. Extinguishing fire therefore cannot be established as the original and certain meaning of ف ت أ without strong evidence.

2. Lexical and Qur’anic Forms

Arabic records several forms in this family:

  • فَتِئَ and فَتَأَ: two recognized verbal forms used in negative expressions of continuation.
  • مَا فَتِئَ and مَا فَتَأَ: did not cease, did not desist, continued.
  • مَا أَفْتَأَ: another form attributed in lexical accounts to the dialect of Tamim.
  • تَفْتَؤُا: the imperfect form used in the Qur’an.

Only the last form occurs in the Qur’an. Other forms recorded in dictionaries should not be presented as Qur’anic derivatives.

The spelling also matters: in Qur’anic orthography, تَفْتَؤُا is written with a waw bearing the hamza and an additional alif after it. These letters do not mark a plural verb. The addressee is one person, Jacob, and the pronounced form is تَفْتَأُ, taftaʾu.

3. Qur’anic Usage

Surah Yusuf 12:85

قَالُوا۟ تَٱللَّهِ تَفْتَؤُا۟ تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ حَتَّىٰ تَكُونَ حَرَضًا أَوْ تَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلْهَٰلِكِينَ

Qālū ta-llāhi taftaʾu tadhkuru Yūsufa ḥattā takūna ḥaraḍan aw takūna mina l-hālikīn.

Translation: “They said: By The God, you keep remembering Joseph until you become worn out or are among those who perish.”

These are the words of Jacob’s sons, addressing their father after observing his grief.

In the preceding verse, Yusuf 12:84, Jacob turns away from his sons and expresses his sorrow over Joseph. The Qur’an describes his eyes becoming white from grief.

In verse 12:85, his sons refer to his continued remembrance of Joseph and speak of his becoming worn out or dying as the outcome of this condition.

In this sentence, تَفْتَؤُا expresses the continuation of an activity. The verb itself does not mean grief, illness, or remembrance. Those meanings come from other words in the verse and the narrative context.

The verb تَذْكُرُ identifies the activity: remembering Joseph. حَتَّىٰ تَكُونَ حَرَضًا expresses the outcome that the speakers anticipate if this condition continues.

The sentence thus conveys: “You do not stop remembering Joseph but continue doing so, to the point where it is feared that you will become worn out or perish.”

This is the sons’ assessment of their father’s condition, rather than an independent divine judgment that remembering Joseph is wrong.

In the next verse, Yusuf 12:86, Jacob responds that he complains of his suffering and grief only to The God and knows from The God what they do not know. His continued remembrance of Joseph should therefore not be detached from the context and treated as a definitive condemnation of paternal love or as evidence of Jacob’s despair.

4. Grammar: How Can the Verb Be Negative without لَا?

The usual construction expressing continuation includes negation, as in مَا فَتِئَ and لَا يَفْتَأُ.

The verse, however, reads:

«تَاللَّهِ تَفْتَؤُا تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ»

Ta-llāhi taftaʾu tadhkuru Yūsuf.

Here تَاللَّهِ is an oath expression, and تَفْتَؤُا is an imperfect, incomplete verb belonging to the grammatical class of كَانَ and its sisters.

Its subject is the implicit pronoun أَنْتَ, “you,” referring to Jacob. The verbal clause تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ serves as its predicate.

In Arabic, the negative particle is sometimes omitted after an oath in this construction while its meaning remains understood. The following is an explanatory grammatical reconstruction, not the wording of the verse:

تَاللَّهِ لَا تَفْتَأُ تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ

That is: “By The God, you do not cease remembering Joseph.”

This omission is not an arbitrary interpretive possibility. It is supported by an established oath construction and the use of this class of verbs in Arabic.

For comparison, a verse attributed to Imru’ al-Qays also uses أَبْرَحُ after an oath without لَا, while conveying لَا أَبْرَحُ. This example shows that omission of negation in such constructions is not confined to the verse in Yusuf.

The central point is that continuity results from the negative construction of the verb. The root ف ت أ should not itself be defined as “always continuing” in a way that erases the contribution of negation.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ت أ (فَتَأَ) and ب ر ح (بَرِحَ)

مَا فَتِئَ and مَا بَرِحَ can have closely related meanings in negative constructions.

In al-Kahf 18:60, لَا أَبْرَحُ expresses Moses’ determination not to stop travelling until he reaches his destination. In Yusuf 12:85, تَفْتَؤُا, with an understood negation, expresses continued remembrance of Joseph.

The two constructions are close in their expression of continuity, but the other uses of ب ر ح must be examined separately.

ف ت أ (فَتَأَ) and ف ت ر (فَتَرَ)

The root ف ت ر is associated with becoming slack, subsiding in intensity, and reduced activity.

In al-Anbiya 21:20, لَا يَفْتُرُونَ describes worship in which no slackening or abatement occurs.

In the construction under discussion, ف ت أ focuses more on an activity’s not stopping. Continuation and undiminished intensity are related but not identical. An activity may continue at a lower intensity.

ف ت أ (فَتَأَ) and ف ت ي (فَتِيَ)

Despite their similar spelling, these are distinct roots in Qur’anic study.

Words such as فَتًى, فِتْيَة, and فَتَيَات, together with forms associated with فُتْيَا and فَتْوَى, are studied under ف ت ي.

تَفْتَؤُا in Yusuf 12:85 belongs to ف ت أ. Meanings such as youth, youthful generosity, or answering a question should not be transferred to it.

Likewise, the similarity between فَتَأَ and فَتَرَ does not justify treating their roots as identical, even where some of their uses overlap in the semantic field of ceasing and slackening.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

When translating ف ت أ, one must consider whether the construction is affirmative or negative and which verb follows it.

In negative expressions such as مَا فَتِئَ يَفْعَلُ, suitable equivalents include “he continued doing,” “he kept on doing,” and “he did not stop doing.”

In Yusuf 12:85, تَفْتَؤُا تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ permits two natural renderings:

“You keep remembering Joseph.”

“You do not stop remembering Joseph.”

The first is more fluent and concise. The second makes the negative construction and the idea of not ceasing more explicit. Both are defensible in this verse’s context.

تَفْتَؤُا should not by itself be translated as “you remember.” Remembrance comes from تَذْكُرُ; the function of تَفْتَؤُا is to express the continuation of that activity.

7. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ت أ is associated with ceasing, desisting, and ending an activity’s continuation. Its prominent, attested use is in negative constructions conveying continuation and not ceasing.

Poetic evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period and early lexical accounts support this usage. Some secondary meanings and disputed readings nevertheless do not warrant definite historical conclusions.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only in Yusuf 12:85, as تَفْتَؤُا. The negation understood from the oath construction gives the sentence the meaning “you do not stop remembering Joseph.”

A defensible semantic core of this root is ceasing or desisting from an activity; in a negative construction, that same idea becomes not ceasing and continuing the activity.

This distinction is essential to an accurate translation: تَفْتَؤُا expresses continuity, تَذْكُرُ identifies remembrance as the activity, and the other parts of the verse clarify the emotional condition and the outcome of concern to the speakers.
