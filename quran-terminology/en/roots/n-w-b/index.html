Returning and turning, repeated visits, turns, and representation: from early Arabic to Qur’anic inābah

In Arabic, N W B concerns returning and turning again toward a place, person, or destination. Some uses describe someone repeatedly visiting a place, performing a task when their turn comes, or returning toward a destination.

The familiar words for a turn, alternation, and representation belong to this family. Its foremost Qur’anic concept, however, is إِنَابَة (inābah): turning and returning to The God.

The Qur’an uses two morphological groups: أَنَابَ (anāba) and its inflected verb forms, and the active participle مُنِيب (munīb). All Qur’anic occurrences fall within these groups. Forms such as نَوْبَة (nawbah), نَائِب (nāʾib), نِيَابَة (niyābah), and تَنَاوُب (tanāwub) do not occur in the Qur’an.

Inābah in the Qur’an is not limited to returning after sin. It describes prophets, believers, people turning to The God, and even those who turn to Him in distress but turn away after relief. A definition must account for all these uses.

1. Semantic core and word family

One lexical analysis defines N W B as follows:

النُّونُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْبَاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى اعْتِيَادِ مَكَانٍ وَرُجُوعٍ إِلَيْهِ

al-nūnu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-bāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun tadullu ʿalā iʿtiyādi makānin wa-rujūʿin ilayhi

“Nūn, wāw, and bāʾ concern becoming accustomed to a place and returning to it.”

Two features stand out: returning to a place with which a connection already exists, and repeating the visits until the destination becomes familiar.

For example, someone repeatedly returning to a watering place is doing more than making a chance journey. There is a particular destination to which they return. This concrete setting helps explain several words in the family.

Nāba and intāba: returning and visiting

نَابَ يَنُوبُ (nāba yanūbu) is recorded for returning, visiting, and turning toward something. اِنْتَابَ (intāba) also denotes repeated visits to someone or a place.

Early Arabic records:

وَانْتَابَ الرَّجُلُ الْقَوْمَ انْتِيَابًا إِذَا قَصَدَهُمْ، وَأَتَاهُمْ مَرَّةً بَعْدَ مَرَّةٍ

wa-intāba al-rajulu al-qawma intiyāban idhā qaṣadahum wa-atāhum marratan baʿda marratin

“A person visits a people in this way when he seeks them out and comes to them time after time.”

Repeated visits are explicit here. However, not every N W B derivative necessarily means an action occurred several times. The word’s construction and context determine the extent of repetition.

Nawbah and tanāwub: turns and alternation

نَوْبَة (nawbah) denotes a share or opportunity that comes to someone as a task or event rotates. تَنَاوُب (tanāwub) denotes taking turns or a task rotating among several people.

Early Arabic connects this clearly with water distribution. When several people use a watering place and each group reaches the water at its allotted time, alternation takes place.

تَنَاوَبُوا الْمَاءَ (tanāwabū al-māʾa) accordingly denotes sharing water by turns.

This had practical importance in daily life: visits to watering places, access to water, and its distribution were fundamental needs in living and herding.

Modern Persian “nobat” and “tanāvob” likewise preserve the ideas of rotation and receiving a turn.

Nāʾib and niyābah: substitution and representation

Another branch concerns standing in someone else’s place.

نَابَ عَنْهُ (nāba ʿanhu) means taking another person’s place or acting on their behalf.

نَائِب (nāʾib) names someone who takes another’s place in a task. نِيَابَة (niyābah) names that substitution or representation.

The connection with turns and taking a place is intelligible, but the properties of representation should not be transferred to every other derivative.

For example, Qur’anic مُنِيب (munīb) comes from أَنَابَ (anāba) and does not mean a deputy. Nāʾib and munīb share a root, while their constructions and uses establish their respective senses.

Nāʾibah: an event that befalls someone

نَائِبَة (nāʾibah) is also recorded for an event or occurrence that befalls a person.

نَوَائِبُ الدَّهْرِ (nawāʾibu al-dahri) usually denotes the hardships, misfortunes, and adverse events of life.

نَابَهُ أَمْرٌ (nābahu amrun) can basically describe something befalling someone. Being adverse does not follow from the idea of arrival alone, although nāʾibah commonly carries an unpleasant sense.

Munīb is also recorded for abundant rain and for a favorable, productive spring. The same lexical account also discusses the desirability of rain followed by another rainfall; that adjacent remark should not simply be treated as the definition of munīb. The Qur’an does not use the word to describe rain.

The example shows why each derivative’s meaning must be distinguished across contexts.

2. Early Arabic witnesses

Visiting water by turns in Hudhalī poetry

A verse attributed to Usāmah ibn al-Ḥārith al-Hudhalī reads:

أَقَبُّ طَرِيدٌ بِنُزْهِ الْفَلَاةِ
لَا يَرِدُ الْمَاءَ إِلَّا انْتِيَابَا

aqabbu ṭarīdun bi-nuzhi al-falāti / lā yaridu al-māʾa illā intiyābā

“A lean, driven creature in the distant wilderness, reaching water only at intervals, by turns.”

The poet describes a wild animal in a remote part of the wilderness that visits a watering place to drink.

اِنْتِيَابًا (intiyāban) here concerns visits made at intervals or by turns.

Some transmissions instead end with ائْتِيَابًا (iʾtiyāban), associated with ʾ W B. Its morphological analysis differs from intiyāb.

With intiyāb, the verse is useful evidence for N W B; its variant transmission must nevertheless be preserved.

Literary accounts identify Usāmah ibn al-Ḥārith as someone who lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. Without dating the verse, it cannot confidently be presented as pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Nawb in Labīd’s poetry

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s collected poetry includes:

إِحْدَى بَنِي جَعْفَرٍ بِأَرْضِهِمْ
لَمْ تُمْسِ مِنِّي نَوْبًا وَلَا قُرُبَا

iḥdā banī jaʿfarin bi-arḍihim / lam tumsi minnī nawban wa-lā qurubā

“A woman of Banū Jaʿfar in her people’s land, neither within my visiting range nor near me.”

The sense is that a woman of Banū Jaʿfar, living among her people, is so far beyond the poet’s reach that she is neither within his customary visiting distance nor near him.

Lexical explanations sometimes relate nawb to a day-and-night journey and sometimes to other distances. Some also associate it with nearness and accessibility.

No single reported distance should therefore become the fixed meaning of nawb in every use.

The verse occurs in Labīd’s collected poetry and lexical records. Labīd lived through both periods; without dating the poem, assigning it categorically to the pre-Islamic era would lack the necessary caution.

Nūb as a name for bees

نُوب (nūb) is also recorded as a name for bees.

One explanation connects it with bees leaving to gather food and returning to their hive. Repeated journeys would then relate to the root’s semantic core.

Another early account links the name with the bees’ color and resemblance to a people called Nūb.

These explanations differ. It cannot confidently be claimed that the bee name necessarily arose from returning to the hive. The usage is recorded; its historical origin requires a separate inquiry.

Study of N Ḥ L

Taken together, the witnesses establish returning, visiting, and repeated journeys within the root’s range. Every form should not be reduced to one physical act, such as visiting water or bees returning to a hive.

3. Inābah: the prominent Qur’anic meaning

Qur’anic anāba and munīb concern turning toward The God.

Anāba is a Form IV verb, and munīb is its active participle. Its verbal noun is inābah, familiar in Arabic, but the verbal noun itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

أَنَابَ إِلَى اللَّهِ (anāba ilā allāhi) is explained lexically through returning to The God, turning to Him, returning to obedience, and repentance.

However, the verses show that Qur’anic inābah extends beyond returning after a particular sin.

Turning to The God and trusting Him

Shuʿayb says in Hūd 11:88:

وَمَا تَوْفِيقِي إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ

wa-mā tawfīqī illā bi-allāhi ʿalayhi tawakkaltu wa-ilayhi unību

“My success is only through The God. In Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I turn.”

أُنِيبُ (unību) occurs alongside تَوَكَّلْتُ (tawakkaltu).

Trust and inābah are related without being identical. Trust concerns reliance and confidence; inābah concerns turning and returning.

The same combination appears in Al-Shūrā 42:10:

ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ رَبِّي عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ

dhālikumu allāhu rabbī ʿalayhi tawakkaltu wa-ilayhi unību

“That is The God, my Lord. In Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I turn.”

Neither passage mentions a particular sin alongside unību. It therefore cannot necessarily be translated as “I repent of my sin” in these contexts.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:4 also says:

رَبَّنَا عَلَيْكَ تَوَكَّلْنَا وَإِلَيْكَ أَنَبْنَا وَإِلَيْكَ الْمَصِيرُ

rabbanā ʿalayka tawakkalnā wa-ilayka anabnā wa-ilayka al-maṣīru

“Our Lord, in You we have placed our trust, to You we have turned, and to You is the final return.”

Inābah here accompanies trust and final return. However, maṣīr concerns the final destination and return; it should not be reduced to the same meaning as anabnā.

The three expressions describe distinct relationships: trust in The God, turning to Him, and the final destination with Him.

Inābah as a description of Abraham

Hūd 11:75 says:

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُنِيبٌ

inna ibrāhīma la-ḥalīmun awwāhun munībun

“Abraham was indeed forbearing, much given to sighing, and turning back.”

Munīb describes Abraham, rather than reporting a particular sin from which he has just returned.

In context, it describes part of his character and orientation.

Ḥalīm, awwāh, and munīb are distinct descriptions. They should neither be made synonyms nor should munīb’s meaning be extracted from awwāh.

The active participle munīb can describe a recognizable trait, but the pattern مُفْعِل (mufʿil) alone does not establish returning at every moment or a particular number of times.

The inābah of David and Solomon

Ṣād 38:24 says of David:

فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ

fa-istaghfara rabbahu wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāba

“He sought his Lord’s forgiveness, fell down bowing, and turned back.”

Inābah follows seeking forgiveness here. The sequence places the expressions in a related context.

Seeking forgiveness is asking to be forgiven; inābah concerns returning and turning. In this setting it can include returning from an error, but the expressions are not completely identical.

Ṣād 38:34 says of Solomon:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ

wa-laqad fatannā sulaymāna wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan thumma anāba

“We certainly tested Solomon and cast a body upon his throne; then he turned back.”

The verse describes Solomon’s test and return. Anāba itself does not identify a particular sin, the body’s nature, or the details of the test.

Inābah can therefore follow a test, lapse, or difficult situation without every use necessarily presupposing a particular earlier sin.

4. Inābah, guidance, and human orientation

Guidance and returning to The God

Al-Raʿd 13:27 says:

قُلْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُضِلُّ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي إِلَيْهِ مَنْ أَنَابَ

qul inna allāha yuḍillu man yashāʾu wa-yahdī ilayhi man anāba

“Say: The God lets go astray whomever He wills and guides toward Himself whoever turns back.”

Al-Shūrā 42:13 also says:

اللَّهُ يَجْتَبِي إِلَيْهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي إِلَيْهِ مَنْ يُنِيبُ

allāhu yajtabī ilayhi man yashāʾu wa-yahdī ilayhi man yunību

“The God chooses for Himself whomever He wills and guides toward Himself whoever turns.”

Both passages connect inābah with guidance.

The person turning is described as receiving guidance. Inābah is therefore not solely a movement after a journey is complete; it can be part of a person’s relationship with finding and accepting guidance.

However, these passages do not establish an absolute chronological rule under which inābah must precede every kind of guidance. They address the relationship, rather than every possible stage of guidance.

Following the path of someone who has turned

Luqmān 31:15 says:

وَاتَّبِعْ سَبِيلَ مَنْ أَنَابَ إِلَيَّ

wa-ittabiʿ sabīla man anāba ilayya

“Follow the path of whoever has turned to Me.”

The context concerns a person’s relationship with their parents, even when the parents press them to associate partners.

The same verse forbids obeying them in that matter, commands good companionship with them, and recommends following the path of someone who has turned to The God.

Anāba here describes more than an inward feeling. The return marks a person’s path and orientation, to the point that following their way is discussed.

However, anāba itself does not mean following. That belongs to اتَّبِعْ (ittabiʿ). Their relationship emerges from the complete construction.

Inābah and religious life

Al-Rūm 30:31 says:

مُنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ وَاتَّقُوهُ وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَلَا تَكُونُوا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

munībīna ilayhi wa-ittaqūhu wa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-lā takūnū mina al-mushrikīna

“Turning toward Him, be mindful of Him, establish ṣalāh, and do not be among those who associate partners.”

This follows the command to turn toward upright religion and the divine fiṭrah in Al-Rūm 30:30.

مُنِيبِينَ (munībīn) is an accusative circumstantial expression and a sound masculine plural describing the addressees’ state. Commands to mindfulness, establishing ṣalāh, and avoiding association follow.

Inābah in this context relates to religious orientation and conduct, beyond a moment of remorse.

Al-Zumar 39:17 also says:

وَالَّذِينَ اجْتَنَبُوا الطَّاغُوتَ أَنْ يَعْبُدُوهَا وَأَنَابُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ لَهُمُ الْبُشْرَىٰ

wa-alladhīna ijtanabū al-ṭāghūta an yaʿbudūhā wa-anābū ilā allāhi lahumu al-bushrā

“Those who avoided worshipping false powers and turned to The God have good news.”

Avoiding false powers and turning to The God appear together. They share an orientation without sharing one lexical meaning.

Al-Zumar 39:54 commands:

وَأَنِيبُوا إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَسْلِمُوا لَهُ

wa-anībū ilā rabbikum wa-aslimū lahu

“Turn back to your Lord and submit to Him.”

Inābah and submission again appear together. Turning to The God is connected with accepting His command, but anībū and aslimū are different verbs, each retaining its own meaning.

Inābah and receiving signs

Sabaʾ 34:9 says:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُنِيبٍ

inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-kulli ʿabdin munībin

“In that there is indeed a sign for every servant who turns.”

Ghāfir 40:13 also says:

وَمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ إِلَّا مَنْ يُنِيبُ

wa-mā yatadhakkaru illā man yunību

“Only whoever turns takes heed.”

And Qāf 50:8:

تَبْصِرَةً وَذِكْرَىٰ لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُنِيبٍ

tabṣiratan wa-dhikrā li-kulli ʿabdin munībin

“As insight and a reminder for every servant who turns.”

These passages connect inābah with attention to signs, insight, and remembrance.

Sabaʾ and Qāf concern signs in creation. Their value as reminders is nevertheless related to the recipient’s state and orientation.

Munīb need not describe someone who has just abandoned a particular sin. It can describe someone oriented and attentive toward The God, taking heed of the signs.

A heart that turns

A particularly clear use appears in Qāf 50:33:

مَنْ خَشِيَ الرَّحْمَٰنَ بِالْغَيْبِ وَجَاءَ بِقَلْبٍ مُنِيبٍ

man khashiya al-raḥmāna bi-al-ghaybi wa-jāʾa bi-qalbin munībin

“Who feared the Entirely Merciful in the unseen and came with a heart that turns.”

Munīb here describes qalb, the heart.

The point matters because Qur’anic inābah is not confined to outward movement or bodily relocation. A heart can also be described this way.

Qalbin munīb can be rendered “a heart that turns” or “a heart returned.” However, munīb itself should not be equated with a pure, sound, or sinless heart. Each expression requires a separate study.

The preceding verse, Qāf 50:32, has لِكُلِّ أَوَّابٍ حَفِيظٍ (li-kulli awwābin ḥafīẓ). This proximity matters for comparing inābah and awbah below.

5. Turning in distress: a limit on the definition

Does munīb always imply a lasting, sincere, and praiseworthy return?

Two Qur’anic passages prevent such a generalization.

Al-Rūm 30:33

وَإِذَا مَسَّ النَّاسَ ضُرٌّ دَعَوْا رَبَّهُمْ مُنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَا أَذَاقَهُمْ مِنْهُ رَحْمَةً إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ بِرَبِّهِمْ يُشْرِكُونَ

wa-idhā massa al-nāsa ḍurrun daʿaw rabbahum munībīna ilayhi thumma idhā adhāqahum minhu raḥmatan idhā farīqun minhum bi-rabbihim yushrikūna

“When harm touches people, they call upon their Lord, turning toward Him. Then, when He lets them taste mercy from Him, some of them suddenly associate partners with their Lord.”

The Qur’an itself uses munībīn to describe turning in distress.

It then says that some of those people return to association after receiving mercy.

Munībīn at the beginning therefore cannot alone mean people whose return is guaranteed to remain permanent.

Al-Zumar 39:8

وَإِذَا مَسَّ الْإِنْسَانَ ضُرٌّ دَعَا رَبَّهُ مُنِيبًا إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَا خَوَّلَهُ نِعْمَةً مِنْهُ نَسِيَ مَا كَانَ يَدْعُو إِلَيْهِ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَجَعَلَ لِلَّهِ أَنْدَادًا

wa-idhā massa al-insāna ḍurrun daʿā rabbahu munīban ilayhi thumma idhā khawwalahu niʿmatan minhu nasiya mā kāna yadʿū ilayhi min qablu wa-jaʿala li-allāhi andādan

“When harm touches a person, he calls upon his Lord, turning toward Him. Then, when He grants him a favor, he forgets what he had called upon Him for before and sets up equals with The God.”

Munīban ilayhi again describes a person’s state in difficulty, followed by forgetting and setting up equals.

The two witnesses distinguish inābah itself from remaining steadfast in it.

Someone may truly turn toward The God in one situation, then change orientation when circumstances change.

This does not make inābah worthless. It means lasting commitment, permanent sincerity, and immunity from returning to error should not be extracted from the word alone.

Conversely, when “a heart that turns” or “a servant who turns” appears in praise, the state belongs within a set of favorable qualities.

The complete assessment comes from context. Inābah denotes turning and returning throughout these examples, while its outcome and duration differ.

6. Distinctions from related roots

N W B (nawb) and T W B (tawb): inābah and repentance

Inābah and tawbah are closely related in many religious uses. Both can concern a person returning to The God.

However, distinct roots and Qur’anic uses prevent making them complete synonyms.

Human tawbah in the Qur’an often concerns returning from sin, error, and disobedience. T W B is also used of The God, as in فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ (fa-tāba ʿalayhi) in Al-Baqarah 2:37. He is not repenting of sin; divine acceptance and turning toward the person are involved.

Qur’anic inābah is not confined to abandoning sin. It can describe a person’s orientation in life, turning in supplication, accepting guidance, or the heart’s attention.

In Ṣād 38:24, David’s seeking forgiveness accompanies inābah. In Al-Zumar 39:54, inābah accompanies submission. The expressions can complement each other without being identical.

Nevertheless, tawbah should not be made exclusively the abandonment of sin, nor inābah invariably a higher stage. Such gradations occur in some ethical and mystical writings, but the roots and full Qur’anic usage do not establish an exceptionless rule.

Study of T W B

N W B (nawb) and ʾ W B (awb): inābah and awbah

ʾ W B likewise concerns returning, but is an independent root.

The Qur’an uses forms including أَوَّاب (awwāb), مَآب (maʾāb), إِيَاب (iyāb), and أَوِّبِي (awwibī).

Qāf 50:32 and Qāf 50:33 place forms from the two roots alongside one another:

هَٰذَا مَا تُوعَدُونَ لِكُلِّ أَوَّابٍ حَفِيظٍ
مَنْ خَشِيَ الرَّحْمَٰنَ بِالْغَيْبِ وَجَاءَ بِقَلْبٍ مُنِيبٍ

hādhā mā tūʿadūna li-kulli awwābin ḥafīẓin / man khashiya al-raḥmāna bi-al-ghaybi wa-jāʾa bi-qalbin munībin

“This is what you were promised, for every frequent returner and keeper: who feared the Entirely Merciful in the unseen and came with a heart that turns.”

Awwāb follows the pattern فَعَّال (faʿʿāl), which, together with its use, can convey frequent or habitual returning.

Munīb is a Form IV active participle, describing the heart’s orientation here.

Their proximity shows related semantic fields. It does not establish that one must be the first stage and the other the final stage of a spiritual journey.

Awwāb in this surah must also be distinguished from أَوَّاه (awwāh), describing Abraham in Hūd 11:75. Their structures and roots differ.

Study of ʾ W B

N W B (nawb) and R J ʿ (rujūʿ): general and directed return

رَجَعَ (rajaʿa) covers a broad range of return, from physically returning to a place to reversing a decision or state.

N W B can likewise concern returning, but its early witnesses emphasize visiting, coming back, and sometimes taking turns.

Qur’anic anāba places returning within a person’s orientation toward The God.

“Return” is therefore correct for inābah, while “turn toward” may be more natural in some verses, particularly concerning the heart, guidance, or continued orientation.

The difference is a contextual tendency, rather than an absolute rule about the roots.

Study of R J ʿ

N W B (nawb) and N Y B (nayb)

Arabic nāb can belong to two different families.

نَابَ يَنُوبُ (nāba yanūbu) belongs to N W B in returning, substitution, or a turn arriving.

نَاب (nāb), a canine tooth or fang, belongs to N Y B.

Their identical written shape should not merge a canine tooth with representation and inābah in one root definition.

7. Proposed translation for Forqan

“Return” and “turn toward” are the two main suitable equivalents for the Qur’anic forms.

In وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ (wa-ilayhi unību) and وَإِلَيْكَ أَنَبْنَا (wa-ilayka anabnā), “to Him I turn” and “to You we have turned” preserve the direct sense.

Munībīna ilayhi and munīban ilayhi, particularly in supplication during distress, can be rendered “turning toward Him” or “one turning toward Him.”

ʿAbdin munīb can be rendered “a servant who turns”; qalbin munīb, “a heart that turns” or “a heart returned.”

Anāba in the accounts of David and Solomon can be rendered “turned back,” with context explaining the return after a test or seeking forgiveness.

However, munīb should not invariably become “penitent,” which confines the reader to returning from sin and does not cover all Qur’anic uses.

Nor should it be replaced with “always obedient,” “entirely pure,” “one whose repentance is accepted,” or “someone who will never sin again.” Munīb’s structure alone does not establish these qualities.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N W B concerns returning, visiting, repeated journeys, and taking turns. Related uses include turns, alternation, representation, and an event befalling someone.

One lexical analysis identifies returning to a familiar place and revisiting it as the core. Early witnesses, particularly visits to water, support this sense, without proving that every derivative historically arose from one physical image.

The Qur’an uses only the anāba and munīb groups, focusing on returning and turning toward The God.

Across the verses, inābah can follow an error or test without being confined to that situation. It also describes prophets, following guidance, the heart’s turning, and attention to divine signs.

Al-Rūm 30:33 and Al-Zumar 39:8 are especially significant limits on the definition. They show turning in distress without guaranteeing its continuation. Lasting commitment and long-term sincerity should therefore not enter the lexical definition without evidence.

A final definition for Forqan is:

N W B: returning and visiting a familiar destination, sometimes with repeated visits or taking turns. Qur’anic anāba means returning and turning toward The God. Munīb describes a person or heart turned toward Him; persistence is understood from context and subsequent conduct, rather than the word alone.
