  <section class="article-section" id="introduction">
    <h2>Introduction</h2>
    <p>In the common image often presented of Islam, religion is usually summarized in a few well-known acts: testimony, prayer, fasting, zakat, and Hajj. These acts have their place. But if our understanding of religion stops there, a major part of Qur’anic ethics disappears from view.</p>
    <p>One of these forgotten pillars is covenant.</p>
    <p>It may be said that after the pillar of tawhid and the pillar of freedom, fulfillment of covenant and solemn pledge is one of the most foundational Qur’anic pillars. In terms of ultimate truth, tawhid is the foundation of religion. But in terms of how faith forms inside the human being, freedom is a necessary condition. Faith is not created in an atmosphere of compulsion and coercion. Faith is born in a space of freedom, choice, will, knowledge, and recognition.</p>
    <p>The Qur’an itself makes this principle clear: there is no compulsion in religion. If the human being is not free to hear, think, choose, worship, or even experience the wrong path, then his faith is not real faith. Freedom of belief, freedom of proclamation, freedom of worship, and freedom of life are the field in which faith and tawhid can form within the human being.</p>
    <p>But once knowledge has been reached, faith has been accepted, and the human being has become responsible before The God and His creation, the next pillar is fulfillment of commitment and covenant. The human being has a covenant with The God, and he also has covenants with the creation of The God. Other pillars such as salat, zakat, fasting, charity, kindness to parents, family formation, people’s rights, social justice, and trustworthiness all gain meaning under the canopy of this covenant with The God.</p>
    <p>The Qur’an repeatedly speaks of covenant, solemn pledge, promise, faith, and contract. These words all belong to one moral field: the human being’s loyalty to the responsibility he has accepted. The Qur’an does not treat fulfillment of covenant as a small secondary virtue. It places covenant beside faith, taqwa, justice, truthfulness, and tawhid. On the other side, it describes breaking covenant as one of the clear marks of fusuq, corruption, and departure from the divine path.</p>
    <p>So covenant in the Qur’an is not merely a simple promise between two people. Covenant is rooted in fitrah, appears in faith, gains meaning in social justice, and will be questioned on the Day of Account.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="fitri-covenant">
    <h2>1. The primordial covenant: the beginning of human responsibility</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an speaks of a covenant that comes before every social, legal, or religious contract: a covenant connected to the human being’s original relation with his Lord.</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَإِذْ أَخَذَ رَبُّكَ مِن بَنِي آدَمَ مِن ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا بَلَىٰ شَهِدْنَا</p>
      <p class="translation">And when your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their backs, their descendants, and made them witness over themselves: Am I not your Lord? They said: Yes, we bear witness.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/007-al-araf/172/">Al-A‘raf 7:172</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This is one of the Qur’an’s deepest verses. We should not rush to make absolute claims about its unseen details. But at the very least, it shows one clear truth: the relation between the human being and The God is not merely a historical teaching or a family inheritance. It has a fitri and existential root.</p>
    <p>Deep within the human being, there is witness to the Lordship of the Lord. Tawhid in the Qur’an is not only a mental belief; it is loyalty to a truth for which the human being is inwardly responsible. From here, covenant is not first an external contract. It is an inner responsibility.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="believer-identity">
    <h2>2. Covenant and the identity of the believer</h2>
    <p>When the Qur’an speaks of successful believers, it places fulfillment of covenant beside prayer, chastity, trustworthiness, and humility:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ لِأَمَانَاتِهِمْ وَعَهْدِهِمْ رَاعُونَ</p>
      <p class="translation">And those who carefully guard their trusts and their covenant.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/023-al-muminun/008/">Al-Mu’minun 23:8</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse appears among the qualities of those about whom the Qur’an says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">قَدْ أَفْلَحَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ</p>
      <p class="translation">The believers have truly succeeded.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/023-al-muminun/001/">Al-Mu’minun 23:1</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>So guarding covenant is part of the Qur’anic definition of falah, success. A believer in the Qur’an is not merely someone who verbally claims faith or externally belongs to a religious identity. A believer is one who guards trust and covenant.</p>
    <p>The same description is repeated in Surah Al-Ma‘arij:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ لِأَمَانَاتِهِمْ وَعَهْدِهِمْ رَاعُونَ</p>
      <p class="translation">And those who carefully guard their trusts and their covenant.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/070-al-maarij/032/">Al-Ma‘arij 70:32</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This repetition shows that covenant is not marginal in the Qur’an. The Qur’an places it twice within the structure of defining the believer and the people of salvation.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="fusuq">
    <h2>3. Breaking covenant and the reality of fusuq</h2>
    <p>Opposite the believer, the Qur’an speaks of the fasiq. One of the first qualities it mentions for the fasiqun is breaking covenant:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">الَّذِينَ يَنقُضُونَ عَهْدَ اللَّهِ مِن بَعْدِ مِيثَاقِهِ وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَن يُوصَلَ وَيُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ</p>
      <p class="translation">Those who break the covenant of The God after its solemn binding, and cut what The God commanded to be joined, and cause corruption on the earth. Those are the losers.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/027/">Al-Baqarah 2:27</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>In its root sense, fusuq means going out from the proper boundary or shell. When the Qur’an makes covenant-breaking one of the marks of the fasiq, it means that covenant is a kind of protective boundary for human ethical life. A society in which covenants are broken loses trust, cuts bonds, and produces corruption on the earth.</p>
    <p>For this reason, the Qur’an does not allow anyone to treat covenant-breaking as small while maintaining an appearance of religiosity. Breaking a covenant is not merely a private mistake. It is departure from the moral order The God has placed for human life.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="terms">
    <h2>4. Ahd, mithaq, and ‘aqd</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an uses several words to describe human commitment. These words are close to one another, but they are not identical.</p>
    <p><span class="term">Ahd</span> has the broadest meaning. It can include any promise, covenant, commitment, or responsibility, whether between the human being and The God or between human beings.</p>
    <p><span class="term">Mithaq</span> is a firmer and more emphasized covenant. It is a pledge with greater weight, often accompanied by emphasis, binding, or witness.</p>
    <p><span class="term">‘Aqd</span> comes from the idea of tying or knotting. When two parties bind their wills to one another, an ‘aqd comes into existence. From this root come social, commercial, family, and legal contracts.</p>
    <p>Through these words, the Qur’an shows that covenant-keeping is not merely private ethics. It begins inside the human being and extends into the social and legal structures of human life.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="scope">
    <h2>5. The scope of covenant: from personal promise to social contract</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an commands directly:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَأَوْفُوا بِالْعَهْدِ ۖ إِنَّ الْعَهْدَ كَانَ مَسْئُولًا</p>
      <p class="translation">Fulfill the covenant; surely the covenant will be questioned.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/017-al-isra/034/">Al-Isra 17:34</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse is very important. The Qur’an does not say covenant is optional or ceremonial. It says covenant is responsible; the human being will be questioned concerning it. Covenant therefore stands independently in the field of divine accounting, alongside acts of worship.</p>
    <p>Surah Al-Ma’idah also opens with a very broad command:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَوْفُوا بِالْعُقُودِ</p>
      <p class="translation">O you who believe, fulfill the contracts.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/005-al-maidah/001/">Al-Ma’idah 5:1</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>It is not accidental that an important Madinan surah opens with the command to fulfill contracts. A society collapses without loyalty to contracts. Worship accompanied by covenant-breaking cannot build a just and secure society.</p>
    <p>The Qur’an also says, in its definition of righteousness:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا</p>
      <p class="translation">And those who fulfill their covenant when they make a covenant.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/177/">Al-Baqarah 2:177</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse comes in the same passage where the Qur’an says righteousness is not merely turning one’s face east or west, but faith, giving, prayer, zakat, patience, and fulfillment of covenant together form the reality of righteousness.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="beyond-religion">
    <h2>6. Covenant beyond religious boundaries</h2>
    <p>One of the clearest signs of the Qur’an’s moral greatness is that it does not limit covenant-keeping to members of one’s own religion. Even regarding mushrikin who had made covenants with the believers, the Qur’an commands:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">إِلَّا الَّذِينَ عَاهَدتُّم مِّنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَنقُصُوكُمْ شَيْئًا وَلَمْ يُظَاهِرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ أَحَدًا فَأَتِمُّوا إِلَيْهِمْ عَهْدَهُمْ إِلَىٰ مُدَّتِهِمْ</p>
      <p class="translation">Except those of the mushrikin with whom you made a covenant, then they did not reduce anything from it and did not support anyone against you; fulfill their covenant to them until their term.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/009-at-tawbah/004/">At-Tawbah 9:4</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse is explicit. The religion of the other party is not permission to break covenant. If the other party stands by the covenant, the believer must also stand by it.</p>
    <p>The same surah says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">فَمَا اسْتَقَامُوا لَكُمْ فَاسْتَقِيمُوا لَهُمْ</p>
      <p class="translation">So as long as they remain upright toward you, remain upright toward them.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/009-at-tawbah/007/">At-Tawbah 9:7</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This means covenant-keeping in the Qur’an does not arise from fear or weakness. It arises from principle. The believer keeps covenant, not because the other party shares his religion, but because covenant-keeping itself is a divine criterion.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="power">
    <h2>7. Power, deception, and covenant-breaking</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an knows well that covenant-breaking often begins when one side sees itself as stronger. In Surah An-Nahl, it says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَلَا تَكُونُوا كَالَّتِي نَقَضَتْ غَزْلَهَا مِن بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ أَنكَاثًا تَتَّخِذُونَ أَيْمَانَكُمْ دَخَلًا بَيْنَكُمْ أَن تَكُونَ أُمَّةٌ هِيَ أَرْبَىٰ مِنْ أُمَّةٍ</p>
      <p class="translation">Do not be like the woman who untwisted her yarn after it had become strong, taking your oaths as a means of deception between you because one community is greater than another.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/016-an-nahl/092/">An-Nahl 16:92</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse is socially profound. The Qur’an says covenant-breaking often happens when one group sees itself as greater, more numerous, more powerful, or more profitable than another. In such a condition, covenant becomes a tool of deception.</p>
    <p>The verse’s image is precise. One who makes a covenant is like someone who twists yarn with effort until it becomes strong. But when he breaks that covenant, he is like someone who undoes that same yarn with his own hands. A covenant-breaking society is the same: it weaves the foundation of its own trust, then tears it apart itself.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="taqwa">
    <h2>8. Covenant and taqwa</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an also connects fulfillment of covenant to taqwa:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">بَلَىٰ مَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِعَهْدِهِ وَاتَّقَىٰ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَّقِينَ</p>
      <p class="translation">Yes, whoever fulfills his covenant and has taqwa, then The God loves the people of taqwa.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/003-ali-imran/076/">Ali ‘Imran 3:76</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse shows that covenant-keeping is not merely legal order. It is a sign of taqwa. Taqwa means that the human being guards himself before truth: from betrayal, deception, injustice, and abuse of power. The one who breaks covenant shows, in that moment, that something has collapsed in his moral guarding.</p>
    <p>In Surah Ar-Ra‘d, the Qur’an describes the people of understanding in this way:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">الَّذِينَ يُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَنقُضُونَ الْمِيثَاقَ</p>
      <p class="translation">Those who fulfill the covenant of The God and do not break the solemn pledge.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/013-ar-rad/020/">Ar-Ra‘d 13:20</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>And opposite them, it describes another group:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَالَّذِينَ يَنقُضُونَ عَهْدَ اللَّهِ مِن بَعْدِ مِيثَاقِهِ وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَن يُوصَلَ وَيُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۙ أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمُ اللَّعْنَةُ وَلَهُمْ سُوءُ الدَّارِ</p>
      <p class="translation">Those who break the covenant of The God after its solemn binding, and cut what The God commanded to be joined, and cause corruption on the earth; for them is distance and deprivation, and for them is the evil abode.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/013-ar-rad/025/">Ar-Ra‘d 13:25</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>These two verses place two paths before each other: fulfilling covenant and preserving bonds, or breaking covenant and spreading corruption.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="imamah">
    <h2>9. Covenant and just leadership</h2>
    <p>One of the key Qur’anic verses about covenant appears in the story of Ibrahim:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَإِذِ ابْتَلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ رَبُّهُ بِكَلِمَاتٍ فَأَتَمَّهُنَّ ۖ قَالَ إِنِّي جَاعِلُكَ لِلنَّاسِ إِمَامًا ۖ قَالَ وَمِن ذُرِّيَّتِي ۖ قَالَ لَا يَنَالُ عَهْدِي الظَّالِمِينَ</p>
      <p class="translation">And when Ibrahim’s Lord tested him with words, and he completed them, He said: I am making you an imam for the people. He said: And from my descendants? He said: My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/124/">Al-Baqarah 2:124</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>In this verse, leadership and imamah over people are called the covenant of The God. But this covenant does not reach wrongdoers. In the Qur’anic logic, religious or social leadership cannot be joined with injustice. Neither lineage, claim, power, nor religious clothing can purify injustice.</p>
    <p>If someone is unjust, he is deprived of the divine covenant, even if he presents himself as close to religion.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="truthful-believers">
    <h2>10. True believers and fulfillment of covenant</h2>
    <p>In Surah Al-Ahzab, the Qur’an speaks of believers who were truthful to their covenant with The God in the moment of hardship:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">مِّنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ رِجَالٌ صَدَقُوا مَا عَاهَدُوا اللَّهَ عَلَيْهِ ۖ فَمِنْهُم مَّن قَضَىٰ نَحْبَهُ وَمِنْهُم مَّن يَنتَظِرُ ۖ وَمَا بَدَّلُوا تَبْدِيلًا</p>
      <p class="translation">Among the believers are men who were truthful to what they covenanted with The God. Some of them fulfilled their vow, and some still wait; and they did not change in any way.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/033-al-ahzab/023/">Al-Ahzab 33:23</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>In this verse, faith is recognized through truthfulness in covenant. The true believer is one who does not change his covenant when pressure comes. Covenant shows its value when the human being stands between benefit and loyalty.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="theology">
    <h2>11. The theology of covenant: The God who is faithful to His promise</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an does not only command the human being to fulfill covenant. It presents faithfulness to promise as one of the acts of The God:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَأَوْفُوا بِعَهْدِي أُوفِ بِعَهْدِكُمْ</p>
      <p class="translation">Fulfill My covenant, and I will fulfill your covenant.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/040/">Al-Baqarah 2:40</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This verse expresses the relation between the human being and The God in deep language. The human being is responsible before The God, and The God does not leave His promise empty or faithless.</p>
    <p>The Qur’an repeatedly says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَعْدَ اللَّهِ ۖ لَا يُخْلِفُ اللَّهُ وَعْدَهُ</p>
      <p class="translation">It is the promise of The God; The God does not fail His promise.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/030-ar-rum/006/">Ar-Rum 30:6</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُخْلِفُ الْمِيعَادَ</p>
      <p class="translation">Indeed, The God does not fail the appointed promise.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/003-ali-imran/009/">Ali ‘Imran 3:9</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This repetition shows that faithfulness, in the Qur’an, is not only a human duty. It is a reflection of a divine act. The God calls His servants to something He possesses in the most perfect form: truthfulness, fulfillment, justice, and no failure of promise.</p>
    <p>In Surah Al-Fath, He also says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَمَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِمَا عَاهَدَ عَلَيْهُ اللَّهَ فَسَيُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا</p>
      <p class="translation">Whoever fulfills what he covenanted with The God, He will give him a great reward.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/048-al-fath/010/">Al-Fath 48:10</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>Covenant in the Qur’an is therefore a living relation between the human being and his Lord. The human being fulfills, and The God does not abandon the promised reward.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section conclusion" id="conclusion">
    <h2>Conclusion</h2>
    <p>In the Qur’an, covenant is not a marginal concept. Covenant is one of the pillars of divine ethics: a pillar that begins in fitrah, gains meaning in faith, appears in social justice, and is questioned on the Day of Account.</p>
    <p>Covenant in the Qur’an has several layers:</p>
    <div class="meaning-map" role="list">
      <p role="listitem"><strong>First:</strong> the primordial covenant, the inner witness of the human being to the Lordship of his Lord.</p>
      <p role="listitem"><strong>Second:</strong> covenant with The God, meaning loyalty to truth, faith, worship, taqwa, and responsibility before the Lord.</p>
      <p role="listitem"><strong>Third:</strong> covenant with human beings, including every promise, contract, trust, and commitment between individuals and groups.</p>
      <p role="listitem"><strong>Fourth:</strong> social contracts, the legal and civic structures upon which collective life is built.</p>
      <p role="listitem"><strong>Fifth:</strong> divine promise and appointed promise, the truth that The God describes Himself in the Qur’an as faithful, truthful in promise, and never failing the appointed time.</p>
    </div>
    <p>From here, it becomes clear that covenant-breaking in the Qur’an is not a small moral weakness. It breaks trust, cuts bonds, and opens the path of corruption on the earth. Conversely, fulfillment of covenant is one of the clearest signs of faith, taqwa, and truthfulness.</p>
    <p class="closing">Religion separated from covenant becomes a soulless appearance. But when faith is joined with fulfillment of covenant, the human being approaches the ethic that the Qur’an names among the signs of successful believers.</p>
  </section>
