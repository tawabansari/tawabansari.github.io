By name of The God The Merciful The Compassionate

Qur’an Terminology

Laʿn in the Qur’an: What Exclusion from Divine Mercy Means

The word “curse” readily brings to mind an angry outburst or a wish that someone else should suffer. A person, furious with another, calls upon a higher power to bring harm upon them. If we carry that understanding into the Qur’an, God’s curse upon a creature may at first appear to be a similar reaction of anger and ill will.

Yet laʿn, the Qur’anic term commonly translated as “curse,” is not confined to an imprecation or a wish for another’s misfortune. When God pronounces laʿn upon someone, the text describes a real condition: through the course they pursue and the choices they make, people become estranged from mercy, guidance, and the path of return to God. The Qur’an attributes this estrangement to God, just as it attributes guidance and misguidance to Him within the patterns and laws He has established in creation and in human life.

The Arabic root l–ʿ–n carries the sense of driving away or removing. When the Qur’an uses it of God’s action, its meaning extends beyond a spoken curse toward exclusion from divine mercy: a condition in which a person’s conduct and chosen course take them farther from God’s guidance and mercy.

To understand this more clearly, we must consider how the Qur’an elsewhere attributes the consequences of human choices to God. Guidance and misguidance offer a telling example. The Qur’an identifies God as the One who guides and allows people to go astray, while also treating deviation, arrogance, defiance, turning back to Him, and accepting the truth as consequential human acts. That connection can help us understand laʿn more precisely.

Divine Ways in Guidance and Misguidance

Some Qur’anic passages attribute guidance and misguidance directly to God’s will:

وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَٰكِنْ يُضِلُّ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَلَتُسْأَلُنَّ عَمَّا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

“Had The God willed, He would have made you one community. But He leads astray whom He wills and guides whom He wills, and you will most certainly be questioned about what you used to do.”

An-Nahl 16:93

Read apart from the rest of the verse, “He leads astray whom He wills and guides whom He wills” might suggest that guidance and misguidance bear no relation to human conduct. Yet the verse ends by affirming human responsibility: “You will most certainly be questioned about what you used to do.” Divine will and human accountability stand together in the same verse.

Other passages make the relationship clearer. Concerning the people of Moses, we read:

وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ يَا قَوْمِ لِمَ تُؤْذُونَنِي وَقَدْ تَعْلَمُونَ أَنِّي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ إِلَيْكُمْ ۖ فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا أَزَاغَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ ۚ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

“And when Moses said to his people, ‘My people, why do you hurt me when you know that I am The God’s messenger to you?’ Then, when they deviated, The God caused their hearts to deviate. And The God does not guide the defiantly disobedient people.”

As-Saff 61:5

The sequence is revealing: “When they deviated, The God caused their hearts to deviate.” The turning of their hearts is not presented without a preceding cause. They knew, yet chose the course of harm and deviation. Conduct that began as a choice gradually becomes an inward condition, making it harder to continue seeing and accepting the truth.

The same relationship works in the other direction:

وَيَقُولُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَوْلَا أُنْزِلَ عَلَيْهِ آيَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ ۗ قُلْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُضِلُّ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَهْدِي إِلَيْهِ مَنْ أَنَابَ

“Those who have disbelieved say, ‘Why has no sign been sent down to him from his Lord?’ Say, ‘The God leads astray whom He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns back to Him.’”

Ar-Ra'd 13:27

Here, guidance is connected with inabah: returning and turning toward God. Someone who turns toward Him enters a path on which further guidance becomes possible. The Qur’an also describes this increase explicitly:

وَالَّذِينَ اهْتَدَوْا زَادَهُمْ هُدًى وَآتَاهُمْ تَقْوَاهُمْ

“And those who have found guidance—He increases them in guidance and grants them their God-consciousness.”

Muhammad 47:17

Guidance, in this account, is not a fixed point. It can grow. Misguidance can deepen as well. A person who repeatedly encounters a truth and turns away through arrogance, self-interest, prejudice, or rebellion does not remain unchanged. Those choices shape how that person will subsequently see, hear, and judge. Conversely, someone who accepts the truth and turns toward it becomes more receptive to further guidance.

The Qur’an does not detach these patterns from God. He is the Creator of human beings, their capacities, and the order within which choices have consequences:

إِنَّ هَٰذِهِ تَذْكِرَةٌ ۖ فَمَنْ شَاءَ اتَّخَذَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِ سَبِيلًا ﴿٢٩﴾

وَمَا تَشَاءُونَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا ﴿٣٠﴾

يُدْخِلُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ فِي رَحْمَتِهِ ۚ وَالظَّالِمِينَ أَعَدَّ لَهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا ﴿٣١﴾

“This is a reminder. So whoever wills may take a path to his Lord. Yet you do not will unless The God wills. Surely The God is Knowing, Wise. He admits whom He wills into His mercy; and for the wrongdoers He has prepared a painful punishment.”

Al-Insan 76:29Al-Insan 76:30Al-Insan 76:31

Human beings choose a path, but they do not live outside God’s order. Nor does God present His will as disconnected from His knowledge and wisdom. For this reason, “divine ways” or “divinely established patterns” may express the idea more accurately than “laws of nature.” The subject extends beyond the material world to patterns operating in ethics, understanding, willing, and the human relationship with truth.

This background matters for understanding laʿn. Just as the Qur’an can attribute to God a deviation that human beings themselves initiated, exclusion from mercy can be the consequence of a path they have chosen to follow. God creates and governs the order within which that consequence takes shape.

L–ʿ–N: Exclusion and Distance

Classical Arabic dictionaries explain the core meaning of l–ʿ–n through removal, expulsion, and deprivation of good. The familiar definition al-ibʿad wa-l-tard min al-khayr expresses this succinctly: distancing and expulsion from good.

This range of meaning was known before the Qur’an. Accounts of pre-Islamic Arabic record abayta al-laʿn as a form of address to kings. It meant more than simply “may you not be cursed” in the everyday modern sense. The expression conveyed the addressee’s distance from conduct that would warrant rejection and condemnation.

A poem attributed to al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani contains these lines:

أَتَانِي أَبَيْتَ اللَّعْنَ أَنَّكَ لُمْتَنِي

وَتِلْكَ الَّتِي أَهْتَمُّ مِنْهَا وَأَنْصَبُ

“Word has reached me—may you be far from what deserves a curse—that you have reproached me; and that is what leaves me troubled and weary.”

The expression shows that laʿn was not merely a term of abuse or a wish for harm. It already carried the ideas of distance, exclusion, and being placed outside a desirable sphere.

An earlier and more direct witness also survives. A funerary inscription from Hegra, dated to 267 CE, calls upon the deity named in it to “curse” anyone who violates the tomb. Such evidence places the word in the region’s religious language several centuries before the Qur’an. A similar form also occurs in Nabataean Aramaic, although linguistic scholarship has raised the possibility of Arabic influence. This evidence therefore cannot confidently establish an independent Aramaic root alongside the Arabic one.

For the present reading of the Qur’an, that is enough: before the Qur’an, laʿn was already a word whose core involved exclusion and distancing. We must now consider how the Qur’an employs that core within its own network of meanings.

When the Qur’an Speaks of Laʿn

One of the clearest examples appears in Al-Baqarah:

وَقَالُوا قُلُوبُنَا غُلْفٌ ۚ بَلْ لَعَنَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِكُفْرِهِمْ فَقَلِيلًا مَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

“They say, ‘Our hearts are covered.’ Rather, The God has excluded them from His mercy because of their disbelief. How little they believe.”

Al-Baqarah 2:88

The verse does not simply state that God has pronounced laʿn upon them. It also identifies the cause: bi-kufrihim—“because of their disbelief.” This exclusion has a context. They say that their hearts are covered, and the Qur’an connects their condition with their own choice and disbelief.

Al-Ma’idah makes the relationship clearer:

فَبِمَا نَقْضِهِمْ مِيثَاقَهُمْ لَعَنَّاهُمْ وَجَعَلْنَا قُلُوبَهُمْ قَاسِيَةً ۖ يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ وَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ ۚ وَلَا تَزَالُ تَطَّلِعُ عَلَىٰ خَائِنَةٍ مِنْهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ ۖ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاصْفَحْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

“So, because they broke their covenant, We excluded them from Our mercy and made their hearts hard. They distort words from their proper places and have forgotten a portion of what they were reminded of. You will continue to discover treachery from them, except for a few. So pardon them and overlook their wrongdoing. Surely The God loves those who do good.”

Al-Ma'idah 5:13

Again, the cause precedes the consequence: fa-bi-ma naqdihim mithaqahum—“because they broke their covenant.” Two conditions then appear together: laʿannahum, “We excluded them from Our mercy,” and jaʿalna qulubahum qasiyah, “We made their hearts hard.” The Qur’an speaks of exclusion and hardness of heart in the same breath, immediately describing the conduct accompanying that condition: displacing words from their proper setting, forgetting part of the reminder, and betrayal.

This picture accords with the divine patterns we have seen in guidance and misguidance. Knowingly breaking a covenant is more than an outward act. It can bring a person to a condition in which the heart grows harder and guidance becomes more distant.

Surah Muhammad draws the connection even more clearly:

فَهَلْ عَسَيْتُمْ إِنْ تَوَلَّيْتُمْ أَنْ تُفْسِدُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَتُقَطِّعُوا أَرْحَامَكُمْ ﴿٢٢﴾

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ لَعَنَهُمُ اللَّهُ فَأَصَمَّهُمْ وَأَعْمَىٰ أَبْصَارَهُمْ ﴿٢٣﴾

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ أَمْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَا ﴿٢٤﴾

“If you turn away, might you then spread corruption in the land and sever your ties of kinship? Those are the ones The God has excluded from His mercy, making them deaf and blinding their sight. Do they not reflect upon the Qur’an, or are there locks upon their hearts?”

Muhammad 47:22Muhammad 47:23Muhammad 47:24

The passage begins with corruption and the severing of kinship, moves to laʿn, deafness, and blindness, and ends with the failure to reflect and the locks upon hearts. The continuation itself shows that physical ears and eyes alone are not the point. It describes people whose capacity to hear and see the truth has been damaged, and whose hearts have closed themselves against the Qur’an.

Here it becomes difficult to understand laʿn as merely a spoken curse. The Qur’an places it within an actual sequence of conduct and consequences: corruption, broken bonds, exclusion from mercy, impaired receptivity, and a locked heart.

Nor is this relationship limited to the realm of understanding. An-Nisa says of intentional killing:

وَمَنْ يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ خَالِدًا فِيهَا وَغَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا

“Whoever deliberately kills a believer, his recompense is Hell, abiding therein. The God is angry with him, excludes him from His mercy, and has prepared for him a tremendous punishment.”

An-Nisa 4:93

Here laʿn appears alongside divine anger and punishment. Exclusion from mercy therefore means more than an increasing difficulty in recognizing truth during earthly life. It can also extend to deprivation of mercy at the final judgment and to consequences in the Hereafter.

The central meaning remains distance and exclusion. The context of each verse clarifies the form and severity of that exclusion.

Exclusion Does Not Make Return Impossible

If laʿn were simply a curse pronounced independently of human conduct, we might expect no possibility of return once someone came under it. Yet the Qur’an introduces repentance and reform in the very passage where it speaks of laʿn:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَكْتُمُونَ مَا أَنْزَلْنَا مِنَ الْبَيِّنَاتِ وَالْهُدَىٰ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا بَيَّنَّاهُ لِلنَّاسِ فِي الْكِتَابِ ۙ أُولَٰئِكَ يَلْعَنُهُمُ اللَّهُ وَيَلْعَنُهُمُ اللَّاعِنُونَ ﴿١٥٩﴾

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ تَابُوا وَأَصْلَحُوا وَبَيَّنُوا فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَأَنَا التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ ﴿١٦٠﴾

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَمَاتُوا وَهُمْ كُفَّارٌ أُولَٰئِكَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَعْنَةُ اللَّهِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالنَّاسِ أَجْمَعِينَ ﴿١٦١﴾

خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا ۖ لَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابُ وَلَا هُمْ يُنْظَرُونَ ﴿١٦٢﴾

“Those who conceal the clear signs and guidance We have sent down, after We have made them clear to people in the Scripture—The God excludes them from His mercy, and those who curse also curse them. Except those who repent, make amends, and make the truth clear: to them I turn in acceptance, and I am the Accepter of Repentance, the Merciful. Those who disbelieve and die while they are disbelievers—upon them rests the curse of The God, the angels, and all people together. They abide therein; their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be granted respite.”

Al-Baqarah 2:159Al-Baqarah 2:160Al-Baqarah 2:161Al-Baqarah 2:162

The order of these verses is illuminating. People conceal truth and guidance and consequently come under laʿn. But the way back opens immediately: they must repent, make amends, and disclose what they had concealed.

Making amends here involves more than feeling remorse. The particular wrong must also be repaired. Whoever concealed the truth must make it clear. A change of course changes the previous relationship, and God Himself says, atubu ʿalayhim: “I turn toward them and accept their repentance.”

The next two verses establish a further boundary: those who remain in disbelief and die in that condition. The subject is now the persistence of that state to the end of life and its consequences in the Hereafter.

Seen in this light, laʿn cannot be treated as a fixed stamp that, merely by being applied, closes every route back for a living person. In the passages concerning human beings, conduct can distance someone from mercy, and genuine repentance and reform can change that course.

This feature brings the meaning of laʿn closer to the divine patterns of guidance and misguidance: the path people take helps determine what subsequently opens or closes to them.

Can Every Use of Laʿn Be Explained as an Inner Process?

No. The core meaning of the root must be distinguished from the different ways it appears in context. The Qur’an also uses the expression al-shajarah al-malʿunah, “the cursed tree”:

وَإِذْ قُلْنَا لَكَ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ أَحَاطَ بِالنَّاسِ ۚ وَمَا جَعَلْنَا الرُّؤْيَا الَّتِي أَرَيْنَاكَ إِلَّا فِتْنَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَالشَّجَرَةَ الْمَلْعُونَةَ فِي الْقُرْآنِ ۚ وَنُخَوِّفُهُمْ فَمَا يَزِيدُهُمْ إِلَّا طُغْيَانًا كَبِيرًا

“And when We told you, ‘Your Lord encompasses all people.’ We made the vision We showed you only a trial for people, and likewise the cursed tree in the Qur’an. We warn them, but it only increases them in great rebellion.”

Al-Isra 17:60

A tree does not make moral choices, so we cannot say that it has turned away from guidance through arrogance or concealment. This usage reminds us not to reduce the root’s entire meaning to one particular psychological process. Exclusion, distance, and being outside the sphere of goodness and mercy form a broader core. When human beings are the object of laʿn, however, the Qur’an repeatedly shows how that exclusion is connected with conduct, hardness of heart, diminished guidance, and final judgment.

Each verse must therefore be read in its own setting. The root supplies the broad direction of meaning; context shows the particular form that distance takes in a given passage.

Suffering Is Not Proof of Exclusion from Mercy

Even if laʿn describes a real condition, we cannot identify every unhappy event as evidence of it. The Qur’an itself calls both adversity and prosperity means of testing:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ ۗ وَنَبْلُوكُمْ بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً ۖ وَإِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ

“Every soul shall taste death. We test you with both evil and good as a trial, and to Us you will be returned.”

Al-Anbiya 21:35

Poverty, illness, failure, or an accident does not by itself show that someone has been excluded from God’s mercy. Neither do wealth, power, and comfort prove that a person has drawn nearer to God. In the passages examined here, laʿn is connected with particular forms of conduct and particular conditions, rather than every experience of suffering.

Someone may face the harshest circumstances and still be moving along a path of guidance and mercy. Another may enjoy every comfort while becoming more distant from truth through daily choices. The Qur’an’s criterion cannot be read off the outward circumstances of people’s lives.

Laʿn: Exclusion from Mercy, Beyond a Spoken Curse

Taken together, these passages offer a more coherent picture of laʿn in the Qur’an.

The root l–ʿ–n denotes exclusion and distancing. When the Qur’an applies it to human beings, it repeatedly identifies the cause within the verse or its immediate context: disbelief, covenant-breaking, concealing guidance, corruption, severing bonds, aggression, and intentional killing. In some passages this exclusion accompanies a hardening heart and a diminished capacity to see and hear truth; in others, it extends to divine anger and punishment.

This relationship accords with the Qur’an’s account of guidance and misguidance. People choose a path, and that path in turn changes them. Deviation can produce further deviation; guidance can lead to greater guidance. God attributes these consequences to Himself because human beings live within the order He has created, whose governing patterns also come from Him.

From this perspective, “God’s curse” cannot be understood merely as speaking an imprecation or wishing harm upon a creature. Laʿn is exclusion from divine mercy and entry into a course that distances a person from guidance and return to God. This exclusion can become visible in the present life as hardness of heart and a closing of receptivity to truth. If someone persists in that course to the end, its consequences can extend into the Hereafter.

At the same time, the Qur’an does not close the way back while repentance and reform remain possible. The very person whose conduct has created distance from mercy can take a different path through repentance, making amends, and sincerely turning back.

Laʿn, understood this way, is more than an angry word. It is a condition: the distance people create between themselves and divine mercy through the course they choose. God, the Creator of that order and the ways that govern it, attributes this exclusion to Himself.
