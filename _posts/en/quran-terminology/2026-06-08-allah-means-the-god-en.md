<h2 id="intro">Introduction</h2>

<p>Many English-speaking readers first encounter the word “Allah” as if it were the personal name of the Muslim God. This misunderstanding is common, but it is not accurate. Allah is not a tribal name, not an ethnic name, and not a private name belonging only to Muslims. It is not a proper name like Zeus, Odin, Jupiter, or even a human personal name. It is an Arabic expression meaning <strong>The God</strong>: the One, the Absolute, the only reality worthy of worship.</p>

<p>This distinction matters.</p>

<p>When people hear “Allah” as a proper name, they may imagine that Muslims worship a different deity from the God of Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. But the Qur’an is not introducing a new god with a new private name. It is restoring the call to <strong>The God</strong>: the same One God known through the line of revelation, but purified from idolatry, confusion, and human-made partners.</p>

<h2 id="not-proper-name">Allah Is Not a Proper Name</h2>

<p>The first point must be made clearly: <strong>Allah is not a proper name.</strong></p>

<p>It should not be treated as an untranslated label, as if the sound itself were the whole meaning. The word has a structure, and that structure carries theology. When the Qur’an says Allah, it is not asking the human being to accept the name of a foreign deity. It is calling humanity back to <strong>The God</strong> above all false gods, false authorities, idols, intermediaries, and imagined powers.</p>

<h2 id="al-ilah">Al-Ilah: The God</h2>

<p>The word Allah comes from a clear Arabic structure.</p>

<p><strong>Ilah</strong> means god, deity, or object of worship.</p>

<p><strong>Al</strong> means the.</p>

<p>So <strong>Al-Ilah</strong> means <strong>The God</strong>, or <strong>The One worthy of worship</strong>.</p>

<p>Through Arabic usage, <strong>Al-Ilah</strong> becomes <strong>Allah</strong>.</p>

<p>This is the key point:</p>

<p><strong>Allah should not be treated as an untranslated proper name. Its meaning should be understood and, when needed, translated as The God.</strong></p>

<p>The = Al.</p>

<p>God = Ilah.</p>

<p>Allah = Al-Ilah = The God.</p>

<p>This is not a small linguistic issue. It changes how the reader understands the Qur’an. The Qur’an is not asking humanity to accept the private name of a specifically Arab or Muslim deity. It is calling humanity back to The God above all invented gods, all false powers, and all claims of ultimate authority.</p>

<h2 id="la-ilaha-illa-allah">La Ilaha Illa Allah</h2>

<p>This is why the central Qur’anic declaration begins with negation:</p>

<blockquote class="quran-quote" dir="ltr" lang="en">
  La ilaha illa Allah.
</blockquote>

<p>There is no ilah except Allah.</p>

<p>Meaning: there is no god, no deity, no object of worship, no ultimate authority worthy of devotion except The God.</p>

<p>This declaration is the heart of <strong>tawhid</strong> (the recognition of divine oneness). Tawhid does not simply mean believing that a supreme being exists. It means recognizing that worship, final authority, judgment, devotion, and ultimate trust belong to The God alone.</p>

<p>This is also why translating Allah as <strong>The God</strong> can help English readers. In English, “God” often functions like a proper name because it is capitalized. But the Qur’anic Arabic is more precise. Allah carries definiteness and exclusivity. It does not mean “a god.” It means <strong>The God</strong>.</p>

<h2 id="abrahamic-language">Allah and the Abrahamic Languages</h2>

<p>The same idea appears in other ancient religious languages. In Hebrew, <strong>El</strong>, <strong>Eloah</strong>, and <strong>Elohim</strong> are used in relation to God. In Aramaic, the language connected to Jesus and his environment, <strong>Alaha</strong> is used for God. These words are not identical, but they belong to a wider Semitic family of sacred language. They show that the Qur’anic Allah is not isolated from earlier revelation. It belongs to the same Abrahamic current that called humanity toward The One.</p>

<p>This is why it is misleading to treat Allah as the name of a specifically Arab or Muslim deity. The Qur’an itself speaks to Jews, Christians, and all people of faith by calling them back to the same foundation: The God who created, guides, judges, forgives, and sustains.</p>

<h2 id="hyper-theos">A Helpful Comparison: Hyper-Theos</h2>

<p>A useful comparison can be made with the Greek theological expression <strong>Hyper-Theos</strong>. <strong>Theos</strong> means God. <strong>Hyper</strong> means beyond, above, or transcending. So Hyper-Theos points to God beyond our ordinary concept of god. It tries to protect the mind from reducing God to something human, limited, or imaginable.</p>

<p>In a similar way, Allah is not merely a sound. It is a theological structure. <strong>Al-Ilah</strong> means <strong>The God</strong>, not one god among many. It lifts the meaning out of the category of ordinary deities and points to the One beyond comparison. It is not “a divine being.” It is The Divine Reality. It is not one member of a class. It is the One before whom the whole class of false gods collapses.</p>

<h2 id="tanzih">Tanzih: Beyond Comparison</h2>

<p>This is why the Qur’an repeatedly protects the mind from <strong>tashbih</strong> (likening God to creation). It says that nothing is like Him. It says that no one is equal to Him. These verses are not abstract slogans. They are safeguards against making The God into a larger version of ourselves.</p>

<p>
  The Qur’an says:
  <a href="{{ '/quran-reflection/en/042-ash-shuraa/011/' | relative_url }}" style="color: inherit; text-decoration: none;">“There is nothing like Him”</a>.
  It also says:
  <a href="{{ '/quran-reflection/en/112-al-ikhlas/004/' | relative_url }}" style="color: inherit; text-decoration: none;">“Nor is there to Him any equivalent”</a>.
</p>

<p>When we say “merciful,” we usually think of human mercy. When we say “compassionate,” we imagine human compassion. But divine mercy is not human kindness multiplied. The mercy of The God is not limited by emotion, weakness, fear, mood, fatigue, or need. His compassion is not a reaction like ours. It is a reality rooted in His own perfection.</p>

<h2 id="forqan-opening">Why Forqan Uses “The God”</h2>

<p>For this reason, in the Forqan project, the English opening is written as:</p>

<p class="opening">By Name of The God The Merciful The Compassionate</p>

<p>This wording is intentional.</p>

<p><strong>The God</strong> translates Allah as meaning, not merely as sound.</p>

<p><strong>The Merciful</strong> points to <strong>Al-Rahman</strong> (The Merciful, whose mercy encompasses creation).</p>

<p><strong>The Compassionate</strong> points to <strong>Al-Rahim</strong> (The Compassionate, whose mercy is intimate, continuous, and sustaining).</p>

<p>These are not casual adjectives. They are divine names. They point toward realities greater than human language can fully contain.</p>

<p>The Qur’an itself opens the door beyond linguistic rigidity:</p>

<blockquote class="quran-quote" dir="ltr" lang="en">
  <a href="{{ '/quran-reflection/en/017-al-isra/110/' | relative_url }}" style="color: inherit; text-decoration: none;">
    Say, “Call upon Allah or call upon Al-Rahman. Whichever you call upon, to Him belong the Most Beautiful Names.”
  </a>
</blockquote>

<p class="verse-reference">
  <a href="{{ '/quran-reflection/en/017-al-isra/110/' | relative_url }}" style="color: inherit; text-decoration: none;">
    Surah Al-Isra, verse 110
  </a>
</p>

<p>This verse is powerful because it breaks the prison of one sound. The issue is not whether the tongue says Allah, The God, El, Eloah, or Alaha. The issue is whether the heart is calling upon the One to whom the Most Beautiful Names belong.</p>

<h2 id="not-linguistic-tribalism">Not Linguistic Tribalism</h2>

<p>The Qur’an does not teach linguistic tribalism. It teaches <strong>tawhid</strong> (the recognition of divine oneness). It does not imprison The God inside Arabic as an ethnic possession. Rather, it uses Arabic revelation to call all humanity back to the One who is beyond every language, while allowing each language to point toward Him according to its capacity.</p>

<p>This distinction is urgently needed today. Some Muslims treat the word Allah as if it must never be translated, almost as if the sound itself were the whole truth. Some non-Muslims hear Allah and assume it means a foreign god. Both misunderstandings come from treating Allah as a proper name.</p>

<p><strong>But Allah is not a proper name.</strong></p>

<p>Allah means The God.</p>

<p>The God of Abraham.</p>

<p>The God of Moses.</p>

<p>The God of Jesus.</p>

<p>The God of Muhammad.</p>

<p>The God who created the heavens and the earth.</p>

<p>The God who cannot be compared to creation.</p>

<p>The God to whom belong the Most Beautiful Names.</p>

<p>The God who cannot be owned by one tribe, one sect, one language, or one religious institution.</p>

<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>

<p>Once this is understood, the word Allah becomes less foreign, not more. It becomes a bridge, not a wall. It reminds us that the Qur’an is not calling humanity to worship an Arab deity, but to return to The God above all idols, all intermediaries, all invented partners, and all false claims of divine authority.</p>

<p>The beauty of the word Allah is that it carries both nearness and transcendence. He is close enough to be called upon, remembered, trusted, and loved. Yet He is exalted enough that no image, doctrine, institution, language, or imagination can contain Him.</p>

<p>So when the Qur’an says Allah, it is not asking the human being to memorize a foreign label. It is inviting the human being to recognize The God: the One who was never absent, the One who cannot be replaced, the One whose mercy and compassion exceed the limits of human language.</p>

<p>He is not a god among gods.</p>

<p>He is not the private deity of one nation.</p>

<p>He is not the property of one religion.</p>

<p>He is not trapped inside one sound.</p>

<p><strong>He is The God.</strong></p>

<p>And to Him belong the Most Beautiful Names.</p>
