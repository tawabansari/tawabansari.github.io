By name of The God The Merciful The Compassionate

Qur’an Terminology

Gabriel and the Spirit in the Qur’an

Across much of the Muslim exegetical tradition, interpreters confronted with gaps in their knowledge—or with matters whose details the Qur’an leaves unstated—have turned to transmitted reports, Isra’iliyyat, and accounts outside the Qur’an to fill those gaps. Over time, some of these explanations have become so closely associated with the text that an ordinary reader can struggle to distinguish what the Qur’an says from what has been said about it. We examine this problem at length in “When Qur’anic Silence Is Filled with Reports”.

Gabriel’s identity and nature are a case in point. In conventional Islamic writing, he is almost invariably introduced as “the angel of revelation.” The phrase has become so familiar that few readers stop to ask: in which verse does the Qur’an itself call Gabriel a malak, an angel, or identify him as one of the mala’ikah, the angels?

Accepting that definition at the outset can quietly determine how the verses are read. Gabriel is first assumed to be an angel; the Trustworthy Spirit and the Holy Spirit are then treated as other titles for that angel; and each subsequent occurrence of the word “spirit” is drawn into the same framework.

Beginning with the Qur’an itself leads us along a different path. We must first ask how it names Gabriel, what actions it attributes to him, how it places him alongside the angels, and what connections emerge between him and the word rūḥ, “spirit,” in passages concerning revelation. Where the text stops short of further explanation, we should not fill its silence with a definition of our own.

How Does the Qur’an Introduce Gabriel?

Gabriel is named in three places in the Qur’an. In none of those three passages does it call him a malak.

Al-Baqarah says:

قُلْ مَنْ كَانَ عَدُوًّا لِجِبْرِيلَ فَإِنَّهُ نَزَّلَهُ عَلَىٰ قَلْبِكَ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ وَهُدًى وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“Say: Whoever is an enemy to Gabriel—he has indeed brought it down upon your heart by The God’s permission, confirming what came before it, and as guidance and good news for the believers.”

Al-Baqarah 2:97

Here the Qur’an describes what Gabriel does, rather than what he is. He has brought something down upon the Prophet’s heart by The God’s permission.

The following verse says:

مَنْ كَانَ عَدُوًّا لِلَّهِ وَمَلَائِكَتِهِ وَرُسُلِهِ وَجِبْرِيلَ وَمِيكَالَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ عَدُوٌّ لِلْكَافِرِينَ

“Whoever is an enemy to The God, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, and Michael—The God is indeed an enemy to the disbelievers.”

Al-Baqarah 2:98

In this verse, “angels,” “messengers,” “Gabriel,” and “Michael” each appear under their own designation. The Qur’an does not subsume Gabriel and Michael under the general heading of angels; it names them separately.

The same pattern appears in Al-Tahrim:

إِنْ تَتُوبَا إِلَى اللَّهِ فَقَدْ صَغَتْ قُلُوبُكُمَا وَإِنْ تَظَاهَرَا عَلَيْهِ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ مَوْلَاهُ وَجِبْرِيلُ وَصَالِحُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ ظَهِيرٌ

“If you two turn in repentance to The God—for your hearts have inclined—and if you support one another against him, then The God is his protector, as are Gabriel and the righteous among the believers; and the angels, beyond that, are a support.”

At-Tahrim 66:4

Once again, Gabriel is named separately, with “the angels” mentioned afterward. The verse also shows that Gabriel’s presence in the Qur’an is not confined to conveying revelation: here he appears in support of the Prophet, although the angels are also described as supporters in the same verse.

Does naming him separately suffice to prove that Gabriel cannot be one of the angels? By itself, it does not.

The Qur’an says:

اللَّهُ يَصْطَفِي مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا وَمِنَ النَّاسِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ

“The God chooses messengers from among the angels and from among human beings. The God is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”

Al-Hajj 22:75

A crucial distinction is needed here: rasūl, “messenger,” designates a mission, not a created nature. The Qur’an describes messengers chosen from among angels and from among human beings. Their shared title does not make their natures identical.

The Qur’an also says of Moses:

وَاذْكُرْ فِي الْكِتَابِ مُوسَىٰ إِنَّهُ كَانَ مُخْلَصًا وَكَانَ رَسُولًا نَبِيًّا

“And remember Moses in the Book. He was indeed chosen, and he was a messenger and a prophet.”

Maryam 19:51

A prophet, then, can be a messenger; a human being can be a messenger; an angel can be a messenger; and, as we shall see in the account of Mary, the “spirit” can also introduce himself as a messenger of the Lord. Rasūl tells us about a commission, not the kind of being or created nature of the one entrusted with it. A shared function does not establish a shared essence.

This matters because several beings may be involved in conveying a message, descending, or carrying out a divine command. Their participation in a common task does not establish that they are identical in nature.

The significance of Gabriel’s separate designation becomes clearer when these verses are read alongside the passages concerning the spirit and descent.

Gabriel, the Trustworthy Spirit, and the Holy Spirit

Al-Baqarah 2:97 states that Gabriel brought it down upon the Prophet’s heart by The God’s permission.

Al-Shu‘ara describes the same field of descent:

وَإِنَّهُ لَتَنْزِيلُ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ ﴿١٩٢﴾

نَزَلَ بِهِ الرُّوحُ الْأَمِينُ ﴿١٩٣﴾

عَلَىٰ قَلْبِكَ لِتَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُنْذِرِينَ ﴿١٩٤﴾

بِلِسَانٍ عَرَبِيٍّ مُبِينٍ ﴿١٩٥﴾

“And it is indeed a sending down from the Lord of all worlds. The Trustworthy Spirit came down with it upon your heart, so that you might be among those who warn, in a clear Arabic tongue.”

Ash-Shu'ara 26:192 Ash-Shu'ara 26:193 Ash-Shu'ara 26:194 Ash-Shu'ara 26:195

The distinction between the verbs must be preserved. Al-Baqarah says of Gabriel, “نَزَّلَهُ”—he brought it down. Al-Shu‘ara says “نَزَلَ بِهِ”: the Trustworthy Spirit came down with it.

Nevertheless, the circumstances are closely aligned: something descends from the Lord, an intermediary is involved, and the destination is the heart of the same Prophet.

Al-Nahl introduces another title into this setting:

وَإِذَا بَدَّلْنَا آيَةً مَكَانَ آيَةٍ وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يُنَزِّلُ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا أَنْتَ مُفْتَرٍ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ ﴿١٠١﴾

قُلْ نَزَّلَهُ رُوحُ الْقُدُسِ مِنْ رَبِّكَ بِالْحَقِّ لِيُثَبِّتَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَهُدًى وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُسْلِمِينَ ﴿١٠٢﴾

“And when We replace one sign with another—and The God knows best what He sends down—they say, ‘You are only a fabricator.’ Rather, most of them do not know. Say: The Holy Spirit has brought it down from your Lord in truth, to make firm those who believe, and as guidance and good news for those who submit.”

An-Nahl 16:101 An-Nahl 16:102

Within the Qur’an itself, then, three designations occupy closely related contexts.

In Al-Baqarah, Gabriel brings something down upon the Prophet’s heart.

In Al-Shu‘ara, the Trustworthy Spirit comes down with it upon the Prophet’s heart.

In Al-Nahl, the Holy Spirit brings it down from the Lord.

This comparison does not entitle us to say that all three designations necessarily carry one fixed meaning and refer to one and the same being everywhere in the Qur’an. Yet the connection between Gabriel and the language of “spirit” in the scene of descent cannot simply be ignored.

The connection is also noteworthy within Al-Baqarah itself. A few verses before Gabriel is named, we read:

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا مُوسَى الْكِتَابَ وَقَفَّيْنَا مِنْ بَعْدِهِ بِالرُّسُلِ وَآتَيْنَا عِيسَى ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ الْبَيِّنَاتِ وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ أَفَكُلَّمَا جَاءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ بِمَا لَا تَهْوَىٰ أَنْفُسُكُمُ اسْتَكْبَرْتُمْ فَفَرِيقًا كَذَّبْتُمْ وَفَرِيقًا تَقْتُلُونَ

“We gave Moses the Book and sent messengers in succession after him. We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear signs and strengthened him with the Holy Spirit. Whenever a messenger brought you something your selves did not desire, did you grow arrogant—denying some and killing others?”

Al-Baqarah 2:87

A few verses later, within the same address to the Children of Israel, hostility toward Gabriel is mentioned. This proximity does not by itself prove that Gabriel and the Holy Spirit are identical, but it makes the conceptual relationship between the two designations in that part of the surah more striking.

The Spirit and the Angels

If the passages of descent connect Gabriel with the Trustworthy Spirit and the Holy Spirit, the next question is how the Qur’an itself speaks of the relationship between “the spirit” and “the angels.”

Al-Qadr should be read in full:

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَاهُ فِي لَيْلَةِ الْقَدْرِ ﴿١﴾

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا لَيْلَةُ الْقَدْرِ ﴿٢﴾

لَيْلَةُ الْقَدْرِ خَيْرٌ مِنْ أَلْفِ شَهْرٍ ﴿٣﴾

تَنَزَّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ فِيهَا بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِمْ مِنْ كُلِّ أَمْرٍ ﴿٤﴾

سَلَامٌ هِيَ حَتَّىٰ مَطْلَعِ الْفَجْرِ ﴿٥﴾

“We sent it down on the Night of Decree. And what will make you know what the Night of Decree is? The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months. The angels and the spirit descend in it, by their Lord’s permission, for every matter. Peace it is until the rising of dawn.”

Al-Qadr 97:1 Al-Qadr 97:2 Al-Qadr 97:3 Al-Qadr 97:4 Al-Qadr 97:5

Within this surah, the referent of “it” in “أَنْزَلْنَاهُ” is not named. The surah does not say, “We sent down the Qur’an.” Identifying the pronoun with the Qur’an, familiar though that reading is in conventional exegesis, does not come from an explicit statement within this surah. If our method is to avoid filling gaps in the text with reports or assumptions treated as certainties, we should say what the surah says: “We sent it down.”

More importantly, verse 4 does not say that the angels and the spirit bring “it” down. The subject of “أَنْزَلْنَاهُ” in verse 1 is The God; in verse 4, it is the angels and the spirit themselves who descend:

تَنَزَّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ

Al-Qadr 97:4

“The descent of it” and “the descent of the angels and the spirit” are therefore two different statements. One should not be substituted for the other.

Al-Ma‘arij also says:

تَعْرُجُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ إِلَيْهِ فِي يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُ خَمْسِينَ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

“The angels and the spirit ascend to Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years.”

Al-Ma'arij 70:4

And Al-Naba says:

يَوْمَ يَقُومُ الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ صَفًّا لَا يَتَكَلَّمُونَ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَقَالَ صَوَابًا

“On the day when the spirit and the angels stand in a row, they will not speak, except one whom The Merciful permits and who says what is right.”

An-Naba 78:38

In Al-Qadr and Al-Ma‘arij, the order is:

The angels and the spirit.

In Al-Naba, however:

The spirit and the angels.

An interpreter may argue that “الملائكة والروح” is an example of conjoining the specific after the general: the spirit is itself one of the angels, but its importance and standing warrant a separate mention after the general category.

That is a possible rhetorical explanation. The problem begins when a possibility is turned into proof that the spirit is an angel.

For when Al-Naba reverses the order and says:

الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ

An-Naba 78:38

If the response is that this is now conjoining the general after the specific, the argument loses its capacity to be tested. When the angels come first, the explanation is “the specific after the general”; when the spirit comes first, it is “the general after the specific.” Either way, the same predetermined conclusion survives: the spirit must be one of the angels.

Such a method no longer draws its conclusion from the Qur’an’s ordering. Instead, it adapts every ordering to a conclusion already accepted. No possible arrangement of the sentence can then challenge the initial assumption. This is a logical weakness in the argument, not a denial that these rhetorical forms of coordination exist in language.

If “the specific after the general” is to count as evidence that the spirit belongs to the angels, we should be able to conceive of a textual construction that would disprove that assumption. When the opposite ordering leads to the same conclusion through a different rule, the explanation no longer proves anything.

For this study, therefore, neither “the specific after the general” nor “the general after the specific” can determine the nature of the spirit. What we actually have before us is the wording of the text:

The Qur’an says:

الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ

Al-Qadr 97:4

And elsewhere it says:

الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ

An-Naba 78:38

In both arrangements, “the spirit” and “the angels” retain separate designations. That linguistic independence does not by itself prove that their natures must differ. But neither can membership among the angels be established through a rhetorical rule that takes two opposite orderings to the same predetermined conclusion.

Al-Nahl presents another relationship between them:

يُنَزِّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةَ بِالرُّوحِ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ أَنْ أَنْذِرُوا أَنَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنَا فَاتَّقُونِ

“He sends down the angels with the spirit, from His command, upon whomever He wills among His servants: ‘Warn that there is no deity except Me, so be mindful of Me.’”

An-Nahl 16:2

The structure is clear: The God is the subject of “يُنَزِّلُ,” and “الملائكة” is what He sends down. The verse does not say that the angels send something down; it says that the angels themselves are sent down “with the spirit.”

This verse, too, places the spirit and the angels in a relationship without merging them into a single designation.

At a minimum, these passages show that the simultaneous presence of the spirit and the angels is not foreign to the Qur’an’s language. Al-Qadr describes their descent together; Al-Ma‘arij speaks of their ascent; Al-Naba places both in a row; and Al-Nahl speaks of the angels descending “with the spirit.”

A Linguistic Difference: Malak and Mala’ikah, but Rūḥ Is Always Singular

Across the Qur’an, the word for angel appears in both singular and plural forms:

ملك (malak) and ملائكة (mala’ikah).

The Qur’an can speak of one angel or a company of angels. Linguistically, the term is used for multiple members of a category.

Rūḥ does not behave in the same way. We find forms such as:

الروح، روح، روح القدس، الروح الأمين، روحنا، روحي، روحه، روح منه، روحا من أمرنا.

But neither the plural arwāḥ nor a dual form of rūḥ appears in the Qur’an.

This observation should not be taken to mean that only one spirit exists in all existence; the Qur’an makes no such statement. The linguistic difference nevertheless matters: the Qur’an does not present rūḥ as a countable class of beings in the way it presents malak/mala’ikah.

This becomes more significant when the spirit appears separately from the angels in Al-Qadr, Al-Ma‘arij, and Al-Naba, while Al-Nahl 16:2 describes the angels as being sent down “with the spirit.”

The Annunciation to Mary: Plural Angels and a Singular Spirit’s Mission

The account of Mary allows us to examine this difference more closely.

Maryam says:

وَاذْكُرْ فِي الْكِتَابِ مَرْيَمَ إِذِ انْتَبَذَتْ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا مَكَانًا شَرْقِيًّا ﴿١٦﴾

فَاتَّخَذَتْ مِنْ دُونِهِمْ حِجَابًا فَأَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهَا رُوحَنَا فَتَمَثَّلَ لَهَا بَشَرًا سَوِيًّا ﴿١٧﴾

قَالَتْ إِنِّي أَعُوذُ بِالرَّحْمَٰنِ مِنْكَ إِنْ كُنْتَ تَقِيًّا ﴿١٨﴾

قَالَ إِنَّمَا أَنَا رَسُولُ رَبِّكِ لِأَهَبَ لَكِ غُلَامًا زَكِيًّا ﴿١٩﴾

قَالَتْ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِي غُلَامٌ وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِي بَشَرٌ وَلَمْ أَكُ بَغِيًّا ﴿٢٠﴾

قَالَ كَذَٰلِكِ قَالَ رَبُّكِ هُوَ عَلَيَّ هَيِّنٌ وَلِنَجْعَلَهُ آيَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَرَحْمَةً مِنَّا وَكَانَ أَمْرًا مَقْضِيًّا ﴿٢١﴾

“And remember Mary in the Book, when she withdrew from her family to an eastern place. She screened herself from them; then We sent Our spirit to her, and he appeared to her as a fully formed human being. She said, ‘I seek refuge in The Merciful from you, if you are mindful of Him.’ He said, ‘I am only your Lord’s messenger, to grant you a pure son.’ She said, ‘How can I have a son when no human being has touched me and I have not been unchaste?’ He said, ‘So it is. Your Lord has said, “That is easy for Me; and [We do this] to make him a sign for people and a mercy from Us.” It was a matter decreed.’”

Maryam 19:16 Maryam 19:17 Maryam 19:18 Maryam 19:19 Maryam 19:20 Maryam 19:21

Everything referring to the spirit in this account is singular:

رُوحَنَا

Maryam 19:17

Our spirit.

فَتَمَثَّلَ

Maryam 19:17

He appeared.

قَالَ

Maryam 19:19

He said.

Most significantly:

إِنَّمَا أَنَا رَسُولُ رَبِّكِ

Maryam 19:19

I am only your Lord’s messenger.

The spirit does not say, “We are your Lord’s messengers.” A single messenger is speaking, sent on a specific mission.

In this account, the spirit does more than announce general good news: he identifies himself as an envoy sent to carry out a particular task.

Al ‘Imran, meanwhile, presents the annunciation as follows:

إِذْ قَالَتِ الْمَلَائِكَةُ يَا مَرْيَمُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكِ بِكَلِمَةٍ مِنْهُ اسْمُهُ الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ وَجِيهًا فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَمِنَ الْمُقَرَّبِينَ ﴿٤٥﴾

وَيُكَلِّمُ النَّاسَ فِي الْمَهْدِ وَكَهْلًا وَمِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ ﴿٤٦﴾

قَالَتْ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِي وَلَدٌ وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِي بَشَرٌ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكِ اللَّهُ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ إِذَا قَضَىٰ أَمْرًا فَإِنَّمَا يَقُولُ لَهُ كُنْ فَيَكُونُ ﴿٤٧﴾

“When the angels said, ‘Mary, The God gives you good news of a word from Him, whose name is the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, honored in this world and the Hereafter, and among those brought near. He will speak to people in the cradle and in maturity, and will be among the righteous.’ She said, ‘My Lord, how can I have a child when no human being has touched me?’ He said, ‘So it is. The God creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it, “Be,” and it is.’”

Al-Imran 3:45 Al-Imran 3:46 Al-Imran 3:47

Here the term is الملائكة—angels, in the plural. The action explicitly attributed to them is an announcement of good news:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكِ

Al-Imran 3:45

The God gives you good news.

The two accounts need not compete with one another.

Maryam speaks of a singular “spirit” sent to Mary, appearing in human form, identifying himself as “your Lord’s messenger,” and stating his particular mission.

Al ‘Imran speaks of “angels” in the plural, announcing divine good news.

Al-Qadr has already shown that the spirit and the angels can be present together in the same scene:

تَنَزَّلُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَالرُّوحُ فِيهَا

Al-Qadr 97:4

The presence of “angels” in Al ‘Imran’s account therefore does not necessarily establish that “Our spirit” in Maryam is one of those angels. One explanation entirely compatible with the structure of the verses is that the angels were present to announce the good news while the spirit was there as a single messenger to carry out his mission.

This possibility must not replace the verses as an established fact; the Qur’an does not specify the precise sequence of their presence. But the reading at least shows that placing the two accounts together does not make “the spirit = one of the angels” an unavoidable conclusion. On the contrary, grammatical number and the roles assigned to each leave fully open the possibility that two distinct phenomena were present together.

Does Every Occurrence of Rūḥ Mean Gabriel?

No. The verses do not support that conclusion. Imposing it on the Qur’an would repeat the very mistake we set out to avoid.

Rūḥ appears in different constructions in the Qur’an, and these do not all give it the same grammatical role.

In Ash-Shura we read:

وَمَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُكَلِّمَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَّا وَحْيًا أَوْ مِنْ وَرَاءِ حِجَابٍ أَوْ يُرْسِلَ رَسُولًا فَيُوحِيَ بِإِذْنِهِ مَا يَشَاءُ إِنَّهُ عَلِيٌّ حَكِيمٌ ﴿٥١﴾

وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ رُوحًا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا مَا كُنْتَ تَدْرِي مَا الْكِتَابُ وَلَا الْإِيمَانُ وَلَٰكِنْ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُورًا نَهْدِي بِهِ مَنْ نَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا وَإِنَّكَ لَتَهْدِي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ ﴿٥٢﴾

“It is not for a human being that The God should speak to him except by revelation, from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger who reveals, by His permission, whatever He wills. He is Most High, Wise. And thus We revealed to you a spirit from Our command. You did not know what the Book was, nor faith; but We made it a light by which We guide whomever We will among Our servants. And you indeed guide to a straight path.”

Ash-Shura 42:51 Ash-Shura 42:52

In verse 52, rūḥan is something revealed, then described as “light.” We therefore cannot treat every occurrence of rūḥ as the name of a particular personage.

Ghafir also says:

رَفِيعُ الدَّرَجَاتِ ذُو الْعَرْشِ يُلْقِي الرُّوحَ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ لِيُنْذِرَ يَوْمَ التَّلَاقِ

“Exalted in rank, Possessor of the Throne, He casts the spirit from His command upon whomever He wills among His servants, to warn of the Day of Meeting.”

Ghafir 40:15

Again, rūḥ is connected with The God’s “command” and with warning. The verse does not, however, explain that this usage is precisely the same as “the Trustworthy Spirit” or “the Holy Spirit.”

We must therefore distinguish two claims:

First, Gabriel is strongly connected with the designations Trustworthy Spirit and Holy Spirit in the passages of descent.

Second, every occurrence of rūḥ in the Qur’an refers to Gabriel.

Comparison of the verses supports the first claim; the text does not establish the second.

How Much Does the Qur’an Tell Us about the Spirit’s Nature?

Here the Qur’an itself sets an important boundary:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الرُّوحِ قُلِ الرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّي وَمَا أُوتِيتُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

“They ask you about the spirit. Say: The spirit is from my Lord’s command, and you have been given only a little knowledge.”

Al-Isra 17:85

This verse should not be forced into an exclusive reference to Gabriel; it does not name him. Yet when the discussion turns to the spirit’s nature, it establishes a clear boundary for our method.

The Qur’an says that the spirit is from the Lord’s command and that human beings have been given little knowledge.

We are therefore under no obligation to define the spirit’s nature.

This study should not replace the traditional definition with a new philosophical or theological account of the spirit. If the Qur’an itself reminds us of the limits of human knowledge concerning it, fidelity to the text requires us to respect those limits.

For the same reason, claims that the spirit is “a particular substance,” “divine energy,” “the principle of life,” or any other comprehensive definition should not enter the discussion as established truths unless the Qur’an itself establishes them.

The same caution applies to the distinction between “creation” and “command.” The Qur’an says:

إِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ يُغْشِي اللَّيْلَ النَّهَارَ يَطْلُبُهُ حَثِيثًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ وَالنُّجُومَ مُسَخَّرَاتٍ بِأَمْرِهِ أَلَا لَهُ الْخَلْقُ وَالْأَمْرُ تَبَارَكَ اللَّهُ رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

“Your Lord is The God, who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then established Himself upon the Throne. He covers the day with the night, which pursues it swiftly; and the sun, the moon, and the stars are subjected to His command. His are creation and command. Blessed is The God, Lord of all worlds.”

Al-A'raf 7:54

The Qur’an names “creation” and “command” separately, but this verse alone does not furnish a complete philosophical system of two “realms.” What our discussion needs is the clear statement in Al-Isra:

The spirit is from the Lord’s command.

Where the Qur’an has not said more, we need not invent more.

Jesus and the Holy Spirit

The relationship between Jesus and the spirit in the Qur’an extends beyond the account of his birth.

Three verses describe a particular theme concerning him:

Jesus being strengthened with the Holy Spirit.

One is Al-Baqarah 2:87, already quoted. We also read:

تِلْكَ الرُّسُلُ فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ مِنْهُمْ مَنْ كَلَّمَ اللَّهُ وَرَفَعَ بَعْضَهُمْ دَرَجَاتٍ وَآتَيْنَا عِيسَى ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ الْبَيِّنَاتِ وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مَا اقْتَتَلَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْهُمُ الْبَيِّنَاتُ وَلَٰكِنِ اخْتَلَفُوا فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ آمَنَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ كَفَرَ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مَا اقْتَتَلُوا وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يَفْعَلُ مَا يُرِيدُ

“Those messengers—We favored some over others. Among them were those to whom The God spoke, and He raised some in rank. We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear signs and strengthened him with the Holy Spirit. Had The God willed, those who came after them would not have fought one another after clear signs had reached them. But they differed: some believed and some disbelieved. Had The God willed, they would not have fought one another; but The God does what He wills.”

Al-Baqarah 2:253

And in Al-Ma’idah:

إِذْ قَالَ اللَّهُ يَا عِيسَى ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ اذْكُرْ نِعْمَتِي عَلَيْكَ وَعَلَىٰ وَالِدَتِكَ إِذْ أَيَّدْتُكَ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ تُكَلِّمُ النَّاسَ فِي الْمَهْدِ وَكَهْلًا وَإِذْ عَلَّمْتُكَ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحِكْمَةَ وَالتَّوْرَاةَ وَالْإِنْجِيلَ وَإِذْ تَخْلُقُ مِنَ الطِّينِ كَهَيْئَةِ الطَّيْرِ بِإِذْنِي فَتَنْفُخُ فِيهَا فَتَكُونُ طَيْرًا بِإِذْنِي وَتُبْرِئُ الْأَكْمَهَ وَالْأَبْرَصَ بِإِذْنِي وَإِذْ تُخْرِجُ الْمَوْتَىٰ بِإِذْنِي وَإِذْ كَفَفْتُ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ عَنْكَ إِذْ جِئْتَهُمْ بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ فَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْهُمْ إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا سِحْرٌ مُبِينٌ

“When The God said, ‘Jesus, son of Mary, remember My favor upon you and your mother: when I strengthened you with the Holy Spirit, so that you spoke to people in the cradle and in maturity; when I taught you the Book, wisdom, the Torah, and the Gospel; when, by My permission, you fashioned from clay the shape of a bird, then breathed into it and it became a bird by My permission; when you healed those born blind and those with leprosy by My permission; when you brought forth the dead by My permission; and when I restrained the Children of Israel from you as you came to them with clear signs, and those among them who disbelieved said, “This is nothing but obvious magic.”’”

Al-Ma'idah 5:110

Jesus being strengthened with the Holy Spirit is mentioned in three verses. Al-Baqarah 2:87 and 2:253 say “وَأَيَّدْنَاهُ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ”—“We strengthened him with the Holy Spirit”—while Al-Ma’idah 5:110 says “أَيَّدْتُكَ بِرُوحِ الْقُدُسِ”—“I strengthened you with the Holy Spirit.”

Being strengthened with a spirit is not, however, mentioned only in relation to Jesus. The Qur’an also says of a group of believers:

لَا تَجِدُ قَوْمًا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ يُوَادُّونَ مَنْ حَادَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَلَوْ كَانُوا آبَاءَهُمْ أَوْ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ أَوْ إِخْوَانَهُمْ أَوْ عَشِيرَتَهُمْ أُولَٰئِكَ كَتَبَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الْإِيمَانَ وَأَيَّدَهُمْ بِرُوحٍ مِنْهُ وَيُدْخِلُهُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُوا عَنْهُ أُولَٰئِكَ حِزْبُ اللَّهِ أَلَا إِنَّ حِزْبَ اللَّهِ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

“You will not find a people who believe in The God and the Last Day showing affection for those who oppose The God and His messenger, even if they are their fathers, sons, brothers, or relatives. Those are the ones in whose hearts He has inscribed faith and whom He has strengthened with a spirit from Him. He will admit them into gardens beneath which rivers flow, where they will remain. The God is pleased with them, and they are pleased with Him. They are The God’s party. Surely, The God’s party are the successful.”

Al-Mujadilah 58:22

The wording here is “بروح منه”, “with a spirit from Him,” rather than “with the Holy Spirit.”

Being strengthened with a spirit is therefore not exclusive to Jesus. The Qur’an does, however, specifically emphasize in three verses that Jesus was strengthened with the Holy Spirit.

Jesus himself is also described elsewhere as “a spirit from Him”:

يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ وَلَا تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ إِنَّمَا الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ وَكَلِمَتُهُ أَلْقَاهَا إِلَىٰ مَرْيَمَ وَرُوحٌ مِنْهُ فَآمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِ وَلَا تَقُولُوا ثَلَاثَةٌ انْتَهُوا خَيْرًا لَكُمْ إِنَّمَا اللَّهُ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ سُبْحَانَهُ أَنْ يَكُونَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ وَكِيلًا

“People of the Book, do not go to excess in your religion, and say nothing about The God except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, is only The God’s messenger, His word which He cast to Mary, and a spirit from Him. So believe in The God and His messengers, and do not say, ‘Three.’ Desist; it is better for you. The God is only one deity. Exalted is He above having a child. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. The God is sufficient as a trustee.”

An-Nisa 4:171

This verse should not be conflated with “the Holy Spirit” either. One describes Jesus himself as “a spirit from Him”; the others say that Jesus was strengthened “with the Holy Spirit.” Preserving these distinctions is part of reading the Qur’an closely.

Is Gabriel an Angel?

We can now return to the opening question with greater precision.

In none of the three passages that name Gabriel does the Qur’an call him a malak.

Al-Baqarah 2:98 names Gabriel separately from the angels.

Al-Tahrim 66:4 likewise gives Gabriel and the angels separate designations.

In the scene of descent, Al-Baqarah speaks of Gabriel, Al-Shu‘ara of the Trustworthy Spirit, and Al-Nahl of the Holy Spirit. These three designations occupy closely connected contexts of revelation.

In Al-Qadr, Al-Ma‘arij, and Al-Naba, the spirit and the angels are named separately, both as “the angels and the spirit” and as “the spirit and the angels.”

Treating this pattern as “the specific after the general” proves nothing if, when the order is reversed, the same conclusion is preserved by invoking “the general after the specific.” The argument then becomes one that no possible textual construction can disprove. Such a rule may explain a rhetorical possibility, but it cannot by itself determine the spirit’s nature.

In Al-Nahl 16:2, The God sends down the angels “with the spirit” from His command.

In terms of word forms, malak/mala’ikah has both singular and plural forms, whereas rūḥ consistently appears in the singular in the Qur’an.

In the account of Mary, “Our spirit” is a single being, introduces himself as “your Lord’s messenger” in the singular, and has come on a specific mission. Al ‘Imran, meanwhile, speaks of “angels” in the plural, whose explicit role there is to announce good news. Al-Qadr also shows that the simultaneous presence of the spirit and the angels is compatible with the Qur’an’s language.

Nor should “messenger” be confused with a created nature. A human being, a prophet, an angel, and the spirit can bear the title of messenger. Sharing a mission does not establish a shared nature.

Even so, this evidence does not authorize us to invent a nature for the spirit or to equate every use of rūḥ with Gabriel. Ash-Shura 42:52 shows that rūḥ can occupy the place of something revealed, rather than simply the person who conveys revelation.

And when the spirit itself is asked about, the Qur’an offers this answer rather than a detailed classification:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الرُّوحِ قُلِ الرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّي وَمَا أُوتِيتُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

“They ask you about the spirit. Say: The spirit is from my Lord’s command, and you have been given only a little knowledge.”

Al-Isra 17:85

That boundary is enough.

The purpose of this inquiry is not to construct a new theory of Gabriel’s or the spirit’s nature. The Qur’an has not supplied us with such a detailed system.

What we can say with confidence is that the categorical definition “Gabriel = the angel of revelation” is not the Qur’an’s own statement.

The Qur’an names him Gabriel.

It introduces him in connection with descent upon the Prophet’s heart.

Other verses speak of the Trustworthy Spirit and the Holy Spirit in the same context.

It repeatedly places the spirit alongside the angels under a separate designation.

In the account of Mary, different roles appear between the singular messenger “Our spirit” and the plural “angels.”

And regarding the spirit’s nature, it reminds us of the limits of human knowledge.

In these circumstances, anyone who says “Gabriel is an angel” should make clear that this is an interpretation, rather than a statement the Qur’an itself makes.

We should not fill the remaining gap with a new definition in the opposite direction, either. If The God has not unfolded the spirit’s nature for us, we can stop there. If the Qur’an does not call Gabriel a malak, we need not attach that title to his name. And if the relationships among Gabriel, the Trustworthy Spirit, the Holy Spirit, the spirit, and the angels are not explained to us in every detail, preserving that complexity is preferable to dissolving it into a simple, categorical definition.

Fidelity to the Qur’an does not always mean having an answer to every question. Sometimes it means declining to invent an answer the Qur’an has not given.
