  <section class="article-section" id="introduction">
    <h2>Introduction</h2>
    <p>An-Nisa 4:34 is one of the most debated verses in the Qur’an, especially the phrase <span class="term">wa-idribuhunna</span>, often understood in traditional readings as “beat them.” This article attempts to examine the verse from within the Qur’an’s own logic, without denying Arabic usage, without relying on external reports, and without forcing a conclusion prepared in advance.</p>
    <p>It would be incorrect to claim that the Arabic root <span class="term">d-r-b</span> has no physical meaning of striking. That physical meaning is real. But <span class="term">darb</span> in Qur’anic and classical Arabic is not a one-meaning word. It can also carry meanings of making an impact, taking a concrete action, setting something forth, moving away, turning aside, creating distance, changing a situation, or bringing a condition to an end.</p>
    <p>So the central question is not whether “strike” is linguistically possible. The question is: when a word has more than one possible meaning, which reading fits better with the structure of the verse, the Qur’an’s method of legislation, divine justice, divine mercy, and human dignity?</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="ayah">
    <h2>1. The verse and a cautious translation</h2>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">الرِّجَالُ قَوَّامُونَ عَلَى النِّسَاءِ بِمَا فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَبِمَا أَنفَقُوا مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ ۚ فَالصَّالِحَاتُ قَانِتَاتٌ حَافِظَاتٌ لِّلْغَيْبِ بِمَا حَفِظَ اللَّهُ ۚ وَاللَّاتِي تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ فَعِظُوهُنَّ وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ وَاضْرِبُوهُنَّ ۖ فَإِنْ أَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيًّا كَبِيرًا</p>
      <p class="translation">Men are qawwamun over women by what The God has favored some of them over others, and by what they spend from their wealth. Righteous women are devout and preserve in the unseen what The God has preserved. As for those whose nushuz you fear, admonish them, separate from them in the beds, and then take decisive separation / cut off the current state of the relationship. If they return from that state, do not seek a way against them. Indeed, The God is High, Great.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/034/">An-Nisa 4:34</a></p>
    </blockquote>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="root">
    <h2>2. The root darb</h2>
    <p>Before entering the structure of the verse, the word itself must be examined. The Arabic root <span class="term">d-r-b</span> has a physical sense: to strike, hit, or bring one thing into forceful contact with another. This meaning appears in early Arabic usage and should not be denied.</p>

    <blockquote class="language-quote">
      <p class="arabic">تَحِيَّةُ بَيْنِهِمْ ضَرْبٌ وَجِيعُ</p>
      <p class="translation">The greeting between them is a painful strike.</p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>So the physical meaning is a real part of Arabic. But accepting that does not mean it must be the meaning in every Qur’anic use.</p>
    <p>The semantic core of the root can be understood as making an impact, creating contact, setting something upon something, changing a condition, or producing a concrete action. From this core, different meanings emerge.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="semantic-range">
    <h2>3. The semantic range of darb in the Qur’an</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا</p>
      <p class="translation">The God set forth an example.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/014-ibrahim/024/">Ibrahim 14:24</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>There is no physical beating here. The Qur’an also says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَإِذَا ضَرَبْتُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ</p>
      <p class="translation">When you travel through the land.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/101/">An-Nisa 4:101</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This is not physical hitting either, but movement away from a state of staying still. Another verse says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">أَفَنَضْرِبُ عَنكُمُ الذِّكْرَ صَفْحًا</p>
      <p class="translation">Should We turn the Reminder away from you completely?</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/043-az-zukhruf/005/">Az-Zukhruf 43:5</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>Here <span class="term">darb</span> with <span class="term">‘an</span> clearly carries the sense of turning aside or pushing away.</p>
    <p>So the meanings of distancing, setting aside, interrupting a condition, and taking a decisive action are not invented from nothing. They are supported by the semantic field of the root in the Qur’an and Arabic. Precision is still necessary: <span class="term">daraba fi al-ard</span> is a specific expression and cannot simply be transferred into <span class="term">wa-idribuhunna</span>. Likewise, <span class="term">daraba ‘an</span> has the preposition <span class="term">‘an</span>, while An-Nisa 4:34 does not.</p>
    <p>Therefore, the reading of separation or decisive distancing is not proven by grammar alone. But it is supported by the family of meanings around the root and by the context of the verse.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="semitic">
    <h2>4. A comparative note from Semitic languages</h2>
    <p>Related Semitic languages can help us notice older layers of meaning, not as decisive proof, but as comparative evidence. In Biblical Hebrew, a nearby root appears with meanings such as tearing, plucking, separating, or taking away. In the story of Noah, the olive leaf brought by the dove is described with a word connected to the idea of a freshly plucked or torn-off leaf.</p>
    <p>This comparison suggests that in the wider Semitic field, nearby sound and meaning families are not limited to “hitting.” They can also involve decisive impact, plucking away, separating, disrupting a condition, and cutting a connection.</p>
    <p>So when we face two possible readings in Qur’anic Arabic, physical striking or decisive action to interrupt a crisis, Semitic comparison allows us to say that the second reading is not rootless or impossible.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="two-readings">
    <h2>5. Two possible readings of wa-idribuhunna</h2>
    <p>Two main readings of <span class="term">wa-idribuhunna</span> can therefore be considered.</p>
    <p>The first reading, which this article sees as stronger in Qur’anic coherence, is decisive nonphysical action: ending the normal state of the relationship, serious distancing, and moving the crisis toward arbitration.</p>
    <p>The second reading is physical striking. This reading is linguistically possible in Arabic and should not be dismissed by simply denying the language.</p>
    <p>But when two readings are possible, the correct method is not to accept one merely because it became famous in later legal tradition. The reading must be measured by the Qur’an itself: the structure of the verse, the following verse, the Qur’an’s method in punishment and bodily injury, its method in protecting honor, its prohibition of transgression, and the end of the verse, where the man with power is reminded that The God is High and Great.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="qiwamah">
    <h2>6. Qiwamah: responsibility, not domination</h2>
    <p>The word <span class="term">qawwamun</span> comes from the root <span class="term">q-w-m</span>: standing, upholding, rising for something, maintaining, managing, and guarding. Qiwamah is not male kingship over women. It is executive, protective, financial, and stabilizing responsibility. A man is qawwam only insofar as he stands for the preservation of the household: providing, protecting, maintaining justice, and carrying practical responsibility. Qiwamah is not an unrestricted privilege; it is a heavy burden.</p>
    <p>The verse itself does not make qiwamah absolute. It gives two reasons: <span class="term">by what The God has favored some of them over others</span>, and <span class="term">by what they spend from their wealth</span>. The verse does not say that all men are inherently superior to all women. It says some are favored over others, which can refer to capacity, responsibility, position, protection, provision, righteousness, and practical ability.</p>
    <p>Biological and social realities also enter this role. At the level of civilization, hard defense, war, policing, military force, and resisting external aggression have usually fallen more heavily on men. This is not humiliation of women; it is recognition of physical and social realities. But that same meaning works against domestic violence: the protector does not strike the entrusted one. The one assigned to guard should not become the source of fear and harm.</p>
    <p>If the man is qawwam, he is responsible for security, stability, provision, and protection. How then could the same verse give him an unrestricted, unsupervised, undefined power over the woman’s body? That contradicts the very meaning of qiwamah.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="qunut-nushuz">
    <h2>7. Qunut and nushuz</h2>
    <p>The verse then says: <span class="term">fa-as-salihat qanitat</span>. The word <span class="term">qanitat</span> comes from <span class="term">q-n-t</span>. Qunut means lasting devotion, standing in obedience, conscious humility, and submission before The God.</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَقُومُوا لِلَّهِ قَانِتِينَ</p>
      <p class="translation">Stand before The God in qunut.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/238/">Al-Baqarah 2:238</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>So <span class="term">qanitat</span> should not be reduced to “obedient to husbands.” Righteous women are women of qunut: responsible, devoted, and humble before The God.</p>
    <p>The next phrase clarifies this: <span class="term">hafizatun lil-ghayb bima hafiza Allah</span>. They preserve in absence what must be preserved by what The God has preserved: trust, sanctity, covenant, privacy, purity of the relationship, and the rights protected by The God. The center of this morality is The God, not male ownership.</p>
    <p>Therefore <span class="term">nushuz</span> should not be reduced to every disagreement with a husband. If qunut is God-oriented, nushuz should be understood as leaving righteousness, trust, balance, and the moral covenant of the household. It means rising out of proper order in a destructive way, breaking relational balance, or acting harmfully against the family covenant. A woman’s objection, independent judgment, or refusal is not automatically nushuz.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="three-stages">
    <h2>8. The three stages in the verse</h2>
    <p>In response to such a crisis, the verse gives three stages.</p>
    <p><span class="term">Fa-‘izuhunna</span>: first, admonition, ethical conversation, reminder, and an attempt to return the relationship to balance.</p>
    <p><span class="term">Wahjuruhunna fi al-madaji‘</span>: then separation in the beds. This is still inside the home and inside the relationship. The relationship has not fully broken, but marital intimacy is suspended so that the crisis is taken seriously.</p>
    <p><span class="term">Wa-idribuhunna</span>: here two readings become possible. The first is decisive nonphysical action: breaking the normal state of the relationship, stronger distancing, suspending normal cohabitation, and preparing for arbitration. The second is physical striking.</p>
    <p>In the first reading, the difference between the second and third stages is clear. Separation in the bed is limited distancing within intimacy. Darb is a stronger step: leaving the ordinary state of the relationship. It can mean practical distancing, a temporary break in normal shared life, or placing the relationship in a stage where advice and bed-separation are no longer enough and outside arbitration is needed.</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا فَابْعَثُوا حَكَمًا مِّنْ أَهْلِهِ وَحَكَمًا مِّنْ أَهْلِهَا</p>
      <p class="translation">If you fear a split between the two, send an arbiter from his family and an arbiter from her family.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/035/">An-Nisa 4:35</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>This continuation fits a crisis of separation and family rupture better than it fits beating. If the third stage means physical beating, the next verse suddenly moves from domestic violence to family arbitration. But if the third stage is decisive distancing, the next verse is the natural continuation of the same path.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="physical-reading">
    <h2>9. The problem with the physical beating reading</h2>
    <p>If <span class="term">wa-idribuhunna</span> is read as physical beating, the verse is left with serious gaps:</p>
    <div class="meaning-map" role="list">
      <p role="listitem">The instrument is unspecified: hand, stick, cloth, toothbrush?</p>
      <p role="listitem">The intensity is unspecified: light, painful, symbolic, frightening?</p>
      <p role="listitem">The limit of harm is unspecified: bruising, pain, fear, humiliation, injury?</p>
      <p role="listitem">The authority to diagnose nushuz is unspecified: does the husband decide alone?</p>
      <p role="listitem">The woman’s right of defense is unspecified: can she answer? Are witnesses required?</p>
      <p role="listitem">The safeguard against abuse is unspecified: if the man is unjust, angry, corrupt, or power-seeking, what prevents this verse from becoming a tool of domestic violence?</p>
    </div>
    <p>Traditional readings had to bring external restrictions in order to control this danger: “non-severe beating,” “not the face,” “no breaking bones,” “with a tooth-stick.” But the need for these outside restrictions shows that the physical reading remains undefined and dangerous inside the verse itself. If the Qur’an truly meant to permit a sensitive bodily act inside marriage, it would have defined its limits clearly.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="maruf">
    <h2>10. Ma‘ruf as a moral boundary in marriage and separation</h2>
    <p>Another Qur’anic problem for the physical beating reading is the repeated command of <span class="term">ma‘ruf</span> in marital life and separation.</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَعَاشِرُوهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ</p>
      <p class="translation">Live with them in ma‘ruf.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/019/">An-Nisa 4:19</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>And when divorce becomes possible, the Qur’an says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ</p>
      <p class="translation">Retain them with ma‘ruf, or part from them with ma‘ruf.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/065-at-talaq/002/">At-Talaq 65:2</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p><span class="term">Ma‘ruf</span> means recognized goodness, fairness, decency, and kindness. It governs both the continuation of marriage and the ending of marriage. This creates a Qur’anic moral boundary: a woman is not to be kept except with ma‘ruf, and she is not to be separated from except with ma‘ruf.</p>
    <p>If physical beating is placed between these two Qur’anic commands, the meaning becomes strained. The man would be told to live with her in ma‘ruf, then physically strike her in the crisis, then either retain her or separate from her in ma‘ruf. This makes ma‘ruf appear suspended precisely at the moment when the woman is most vulnerable. Such a reading does not fit the Qur’an’s own moral language.</p>
    <p>But if <span class="term">wa-idribuhunna</span> is read as decisive nonphysical separation, the sequence remains coherent: live with them in ma‘ruf; if a serious breach occurs, admonish, separate in bed, then create decisive distance and move toward arbitration; if the marriage continues, retain with ma‘ruf; if it ends, part with ma‘ruf.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="legal-method">
    <h2>11. The Qur’an’s method in bodily harm, honor, and punishment</h2>
    <p>The Qur’an’s method in sensitive matters is not vague. Concerning bodily injury, it says:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَالْجُرُوحَ قِصَاصٌ</p>
      <p class="translation">For wounds there is retribution.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/005-al-maidah/045/">Al-Ma’idah 5:45</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>Bodily harm is not left unaccounted for. If striking a woman can lead to injury, bruising, pain, fear, or humiliation, how could the Qur’an leave such an act without limit or supervision here?</p>
    <p>In matters of sexual accusation and human honor, the Qur’an requires four witnesses. A person’s honor is not destroyed by suspicion, anger, or a one-sided claim.</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">فَاجْلِدُوهُمْ ثَمَانِينَ جَلْدَةً وَلَا تَقْبَلُوا لَهُمْ شَهَادَةً أَبَدًا</p>
      <p class="translation">Flog them with eighty lashes and never accept testimony from them.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/024-an-nur/004/">An-Nur 24:4</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>Even when a husband accuses his wife, the Qur’an does not give him absolute authority. In An-Nur 24:6-9, the process of li‘an appears: sworn testimonies from the man, and then a right of response and defense for the woman. Even in marital conflict, the Qur’an does not accept a man’s claim without structure.</p>
    <p>In theft, although the ruling is severe, the Qur’an mentions the subject, equal application to male and female, the reason for the penalty, and the path of repentance and reform:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">وَالسَّارِقُ وَالسَّارِقَةُ ... جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا ... فَمَن تَابَ مِن بَعْدِ ظُلْمِهِ وَأَصْلَحَ</p>
      <p class="translation">The male thief and the female thief... as recompense for what they earned... whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/005-al-maidah/038/">Al-Ma’idah 5:38-39</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>So in matters of body, honor, accusation, and punishment, the Qur’an does not leave rulings floating without limits. If <span class="term">wa-idribuhunna</span> means physical beating, then suddenly, in the most sensitive family space, an undefined and unsupervised bodily authority is placed in the husband’s hand. This does not fit the Qur’an’s method of justice and mercy.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section" id="ending">
    <h2>12. The end of the verse: restraining male power</h2>
    <p>The end of the verse is very important:</p>

    <blockquote class="quran-quote">
      <p class="arabic">فَإِنْ أَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيًّا كَبِيرًا</p>
      <p class="translation">If they return from that state, do not seek a way against them. Indeed, The God is High, Great.</p>
      <p class="source"><a href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/034/">An-Nisa 4:34</a></p>
    </blockquote>

    <p>If they return from nushuz, the man has no right to seek a path of pressure, revenge, control, or oppression against them. <span class="term">Ata‘nakum</span> here does not mean absolute obedience to the man; it means return from the crisis of nushuz.</p>
    <p>Then The God ends the verse with two names: <span class="term">High</span> and <span class="term">Great</span>. This ending warns the man who holds power. If he imagines himself high because of qiwamah, the Qur’an reminds him that The God is higher. If he imagines himself great, true greatness belongs to The God. The verse restrains male power and arrogance; it does not grant a license for violence.</p>
  </section>

  <section class="article-section conclusion" id="conclusion">
    <h2>Conclusion</h2>
    <p>Linguistically, the physical meaning of <span class="term">darb</span> is possible. This should not be denied. But in the Qur’an and classical Arabic, darb does not only mean hitting. Its range includes action, impact, setting forth, movement, distancing, turning aside, and changing a situation. Comparative Semitic evidence also shows layers such as tearing away, separating, disrupting, and cutting off a condition in nearby semantic fields.</p>
    <p>Therefore, in An-Nisa 4:34, two readings are possible: physical beating, or decisive nonphysical action that stops the normal state of the relationship and moves the crisis toward arbitration. When these two readings are measured against the Qur’an itself, the physical beating reading requires external restrictions and produces an ambiguous, dangerous, and easily abused ruling over the body and dignity of a woman. The decisive nonphysical reading fits better with qiwamah as protection, qunut before The God, the meaning of nushuz, the stage of bed-separation, the arbitration verse in 4:35, and the Qur’an’s justice-centered method.</p>
    <p>A more Qur’an-coherent translation would therefore be: As for women whose nushuz and departure from the righteous covenant you fear, first admonish them; then separate from them in the beds; and if the crisis continues, take decisive nonphysical action and suspend the normal state of the relationship. If they return from that state, do not seek any way of pressure or oppression against them, for The God is High and Great.</p>
    <p class="closing">This reading does not deny the Arabic range of darb, does not sacrifice divine justice, and does not need later reports to soften a violence that the Qur’an itself did not clearly authorize.</p>
  </section>
