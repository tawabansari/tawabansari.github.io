Surah Al-Baqarah

(The Cow)

286 verses

بِسمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحمٰنِ الرَّحيمِ

By Name of The God The Merciful The Compassionate

Verse 1

الم

Alif, Lam, Meem.

Our Reflection

This verse is from the disconnected letters, the ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt; letters that appear at the beginning of some chapters of the Qur’an, while their definite and verifiable meaning is not clear to us. At the very opening, the Qur’an places the human being before an important truth: not everything is under the control of human understanding.

So the right approach to these letters is not the claim of knowing. It is humility before a knowledge that rests with The One God. A believer may reflect on these letters, but must not attribute a definite meaning to God when the Qur’an itself has not made that meaning clear.

Linguistic Notes

الم is from the disconnected letters, meaning letters that are recited separately, not as an ordinary word. For a fuller discussion of the ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt, see: The Disconnected Letters in the Qur’an.

Verse 2

ذٰلِكَ الكِتٰبُ لا رَيبَ ۛ فيهِ ۛ هُدًى لِلمُتَّقينَ

This is that Book; there is no doubt in it; a guidance for the mindful.

Our Reflection

At the beginning of Surah Al-Baqarah, the Qur’an makes a very weighty claim: “lā rayba fīhi” — there is no rayb in this Book.

This claim cannot be made about every writing. Every human book, however valuable it may be, is exposed to error, forgetfulness, incomplete transmission, alteration, variant copies, personal interpretation, the bias of the writer, and the limits of the knowledge of its time. Even books that Muslims later came to call “authentic” are, in their structure of knowledge, built upon human transmission, chains of narrators, memory, the evaluation of transmitters, and the judgment of scholars. Therefore, even if a report is considered by them to have the strongest chain, it still remains at the level of strong probability and historical confidence, not at the level of “lā rayba fīhi.”

But the Qur’an presents itself on another level. The Qur’an does not merely say, “Believe in me.” It also shows the path of testing. It says that if this Book were from other than God, they would have found much contradiction in it: An-Nisa 4:82. It says that if you are in doubt about what has been sent down upon His servant, then bring a chapter like it: Al-Baqarah 2:23. Elsewhere, the same challenge is repeated: Yunus 10:38 and Hud 11:13.

The Qur’an also attributes its preservation to God: Al-Hijr 15:9. This means that the preservation of this Reminder was not left only to the memory of individuals, governments, copyists, or religious institutions. Rather, God Himself has taken its guardianship upon Himself.

So “lā rayba fīhi” is not a simple slogan. This statement comes with several signs: absence of internal contradiction, the challenge to bring something like it, divine preservation, meaningful coherence, linguistic force, and the ability to guide human beings across generations. The Qur’an is not a book of physics or biology, yet when understood with linguistic care, context, and humility, it does not stand against manifest truth or certain knowledge.

But the end of the verse is also important: “hudan lil-muttaqīn” — a guidance for the mindful. The Qur’an is guidance, but for the one who has taqwā: the one who approaches it with care, sincerity, humility, and readiness to be corrected. A text may be guidance for a prepared heart, while becoming an excuse for argument and denial for an arrogant heart. So the absence of rayb in the Book does not mean the absence of stubbornness in the reader.

Taqwā here does not simply mean “fear.” Taqwā comes from the root w-q-y, connected with guarding, protecting, taking a shield, and preserving oneself from collapse. The muttaqīn are not those who approach the Qur’an with an unguarded heart; they are ready to guard themselves from arrogance, blind imitation, desire, injustice, and irresponsibility. This is why the guidance of the Qur’an becomes alive within them. For a fuller discussion of the Qur’anic meaning of taqwā, see: What Is Taqwa in the Qur’an?

Linguistic Notes

Rayb does not simply mean ordinary doubt. Ordinary doubt can be the beginning of seeking. But rayb is a kind of doubt mixed with disturbance, suspicion, distrust, or inner instability. When the Qur’an says “lā rayba fīhi,” it means this Book is not, in itself, a source of distrust or instability. The problem is not the instability of the text. If rayb appears, one must ask where it came from: from the reader’s carelessness, assumptions, imitation of others, or separation of the verses from one another?

The expression “dhālika al-kitāb” uses a distant demonstrative: “that Book.” This can indicate the greatness and elevation of the Book, as though it is a Book of high rank, firmness, and distinction beyond ordinary human writings.

Verse 3

الَّذينَ يُؤمِنونَ بِالغَيبِ وَيُقيمونَ الصَّلوٰةَ وَمِمّا رَزَقنٰهُم يُنفِقونَ

Those who believe in the unseen, establish Salat, and spend from what We have provided them.

Our Reflection

After the Qur’an introduced itself as a Book without rayb and as guidance for the mindful, it now describes the first signs of those mindful ones. Taqwā in the Qur’an is not merely an inner feeling or a religious claim. Taqwā has signs in life. In this verse, three foundational signs are mentioned: belief in the unseen, establishing Salat, and spending from what God has provided.

The first sign is belief in the unseen. The unseen is that part of reality which is hidden from our direct sight, yet is not outside reality. God, the Hereafter, the Account, the angels, the promise of God, and many dimensions of existence belong to the realm of the unseen. The mindful person is not someone who only looks at the tangible world and immediate benefit. They know that life is not only this near appearance. Behind this world there is meaning, accountability, responsibility, and return to God.

But faith in the Qur’an is not merely a mental belief. Faith comes from the root of security, trust, and entrusted responsibility. A believer is one who reaches trust and confidence before truth, and that trust becomes visible in action. This is why the Qur’an immediately connects faith with Salat and spending. If faith remains only on the tongue and does not become orientation, worship, ethics, responsibility, and service, it has not yet reached its Qur’anic meaning. For a fuller discussion of faith in the Qur’an, see: What Is Faith in the Qur’an?

The second sign is establishing Salat. The Qur’an does not say they merely perform or read Salat. It says they establish it. This difference matters. Iqāmah means to establish, make firm, and keep standing. So Salat is not only a momentary act or the recitation of a few words; it must stand in a person’s life as something living, effective, and directional.

The Qur’an itself clarifies the purpose of Salat: “wa aqimiṣ-ṣalāta li-dhikrī” — establish Salat for My remembrance. So Salat is the living remembrance of God: returning human awareness to God, to truth, to responsibility, and to one’s place in the world. If this remembrance is separated from Salat, only the form remains, not the spirit.

The Qur’an also gives Salat a practical criterion: “innaṣ-ṣalāta tanhā ʿani al-faḥshā’i wal-munkar” — Salat should restrain from indecency and wrongdoing. Therefore, a Salat that does not affect a person’s ethics, speech, justice, mercy, trustworthiness, and behavior must be understood again. Establishing Salat means that connection with God must affect life, not remain separate from it. For a fuller discussion of Salat in the Qur’an, see: What Is Salat in the Qur’an and How Is It Established?

The third sign is spending from God’s provision. The verse does not say they spend only from their money. It says: “from what We have provided them.” Provision is not only wealth. Knowledge, time, ability, skill, experience, social standing, physical strength, the ability to write, heal, build, and even the ability to listen to another person’s pain can all be from God’s provision. The mindful person does not keep what God has given only for themselves. They allow part of it to flow in the path of goodness, justice, help, reform, and service to others.

This verse shows that the guidance of the Qur’an becomes alive for those who carry these three directions together: their heart is open to the unseen, their relationship with God is established through Salat, and their hand is not closed toward the creation of God. Faith, Salat, and spending together form a balanced human being: one who is not only a person of belief, not only a person of private worship, and not only a person of social work without divine grounding; rather, their heart, worship, and service are joined together.

Linguistic Notes

Yu’minūn comes from the root ʾ-m-n, a root connected with security, trust, confidence, and entrusted responsibility. So faith in the Qur’an is not merely “mental belief”; it is an existential trust in truth that must appear in trustworthiness and righteous action.

Al-ghayb means the reality hidden from our direct perception. The unseen does not mean fantasy or something meaningless. It is part of reality that is not seen by the outward eye, yet the Qur’an calls the human being to awareness and responsibility before it.

Yuqīmūnaṣ-ṣalāh comes from the root q-w-m. Iqāmah means establishing, setting upright, and making something stand firmly. Therefore, the Qur’anic expression goes beyond the outward performance of an act. Salat must become established in the soul and life of the human being.

Aṣ-ṣalāh in the Qur’an should not be reduced to “reading prayer.” The Qur’an uses the verb establish, not the verb “read.” This shows that Salat is a living connection to be established, not merely a verbal recitation.

Razaqnāhum means “what We have provided them.” Attributing provision to God removes the human being from the arrogance of ownership. What is in our hands is a trust and a gift from God, not our absolute property.

Yunfiqūn comes from the root n-f-q, and here it means to bring out and let something flow from one’s wealth or capacity in the right path. Spending is not only financial charity; every blessing God has given can be spent in the path of good.

Verse 4

وَالَّذينَ يُؤمِنونَ بِما أُنزِلَ إِلَيكَ وَما أُنزِلَ مِن قَبلِكَ وَبِالـٔاخِرَةِ هُم يوقِنونَ

And those who believe in what has been sent down to you, and what was sent down before you, and in the Hereafter they are certain.

Our Reflection

In the previous verse, the Qur’an described the first three signs of the mindful: belief in the unseen, establishing Salat, and spending from God’s provision. In this verse, the vision of the mindful person moves beyond individual and ethical life and connects to the history of revelation.

The mindful person does not only believe in what was sent down upon the Prophet of the Qur’an; they also believe in what was sent down before him. This point is very important. The Qur’an does not present itself as separate from the previous stream of guidance. The Qur’an speaks in continuation of the same path that had reached human beings before through the messengers and the divine books.

So Qur’anic faith is not an exclusive or tribal faith. The true believer does not tear the history of revelation into pieces. They do not place God’s messengers against one another. They do not say: only our messenger and our book. Rather, they understand that throughout history, God has guided human beings repeatedly and sent His messengers to remind, reform, and return people to truth.

This verse also shows that faith in the Qur’an comes with respect for the origin of previous revelation. Of course, elsewhere the Qur’an speaks about distortion, concealment, disagreement, and misuse among the People of the Book. But it still confirms the origin of previous revelation. So the Qur’anic believer is neither trapped in blind denial of the past nor blind imitation of the past. They measure everything by the light of what God has sent down.

The end of the verse says: “wa bil-ākhirati hum yūqinūn” — and in the Hereafter they are certain. This is not merely a simple belief in life after death. Certainty in the Hereafter means that a person knows life is not without accountability, injustice will not remain unanswered, goodness will not be lost, and the final return of all matters is to God.

Faith in the Hereafter keeps human ethics alive. The one who is certain of the Hereafter cannot see the world as a field of irresponsibility. They know that power, wealth, speech, judgment, relationships, injustice, forgiveness, service, and betrayal all have meaning and consequence before God.

So in this verse, two more pillars of the mindful person become clear: connection to the whole stream of revelation, and certainty in the final outcome before God.

Linguistic Notes

Yu’minūn is repeated again in this verse. This repetition shows that faith is not limited to the unseen; faith also shapes the human being’s relationship with revealed guidance.

Bimā unzila ilayka means “in what has been sent down to you.” The address is to the Prophet, but its guidance is for all who encounter the Qur’an.

Wa mā unzila min qablika means “and what was sent down before you.” This expression shows the continuity of revelation and takes faith out of historical and sectarian exclusivity.

Al-ākhirah means the final abode and the final stage of the human return to God. The Hereafter is not only a distant future; belief in the Hereafter must affect human ethics and choices now.

Yūqinūn comes from certainty: firm conviction, deep assurance, and release from unstable doubt. In this verse, faith in the Hereafter appears at the level of certainty, not mere assumption or vague hope.

Verse 5

أُولٰئِكَ عَلىٰ هُدًى مِن رَبِّهِم ۖ وَأُولٰئِكَ هُمُ المُفلِحونَ

Those are upon guidance from their Lord, and those are the successful.

Our Reflection

After describing the signs of the mindful, the Qur’an announces the result in this verse: these are upon guidance from their Lord, and these are the successful.

The expression “ʿalā hudā” is very beautiful. The Qur’an does not only say that guidance has reached them. It says they are upon guidance. It is as though guidance has become firm ground, a clear road, and a secure standing place for them. They are not standing upon air, assumption, imitation, fear, fanaticism, or group identity. They are standing upon guidance from their Lord.

This guidance is not from themselves. The verse says: “min rabbihim” — from their Lord. This means a person is not guided by arrogance, religious affiliation, outward knowledge, or group claims. Guidance is from God. But a prepared heart, an awake intellect, righteous action, Salat, spending, belief in the unseen, belief in revelation, and certainty in the Hereafter place the human being on the path to receive it.

Then the Qur’an says: “wa ulā’ika humu al-mufliḥūn” — and those are the successful. Success in the Qur’an is not only worldly success. Falāḥ means to break out of ruin, to reach a sound outcome, to come to fruition, and to be saved in the final path of the human being. The mindful person is not only one who knows the path; they stand upon the path and reach its fruit.

This verse closes a complete unit of meaning. Verses 2 to 5 together build a complete picture of the human being who is able to receive guidance: one who stands before the Book without rayb with taqwā, believes in the unseen, establishes Salat, spends from God’s provision, believes in what was sent down upon the Prophet and what was sent down before him, has certainty in the Hereafter, and as a result stands upon guidance from their Lord.

So success here is not the result of a claim. It is the result of right orientation, living faith, responsible action, and acceptance of God’s guidance.

Linguistic Notes

Ulā’ika points back to the same people described in the previous verses. This means success is not presented in a vague and signless way; the Qur’an has already clarified the signs of this group.

ʿAlā hudā means “upon guidance.” The preposition ʿalā gives the sense of firmness, placement, and standing upon something. This expression shows that guidance has become a foundation and a path for them.

Min rabbihim shows that guidance is from the Lord. The human being does not create the path through arrogance. Rather, through readiness, taqwā, and acceptance of truth, the human being enters the path of divine guidance.

Al-mufliḥūn comes from the root f-l-ḥ, a root connected with splitting open and bringing something to fruit. Falāḥ means reaching a sound and saving outcome; a success that reforms the inner human being and brings their final end to good.
