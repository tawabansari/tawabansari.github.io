Al-Baqarah 2:183

O you who have believed, abstention has been prescribed for you, just as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may attain taqwa.

Our Reflection

In common translations of the Quran, the word al-siyam is usually rendered as “fasting.” But does this translation convey the word’s precise meaning? When we restrict a Quranic word from the outset to a particular form of worship, we may replace its meaning with an inherited understanding before the Quran itself explains how that worship is to be practiced.

Al-siyam comes from the root ص و م, whose basic meaning is abstaining, stopping, and refraining from an activity. This abstention is not limited to eating and drinking: a person can also refrain from speaking or from doing something else. The root expresses the basic idea of stopping and abstaining, while what someone refrains from, and under what conditions, becomes clear from the context and the relevant command.

The Quran gives a clear example of this meaning in the story of Maryam:

فَكُلِي وَاشْرَبِي وَقَرِّي عَيْنًا ۖ فَإِمَّا تَرَيِنَّ مِنَ الْبَشَرِ أَحَدًا فَقُولِي إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ صَوْمًا فَلَنْ أُكَلِّمَ الْيَوْمَ إِنْسِيًّا

“So eat and drink, and let your eyes be comforted. If you see anyone among humankind, say: I have vowed a sawm to the Entirely Merciful, so today I will not speak to any human being.”

Maryam 19:26

In this verse, Maryam is commanded to eat and drink, while the verse also speaks of the sawm she has vowed. The verse itself makes its object clear: she will refrain from speaking to human beings. Maryam’s sawm is therefore not abstention from food and drink but abstention from speech. This Quranic example shows that the meaning of sawm cannot be restricted to refraining from food and drink.

With this same care, we should return to Al-Baqarah 2:183. The God says in this verse that al-siyam has been prescribed for believers, just as it was prescribed for those before them. Yet in this verse itself, He has not specified which activities the Quran’s addressees must refrain from, at what times, or under what conditions. The following verses explain these details. In translating this verse, therefore, there is no need to give al-siyam the name “fasting” in advance and incorporate a particular form of abstention into its meaning. Faithfulness to the text requires that we first preserve the meaning of abstention and learn how it is practiced from the Quran’s subsequent commands.

That devotional abstention existed before the Quranic dispensation is a reality whose living examples can still be seen today among followers of other religious traditions.

In Christianity, for example, Lent is a period of worship, abstention, and reforming one’s conduct. In the Catholic tradition, abstaining from meat is prescribed on certain days during this period. In addition, some Christians voluntarily choose to give up something they enjoy or habitually do for a time, such as eating sweets, drinking certain beverages, or smoking. These voluntary abstentions vary from person to person and should not be equated with the Church’s binding rules. Nevertheless, they show that a person can consciously forgo a desire or habit for a devotional purpose.

There are also clear examples in Judaism. On Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, Jews who observe this tradition refrain not only from eating and drinking but also from certain other activities, such as marital relations, washing the body for comfort, and wearing leather shoes. These abstentions are connected with repentance, reviewing one’s conduct, and worship.

These present-day examples show that devotional abstention is not confined to one form or one religious dispensation. Of course, the existence of these traditions does not establish that all their present-day details are exactly the original divine rulings. What matters for understanding Al-Baqarah 2:183 is that the Quran does not present siyam as something new and unprecedented, but connects it with the worship of those who preceded its addressees.

But what is the purpose of this abstention? The end of the verse makes the answer clear: “لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ”—so that you may attain taqwa.

This same purpose is also stated for worship in Al-Baqarah 2:21. The God calls people to worship their Lord and says: “لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ”. Worship is therefore not merely performing an act or observing a set of instructions; it should cultivate taqwa in a person. Someone who consciously refrains from fulfilling a desire learns that not every inclination or habit should govern their conduct. The ethical value of this ability becomes apparent when a person can also restrain themselves from lying, injustice, greed, anger, and violating other people’s rights.

Taqwa is not only refraining from wrongdoing; it should also lead a person toward ihsan—doing good. In An-Nahl 16:128, the Quran says: “إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ مُحْسِنُونَ”—indeed, The God is with those who have practiced taqwa and those who do good. A person who is watchful over their desires should also show this care in their treatment of others: refraining from oppression and injustice and turning toward goodness, fairness, and respect for people’s rights. The relationship between worship, taqwa, and ihsan is examined in detail in the article “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

The value of devotional abstention thus lies not merely in temporarily depriving oneself of something desired, but in the change it should bring about in thought, will, and conduct. Before setting out the details of the siyam prescribed for the Quran’s addressees, The God has made its purpose clear: “لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ”. Abstention should bring a person closer to taqwa: a conscious watchfulness that restrains them from wrongdoing and opens the way to goodness and ihsan.
