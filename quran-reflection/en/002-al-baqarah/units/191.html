Al-Baqarah 2:191

ۚ ۚ ۖ ۗ

And kill them wherever you gain the upper hand over them, and expel them from where they expelled you. And fitnah is more severe than killing. Do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they fight you there; then if they fight you, kill them. Such is the recompense of the disbelievers.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:190, The God stated the principle of fighting in His way and limited it to those who fight you. He then warned: “وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا”—do not transgress. This verse now concerns a particular group who, in addition to fighting, expelled those addressed from their homes and land. The verse therefore belongs to the context of a specific confrontation with aggressive combatants, not a general command to kill people who hold different beliefs.

The God says:

“وَاقْتُلُوهُمْ حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ وَأَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَخْرَجُوكُمْ”

“And kill them wherever you gain the upper hand over them, and expel them from where they expelled you.”

“ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ” comes from the root ث ق ف, associated in Arabic usage with skill, apprehending, gaining access to, and overcoming. In the context of war, this word can indicate gaining the upper hand over the enemy, rather than merely seeing or happening to find a person.

In Al-Anfal 8:57, The God also says: “فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ فِي الْحَرْبِ”—if you gain the upper hand over them in war. In Al-Mumtahanah 60:2, the same root concerns enemies who, if they gain the upper hand over those addressed, will act with hostility toward them.

Thus, “حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ” cannot be turned into permission to hunt people, assassinate opponents, or settle personal scores in every street and neighborhood. “حَيْثُ” expresses the spatial scope, but the pronoun “هُمْ” still refers to the same group in the context of fighting: those who fought and expelled the people addressed from their land.

This command neither extends the identity of the enemy to every follower of a religion nor permits anyone to kill every person after capture without investigation and fair adjudication.

A distinction must be made between a combatant confronted during battle and a person who has been arrested, taken prisoner, or surrendered. In Muhammad 47:4, after describing fighting and gaining the upper hand on the battlefield, the Quran speaks of securing prisoners and then releasing them freely or for ransom. In An-Nisa 4:90, it also closes the way to aggression against those who withdraw from fighting and offer peace.

Even in At-Tawbah 9:6, regarding a polytheist who asks for protection, it says:

“وَإِنْ أَحَدٌ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ اسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ حَتَّىٰ يَسْمَعَ كَلَامَ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ أَبْلِغْهُ مَأْمَنَهُ”

“If one of the polytheists asks you for protection, grant him protection so that he may hear the word of The God; then convey him to his place of safety.”

Even amid hostility and war, a request for protection, withdrawal from fighting, and the condition of captivity cannot be disregarded. If, after arrest, a person is accused of killing or another crime, their responsibility must be investigated and pursued, and established through justice. As we discussed in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:178, qisas concerns pursuing the crime and fair adjudication, not unchecked revenge.

The second part of the same command reveals the background to the confrontation: “وَأَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَخْرَجُوكُمْ”—expel them from where they expelled you.

This is therefore not about initiating conquest or taking others’ lands at will. Those addressed had already suffered expulsion and aggression, and the command now concerns confronting those same aggressors. This differs fundamentally from general permission to expel people from their homes merely because of their ethnicity or beliefs.

The God then says:

“وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ”

“And fitnah is more severe than killing.”

“Fitnah” comes from the root ف ت ن, connected with testing and placing someone in difficult conditions. In Quranic usage, it can also refer to persecution, pressure, torture, and afflicting people. Its meaning must be understood from context.

Here the subject is people who have suffered persecution and aggression because of their beliefs and have been expelled from their homes and land. In Al-Baqarah 2:217, The God also speaks of obstructing people from His way, preventing access to the Sacred Mosque, expelling people from it, and trying to turn them away from their religion, and then says: “وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ”.

Fitnah in this context is therefore not mere disagreement in belief; it is persecution, torture, denial of freedom of choice, repression, and efforts to force people to abandon their beliefs and way of life—oppression that can lead to their expulsion from their land and deprivation of their rights.

But this phrase must not be understood in reverse. If imposing beliefs and denying freedom of choice are instances of fitnah, how can one impose the same coercion on others in order to end it? Is killing a person merely for following another religion or religious law not itself an example of that same oppression?

In Al-Baqarah 2:256, The God says: “لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ”. Removing fitnah in this context therefore cannot mean forcing people to accept a belief or religious law. This matter is discussed in detail in “There Is No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey”.

The God then does not disregard the sanctity of the Sacred Mosque, even in confronting these aggressors:

“وَلَا تُقَاتِلُوهُمْ عِنْدَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ حَتَّىٰ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ فِيهِ”

“And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they fight you there.”

Although this group had previously fought and expelled people, The God does not permit those addressed to initiate fighting in the precincts of the Sacred Mosque. But if those same aggressors begin fighting there as well, the command to respond applies: “فَإِنْ قَاتَلُوكُمْ فَاقْتُلُوهُمْ”.

The sanctity of the Sacred Mosque must therefore not become a refuge for initiating attacks while shielding the aggressor from response; likewise, hostility toward aggressors does not authorize violating this place’s sanctity and initiating fighting without aggression on their part.

The verse ends:

“كَذَٰلِكَ جَزَاءُ الْكَافِرِينَ”

“Such is the recompense of the disbelievers.”

“The disbelievers” in this verse must not be generalized, without regard to context, to all non-Muslims in every time and place. The subject is the same group who fought those addressed, expelled them from their homes, and might initiate fighting even within the precincts of the Sacred Mosque.

In Al-Mumtahanah 60:8, the Quran does not prohibit kindness and justice toward those who have not fought you because of religion or expelled you from your homes. Following a different religion or religious law is therefore not, by itself, a war crime or grounds for killing. The distinction between din, Islam, and shariah is also discussed in “Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Quran”.

The very next verse immediately connects the command to respond with the other side’s desisting: “فَإِنِ انْتَهَوْا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ” (Al-Baqarah 2:192). Permission to fight cannot therefore be separated from the conditions governing its beginning and end.

Al-Baqarah 2:191 concerns a particular confrontation with combatants and those who expelled others, but its message is not limited to that time. No group may turn “حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ” into permission for the indiscriminate killing of people with different beliefs, civilians, or prisoners. Fighting is to confront aggression and remove oppression, not to impose beliefs, inflict collective revenge, or kill those who bear no responsibility for the war. The preceding verse’s command, “وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا”, continues to govern this fighting: even confronting an aggressor must not become a new act of aggression.
