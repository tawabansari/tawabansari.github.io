Al-Baqarah 2:193

ۖ

And struggle against them until there is no fitnah and din belongs to The God; so if they desist, let there be no further aggression except against the wrongdoers.

Our Reflection

To understand this verse, we must attend to the sequence of The God’s words in the three preceding verses. In Al-Baqarah 2:190, fighting was prescribed against those who fought the people addressed, and The God prohibited transgression. In Al-Baqarah 2:191, the command “وَاقْتُلُوهُمْ” concerned that same specific group who, besides fighting, had expelled people from their homes—in the context of war and subject to the limits stated in the verse itself. Then, in Al-Baqarah 2:192, He said: “فَإِنِ انْتَهَوْا”—so if they desist—and described Himself as Forgiving and Merciful.

Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:193, He says:

“وَقَاتِلُوهُمْ حَتَّىٰ لَا تَكُونَ فِتْنَةٌ”

“And struggle against them until there is no fitnah.”

Here, the distinction between “اقْتُلُوهُمْ” and “قَاتِلُوهُمْ” is essential. Both come from the root ق ت ل, but their forms and meanings are not identical. “اقْتُلُوا” is an imperative of the basic triliteral form and means “kill”, whereas “قَاتِلُوا”, in verbal form III, means “fight and engage in confrontation”.

“اقتلوا” commands killing, while “قاتلوا” commands struggle and confrontation. They must not be treated as synonyms, nor should every command to fight be taken as an independent authorization to kill people.

The Quran itself makes this distinction evident in Al-Baqarah 2:191: “فَإِنْ قَاتَلُوكُمْ فَاقْتُلُوهُمْ”—if they fight you, then kill them. If “قاتلوا” and “اقتلوا” meant the same thing, why would this very phrase use two different forms?

Of course, qital in Quranic language can also refer to military confrontation; but fighting is not the same as killing every individual on the opposing side. We must therefore distinguish between the struggle itself, its purpose, and the conditions under which confrontation resulting in killing has been permitted.

In the preceding verse, The God opened the way to desisting from war and killing. Now He states the purpose of continuing the struggle: ending fitnah. This struggle is a confrontation intended to deter oppression and repression, not a new command for unrestricted killing.

Other approaches also have a place in the Quran in pursuing this goal: conveying the message, dialogue, debating with wisdom, social relations, political pressure, negotiation, agreements, and diplomacy. These are not the lexical meaning of “قاتلوا”, but peaceful approaches that can be used alongside deterrent confrontation, or before war is reached, to end fitnah and hostility.

But if the enemy, after desisting from killing and expelling people, breaks its agreement and returns to killing, expulsion, and aggression, the same permission to respond in Al-Baqarah 2:191 applies again. Nevertheless, we too have no right to transgress and must observe that verse’s limits and the command “وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا” in Al-Baqarah 2:190.

Thus, struggle does not in itself authorize killing, nor does breaking an agreement, without a return to aggression and the relevant conditions, authorize taking human lives.

But what is the purpose of this struggle? The God has made the answer clear:

“حَتَّىٰ لَا تَكُونَ فِتْنَةٌ”

“Until there is no fitnah.”

In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:191, we explained that fitnah in the context of these verses is not merely a difference of belief; it is persecution, torture, expulsion, repression, and pressure to force people to abandon their religion and way of life. The purpose of the struggle is therefore to end this oppressive situation, not to destroy opponents until they all follow one religion or religious law.

If fitnah is the denial of freedom of belief and the imposition of religion, removing fitnah cannot be accomplished by imposing another belief.

This distinction can also be observed in Muhammad’s life. At Badr, Uhud, and the Trench, military confrontations took place and war was answered with war. But Muhammad’s struggle with his enemies and opponents was not confined to the battlefield. He established relations with different peoples and tribes, concluded agreements, and also used dialogue and political approaches.

The peace of Hudaybiyyah is an important example of this approach. Muhammad and his companions set out toward Mecca to perform the rites, but Quraysh prevented their entry. Instead of initiating war, negotiations took place and an agreement was concluded to halt hostility and regulate relations between the two sides. In Al-Fath 48:1, the Quran speaks of “فَتْحًا مُبِينًا”, and in Al-Fath 48:24 it also refers to restraining the hands of both sides from one another.

According to historical accounts, after this peace, an attack on a tribe allied with Muhammad and the violation of the Hudaybiyyah agreement created the conditions for renewed confrontation. Eventually, Muhammad entered Mecca with a large army; but the conquest of Mecca did not become a general massacre of the city’s people, and historical accounts describe a broad pardon of opponents.

These events show that confronting an enemy can have different stages: war in response to aggression, negotiation and agreements to end hostility, responding to renewed aggression, and ultimately ending hostility and pardoning opponents after victory. The details of these events are known from historical accounts and do not constitute an independent authority for religious legislation; nevertheless, they offer thought-provoking examples for recognizing the difference between the ways of struggle and killing.

If the sole aim were to kill opponents, what place would the peace of Hudaybiyyah, political agreements, and the broad pardon after the conquest of Mecca have?

In An-Nahl 16:125, the Quran commands inviting with wisdom and debating in the best manner, and in Al-Anfal 8:61 it says that if the other side inclines toward peace, you should incline toward peace. The way to end oppression and fitnah must therefore not be confined to violence and killing. Peaceful and political approaches should be used to remove aggression and restore freedom and security.

We now come to the verse’s second phrase:

“وَيَكُونَ الدِّينُ لِلَّهِ”

“And din belongs to The God.”

This phrase, too, must not be understood apart from the context of fitnah and the denial of freedom. If we immediately take “din” to mean a particular historical religious law or denomination, we may imagine that the struggle must continue until all people follow that same religious law.

But in Al-Baqarah 2:256, the Quran explicitly says: “لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ”—there is no compulsion in religion. The God has not given even His messenger authority to impose faith on people. The messenger’s duty was to convey the message so that people could hear it, reflect upon it, and freely accept or reject it.

In “Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Quran”, we have examined how “din” in the Quran is not simply the name of a denomination or a collection of rites, but is connected with servanthood, the source of command, reckoning, and human responsibility. Islam is submission to The God, while shariah is the practical path prescribed for following His guidance. In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Quran also speaks of differences in the laws and paths prescribed for communities.

Therefore, “الدِّينُ لِلَّهِ” does not mean that people’s religion and conscience become the property of a government, leader, or religious community. Din belongs to The God, not to powers seeking to impose their beliefs and religious law on others.

In the context of these verses, when fitnah and repression end, the way to convey the message must also be free. People should be able to hear The God’s word, ask questions, reflect, critique, and decide for themselves how to respond. The messenger’s responsibility is to convey; the individual’s responsibility is to respond consciously to guidance.

If a government uses threats, torture, expulsion, or violence to prevent people from hearing the message and choosing freely, it stands against this principle. Yet someone who, in the name of defending religion, forces people to accept their own religious law also destroys the very freedom they claim to be struggling to establish.

This relationship between freedom to convey the message, the rejection of compulsion, and the limits of fighting is discussed in detail in “There Is No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey”.

At the end, The God repeats the same condition as in the preceding verse:

“فَإِنِ انْتَهَوْا فَلَا عُدْوَانَ إِلَّا عَلَى الظَّالِمِينَ”

“So if they desist, let there be no further aggression except against the wrongdoers.”

This repetition is not insignificant. After stating the purpose of the struggle, The God again specifies the way to end it. If the enemy desists from fitnah, repression, and aggression, hostility toward them must not continue under the pretext of a difference of belief or past enmity.

“عُدْوَان”, from the root ع د و, is connected with transgressing and exceeding limits. Here, “إِلَّا عَلَى الظَّالِمِينَ” directs continued confrontation toward wrongdoers, not everyone belonging to their people, land, or religion. This exception does not authorize exceeding the limits of justice, because the command “وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا” in Al-Baqarah 2:190 remains in force.

Someone who has desisted from aggression and accepted the path of peace cannot be targeted merely because of past hostility. But if an individual or group continues to oppress, ending their aggression must also be pursued within the bounds of right and justice.

This verse is neither a command for perpetual killing nor a call to surrender to oppression. The struggle must aim to end fitnah, and peaceful and political approaches must have their place. If the enemy returns to killing and expulsion, the ruling on responding under the conditions set out in the preceding verses becomes applicable; but none of those rulings permits transgression against innocent people or unchecked revenge.

The distinction between “اقتلوا” and “قاتلوا” in this context must therefore be taken seriously. Killing is a specific action resulting in the loss of life; struggle and confrontation to remove fitnah cannot be reduced to that action. If the aim is to end repression and restore freedom, the struggle itself must not become a means of repressing others and taking away their freedom.

Din belongs to The God, and people are accountable to Him. No government, group, or leader has the right to take possession of people’s consciences in The God’s name.

The goal of the struggle is to end fitnah, not to destroy opponents. A sign of attaining that goal is that people are no longer forced to accept a belief out of fear of torture, expulsion, threats, or death. When aggression ends, continued hostility must also end. Someone struggling for freedom cannot take freedom away from others and still claim to be struggling in the way of The God.
