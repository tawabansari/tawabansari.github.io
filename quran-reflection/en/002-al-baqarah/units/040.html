Al-Baqarah 2:40

O children of Israel, remember My favor which I bestowed upon you; and fulfill My covenant, and I will fulfill your covenant; and Me alone, so have vigilant reverence of Me.

Our Reflection

With this verse, the Qur'an moves from the general horizon of the human being, descent, guidance, kufr, and denial into a specific historical field: the Children of Israel. The previous verses stated the general law of earthly life: guidance comes from God; whoever follows it is released from destructive fear and grief; and whoever covers truth and denies the signs becomes associated with the Fire. Now the Qur'an places that same law before a community that carries Book, covenant, and history.

In this sense, the Children of Israel in this section are not only a historical group. They are the first clear example of a post-descent community that had been given guidance, Book, prophets, covenant, and a long social experience. In the previous verses, the law of guidance was stated generally; now that law is tested in the field of a real history: when a community receives the favor of Book, prophet, and covenant, what does it do with that trust? Does it turn it into responsibility, or into ethnic pride and religious privilege?

The address begins with ya bani Isra'il. The Qur'an does not first call them with humiliation, but with a name that recalls prophetic origin, the history of covenant, and their relation to revelation. Bani comes from the field of building and structure. So this expression does not point only to biological descent; it points to a generation formed upon a historical, religious, and monotheistic building. They are carriers of a historical structure known through Book, deliverance, prophets, and covenant.

Israel in the Qur'an points to Jacob. In the scriptural tradition, this name carries a direct relation to the divine order, whether through Semitic accounts of struggle and striving with the divine command, or through the name's relation to God. The Qur'an does not enter a philological explanation of the name here, but this much is clear: “Children of Israel” is not merely the title of a people detached from responsibility. It is the title of a lineage that carries its relation to a prophetic root and a divine covenant.

This becomes more precise when the atmosphere of Medina is considered. Muhammad did not build in Medina a closed religious group cut off from all others. He established peace between Aws and Khazraj in Yathrib, and those tribes, previously marked by war and rupture, became the Helpers in the new order of Medina. Jewish tribes were also present in the same city; tribes that entered with Muhammad into covenants of coexistence and shared responsibility. Therefore, when the Qur'an addresses the Children of Israel in this atmosphere, it should not be read through the later lens of “Muslims versus Jews.” This address is heard within a covenantal, civic, multi-group society, in which believers, People of the Book, and allies lived under an order of covenant and social responsibility.

From here it becomes clear that verse 40 must not be reduced to sectarian hostility. The Qur'an speaks with a group that has Book, a history of revelation, and in Medina was part of the social and covenantal reality. The Qur'an does not declare their Book entirely void. Rather, it calls them to the very thing that, if they remain faithful to it, should lead them to recognize truth: remembering favor, fulfilling covenant, and having rahbah of God instead of rahbah before other powers. In this same logic, the Qur'an speaks of the Torah and Gospel as books in which there had been guidance and light, and it asks the People of the Book to judge by what God sent down in their Book, while the Qur'an itself remains the final criterion of confirmation, correction, and clarification of truth.

The phrase udh-kuru ni'mati allati an'amtu 'alaykum is the first axis of the verse. Dhikr in the Qur'an is not only mental recollection. Dhikr means making a truth present in awareness, speech, decision, and action. Favor here is not merely material comfort. God's favor upon the Children of Israel includes guidance, Book, deliverance, prophets, the opportunity to carry revelation, and their historical place among communities that carry Book. But favor, when separated from covenant, becomes ethnic pride and religious privilege. For this reason, the verse immediately moves from favor to covenant.

Wa-awfu bi-'ahdi ufi bi-'ahdikum is the heart of the verse. Covenant in the Qur'an is not a simple marginal promise. Covenant begins with the primordial bond between the human being and the Lord, gains meaning in faith, appears in social justice, and is questioned on the Day of Reckoning. The true believer in the Qur'an is one who guards trusts and covenants. In contrast, the fasiq is one who breaks God's covenant after it has been made firm, cuts what God commanded to be joined, and spreads corruption in the earth.

So when God says to the Children of Israel, “Fulfill My covenant,” the speech is not only about one limited historical obligation. This command includes all layers of covenant: tawhid, not concealing truth, upholding the Book, justice, faithfulness to the covenant, and receiving guidance when its signs become clear. They cannot desire the favor of Book and prophets while setting aside the responsibility of covenant. Favor without fulfillment leads the human being to claims of superiority; favor joined with covenant draws the human being toward responsibility and taqwa.

The structure of the verse is also very important: awfu bi-'ahdi ufi bi-'ahdikum. Covenant here is not like the command of a tyrant king who demands obedience from subjects while placing himself outside faithfulness. The Qur'an presents the covenantal relation between God and the human being as a living and responsive relation; not equal in rank or station, but two-sided in faithfulness and response. The human being fulfills God's covenant, and God fulfills His promise and appointment. This expression grants the human being covenantal dignity: the human being is not merely condemned under a blind power, but is addressed by covenant, responsible for faithfulness, and recipient of divine promise.

The phrase ufi bi-'ahdikum shows that the human relation with God is not ruleless or without response. God is faithful to His promise and appointment. He does not fail His promise. If the human being does not break the covenant, God does not leave the promise of guidance, reward, mercy, and opening of the path unfulfilled. Here the theology of covenant becomes visible: the human being fulfills God's covenant, and God fulfills His covenant toward the human being. But when the human being breaks the covenant, he removes himself from the field of that divine fulfillment.

The verse ends with a very important expression: wa-iyyaya farhabun. A flat translation as “fear Me” is not enough. The root R H B carries a field of awe, vigilant reverence, careful restraint, and contraction before greatness and consequence. Rahbah is not blind fear. It is a weighty awareness of God's station, the greatness of the covenant, and the consequence of betraying truth. The Qur'an does not say: have irrational terror before God. It says: the center of your awe, vigilance, and careful reverence must be Me.

This point is deeply meaningful in the address to the Children of Israel. From the same root comes the word rahib, monk. Elsewhere the Qur'an criticizes those who took their ahbar and ruhban as lords besides God. Here God speaks with a root that carries heavy religious meaning in the memory of the People of the Book. It is as though the verse says: do not fear scholars, monks, tribal power, social pressure, preservation of religious rank, or loss of status. If you must have vigilant reverence, that reverence must be before Me alone.

Therefore wa-iyyaya farhabun is understood in the continuation of covenant. Whoever is more wary of people than of God changes the covenant in favor of people. Whoever fears the religious institution, clergy, elders of the people, or political power more than God may conceal truth, sell the signs for a small price, or cover the signs of revelation when they do not serve his interests. From the very beginning, verse 40 frees the Children of Israel from fears that replace God: remember the favor, complete the covenant, and have rahbah only of Me.

In this reading, verse 40 is not the beginning of hostility toward the Children of Israel. It is the beginning of summoning the people of covenant. The Qur'an says to them: you are not rootless and without light. You have seen favor, you have had Book, prophets have come among you, and you are known through divine covenant. But that station brings responsibility. If you turn favor into ethnic privilege, if you break the covenant, if you have more rahbah before monks, scholars, and social powers than before God, the very favor that should have guided you becomes a proof against you.

To understand this setting more deeply, the verse should be read beside two foundational discussions: first, the discussion of believer and Muslim, so that the first community of Medina and the Qur'anic address are not confined within later sectarian identity; and second, the discussion of covenant in the Qur'an, so it becomes clear that fulfilling covenant, after tawhid and freedom of faith, is one of the pillars of Qur'anic ethics.
