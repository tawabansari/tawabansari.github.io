Al-Baqarah 2:110

ۚ ۗ

And establish prayer (salat) and give purifying contributions (zakat); whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with The God. Indeed, The God sees what you do.

Our Reflection

In the name of The God.

The preceding verse commanded pardon and overlooking in response to envy and attempts to turn believers away from faith. Yet the Qur’an does not leave a person continually reacting to other people’s opposition. It immediately turns attention toward building:

“Establish prayer and give purifying social contributions.” These are the commands to establish prayer and conscious connection (salat) and to give what purifies and nurtures growth (zakat).

Instead of spending all your energy on other people’s envy, their hostility, and answering them, sustain your relationship with The God and fulfill your responsibilities toward others. Pardon and overlooking keep a person from becoming trapped in a cycle of hostility; prayer and purifying giving turn that person back toward building both the self and society.

But the verse does not stop at these two acts:

“Whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with The God.”

“Good” is left open in scope. Pardoning and overlooking can be good; establishing prayer is good; giving what purifies is good. But the sentence is not restricted to these things. Whatever good a person does is ultimately something sent ahead for that person’s own benefit.

The expression “for yourselves” changes the perspective. When someone gives, overlooks a wrong, helps a person in need, or parts with wealth, something appears to be leaving their possession. Yet the Qur’an says that this very good is being sent ahead for yourselves. The benefit of a good deed does not end with its recipient: the person doing it is also shaped by the act.

This image appeared earlier with a different emphasis. Al-Baqarah 2:95 spoke of people afraid to face the consequences of “what their hands have sent ahead.” Here the same idea of sending ahead is directed toward good. A person can send ahead something they will one day fear to encounter, or send ahead good that they will find again.

Yet “you will find it with The God” should not be restricted to the hereafter without a reason. The verse does not say, “You will find it in the hereafter.” It says that you will find it with The God.

An-Nahl 16:96 sheds further light on this expression, “with The God”:

“What is with you runs out, but what is with The God remains.”

Good that is with Him, then, is neither lost nor destroyed. The hereafter is certainly a setting in which the consequences of deeds are fully encountered, but the Qur’an does not defer every consequence of good to the world after death. An-Nahl 16:97 connects righteous action with a good life in this life, while Ar-Ra‘d 13:11 connects a people’s change in circumstances with a change in what is within themselves.

This does not describe a mechanical transaction in which every good deed immediately receives a particular reward. A person who does good may still become ill, suffer a loss, or face hardship. But good is not without effect.

Pardon can interrupt a cycle of revenge. Prayer, when truly established, keeps remembrance of The God and attention to one’s conduct alive. Purifying giving can change a person’s relationship with wealth and miserliness, while alleviating some need and reducing some of the distance between people in society. Honesty, justice, generosity, and cooperation also build trust and social relationships over time.

If the members of a society each do a little good, those small acts accumulate. Individual conduct becomes culture, and culture shapes the condition of society. Just as small acts of corruption, repeated over time, can wear a society down from within, small acts of good, when widespread, can change its direction.

The same happens within the individual. Someone who repeatedly pardons, gives, offers sincere help, and keeps a living relationship with the Lord is not merely storing something for the future. That person also changes along the way. What we do becomes part of the character with which we live.

The recognition that human conduct has consequences is not confined to the Abrahamic religions. In some Indian religious traditions, karma also addresses the relationship between action and consequence. But karma should not be treated as identical to the meaning of this verse; each tradition has its own framework. The Qur’an presents action and consequence within its own framework: good is not abandoned in a system with no one presiding over it. “You will find it with The God.”

The verse’s ending brings this distinction into focus:

“Indeed, The God sees what you do.”

In the Qur’an, the relationship between action and consequence is not entrusted to an unaware law with no one presiding over it. The God sees; nothing a person does escapes His sight.

Once again, the words are “what you do.” Good is not merely a worthy intention, a religious slogan, or talk about goodness. Pardon and overlooking must appear in conduct, prayer must be established, purifying contributions must be given, and good must become action.

Whatever good a person does for someone else, for society, or for their relationship with The God also builds something within that person. Some of its fruit may appear today in their own inner life and in society; and what is with The God does not pass away.
