Al-Baqarah 2:111

ۗ ۗ

And they said: No one will enter the Garden unless they are Jewish or Christian. Those are their wishes and suppositions. Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful.

Our Reflection

In this verse, salvation is tied to religious identity:

“No one will enter the Garden unless they are Jewish or Christian.”

Two verses later, Al-Baqarah 2:113 will make it clearer that this was not a shared claim with one agreed meaning: Jews and Christians also denied one another’s standing. Each side could see salvation as confined to its own identity and place the other outside it.

But the Qur’an does not enter a lengthy dispute. It first reports their words, then delivers its own judgment:

“Those are their wishes and suppositions.”

It then addresses the Messenger:

“Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful.”

Within one short verse, three stages follow one another: the human claim, the Qur’an’s judgment upon it, and then the demand for proof.

The Arabic word for these wishes and suppositions (amānī) belongs to the root M N Y, the same word family associated with wishing and longing. This root recurs several times in this part of the surah. Al-Baqarah 2:78 spoke of people who did not know the Book and relied on wishes and conjecture. In 2:94, those who considered the hereafter exclusively theirs were tested with the challenge, “Then wish for death”; 2:95 continued, “Yet they will never wish for it.” Now that same word family reappears in a claim to exclusive entry into the Garden: “Those are their wishes and suppositions.”

The connection with Al-Baqarah 2:94 is closer still. After the claim to exclusive possession of the hereafter came the words:

“If you are truthful.”

This verse also closes with precisely the same words:

“If you are truthful.”

There, the truth of their claim was tested by asking them to wish for death; here, they are asked for proof. A sweeping claim about the hereafter and salvation is not established by religious certainty.

The same malady had appeared in another form in Al-Baqarah 2:80. They said the Fire would touch them only for a limited number of days. The Qur’an did not ask how many generations had held this belief or how many scholars had accepted it. It asked:

Have you taken a covenant with The God?

There, the claim concerned a limit on punishment, and a covenant was demanded. Here, the claim concerns exclusive entry into the Garden, and proof is demanded.

Verses 2:81 and 2:82 likewise did not make a community’s name the basis of the answer. They spoke of conduct, wrongdoing that encompasses a person, faith, and righteous deeds. Now, in 2:111 and 2:112, the same pattern appears again: first the guarantee claimed for a group is dismantled, then the following verse sets out the Qur’an’s own criterion.

This matters greatly for the reader of the Qur’an. A Muslim cannot read this verse about Jews and Christians, dismiss their claim as mere wishes, and then reproduce the same structure under another name for their own community.

An-Nisa 4:123 closes off this double standard:

“Neither according to your wishes nor the wishes of the People of the Book.”

This time the Qur’an puts “your wishes” first. Its own audience has no right to shelter behind these verses and direct them only at others.

Within part of the Muslim theological heritage, a belief developed that anyone who dies adhering to the understanding of divine oneness or faith defined by that school will ultimately be brought out of the Fire, even if they have sinned, whereas someone outside that doctrinal framework has no such guarantee, however much good they do. Different schools do not formulate this belief identically. Yet the Qur’anic question facing every categorical guarantee remains the same:

Where is its covenant? Where is its proof?

This is different from hoping for mercy. A person can hope for the mercy of The God, repent, and ask forgiveness. But hope for mercy cannot be turned into a sect’s ultimate insurance policy. Even where the Qur’an speaks of forgiving sins short of associating others with Him, it makes that forgiveness dependent upon “whomever He wills.” The will of The God cannot be converted into a community’s inherited guarantee.

A Christian who claims that mere doctrinal allegiance to Christ guarantees salvation, regardless of conduct and responsibility, faces the same question. If a Jew treats ethnic and religious affiliation as proof of a guaranteed standing with The God, the same demand remains. And if a Muslim imagines that the name “the community of Muhammad” has taken the key to Paradise away from others and placed it in their own hands, the malady has not been cured. Only the name of the claimant to the monopoly has changed.

The Qur’an does not replace the name of one privileged community with another. It questions the very principle of inherited privilege in salvation.

This subject is examined more fully in Paradise Is Not the Monopoly of One Sect or One Religious Law: from the claim of “a few days in the Fire” and guarantees of group salvation to faith and righteous deeds, intercession, differing religious laws, the position of the People of the Book, and whether hearing the name of a religion amounts to having its truth made clear to a person.

For the distinction between religion itself, surrender to The God, and a prescribed religious way, the article Religion, Surrender, and Religious Law in the Qur’an provides a separate conceptual foundation.

For now, this verse itself gives us a sufficient criterion. Whenever we pronounce a categorical judgment about a community’s ultimate destiny, we must ask ourselves:

Does this claim truly have proof from The God, or is it merely our own wishful thinking?
