Al-Baqarah 2:261

ۗ ۗ

The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of The God is like a grain that grows seven ears, with a hundred grains in each ear; and The God multiplies for whomever He wills; and The God is expansive in bounty and knowing.

Our Reflection

In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:254, we discussed a simple but important rule in life: every blessing placed at a person’s disposal reveals its true value and effect when it is used and put into circulation for good. In that reflection, we examined how provision is not limited to money and possessions: knowledge, talent, skill, experience, time, and human abilities are also blessings that can be used for good.

Now, through a beautiful parable, the Quran shows how spending wealth in God’s way can become a source of growth and fruits far exceeding its original amount.

“مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ”

“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of The God.”

“كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ”

“Is like a grain that grows seven ears, with a hundred grains in each ear.”

A grain is planted in the earth, but its yield is not merely one other grain: seven ears grow, each containing a hundred grains. What began as one grain has now become seven hundred.

Yet the beauty of the parable does not end with this number. Each of those seven hundred grains also has the potential to be planted again and, under suitable conditions, produce more grains. Thus, a small beginning can become the source of a chain of growth and fruitfulness whose yield far exceeds the initial amount.

This image reveals an important truth about the role of giving in individual and social life. Imagine two people, each with capital at their disposal.

The first person has converted his capital into gold and stored it in a treasury. Each day he counts his possessions and feels pleased to have accumulated more gold. His goal is to preserve and increase his personal wealth without using it to meet others’ needs or create new opportunities.

In Surah Al-Humazah, the Quran presents an image of such attachment to accumulating and counting wealth:

“الَّذِي جَمَعَ مَالًا وَعَدَّدَهُ” Al-Humazah 104:2

“The one who gathered wealth and counted it.”

The issue is not merely possessing wealth or counting it, but an outlook in which accumulating wealth becomes life’s principal aim and a person neglects responsibility toward others. In At-Tawbah 9:34, the Quran also speaks of those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in God’s way.

Now consider the second person.

Rather than turning all his capital into accumulated assets, he puts it into circulation through beneficial activities. With some of his wealth, he helps people in need start businesses, builds a factory, makes production possible, creates jobs, and uses his capital and resources to improve people’s lives.

But the effects of this capital do not end there.

Workers spend their wages on life’s necessities. These purchases help other businesses thrive. Producers expand their activities to meet growing needs. New employment opportunities arise, and other families also benefit from this flow.

Children growing up in families benefiting from these opportunities gain access to better education, the development of their talents, and the acquisition of skills. They too may become entrepreneurs, physicians, teachers, scientists, or producers and create new opportunities for others.

Capital initially held by just one person can now become a source of livelihood, education, production, capability, and progress for several generations.

This is the image of growth and multiplication that the parable of the grain and ears brings closer to our understanding.

Of course, the mere circulation of money or the profitability of an investment does not by itself make it spending in God’s way. The Quran includes the qualification “فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ.” How wealth is used, respect for people’s rights, and the purpose that capital serves are essential. Wealth that grows through exploitation, deception, or corruption cannot count as goodness and beneficence merely because it generates financial activity.

Another example is someone who uses their wealth to educate a deprived child.

That person may have paid for only one child’s education, yet the child may later become a physician who saves thousands of patients’ lives, or a teacher who educates hundreds of other children. Those pupils, in turn, also serve other people.

The value of that initial act of giving therefore cannot be measured solely by the amount of money paid. A small amount of wealth, placed appropriately and for a good purpose, may produce fruits that outlast the giver’s life and benefit later generations.

The Quran then says:

“وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ”

“And The God multiplies for whomever He wills.”

Seven hundred is not a ceiling on growth or reward. God has not confined the prospect of increase to a fixed number.

In Al-Baqarah 2:245, the Quran speaks of abundant multiplication in connection with a good loan to God; in Al-Hadid 57:18, it connects this same increase with charity and a good loan.

“وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ”

“And The God is expansive in bounty and knowing.”

“Wāsiʿ,” from the root W S AYN, denotes breadth, capacity, and expansiveness, while “ʿalīm” denotes all-encompassing knowledge.

These two attributes at the end of the verse are deeply meaningful. The extent of God’s giving and ability does not depend on limited human calculations. His knowledge encompasses not only the apparent amount given, but also its intention, circumstances, needs, and effects.

A person may have performed just one good deed without knowing the reach of its effects on others’ lives. Sometimes the fruit of a gift becomes apparent during that person’s own life, and sometimes it emerges in times and places they will never see.
