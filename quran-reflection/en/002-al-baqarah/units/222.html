Al-Baqarah 2:222

ۖ ۖ ۖ ۚ

And they ask you about menstruation; say: it is discomfort. So keep apart from women during menstruation, and do not approach them until they become clean. Then, when they have cleansed themselves, go to them from where The God has commanded you. Indeed, The God loves those who return and those who seek cleanliness.

Our Reflection

In many patriarchal societies, menstruation—a natural phenomenon in women’s lives—has become a pretext for considering women impure, restricting them, and pushing them to the margins. This outlook existed in various cultures before the Quran was revealed and continued after Islam in some religious rulings.

In Jewish tradition, Leviticus, chapter 15 regards even contact with a menstruating woman or the place where she sits or lies as a cause of ritual impurity.

In some Christian traditions, menstruating women were also barred from entering the church and participating in sacred rites, as stated in the second canon of Dionysius of Alexandria.

In Zoroastrian tradition, Vendidad, chapter sixteen gives instructions for isolating a menstruating woman and keeping her at a distance from water, fire, and other people.

In the hadith tradition after Islam, too, as in some Christian traditions, women have been prohibited during menstruation from acts of worship such as salat and siyam.

But what does the Quran say? Does it endorse and extend such restrictions, or correct people’s outlook?

“وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْمَحِيضِ قُلْ هُوَ أَذًى”

“And they ask you about menstruation; say: it is discomfort.”

The Quran’s answer is brief and clear: “هُوَ أَذًى”—menstruation is suffering and discomfort.

Menstruation is part of the natural functioning of a woman’s body and may be accompanied by bleeding, pain, weakness, and discomfort. The God has not presented it as a sin or a sign of women’s inferiority.

The word “أَذًى” is also used in Al-Baqarah 2:196 for physical discomfort affecting the head—a condition for which The God provides an easier course and a compensatory measure. Here, too, menstruation is presented as a bodily condition, not a spiritual defect in a woman’s being.

The God then says:

“فَاعْتَزِلُوا النِّسَاءَ فِي الْمَحِيضِ وَلَا تَقْرَبُوهُنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَطْهُرْنَ”

“So keep apart from women during menstruation, and do not approach them until they become clean.”

Does this keeping apart mean separating a woman from her family, withholding companionship and affection, or depriving her of worship?

The continuation of the verse makes the answer clear:

“فَإِذَا تَطَهَّرْنَ فَأْتُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ”

“Then, when they have cleansed themselves, go to them from where The God has commanded you.”

The expression “فَأْتُوهُنَّ” and the reference to the place of intimacy show that the subject is sexual intercourse. The next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:223, continues this same subject of marital relations.

In the context of this verse, then, the command to keep apart is a prohibition of sexual intercourse during menstruation, not the removal of a woman from her family, society, or worship.

This restriction makes sense in light of a woman’s physical discomfort and consideration for her comfort and hygiene. Rather than isolating her because of a natural phenomenon, the man is asked to observe the limits of sexual relations during this time.

The expressions “يَطْهُرْنَ” and “تَطَهَّرْنَ” are also important, since these very words have sometimes been used to infer that a woman is impure and forbidden to worship.

“يَطْهُرْنَ” refers to becoming free of the menstrual condition and the ending of bleeding, while “تَطَهَّرْنَ” denotes cleansing oneself afterward. Both expressions follow the command concerning intercourse and clarify when it may resume.

The cleanliness required for resuming sexual intercourse does not mean that a woman is inherently impure or that her relationship of worship with The God is interrupted. If such a prohibition of worship existed, its command would also need to be stated in the Quran.

Nevertheless, proponents of juristic opinion and hadith have relied on reports and transmitted sayings to extend the prohibition concerning sexual intercourse to acts of worship such as siyam and salat, although the Quran has not prescribed such a prohibition for menstruating women.

Regarding siyam (fasting), there is no Quranic command prohibiting a woman from practicing it solely because she is menstruating. On the contrary, when setting out the rules of siyam, the Quran itself takes people’s physical condition and capacity into account and makes the path of ease clear.

In Al-Baqarah 2:184, practicing siyam on other days is stated for the sick, while for those who bear siyam with severe hardship, the path of fidyah through feeding a person in need is given.

In Al-Baqarah 2:185, after recalling the ruling for the sick and the traveler, The God also says:

“يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ”

“The God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you.”

Therefore, if a woman is able to practice siyam during menstruation, the Quran has not forbidden her this worship. If pain and discomfort make her ill, she can practice siyam on other days. If siyam entails severe, exhausting hardship for her, the ruling on fidyah in Al-Baqarah 2:184 also comes into consideration according to her condition and capacity.

The same verse says:

“وَأَنْ تَصُومُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ”

“And that you practice siyam is better for you.”

A woman who is able can thus benefit from the good of siyam; and a woman who uses a Quranic means of compensation because of illness or severe hardship should not be blamed.

In Al-Baqarah 2:184, The God also says:

“فَمَنْ تَطَوَّعَ خَيْرًا فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَهُ”

“So whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them.”

If someone willingly feeds a person in need in addition to practicing siyam on other days, they have done further good; but this voluntary act cannot be turned into a mandatory obligation.

The principle of siyam, the meaning of “يُطِيقُونَهُ”, the ruling on illness, fidyah, and ease are examined in the article “Siyam in the Quran: Taqwa, Human Capacity, and the Place of Fidyah”.

Regarding salat (prayer), too, the Quran has not identified menstruation as a reason to prohibit worship.

An-Nisa 4:43 speaks of not approaching salat while intoxicated until one knows what one is saying, and also gives a ruling on purification for janabah.

In Al-Maidah 5:6, The God explains ablution, cleansing from janabah, and the provision of tayammum.

But menstruation is not janabah. Nowhere does the Quran say that a woman has no right to establish salat during menstruation.

A woman can therefore continue her salat while observing the purification prescribed in the Quran. Establishing salat and giving zakat do not disappear from her life because of menstruation.

But if severe pain, weakness, or bodily discomfort prevents a woman from performing ritual prayer with its usual movements, should she be made to suffer beyond her capacity?

The Quran itself provides clear principles for such circumstances.

The principle of capacity and ability: The God says in Al-Baqarah 2:286:

“لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا”

“The God does not burden any soul beyond its capacity.”

No woman is required to do something beyond her physical capacity.

The principle of ease in religion: In Al-Baqarah 2:185, The God desires ease for His servants, not hardship. In Al-Maidah 5:6, when setting out the rules of purification, He also says:

“مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ”

“The God does not intend to place you in hardship.”

Al-Hajj 22:78 also states that The God has placed no hardship upon people in religion.

The principle of continually establishing salat: The God says in Ta-Ha 20:14:

“وَأَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ لِذِكْرِي”

“And establish salat for My remembrance.”

Salat is not limited to the movements of ritual prayer; establishing it is connected with a person’s ongoing remembrance of and relationship with The God. Inability to perform the movements of prayer does not mean that this connection is severed. Nor has the Quran prescribed a separate ruling requiring prayers missed during menstruation to be made up.

The principle of no compulsion and hope in mercy: Al-Baqarah 2:256 states:

“لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ”

“There is no compulsion in religion.”

And in Az-Zumar 39:53, The God says:

“لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ”

“Do not despair of the mercy of The God.”

A woman who needs rest because of pain and incapacity should not be forced or threatened into performing rites beyond her ability. The God’s mercy and forgiveness are not closed to her.

The principle of dhikr and remembrance of The God: Al-Ahzab 33:41 states:

“اذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ ذِكْرًا كَثِيرًا”

“Remember The God abundantly.”

In An-Nisa 4:103, The God also speaks of remembering Him while standing, sitting, and lying down.

Remembrance of The God is therefore not dependent on one bodily position or the performance of particular movements. Even when lying in bed in pain, a person can remember their Lord.

The principle of taqwa: Al-Araf 7:201 states:

“إِنَّ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا إِذَا مَسَّهُمْ طَائِفٌ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ تَذَكَّرُوا”

“Indeed, those who practice taqwa remember when a prompting from Satan touches them.”

Taqwa is conscious, continual care over one’s conduct and relationship with The God. This responsibility is not suspended when menstruation begins.

A woman suffering from pain and incapacity during menstruation can therefore rest without setting aside remembrance of The God, taqwa, and the continual establishment of salat.

The broader meaning of salat and its distinction from ritual prayer are examined in the article “Salat in the Quran: Meaning, Establishment, and the Practice of Prayer”.

The God ends the verse with these words:

“إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ التَّوَّابِينَ وَيُحِبُّ الْمُتَطَهِّرِينَ”

“Indeed, The God loves those who return and those who seek cleanliness.”

Menstruation is not a sin for which a woman needs to repent. Tawbah is a person’s return to The God, and cleanliness must not become a pretext for considering a woman impure.

We must now ask: does menstruation mean that a woman should postpone remembrance of The God? Should she set aside repentance and return, or refrain from taqwa during this time?

How could The God love those who return and seek cleanliness, yet deprive a woman of remembering and worshiping Him because of a natural phenomenon?

Remembrance, repentance, and taqwa do not depend on menstruation ending. Taqwa itself calls upon a person always to remember The God, examine their conduct, and avoid straying from the path of truth. This continual remembrance and care are among the foundations of continually establishing salat.

The meaning of taqwa and its place in human life are examined in the article “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

The verse’s message is clear: menstruation is a natural and sometimes painful phenomenon, not a sin or a sign that a woman is impure. During this time, the Quran prohibits sexual intercourse—not siyam and salat, not remembrance and repentance, and not taqwa or a woman’s relationship with The God.

The God Himself has placed paths of ease and compensation before His servants in illness and hardship. Why, then, should opinion and transmitted reports be used to prohibit women from acts of worship that the Quran has not prohibited?

Adding such prohibitions to The God’s command does not correct the excessive attitudes of the past; it continues those same restrictions in the name of religion.
