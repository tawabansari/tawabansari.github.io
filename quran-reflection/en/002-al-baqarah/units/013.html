Al-Baqarah 2:13

And when it is said to them, “Enter faith (iman) as the people have entered faith,” they say, “Shall we enter faith as the foolish have entered faith?” Be aware: indeed, they themselves are the foolish, but they do not know.

Our Reflection

This verse continues the same case that began in verse 8. First, they claimed faith while they were not truly believers. Then they imagined that they were deceiving the One God and those who had believed, while deception returned to their own selves. After that, the Qur’an spoke of a disease in their hearts, and then showed how that disease appears socially as corruption while they imagine themselves to be reformers.

Now verse 13 removes another veil: they call the believers “foolish.” Their problem is not only a claim of faith, deception, self-deception, or corruption hidden under the name of reform. They also invert the measure of reason and faith. In their view, sincere faith, trust in truth, and surrender before the One God appear as naivety.

The verse begins: wa-idhā qīla lahum āminū kamā āmana an-nās — and when it is said to them, believe as the people have believed. The speaker is not specified, just as in verse 11: “when it is said to them.” The focus is on the call itself, not on the person calling. The call is to leave verbal claim and double play, and to enter faith sincerely like the people.

The word an-nās, the people, matters here. The Qur’an does not say: believe as a powerful class, an elite group, or a privileged party has believed. It says: as the people have believed. This expression returns faith to the human and natural field. Faith, at its root, is the truthful human response to the Real; it is not an instrument of power, superiority, class, tribe, party, or the display of intelligence.

But their answer is: anu’minu kamā āmana as-sufahā’ — shall we believe as the foolish have believed? This is not a truth-seeking question. It is a contemptuous one. They look at the people’s faith from a place of arrogance, self-regard, and imagined superiority. Simple, sincere, undeceiving faith appears to them as immaturity and lightness of mind.

Sufahā’ comes from the root S F H. Its field of meaning is connected to lightness, instability, immature judgment, and inability to weigh matters rightly. A foolish person is not merely someone who lacks information. A foolish person is someone who does not weigh values correctly: taking what is small as great, and what is great as light; preferring a near benefit over lasting truth; elevating the appearance of power over truthfulness, and deceptive cleverness over pure faith.

For that reason, the Qur’an’s answer is decisive: alā innahum humu as-sufahā’ — be aware: indeed, they themselves are the foolish. Just as in the previous verse they named themselves reformers and the Qur’an named them corrupters, here they call the believers foolish and the Qur’an returns foolishness to them. This inner inversion is a sign of the same disease of the heart: a person belittles truth and imagines their sickness to be intelligence.

This verse shows that hypocrisy can move from hidden behavior into judgment and theory-making. The hypocrite does not only deceive, and does not only name corruption as reform. He also constructs a language of rationality and superiority in order to belittle sincere faith. He sees the believer as naive because the believer is willing to sacrifice near benefit for lasting truth. He sees truthfulness as immaturity, trustworthiness as weakness, and deception as cleverness.

This pattern is not found only here. In Surah Hud, the chiefs of Noah’s people said that only the lowly and shallow-minded had followed him. This is the same arrogant logic: when truth is accepted by sincere hearts, the people of arrogance explain it away as thoughtlessness, weakness, or low social rank. But the Qur’an repeatedly shows that the measure of truth is not social position, wealth, power, or people’s language of contempt.

Within Al-Baqarah itself, verse 130 gives a deeper measure of foolishness: who turns away from the way of Abraham except one who has made his own self foolish? This shows that real foolishness is turning away from the path of truth, sincere surrender, and the Abrahamic direction. The one who treats pure faith as light has, in reality, made his own self light and weightless.

The verse ends: wa-lākin lā yaʿlamūn — but they do not know. Here there is a subtle difference from earlier verses. In verse 9 and verse 12, the Qur’an said lā yashʿurūn: they do not perceive. There, the concern was the loss of inward sensing and subtle awareness regarding self-deception and corruption. Here, it says lā yaʿlamūn: they do not know. Their claim has entered the field of knowledge, judgment, and the measure of reason. They call the believers foolish, but they do not know that real foolishness lies in themselves.

Verse 13 therefore shows one of the signs of the disease of hypocrisy: belittling sincere faith. The hypocrite imagines pure faith to be naivety, truthfulness to be immaturity, trustworthiness to be weakness, and deception to be cleverness. But the Qur’an does not leave the measure inverted. Those who have treated truth as light, and have called deception, corruption, and imagined superiority “intelligence,” are themselves the foolish, even if they call themselves knowledgeable, reformers, or people of religious standing.

As-sufahā’ comes from the root S F H and refers to lightness, instability, immature judgment, and inability to weigh matters rightly. They call the believers foolish, but the Qur’an returns foolishness to them because they have measured values upside down and belittled sincere faith.

Yaʿlamūn comes from the root ʿ L M and means knowing, recognizing, becoming aware, and something becoming clear to human understanding. In this verse, they do not know the truth of their own foolishness. The problem is not only failure to sense corruption, but ignorance of the right measure of reason, faith, and value.

Āminū and āmana come from the root A M N, connected with security, trust, assurance, faithfulness, and entering a truthful relation with the Real. The call of the verse is for them to leave verbal claim and deception and to trust the truth sincerely like the people.

An-nās opens the field of people and human beings. In this verse, faith is presented as a human and natural response to truth, not as a class, tribal, sectarian, or power-based privilege.
