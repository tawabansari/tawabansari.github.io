Al-Baqarah 2:112

Yes, indeed; whoever surrenders their whole orientation to The God while doing good has their reward with their Lord; no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse reported a claim to exclusive entry into the Garden: no one would enter unless they were Jewish or Christian. The Qur’an called that claim wishful thinking and demanded proof. Now it begins its affirmative answer with a brief “Yes, indeed.”

The decisive point is that the Qur’an does not replace one religious name with another. It does not say that Jews and Christians are mistaken because the Garden belongs exclusively to some other community. It says:

“Whoever surrenders their whole orientation to The God while doing good.”

In the preceding verse, the proposed criterion was a name. Here the criterion becomes a person’s direction and quality of being: surrendering oneself, and being someone who does good. This rejection of religious monopoly is examined in detail in Paradise Is Not the Monopoly of One Sect or One Religious Law.

The Arabic verb meaning “surrendered” (aslama) comes from S L M. Here surrender is an action, not the name of a historical community: a person surrenders their orientation to The God. For the distinction between surrender and a later religious identity, and its relationship to religion and prescribed religious law, see Religion, Surrender, and Religious Law in the Qur’an.

But the verse does not leave surrender on its own:

“While being one who does good.”

It does not merely say that this person turns toward The God. It says that they do so as someone characterized by goodness.

The word for such a person (muḥsin) comes from Ḥ S N, a family of words concerned with goodness, beauty, and making things good. Here, too, doing good is not simply the name of an isolated act: the person is characterized by it. Their direction is toward The God, and that direction finds expression in the goodness of their being and conduct.

To understand this description, three surah openings are particularly striking when read together.

At the beginning of Al-Baqarah we read:

“That is the Book, in which there is no doubt, guidance for those who guard themselves mindfully: those who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and give from what We have provided them; and those who believe in what has been sent down to you and what was sent down before you, and who are certain of the hereafter.” Al-Baqarah 2:2–4.

The Book is guidance for those who guard themselves mindfully (muttaqīn). They believe in the unseen, establish prayer, give from what they have been provided, accept revelation, and are certain of the hereafter.

The opening of An-Naml presents another formulation:

“Ṭā Sīn. These are the signs of the Qur’an and a clear Book: guidance and good news for the believers, who establish prayer, give purifying contributions, and are certain of the hereafter.” An-Naml 27:1–3.

Here the Qur’an is guidance and good news for believers: those who establish prayer, give purifying contributions, and are certain of the hereafter.

Then, at the beginning of Luqman, we read:

“These are the signs of the wise Book: guidance and mercy for those who do good, who establish prayer, give purifying contributions, and are certain of the hereafter.” Luqman 31:2–4.

Here the Book is guidance and mercy for those who do good. The description that follows is, word for word, the same as the description of believers in An-Naml:

“Who establish prayer, give purifying contributions, and are certain of the hereafter.”

This correspondence in wording is striking. In An-Naml the same actions and the same certainty about the hereafter appear under the description believers; in Luqman they appear identically under those who do good. The opening of Al-Baqarah places a closely related core of the same path under those who guard themselves mindfully: faith, prayer, giving, and certainty about the hereafter.

The order of the surahs alone cannot establish a definitive theory that every person necessarily becomes first mindfully guarded, then a believer, and finally a doer of good. But placing these three descriptions together raises a serious question: are mindful guarding, faith, and excellence in goodness merely three separate names, or growing qualities along one path?

Al-Ma’idah 5:93 makes this possibility much stronger:

“There is no blame upon those who believe and do righteous deeds for what they have consumed, provided they guard themselves mindfully, believe, and do righteous deeds; then guard themselves mindfully and believe; then guard themselves mindfully and do good. And The God loves those who do good.”

The verse’s structure deserves attention:

Mindful guarding, faith, and righteous action;
then mindful guarding and faith;
then mindful guarding and doing good.

Mindful guarding does not disappear along the way; it continues. Faith is also present in the middle. But the movement reaches “guard themselves mindfully and do good,” followed by the statement that The God loves those who do good.

From this perspective, in our reading, excellence in goodness can be the maturation of the same path of mindful guarding and faith. This does not mean that someone who does good is no longer mindfully guarded or a believer. Rather, these can be overlapping dimensions of growth. Mindful guarding protects the boundaries of truth; faith brings trust and certainty alive within a person; and excellence in goodness brings this direction into the quality of their intentions, conduct, and relationship with The God and others.

Aal Imran 3:133–134 shows the same relationship from another angle. The passage begins with the Garden prepared for those who guard themselves mindfully. Yet when their qualities are described as giving, restraining anger, and pardoning people, it concludes that The God loves those who do good. Mindful guarding and excellence in goodness do not describe two unrelated communities: goodness can be the flowering of that same guarding in the quality of one’s actions.

Thus “while being one who does good” in Al-Baqarah 2:112 should not be reduced to having performed a few good deeds. The Qur’an speaks of a person who has surrendered their direction to The God, and in whom that surrender has become goodness.

Turning Sincerely—but Toward What?

The Arabic word for “face” (wajh) also needs careful attention here. It is not limited to the physical face: it can express a person’s orientation and the direction in which they turn. Surrendering one’s face to The God portrays a person who has entrusted their direction to Him.

But sincerely imagining that one is facing toward God is not, by itself, the end of the journey. A Jew, Christian, Muslim, Hindu, or person in any other tradition may sincerely believe they are seeking God. Sincerity matters, yet a person can be sincere while some of their assumptions remain mistaken.

In An-Nisa 4:125, the Qur’an expands a formulation very close to that of Al-Baqarah 2:112:

“Who is better in religion than someone who surrenders their whole orientation to The God while doing good, and follows Abraham’s way, turning single-mindedly toward truth?”

The same two elements reappear: surrendering one’s orientation to The God and being one who does good. This time, following Abraham’s way with an upright orientation toward truth (ḥanīf) is added.

Al-An‘am 6:79 likewise places orientation and upright turning directly together, while Ar-Rum 30:30 commands a person to set their face toward religion with that same orientation toward truth.

This connection matters. A person must not only turn sincerely; they must be ready to correct their direction if what they have turned toward is inconsistent with truth. Upright turning does not allow us to exempt our religious tradition, people, family, or assumptions from being tested against what is true.

Abraham is the example at precisely this point. The Qur’an places him in opposition to the beliefs of his father and people, showing that he did not accept their inheritance simply because it was inherited. He questioned their idols and beliefs and directed himself toward the Creator of the heavens and the earth (Al-An‘am 6:74–79; Al-Anbiya 21:51–67; Ash-Shu‘ara 26:69–82).

There is no need to say that Abraham became “godless” or tried every existing religion in turn; the Qur’an says neither. What we can say is that he did not equate inherited religion with truth. To find what was true, he was prepared to examine the beliefs of his ancestors and people anew and separate himself from them if they were false. This is the disposition that distinguishes upright turning from religious partisanship.

In this sense, true surrender is not merely saying, “I am facing toward God.” We must be prepared to reexamine anything standing between us and truth, even if it is regarded as sacred and inherited.

The Same Criterion as Al-Baqarah 2:62

Reading this verse alongside Al-Baqarah 2:62 makes the Qur’an’s answer to exclusive claims of salvation clearer:

“Those who believed, the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians—whoever believes in The God and the Last Day and does righteous deeds—their reward is with their Lord; no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.”

That verse names several religious communities, but does not make their names the final criterion. All are assessed anew by faith in The God and the Last Day, and righteous action. Al-Ma’idah 5:69 repeats the same principle in a closely similar form.

Now, in 2:112, following the claim about being “Jewish or Christian,” the Qur’an again introduces no alternative religious name. It expresses the same criterion in another way: surrendering one’s orientation to The God and doing good.

An-Nisa 4:124–125 likewise places these two formulations one after the other: faith and righteous action in one verse, then surrendering one’s orientation and doing good in the next. These concepts are not rivals in the Qur’an; they are closely related dimensions of one reality.

Why “To The God,” but “With Their Lord”?

One of the verse’s most subtle choices of wording is this change of expression:

“Surrenders their whole orientation to The God.”

But then:

“Their reward is with their Lord.”

One’s orientation is surrendered to The God (Allah); the reward is with that person’s Lord (Rabb).

The same movement appears in Al-Baqarah 2:62:

“Whoever believes in The God … their reward is with their Lord.”

Faith is in The God; the reward is with their Lord.

This is not merely a coincidence confined to these two verses. The same change of expression occurs elsewhere, including Al-Baqarah 2:262, Aal Imran 3:199, and Al-Hadid 57:19: action, faith, or orientation is connected with The God, but when standing and reward are discussed, the wording becomes “with their Lord.”

Linguistically, this is not simply a change of pronoun. It is a change from the designation The God to Lord, accompanied by a personal possessive: “his” or “their.”

The designation “The God” emphasizes the one divine authority: the One to whom a person must surrender their direction and in whom they place faith. “Lord,” however, emphasizes that same God’s relationship with the human being: that person’s own Lord and Nurturing Master.

This matters in the context of exclusive claims to salvation. A person’s reward is not held by their people, their religious law, or their religious class. No sect has become the treasurer of divine reward:

“With their Lord.”

With that person’s own Lord.

This choice of word makes the relationship personal and direct. Someone who has surrendered their orientation to The God is under the nurturing lordship of that same Lord: the One who knows them, nurtures them, sees their deeds, and with whom their reward rests.

For a detailed examination of the different uses of the Arabic designations for The God and Lord, see The God and Lord in Qur’anic Language.

From One Person to All Who Meet the Criterion

The verse begins with an individual:

“Whoever surrenders … that person’s reward is with that person’s Lord.”

Whoever does this has their own reward with their own Lord. The Arabic possessives here are singular.

At the end, however, the Arabic switches to the plural:

“No fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.”

Here “them” and “they” refer to all the people covered by the promise.

The opening “whoever” does not identify one particular individual. It includes anyone who meets the criterion. The sentence therefore begins with the individual and gathers everyone described into a shared promise.

Precisely the same ending appears in Al-Baqarah 2:62. In 2:38, following guidance had also been connected with this promise, which recurs in later parts of the surah.

Fear and grief should not be confined to fear of one particular thing or sorrow over one particular thing. The verse speaks of a broader security. Nor does this necessarily mean that such a person never experiences a moment of fear or grief in worldly life: the Qur’an itself portrays righteous servants in situations of fear and sorrow. The promise here concerns their ultimate outcome and a deeper security.

Al-Baqarah 2:111 said: if you claim that the Garden belongs exclusively to your religious identity, bring your proof.

Al-Baqarah 2:112 gives the Qur’an’s answer. Instead of adding a new sectarian name, it describes a person who has surrendered their direction to The God and attained excellence in goodness. Their reward is not with their community, but with their own Lord.

In our reading, mindful guarding, faith, and excellence in goodness are not three separate walls. The Qur’an repeatedly shows them intertwined, and Al-Ma’idah 5:93 carries the movement through to “guard themselves mindfully and do good.” Excellence in goodness can be the maturation of the path formed through mindful care and trusting faith, where a person’s inward direction toward The God becomes visible in the goodness of their being and actions.
