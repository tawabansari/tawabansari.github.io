Al-Baqarah 2:92

And indeed Moses came to you with clear signs; then, after him, you took the calf, while you were wrongdoers.

Our Reflection

The verse continues to test the claim made in the preceding verse: “We believe in what was sent down to us.” This time, the Qur’an does not appeal to a prophet outside their tradition; it returns to Moses himself:

“And indeed Moses came to you with clear signs”; Moses came to them bearing clear evidence.

If the criterion of faith is what was “sent down to us,” Moses should be the clearest witness to this claim. The message had come from the very people with whom they now proudly identified, and it was accompanied by clear signs. The problem was not a shortage of prophets or the obscurity of the evidence.

But their history also had another side:

“Then, after him, you took the calf”; afterward, they adopted the calf for themselves.

The contradiction in the claim becomes apparent. They said that they believed only in what had been sent down to their own community; to test this assertion, the Qur’an does not turn to a foreign book or prophet. It sets that same Moses before them: if affiliation with your own prophet is sufficient to establish faith, how do you explain the worship of the calf after Moses?

Having a prophet is not the same as following a prophet, just as possessing scripture is not the same as submitting to scripture.

Surah Al-A‘raf makes the image of the calf clearer:

“After him, the people of Moses took from their ornaments a calf, a body that made a lowing sound.” Al-A‘raf 7:148

After Moses, his people made a calf from their own ornaments. What later occupied an improper position was not something that had come down from heaven; it had been fashioned from their own possessions.

This image offers deep grounds for reflection. Sometimes what stands between us and The God’s command has not been imposed upon us from outside; we have made it ourselves, invested it with value, grown accustomed to it, and finally sanctified it so thoroughly that parting with it has become difficult.

In Surah Ta-Ha, too, the Samiri says at the end of his explanation:

“Thus my own self made it seem attractive to me.” Ta-Ha 20:96

My own self made it appear attractive to me in this way.

Before this, the Qur’an also speaks of “the trace of the messenger,” but it does not explain the precise nature of this “trace” to us, and we need not fill the gap in the text with conjecture. What the verse itself explicitly says is sufficient: the Samiri understands his act as something his own self had made attractive to him. Deviation does not always appear ugly to the person involved; in their mind it may appear beautiful, justified, and even religious.

The verse’s ending makes the judgment clear:

“While you were wrongdoers”; they were acting unjustly.

Taking the calf was not merely a ritual mistake; the Qur’an calls it wrongdoing (ẓulm). The root Ẓ L M is connected with exceeding bounds and something occupying an improper place. Something made by human hands was placed in a position to which it had no right, and servitude was transferred from The God to something the people themselves had made.

History shows that this disease did not end with the breaking of that calf in the wilderness. Centuries later, 1 Kings, chapter 12, recounts that Jeroboam, after the separation of the northern kingdom of Israel, made two golden calves, placing one at Bethel and the other at Dan. He discouraged the people from going to Jerusalem to worship and even used the memory of the exodus from Egypt to legitimize these calves.

This event is not the direct meaning of “the calf” in Al-Baqarah 2:92; the calf in this verse belongs to the episode in Moses’ time. Yet the episode of Jeroboam shows how a deviation can return generations later in a new form. This time the calf was not merely the product of collective excitement; religion, identity, and political expediency were also intertwined in its creation.

This same issue keeps the verse alive for us. A calf is not always made of gold. It may be made of an opinion, a report, a legal ruling, a tradition, an institution, or a system that we ourselves have produced and then sanctified so completely that we consider criticism of it to be criticism of The God.

Human creations are not necessarily false. Jurisprudence, exegesis, religious institutions, and social traditions may result from sincere human effort. The problem begins when a human creation is elevated beyond its proper position and acquires the right to rule in opposition to The God’s clear command.

Faith is not a deed of ownership to a book; it is fidelity to the truth that the book has brought. The calf is made anew when something we ourselves have created takes the place of The God’s command, and to preserve it we become willing even to set aside the Book’s clear message.
