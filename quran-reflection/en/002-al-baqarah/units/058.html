Al-Baqarah 2:58

And when We said: Enter this town, and eat from it wherever you wish, in easeful abundance (raghadan); and enter the gate in prostrating humility (sujjadan) and say: a lowering of the burden (ḥiṭṭah). We will forgive your faults, and We will soon increase the doers of good (muḥsinīn).

Our Reflection

This verse comes after the shade of the cloud, the mann, the salwa, and the command to eat from the wholesome things. In the previous verse, God placed the Children of Israel in the desert under the covering of mercy and provision. Now, in verse 58, a new stage begins: entering the town, encountering abundance, and facing the ethical test of entering a social space.

Udkhulū hādhihi al-qaryah means: enter this town. The Qur'an does not name the town. If its name were necessary for our guidance, it would have been stated. So the center of the verse is not geography; it is the etiquette of entry. The town comes from a root connected with gathering and coming together. Here the town is the place where people, provision, resources, relations, and social responsibilities gather.

In the desert, life was more scattered. Provision came as mann and salwa, and the human being was less directly faced with ownership, storing, sharing resources, and social order. But the town means entering a space where provision gathers, people live beside one another, others' rights become present, and the human self is tested by abundance and accumulation.

So entering the town is not merely entering a settlement; it is entering a new stage of training. In the desert, their test was patience, trust in provision, and gratitude. In the town, their test is humility, social order, the etiquette of entry, and the right way of dealing with abundance. Every ni'mah has its own kind of test.

God says: fa-kulū minhā ḥaythu shi'tum raghadan. Eat from it, wherever you wish, in abundance. This expression shows that the town was not a place of narrowness, deprivation, and hunger for them. There was openness, spaciousness, and abundant provision there. After years of slavery, desert, anxiety, and testing, the field of abundance now opens before them.

The word raghadan is very important. It means easeful abundance, spaciousness in life, provision without constriction, and consumption not accompanied by pressure and hardship. This word had already appeared in the story of Adam, where it was said: eat from the garden, wherever you wish, in abundance. So here too a kind of test of abundance stands before the human being. When provision is limited, the human being is tested through patience; but when provision is abundant, he is tested through gratitude, justice, and the etiquette of consumption.

In the story of Adam, eating in abundance came with an ethical boundary: do not approach that tree. Here too eating in abundance is not released without condition. Immediately after the openness of food comes the command of prostrating humility and hittah. This means that abundance, in the logic of the Qur'an, is dangerous without ethical boundary and humility.

Abundance does not always reform the human being. Sometimes poverty breaks the human being; but sometimes abundance ruins the human being from within. If a person does not enter ni'mah with khushu', he begins to imagine the ni'mah as his guaranteed right. If he does not enter the town with prostrating humility, abundance becomes entitlement, accumulation, arrogance, and irresponsible consumption. For this reason, God does not simply say: enter and eat. He says: enter the gate in prostrating humility.

Entering through the gate (bāb) in a state of prostrating humility (sujūd: humility, submission, khushu', and etiquette) should not be reduced immediately to bending the body and placing the forehead on the ground. The structure of the verse itself shows that the subject is entry and movement: pass through the gate while being sujjadan. A fixed bodily prostration, with the forehead on the ground, is a state of stopping; but here the speech is about movement and crossing a threshold. So prostration in this verse must have a much wider meaning: entering with humility, submission, etiquette, khushu', and the lowering of the self before God's command, which means entering through the gate while abiding by the laws. In our contemporary terms, the best equivalent is migrants entering a host country through the lawful path, completing the official process, and obeying the laws of the host country. It should also be noted that for a believer, entering a land and accepting its laws is legitimate and possible only when the structure and laws of that society do not force him into shirk, kufr, or violating the definite principles of his faith. If entering a house requires selling one's belief and breaking one's covenant with God, that threshold is no longer a place to enter; the human being is first responsible to preserve the sanctuary of tawhid.

Of course, this does not mean removing bodily prostration from the Qur'an. The Qur'an knows bodily prostration too. But it does not reduce prostration to one outward movement. The sun, moon, stars, mountains, trees, living creatures, and many among humankind prostrate to God; and the prostration of each is according to its own existence. So the essence of prostration is being placed in one's proper position before the One worthy of worship.

The relation between prostration and khushu' appears very clearly in Surah Al-Isra. God says about those who had been given knowledge before: when the ayat are recited to them, they fall upon their chins in prostration; they say, glory be to our Lord, surely the promise of our Lord is fulfilled; and they fall upon their chins again, weeping, while the ayat increase them in khushu' (Al-Isra 17:107-109).

This is a very beautiful image. There, true knowledge, hearing the ayah, prostration, weeping, and the increase of khushu' come together. So prostration is not only a bodily motion, and khushu' is not only an inward feeling. When the ayah settles in the human being, the inside becomes humbled, and the body carries the sign of that humility. Prostration without khushu' is an empty form; and khushu' with no effect in behavior is an unfinished claim.

So in verse 58, entering the gate in prostrating humility means entering a new stage of life with complete humbleness: not with the pose of a conqueror, not with ethnic arrogance, not with entitlement, not with the feeling of ownership. They were to enter the town, but not like people who say: this ni'mah is our right. They were to enter like people who know that if God had not pardoned, raised them again, provided for them, and opened the path, they had nothing from themselves.

Wa-qūlū ḥiṭṭah: say, hittah. This word comes from the root Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ, meaning to bring down, lower, set down, and lighten a burden. In ordinary life, a load is lowered from the back of a mount so its heaviness is removed. In this verse, hittah means setting down the heavy burdens of fault, arrogance, calf-worship, entitlement, slave-fears, and old claims.

This word is harmonious with sujjadan. Prostration means the human being lowers himself. Hittah means the heavy burdens of fault and self-importance are lowered from the human being's shoulders. The one who enters the gate in prostrating humility must also say hittah with his tongue and inner state: O God, set down our burdens, let our faults fall away, reduce our claims, and let us enter this new stage lightened.

So entering the town is not merely a change of location; it is entering a new ethical school. After the desert, they were to enter collective life. Collective life is impossible without humility. If each person enters society with a Pharaonic self, ethnic entitlement, fear of scarcity, and the lust of accumulation, the town becomes a field of conflict, hoarding, corruption, and wrongdoing. For this reason, the first etiquette of entry is prostrating humility.

Naghfir lakum khaṭāyākum: We will forgive your faults. Here forgiveness is connected to the etiquette of entry: enter, be humble, say hittah. This means forgiveness in the Qur'an is not a slogan detached from behavioral change. Faults fall away when the human being enters a new gate with humility, not when he carries the same old self into a new city.

The word khaṭāyākum is also important. A fault is not merely a small mistake; it is a slipping away from the right path. In the previous verses, the Children of Israel had slipped several times: the calf, lack of trust, demanding to see God, ingratitude before ni'mah. Now a path is shown to them by which the burden of these faults can be lifted from their shoulders: entry with prostrating humility and saying hittah.

At the end He says: wa-sanazīdu al-muḥsinīn. We will soon increase the doers of good. This sentence is very subtle. God does not say only: We will forgive your faults. He says: We will increase the muhsinin. This means that on this path the human being is not only purified from fault; he can also grow. Forgiveness is not the end of the path; it is the beginning of ihsan.

A muhsin is one who makes action good: someone who uses ni'mah rightly, enters society with etiquette, sees the rights of others, and moves from humility into good conduct. Ihsan is not only a good inward feeling; it is a higher quality of action. When the human being enters the gate in prostrating humility and says hittah, he can then move from seeking forgiveness into the stage of ihsan.

Sanazīd comes from the root Z Y D: to increase, cause to grow, and add. But this increase is not only an increase in wealth or food. The verse says: We will increase the muhsinin. In other words, the muhsin himself grows: his capacity, his ethics, his light, his ability to do good, and his share of guidance are increased.

In the Qur'an, true increase is connected with gratitude, ihsan, zakat, spending, and pure growth. If you give thanks, you are increased. If you give from your wealth for purification and growth, that is true increase. But riba is outward increase and not true growth. Riba makes wealth appear greater, but it does not increase with God. By contrast, zakat and spending may reduce the outward amount of wealth, but they grow the self, society, and blessing. So wa-sanazīdu al-muḥsinīn takes us into the Qur'anic logic of increase: increase belongs to the one who enters ni'mah beautifully and responsibly, not to the one who turns ni'mah into greed and superiority.

This verse shows the opening of provision from one side: eat, wherever you wish, in abundance. From the other side, it states the etiquette of that opening: enter the gate in prostrating humility and say hittah. This means abundance without humility is dangerous. If the human being takes only the first part of the verse and forgets the second, ni'mah turns into corruption. Eating in abundance without prostrating humility and hittah does not make the human being grateful; it makes him entitled.

From this angle, verse 58 continues verse 57. In verse 57, God said: eat from the wholesome things of what We provided you, and warned that they did not wrong God but wronged their own selves. In verse 58, food and abundance appear again; but this time together with the etiquette of entry, prostrating humility, hittah, forgiveness, and ihsan. It is as if the Qur'an says: wholesome provision, if not joined to khushu' and ihsan, returns again to wrongdoing against the self.

This verse is also clear for the human being today. Whenever a person enters a new stage of ni'mah, a city, power, comfort, a home, work, a country, knowledge, or freedom, he must enter through the gate in prostrating humility. That means with humbleness, respect for the order of truth, acceptance of responsibility, and readiness to set down the burdens of fault. If the human being enters ni'mah with arrogance, that same ni'mah will test and break him.

So the lesson of the verse is this: entering abundance requires etiquette. Eating from ni'mah requires gratitude. Entering society requires prostrating humility. Freedom from the past requires hittah. And true growth belongs to the muhsinin: those who do not merely seek consumption, but turn ni'mah into ihsan.

Cross-References
  • Al-Baqarah 2:35— The command to Adam and his spouse to eat in abundance from the garden; verse 58, with “wherever you wish, in abundance,” recalls the test of abundance and the ethical boundary of consumption.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:45-46— Khushu' and returning to the Lord; useful for understanding that prostration in verse 58 must be joined to inward khushu'.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:57— Eating from the wholesome things of provision and the warning about wronging the self; verse 58 shows a new stage of eating, abundance, and responsibility.
  • Al-Isra 17:107-109— Prostration, weeping, and the increase of khushu' when hearing the ayat; an important witness for the relation between outward prostration, inward khushu', and the effect of the ayah on the human being.
  • Ibrahim 14:7— If you give thanks, I will surely increase you; useful for understanding the relation between gratitude, ni'mah, and true increase at the end of verse 58.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:261— The image of growth and increase in spending; a witness that Qur'anic increase is not merely an outward increase of wealth, but includes growth and blessing.
  • Ar-Rum 30:39— Riba does not increase with God, while zakat given for God's Face brings increase; a witness for the distinction between outward increase and true increase.
