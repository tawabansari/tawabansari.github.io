Al-Baqarah 2:147

ۖ

The truth is from your Lord; so do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute.

Our Reflection

After several verses about the direction of prayer, knowledge, recognition, and concealing truth, the Qur’an gives a brief but weighty statement:

“The truth is from your Lord.”

In verse 144, the People of the Book were described as knowing that this matter was the truth from their Lord. Verse 145 spoke of knowledge that had reached the prophet. Verse 146 discussed clear recognition and a group who concealed truth while knowing it. Now the prophet himself is addressed directly:

“So do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute.”

The final word, often translated simply as “doubters” (al-mumtarīn), has a broader meaning in its root and Qur’anic usage.

It comes from the root M R Y and the verb imtarā. The same family includes miryah, mirāʾ, tumārī, and mumārāh. These words concern more than a simple mental condition: they also encompass wavering, repeated reconsideration, contention, and disputation over a matter.

In older Arabic usage, marā was used for stroking and pressing an animal’s udder to draw out milk. In the realm of speech, the same root came to encompass drawing out and turning over a question, arguing about it, and continuing the back-and-forth over it.

For this reason, contentious doubt (imtirāʾ) should not simply be equated with doubt without explanation.

The Qur’an itself illustrates this distinction clearly in Yunus 10:94:

“If you are in doubt about what We have sent down to you, ask those who read the Book before you. The truth has certainly come to you from your Lord, so do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute.” — Yunus 10:94

The beginning of that verse uses the word “doubt” (shakk), while its ending uses “those engaged in contentious doubt” (al-mumtarīn). If the two meant exactly the same thing, choosing different words within one verse would be without purpose.

Doubt can be a natural stage in coming to know. Someone does not know something, is uncertain about it, asks questions, and investigates. Yunus 10:94 itself opens the way to asking when in doubt: “Ask.”

Once truth has arrived, however, the issue is no longer merely not knowing. The danger is that a truth made clear will be turned over again and again and returned to a field of contention and wavering.

Contentious doubt is therefore distinct from sincere inquiry.

Inquiry seeks an answer. Contentious doubt can become a condition in which, even after an answer has become clear, a person continues to drag it into back-and-forth dispute.

The same structure appears in this verse of Al-Baqarah:

“The truth is from your Lord, so do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute.”

Truth is from your Lord; do not return it to the cycle of contentious doubt.

But the fact that the prophet himself is addressed also deserves attention.

Why does the Qur’an tell Muhammad not to be among those engaged in contentious doubt?

If the prophet has recognized truth through revelation, is such a warning meaningless?

The answer must be sought in the Qur’an’s own portrayal of the messenger. Receiving revelation does not remove Muhammad from humanity. The Qur’an commands him to say that he is a human being like others, to whom revelation is given. He is not an angel, does not possess God’s treasures, and has no independent knowledge of the unseen. The knowledge he has is what God has taught him.

In Al-Kahf 18:110, we read:

“Say: I am only a human being like you, to whom it is revealed that your god is one God. Whoever hopes to meet their Lord should therefore do righteous work and associate no one in the worship of their Lord.” — Al-Kahf 18:110

Being a messenger does not cancel human nature.

People are affected by others’ words, feel grief and social pressure, and sometimes need reminders to remain firm in what they have recognized. The Qur’an speaks of Muhammad’s own chest tightening because of his opponents’ words (Al-Hijr 15:97), and at times directly corrects some of his decisions (At-Tawbah 9:43; At-Tahrim 66:1).

The prohibition, “Do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute,” need not therefore be a merely figurative address really intended only for others. It can genuinely address the prophet himself.

He knows the truth, but remains human.

Being human means that knowing a truth does not necessarily make someone immune to every psychological and social pressure.

In this context, different groups are speaking about the direction of prayer. Some do not accept the prophet’s direction, he does not accept theirs, and they do not even share a single direction among themselves. Repeated objections and disagreements can return what has become clear to a person to a state of wavering.

Here, the Qur’an gives the prophet a point of stability:

“The truth is from your Lord.”

Truth is not measured by this group’s approval or that group’s approval. Numerous opponents, repeated objections, and prolonged argument do not invalidate a truth established through knowledge.

This also carries another lesson: being guided does not mean no longer needing guidance.

The prophet himself is repeatedly addressed with commands to be patient, remain steadfast, follow, avoid a certain action, or not be a certain way. Revelation is not merely a body of information delivered once, after which the work is finished. Throughout his life, it guides, reminds, and strengthens him.

The same is true of other people.

Someone may recognize a truth after research, yet pressure from family, society, a denomination, a teacher, or the majority may drive them to turn it over repeatedly—not to understand more, but to find a way to preserve a cherished former belief.

At that point, doubt is no longer simply a stage on the way to knowledge. It may become contentious doubt.

Of course, this boundary is not always easy to identify. People should not close themselves to questions and reconsideration on the grounds that “the truth is clear to me.” What they take for certainty may be the product of habit or prejudice. But just as inquiry can serve the search for truth, perpetual doubt can become a means of escaping a conclusion one does not want to accept.

The Qur’an distinguishes between these two paths.

In Yunus 10:94, its response to doubt is ask; after truth has arrived, it says not to be among those engaged in contentious doubt.

In Al-Baqarah 2:147, after repeated discussion of knowledge, recognition, and truth, the same statement appears:

“The truth is from your Lord, so do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute.”

The same expression also appears in Al Imran 3:60 after the discussion of Jesus:

“The truth is from your Lord, so do not be among those who draw it into doubt and dispute.” — Al Imran 3:60

In both places, truth stands amid religious disagreements and disputes. The Qur’an’s response is not to forbid questions, but to say that once truth has become clear, endless argument should not be allowed to draw it back into contentious doubt.

Here too, the messenger’s humanity is part of the lesson. The Qur’an does not describe a prophet who, upon becoming a messenger, knows everything, is unaffected by human pressures, and needs no reminders. It speaks of a human being who receives revelation, and whom that revelation keeps on the path of truth.

Perhaps this is precisely what makes him someone other human beings can follow.

If the prophet were a being who never experienced pressure, needed no reminders, and had no capacity to be affected as a human being, many of the Qur’an’s direct commands to him would have little meaning for our lives.

But he is human, and is guided within that humanity.

The lesson is therefore more than “do not doubt.”

The deeper lesson is: seek truth and ask questions on the way to understanding; but when truth has become clear through knowledge, do not let pressure, attachment, and argument plunge it back into an endless cycle of contentious doubt.
