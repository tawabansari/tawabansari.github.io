Al-Baqarah 2:152

So remember Me, and I will remember you; give thanks to Me, and do not reject Me.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse spoke of God sending a messenger who recites His signs to people, purifies them and helps them grow, and teaches them the Book and wisdom. Now the address immediately returns to the direct relationship between the servant and the Lord: “Remember Me, and I will remember you.” The messenger conveys and teaches the divine message, but does not become a gatekeeper or intermediary required for communication between a person and God. God does not tell people to find someone to mention them before Him. He calls His servants Himself and Himself promises to remember them.

This relationship is explicit in Al-Baqarah 2:186, where God describes Himself as near and attributes the response to His servants’ prayers directly to Himself. Ghafir 40:60 likewise invites people to call upon Him directly. Prophets, scripture, and divine rites therefore do not remove the direct relationship between people and their Lord. No individual or religious institution can claim to be the exclusive channel through which remembrance and prayer reach God.

Remembrance belongs to the root DH K R and concerns recalling something and keeping it present in awareness. Remembering God is more than repeating His name aloud, although verbal remembrance has its place. Those who remember God do not forget their relationship with Him in their decisions and conduct. When they possess power, they remember their responsibility; when confronted with an unwelcome truth, they do not dismiss it merely because it conflicts with their wishes. Ta-Ha 20:14 connects establishing prayer with remembering God, and Ar-Ra‘d 13:28 identifies remembrance of God as a source of reassurance for hearts. Remembrance should be present in life, not only on the tongue.

In response to human remembrance comes the promise: “I will remember you.” This should not be understood as a person’s recollection after forgetting. God is not unaware of His servants, and His knowledge does not require reminders. The verse speaks of divine response and attention toward servants who remember their Lord, without explaining every aspect of that response. The same root appears on both sides of the relationship, but human remembrance and God’s remembrance are not of the same nature or manner.

This promise can also be better understood in light of the divine names “the Entirely Merciful” and “the Especially Merciful.” The former directs attention to encompassing mercy that provides life, sustenance, opportunities for understanding, and the possibility of return—even before a person knows or remembers God. The latter directs attention to close, continuing, special mercy manifest in the servant’s conscious relationship with the Lord through faith, remembrance, repentance, and reform. In Al-Ahzab 33:41, Al-Ahzab 33:42, and Al-Ahzab 33:43, the Qur’an first calls believers to remember God abundantly, then speaks of divine attention that brings them from darkness into light and describes God as merciful toward believers. In Al-A‘raf 7:156, God speaks of mercy encompassing everything and then of ordaining mercy for those mindful of Him and those who believe. Remembering God can thus be understood as a way of receiving that close, nurturing mercy which accompanies the servant along the path of guidance and return. For a broader study of these names, see The Qur’anic Root R Ḥ M.

The Qur’an then places gratitude directly against rejection: “Give thanks to Me, and do not reject Me.” This contrast matters for understanding both terms. Gratitude means more than saying “thank God”: it means recognizing a favor, not disregarding its value, and responding rightly to what one has received. Rejection or ingratitude belongs to the root K F R, associated with covering and disregarding. Here it stands against gratitude: people may receive God’s favor and guidance yet disregard what they owe in response, or act as though that favor had never reached them.

Real gratitude must be visible in conduct. Gratitude for wealth, for example, means more than saying “praise be to God” after obtaining money. It appears in giving charity, helping those in need, respecting others’ rights, not taking usury, and avoiding waste. Those with financial resources must act responsibly in how they acquire and spend them. If people use their wealth to pollute water and soil, destroy animals’ habitats, or harm the environment and wildlife for greater profit, a few words of thanks cannot make them grateful for that favor. Al-Baqarah 2:275 prohibits usury; Al-An‘am 6:141 discusses respecting others’ rights and avoiding waste; and Al-Baqarah 2:205 identifies destroying crops and offspring as corruption on earth. These are practical examples of the responsibility that comes with receiving a favor.

The same applies to other gifts. Gratitude for knowledge means using it in service of truth and not avoiding learning, teaching, or correcting mistakes. Gratitude for power appears in justice, preventing oppression, and respecting the rights of those unable to defend themselves. Gratitude for health means caring for the body and using one’s strength for healthy living and good deeds. Each favor calls for a fitting response. People may express thanks verbally while turning the very gift they received into a means of harming themselves and others.

This meaning is not confined to the present verse. In Ibrahim 14:7, gratitude and rejection again stand opposite one another. In An-Naml 27:40, Solomon recognizes his Lord’s favor as a test of whether he will give thanks or be ungrateful, and recalls that whoever gives thanks does so for their own benefit, since God is free of need. For further examination, see Gratitude in the Qur’an: From Saying “Thank God” to Responding Responsibly to Favor and Who Is a Kāfir, and What Is Kufr?.

At the beginning of the address to the Children of Israel, God repeatedly called them to remember His favors. Yet receiving scripture and messengers did not itself guarantee lasting guidance. Muhammad’s community has now likewise received the favor of a messenger, the Book, and wisdom. Gratitude for this favor means learning the divine signs, not concealing their truth, and allowing teaching and purification to affect conduct and life. We cannot thank God for the Qur’an yet set aside a command from that same Qur’an when it conflicts with our interests and inherited beliefs.

After the long discussion of Abraham, the House, the direction of prayer, and Muhammad’s mission, the Qur’an returns people to the foundation of their relationship of service to God. The House, direction of prayer, messenger, and Book each have their place, but none takes the place of the one God. People must remember Him themselves and make gratitude for His favors visible in life. God does not need our remembrance or thanks. We are the ones who, by remembering Him, avoid heedlessness, and by giving thanks for His favors, avoid wasting them.
