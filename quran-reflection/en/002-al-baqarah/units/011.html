Al-Baqarah 2:11

And when it is said to them, “Do not cause corruption (fasad) in the earth,” they say, “We are only reformers (muslihun).”

Our Reflection

This verse continues the path of the previous verses. In verse 8, the issue was the claim of faith; in verse 9, deception and self-deception; and in verse 10, a disease in the heart that grows through false claims and flight from truthfulness. Now verse 11 shows how that disease appears in the social field: corruption in the earth, joined with the claim of reform.

The verse begins: wa-idhā qīla lahum — and when it is said to them. The speaker is not specified. This matters, because the Qur’an moves attention away from the identity of the speaker and toward the warning itself. What matters is that they are told: lā tufsidū fī al-arḍ — do not cause corruption in the earth. But their response is: innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn — we are only reformers.

The danger here is not only corruption. The greater danger is that corruption becomes covered with the name of reform. A human being may not see their own action as ruin, wrongdoing, falsehood, or disruption of order. Instead, they may give it beautiful names: reform, public interest, order, security, protective zeal, defense of religion, defense of family, or service to people. When the measure of truth and rightness becomes diseased inside a person, they may see corruption as reform and view real reform as a threat.

The word al-arḍ in this verse is not only soil or the surface of the earth. It is the field of human life. The earth is where human beings build relationships, carry trusts, establish justice or wrongdoing, preserve life or ruin it, build trust or break it, and move society toward soundness or corruption. So corruption in the earth may include corruption of ethics, trust, family, judgment, religion, economy, speech, and human relationships.

Their claim is also weighty: innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn. They do not merely say that they reform. They place themselves in the identity of reformers. Innamā carries emphasis and restriction, as if they are saying: we do nothing but reform. They also use a nominal construction: “we are reformers,” not merely “we reform.” This difference shows that they have built for themselves a stable and confident identity called reform. In such a state, a person no longer defends only an action; they defend the image they have made of themselves.

The Qur’an gives a clear example of this inversion in the chapter of Joseph. Joseph’s brothers did not deal with their father or Joseph from a position of truthfulness and justice. They said: “Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we are, while we are a strong group.” In their view, number, group strength, and collective power should have been the measure of superiority and entitlement. They thought that because they had more numbers and strength, they had more right.

Then they said: “Kill Joseph or cast him into some land, so your father’s face may be free for you, and after that you may become a righteous people.” This verse is deeply striking. They chose the path of wrongdoing, separation, falsehood, and grief-making, but they still left a place in their minds for righteousness afterward. It is as though they wanted to remove Joseph, solve the family problem, and then become righteous.

This is the disease of the criterion. When partisanship, envy, group power, or reliance on number enters the heart, a person may begin to see soundness in being many, dominant, tribal, partisan, or stronger in force. The Qur’an also warns in the chapter of At-Takathur: “Competition in increase distracted you.” This connection is secondary here, but it shows that seeking increase, when separated from truth and sincerity, can distort a person’s vision of soundness and corruption.

Al-A‘raf 7:56 also clarifies this meaning: “Do not cause corruption in the earth after its reform.” The earth, in the order of creation and guidance, is not a meaningless or lawless field. The possibility of soundness, balance, mercy, justice, and right living has been placed within it. Corruption means interfering with this order and pulling relationships, ethics, society, and the earth out of the path of health and benefit.

So verse 11 is not only about a group in the past. It is a mirror for every society and every human being. Whenever someone uses the language of religion, reform, security, justice, or public interest to cover truth, while the result of their action is corruption in the earth, they stand in the danger of this verse. The measure of reform is not the name a person gives to their work. The measure of reform is return to truth, sincerity, justice, trust, mercy, and the real health of society.

The next verse makes the Qur’anic response to this claim clearer: they say, “We are reformers,” but the Qur’an removes the veil and says that they are the very corrupters, yet they do not perceive. Therefore, the claim of reform is not enough. One must look at what a person’s action actually builds in the earth: soundness or corruption.

In this verse, tufsidū comes from the root F S D, connected with corruption, ruin, breakdown of soundness, and departure from a beneficial and balanced state. Its use here is corruption in the earth: disrupting ethical, social, faithful, and human order, even if it is covered with the language of reform.

The word al-arḍ comes from the root A R Ḍ and points to the earth, the expanse of living, a place of settlement, and the field of human action. In this verse, it is the field of human life, where human action can produce either soundness or corruption.

The word muṣliḥūn comes from the root Ṣ L Ḥ, connected with soundness, rightness, well-being, reform, and restoring something to a proper state. They identify themselves as reformers, while the context shows that their claim does not match the truth of their action.

Qīla and qālū come from the root Q W L, meaning to say, speak, express, or declare. The verse creates a contrast between the warning addressed to them and their claimed response: they are told not to cause corruption, but they say they are reformers.
