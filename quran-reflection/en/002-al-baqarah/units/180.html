Al-Baqarah 2:180

ۖ

It is prescribed for you that, when death approaches one of you, if they leave property, they should make a bequest for parents and close relatives in a proper manner—a duty incumbent on the mindful.

Our Reflection

In this verse, The God prescribes a bequest for parents and close relatives and calls it a duty incumbent on the mindful. The wisdom of this command is that people’s needs are not identical, and distributing an inheritance solely according to fixed shares will not always meet the differing circumstances of family members.

An elderly parent may be frail and need greater support. Among the children, one may have higher treatment and care costs because of a physical disability, illness, or developmental circumstances. For example, an autistic child may depend on others for care to such an extent that they cannot live independently, earn an adequate income, or meet their everyday needs. Such a person may need help from family, a caregiver, or a nurse throughout their life. Does justice not require that these real needs also be considered when distributing the family’s assets?

Here the wisdom of a bequest becomes clear. Through the expression “بِالْمَعْرُوفِ”, The God has opened a way to take account of propriety, fairness, and the different circumstances of close relatives. What is proper does not mean that someone may disregard relatives’ rights out of greater affection for one child, hostility toward another, or personal inclination. A bequest should rest on fairness and actual needs, not discrimination and favoritism. The verse does not set a fixed amount for the bequest, allowing different needs to be considered. At the same time, a bequest must not become a means of unjustly depriving or harming others; An-Nisa 4:12 also includes the qualification “غَيْرَ مُضَارٍّ”—without causing harm. In An-Nisa 4:9, the Quran particularly draws attention to concern for vulnerable children who may be left behind.

Another wisdom may also lie in the expression “إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ”. The God connects making a bequest with the approach of death, a time when a person can better understand the actual circumstances of their family and close relatives. If someone makes a bequest while young, many circumstances may have changed by the time of their death. A person named in the bequest may have died, or a child who previously needed no additional support may later have experienced illness, disability, or financial difficulties. Thus, we may understand the Quran’s attention to making a bequest as death approaches as opening the way to reassess relatives’ needs and rights, so that the final decision rests on their actual circumstances rather than a situation that existed years earlier. This does not, of course, mean that making a bequest early is prohibited; rather, it shows the importance of reviewing it as life’s circumstances change.

Yet some proponents of juristic opinion and hadith, relying on rulings and reports outside the Quran, have prohibited bequests to an heir and have even declared this verse’s ruling abrogated. Meanwhile, the Quran itself, in An-Nisa 4:11 and An-Nisa 4:12, explicitly places the distribution of assets after a bequest or debt when assigning inheritance shares. Bequest and inheritance are therefore not two conflicting rulings: one allows the particular needs and circumstances of close relatives to be addressed, while the other specifies their prescribed shares. These verses themselves do not deprive someone of inheritance rights because they benefit from a bequest on account of greater need.

Disregarding this provision and declaring the Quran’s clear command invalid, without the Quran itself announcing the end of its ruling, means setting aside part of divine wisdom in favor of an inherited ruling. In Al-Baqarah 2:159, the Quran condemns the deliberate concealment of guidance and clear proofs, and in Al-Baqarah 2:174 it warns against concealing what The God has sent down of the Book in exchange for gain. Setting aside the clear ruling on bequests and putting an extra-Quranic ruling in its place covers the revealed command with another ruling. This is the very danger against which the verses about concealment warn.

This verse is itself one of the indications that there is no abrogation within the Quran. The Quran has not only given the command to make a bequest, but has also placed the execution of the bequest before the distribution of shares in the inheritance verses. The subject of abrogation is examined further in the article “Abrogation in the Quran: A Critique of Setting Aside Verses in the Name of Abrogation”, which you can consult for further reading.

The wisdom of this verse is that justice is not only the distribution of fixed shares: recognizing people’s different needs and providing a way to support those who require greater care are also part of justice. The God has opened this path through bequests and connected its implementation with propriety and mindfulness.
