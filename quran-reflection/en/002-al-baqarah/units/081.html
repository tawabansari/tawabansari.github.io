Al-Baqarah 2:81

ۖ

Yes indeed; whoever earns evil and is encompassed by their sin—such people are the companions of the Fire; they will remain in it.

Our Reflection

They had said: “The Fire will not touch us except for a few numbered days.” After asking what supports this claim, the Qur'an now continues its answer with an affirmative response to a negation (bala). This “yes” does not affirm their statement; it overturns their denial and declares a judgment contrary to their confidence: whoever earns evil and is encompassed by their sin belongs to the Fire and will remain in it.

The response begins with whoever (man). The passage continues a criticism of a group among the Children of Israel, but the criterion is not expressed through that group's name. The Qur'an speaks of what a person has earned and the condition their sin has brought about for them. Belonging to a religious community does not, in itself, answer this question.

Earning evil connects wrongdoing to the result of a person's own actions. Earning (kasb) means acquiring, and an evil deed (sayyi’ah) here is a wrong the person has committed. But the judgment of the verse does not attach to this phrase alone; its continuation must also be read: and their sin encompasses them. Leaving out this qualification changes the meaning of the verse. The Qur'an has not said that anyone who commits any wrong will remain in the Fire without any further qualification.

Encompassing (ihatah) presents the image of surrounding on all sides. In this sentence, sin is what has enclosed the person themselves. This image goes beyond the mere occurrence of a bad act: the person has become caught in what they have earned. Sin (khati’ah) in this context is also a moral wrong, not simply an error of judgment or an unintended slip. Nevertheless, this expression alone does not allow us to construct a specified number of sins or a definitive list of behaviors; the qualification stated in the verse is the person's being encompassed by their own sin.

To approach this image, we can consider the difference between facing a sin and taking refuge in justifying it. One person may recognize their wrongdoing and rise to correct it; another may set truth aside, reject reminders, and even enlist religious beliefs in its defense in order to preserve that same wrongdoing. In such a condition, sin is not merely an act in the past; it also affects the person's later choices.

The Qur'an distinguishes the path of return from this condition. In Ali 'Imran 3:135 it speaks of those who, when they commit an indecency or wrong themselves, remember The God and seek forgiveness. It then adds: they do not knowingly persist in what they have done. The next verse speaks of forgiveness and Paradise for them. Committing a wrong and returning from it must therefore not be equated with remaining trapped in sin.

Both boundaries must also be preserved when understanding Al-Baqarah 2:81. The qualification that a person's sin encompasses them must not be removed so that every slip receives the same outcome. Nor should the meaning of evil and sin be narrowed so far that a person considers themselves outside the warning merely by relying on their religious name. The verse asks about the reality of a person's actions and condition, not the confidence they have constructed about themselves.

Then comes the judgment: such people are the companions of the Fire. Companions (ashab) expresses their relationship with the Fire, and the next sentence makes their remaining there clear: they will remain in it. This ending stands against the claim of a few numbered days, a claim they had made for themselves without a divine covenant.

The next verse shows the other side: those who believe and do righteous deeds are the people of Paradise and remain in it. In these two verses, earning evil and being encompassed by sin stand on one side, and faith and righteous action on the other. The Qur'an's response turns a person from the wish for salvation back to responsibility for faith and action.

The warning of the verse is this: we must not imagine that the evil we have earned is rendered harmless by confidence in our name and group. Sin that has encompassed a person does not disappear through the claim, “I will ultimately be saved.”
