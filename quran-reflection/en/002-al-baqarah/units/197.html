Al-Baqarah 2:197

ۚ ۗ ۗ ۚ

Hajj is in known months; so whoever undertakes Hajj in those months: let there be no rafath, no fusuq, and no jidal during Hajj. And whatever good you do, The God knows it. And take provisions, for the best provision is taqwa; and practice taqwa toward Me, O people of understanding.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, The God commanded the completion of Hajj and Umrah and explained the conditions of being prevented, hady, fidyah, and compensatory avenues. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:197, He clarifies the timing of Hajj and its limits of conduct:

“الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ”

“Hajj is in known months.”

The God speaks of “أَشْهُرٌ”—months, not merely a few days. These months were known to those addressed, but the verse itself does not list their names. The relationship between the months of Hajj, the sacred months, and the known and numbered days is examined in “Hajj in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights”. This span of time does not mean that all pilgrims must remain in one place throughout these months.

What matters here is the greater wisdom revealed through Hajj’s connection with the security of the House, the sanctity of time, people’s benefits, and taqwa.

In Al-Baqarah 2:125, The God describes the House as “مَثَابَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَأَمْنًا”—a place of return and security for people. In Al-Hajj 22:27, Ibrahim is commanded to call people to Hajj, and in Al-Hajj 22:28 their attendance is given a purpose: “لِيَشْهَدُوا مَنَافِعَ لَهُمْ”—to witness benefits for themselves. The same verse also speaks of remembering The God’s name and feeding those in need.

In Al-Maidah 5:97, The God places the Kaaba, the sacred month, hady, and garlanded offerings in one context and describes the Kaaba as “قِيَامًا لِلنَّاسِ”—something connected with sustaining and strengthening people’s lives. In Al-Maidah 5:2, the sanctity of the sacred month, hady, and those heading toward the Sacred House also appears alongside the prohibition of transgression.

Taken together, these verses reveal a great wisdom: The God established a House for people and, through the sanctity of time and place, provided conditions for people from different lands and societies to gather safely, share benefits, remember The God, care for those in need, and work to reduce hostility and establish justice and peace.

Such a gathering can offer even people who disagree and are hostile toward one another outside it an opportunity to meet, converse, and find a path to peace.

This potential is not merely an idea far removed from Quranic usage. In At-Tawbah 9:7, the Quran speaks of those with whom agreements had been made at the Sacred Mosque:

“إِلَّا الَّذِينَ عَاهَدْتُمْ عِنْدَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ”

“Except those with whom you made an agreement at the Sacred Mosque.”

It then commands mutual fidelity to the agreement. This verse shows that the Sacred Mosque was also connected with agreements and political commitments in relations between different groups.

At-Tawbah 9:3 also mentions a public announcement on “يَوْمَ الْحَجِّ الْأَكْبَرِ”. Although this concerned a declaration of disassociation under the particular circumstances of those verses, the usage shows that the Hajj gathering could also serve as a place for conveying important public messages.

The security of the House and the gathering of people therefore matter beyond individual acts of worship; these verses also reveal a setting for communication, conveying messages, and regulating human relations.

Guiding people is also part of this responsibility. In Al-Hajj 22:67, after speaking of the rites of different communities, the Quran commands the messenger:

“وَادْعُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ”

“And invite to your Lord.”

It then says: “إِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ هُدًى مُسْتَقِيمٍ”—indeed, you are upon straight guidance.

This connection matters. Differences in communities’ rites should not prevent inviting people to The God, hearing the message, and discussing guidance. Al-Hajj 22:25 also identifies the Sacred Mosque as being for people and speaks of equality between the resident and the one arriving from outside.

A House established for people must not become an instrument for placing servanthood and guidance under the exclusive control of one people, government, or denomination.

The God then says:

“فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ”

“So whoever undertakes Hajj in those months: let there be no rafath, no fusuq, and no jidal during Hajj.”

“فَرَضَ”, from the root ف ر ض, is connected with determining and prescribing. Aal Imran 3:97 states the obligation of pilgrimage to the House for those with the means. Here the subject is someone who has undertaken Hajj in the known months and must observe its limits.

The God prohibits three behaviors during Hajj: rafath, fusuq, and jidal.

“Rafath” is connected with sexual matters; in Al-Baqarah 2:187, the same word concerns relations with spouses. Here, refraining from rafath is one of the limits of the state of Hajj.

“Fusuq”, from the root ف س ق, is connected with departing from proper bounds and disobedience. It must not be confined to breaches of ritual formalities. In Al-Baqarah 2:282, after prohibiting harm to a scribe or witness in transactions, the Quran says: “وَإِنْ تَفْعَلُوا فَإِنَّهُ فُسُوقٌ بِكُمْ”. Harming others and violating their rights can therefore also be instances of fusuq.

Someone going to Hajj to serve The God cannot simultaneously violate people’s rights, engage in corruption, or humiliate others and imagine that performing the rites compensates for such conduct.

“Jidal”, from the root ج د ل, is connected with argument and verbal conflict. In the context of Hajj, contentious argument that generates strife conflicts with the gathering’s security and peace.

But prohibiting jidal must not be turned into a ban on every question, criticism, exchange of ideas, or respectful conversation.

In An-Nahl 16:125, the Quran commands inviting with wisdom and good counsel and debating in the best manner. In Al-Mujadilah 58:1, it uses both “تُجَادِلُكَ” and “تَحَاوُرَكُمَا” concerning a conversation. Not every debate can therefore be considered inherently blameworthy; its manner, purpose, and outcome also matter.

In Al-Hajj itself, after the command to invite to The God in Al-Hajj 22:67, the next verse, Al-Hajj 22:68, says:

“وَإِنْ جَادَلُوكَ فَقُلِ اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ”

“And if they argue with you, say: The God knows best what you do.”

This response does not open the way to continued conflict and hostility; instead, it prevents argument from escalating.

Hajj should therefore be an opportunity for constructive dialogue and hearing others, not a battleground for ethnic, sectarian, and religious disputes.

Dialogue seeking truth is not the same as argument seeking to humiliate, antagonize, or overcome others. If Hajj aims to gather people in security and taqwa, one cannot destroy that very security and peace through religious conflict in the name of defending religion.

A serious question arises here about the distance between the Quranic wisdom of Hajj and what has remained of this worship in practice.

Centuries ago, The God identified a House as a place of return and security for people; directed the call to Hajj toward people; placed their benefits, remembrance of The God, and care for those in need within it; identified known months for Hajj; and prohibited rafath, fusuq, and jidal.

Alongside the actual rites of Hajj, these elements could provide a broad setting for peoples to meet, guidance to be conveyed, ideas to be exchanged, disputes to be resolved, cooperation to develop, and peace to be strengthened.

But what have the followers of the Quran done with this great wisdom?

In practice, much Muslim attention has focused on ceremonies, counts of ritual acts, jurisprudential details, formal prohibitions, and religious disagreements. Lengthy discussions have addressed the number of circuits of circumambulation, how to wear ihram, the number of pebbles, and many details that the Quran itself does not state as specified rulings. Yet Hajj’s role in dialogue between peoples, reducing hostility, resolving disputes, and cooperating for the public benefit has not received equal attention.

“Hajj in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights” examines the distinction between rites prescribed in the Quran and obligations added over history. The issue is not abandoning the rites, but preventing secondary details and formalities from overshadowing principles such as monotheism, security, taqwa, and people’s rights.

Here we can speak of part of the Quran’s wisdom being neglected in its followers’ practice. A House established for people may in practice fall under religious and political monopolies, while worship capable of bringing peoples together becomes confined to ceremonies whose participants have little opportunity to discuss their suffering, disagreements, and shared responsibilities.

This neglect becomes more apparent when we consider the experience of today’s world.

Every year, leaders and representatives of different countries gather at the United Nations General Assembly in New York. Among them are governments politically hostile to one another, and even potentially at war. Nevertheless, their representatives attend one place, express their views, and find opportunities to meet, converse, and negotiate.

Today’s world has recognized that even enemies must be able to gather somewhere and, instead of confronting one another only through threats and war, have opportunities for dialogue, reducing differences, and finding peace.

This gathering does not, of course, guarantee an end to wars or the realization of justice; but providing such an opportunity itself demonstrates the importance of dialogue in relations between nations.

Nevertheless, in some respects this example is more limited than the potential visible in the Quranic wisdom of Hajj.

The annual UN General Debate lasts only a few days; in 2026, for example, it was held over six working days. In contrast, the Quran connects Hajj with “أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ”. These months provide a broader period for Hajj, although this does not mean that all pilgrims remain continuously throughout every day of it.

Furthermore, the call to Hajj is not addressed only to government leaders; it addresses people. The House is also established for people and their security, and their attendance is connected with benefits, remembrance of The God, taqwa, and good.

If today the international community has built an institution such as the United Nations to bring together leaders of hostile countries for a few days, why have the Quran’s followers not adequately used Hajj’s broader capacity to bring people together, foster dialogue, convey guidance, resolve differences, and strengthen peace?

Why should Hajj not be an opportunity for representatives of different communities, thinkers, reformers, leaders, and ordinary people to discuss wars, poverty, injustice, and avenues of cooperation alongside performing the rites?

Why should those who disagree and are hostile outside this gathering not be able to approach this House safely and work to reduce hostility and reach understanding?

The Quran’s wisdom lies in identifying a House for people, sacred time for security, and a gathering for their benefits. But when this capacity is confined to rigid formalities and religious monopolies, part of this worship’s greater purpose is pushed aside.

The UN example shows us that even in politics, people have recognized the need for such a space. It is therefore fitting for the Quran’s followers also to take Hajj’s social and human capacity seriously alongside its actual acts.

This responsibility does not mean abandoning Hajj’s sacred rites, but restoring them to a place where they serve servanthood to The God and people’s security and benefits.

After prohibiting rafath, fusuq, and jidal, The God says:

“وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ”

“And whatever good you do, The God knows it.”

The Quran here does not stop at avoiding wrongful behavior; it opens a broad field for doing good.

“مِنْ خَيْرٍ” is not confined to one particular act. Al-Hajj 22:28 explicitly mentions feeding the poor person in need, and Al-Hajj 22:36 also mentions feeding others in need alongside benefiting from the sacrifice.

From these clear examples, we can also consider other good deeds: helping travelers, preserving people’s security, preventing oppression, reconciling disputes, caring for those in need, and cooperating for the public benefit.

Someone going to Hajj should therefore ask not only which acts they have performed and which prohibitions they have avoided, but also what good their presence has brought to others.

The verse continues:

“وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ”

“And take provisions, for the best provision is taqwa.”

“Zad” is the provision a person prepares for travel and continuing the journey. The Hajj journey also requires preparation and real resources. Alongside travel provisions, however, The God identifies the best provision: taqwa.

A person may go to Hajj with abundant resources and perform every outward act, yet if they treat others with selfishness, discrimination, prejudice, humiliation, and injustice, they lack the best provision.

In Al-Hajj 22:37, The God also says concerning sacrifice:

“لَنْ يَنَالَ اللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَاؤُهَا وَلَٰكِنْ يَنَالُهُ التَّقْوَىٰ مِنْكُمْ”

“Neither their meat nor their blood reaches The God, but your taqwa reaches Him.”

This verse shows that even actual ritual acts are not separate from taqwa. Performing an outward act without regard for its purpose and divine limits does not fulfill the whole reality of servanthood.

Taqwa, from the root و ق ي, is connected with care, protection, and conscious self-restraint. Here it means watching one’s conduct and speech amid crowds, during disagreements, and when interacting with people of different beliefs and cultures.

The same principle is essential for dialogue and peace. Someone seeking justice must also act justly. Someone wanting their words to be heard must also be ready to hear others.

A fuller explanation appears in “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

Finally, The God says:

“وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ”

“And practice taqwa toward Me, O people of understanding.”

“أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ” are people of understanding and thought. At the end of these commands, The God calls people to taqwa and the exercise of reason.

Why is Hajj in known months? Why must the House be a place of security? Why are people called to it from distant places? Why are fusuq and jidal prohibited during Hajj? And why, after these rulings, does the verse speak of good and the best provision, taqwa?

These questions lead us from the form of the acts to their wisdom, without requiring us to abandon the acts The God has prescribed.

In the Quran’s verses taken together, Hajj is real worship with a prescribed time, place, and rites; but its wisdom does not end with performing ceremonies. The God established the House for people and their security and connected the Hajj gathering with people’s benefits, remembrance of The God, care for those in need, good, and taqwa.

Today, when the world has created institutions such as the United Nations to gather representatives of hostile countries for a few days, the Quran’s followers must ask themselves why they have not brought Hajj’s broader capacity for dialogue, conveying guidance, justice, resolving disputes, and peace to its rightful place. Hajj should not merely be ceremonies from which people return after a few days; it should be an opportunity from which they return with greater understanding, greater taqwa, and greater responsibility toward one another.
