Al-Baqarah 2:276

ۗ

The God eradicates riba and causes charities to grow; and The God does not love any ungrateful sinner.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:275, the Quran distinguished trade from riba, declaring trade lawful and riba forbidden. Now, through two contrasting verbs, it speaks of the outcome of riba and charities: the eradication of riba and the growth of charities.

“يَمْحَقُ اللَّهُ الرِّبَا”

“The God eradicates riba.”

“يَمْحَقُ,” from M Ḥ Q, refers to diminishing, eroding, effacing, and eliminating. It can also describe an erosion that gradually removes something’s effect, durability, and value.

For example, as the moon approaches its dark phase, its mass is not destroyed; rather, its light diminishes from our perspective until it disappears. This image helps us understand the difference between something’s apparent existence and the disappearance of its effect.

With riba, too, a person’s assets may increase numerically without that increase constituting real growth in wealth or society’s flourishing.

In today’s economy, inflation clearly illustrates the difference between a numerical increase in money and real growth in wealth. If the volume of money and credit grows faster than the capacity to produce and supply goods and services, it can raise prices and reduce purchasing power. In such circumstances, incomes and nominal asset values may rise while people cannot obtain as many goods and services as before.

Likewise, if new credit flows mainly into purchasing homes, land, and financial assets, their prices may rise without a proportionate increase in housing construction, real production, or the resources available for people’s lives.

In such a situation, the quantity of money and asset prices have increased, but society’s real wealth has not necessarily grown.

In modern banking, commercial banks can create new deposits and bank money when making loans. Creating money, however, is not the same as creating real wealth. Money can contribute to economic growth when used for production, meeting real needs, creating opportunities, and the healthy circulation of capital.

Inflation itself is not equivalent to riba, and not all inflation arises from relations involving riba. Prices rose even before fiat money, in economies based on gold and silver. Thus, neither can all inflation be attributed to riba nor all credit and lending be considered a source of economic corruption.

The issue is not as simple as calling every loan riba. Financial credit can exploit others’ needs, but it can also support production, employment, housing, and households’ economic capacity.

For example, consider a working family that saves for years while home prices and rent rise faster than its savings. Access to a loan with clear terms, installments proportionate to income, and affordable costs can enable the family to own a home and give it greater security against rising housing costs.

If the loan rate is fixed, principal and interest payments can remain fixed over the contract, although other costs such as taxes and insurance may rise. Under inflation, the real value of a fixed debt may also fall. While preserving its ability to meet essential needs, and provided there is no contractual penalty, the family can reduce the loan’s final cost by repaying principal earlier.

Conversely, if the same loan entails installments beyond the family’s means, hidden costs, unfair terms, or uncontrolled debt growth, it can create financial pressure and a risk of losing the home instead of providing security. Homeownership itself does not guarantee profit or economic security.

A loan that provides capital for production, job creation, or meeting real needs can also improve people’s lives; but both parties’ rights and responsibilities, the real cost, repayment capacity, and allocation of risk must be examined.

Thus, to distinguish sound from unsound financial relations, the label of a loan, its interest rate, or its contractual form is not enough. We must examine what value the relationship actually creates, who benefits, and who bears the risk and loss.

For a detailed examination of credit money, inflation, and contemporary financial relations, see Chapters Four, Seven, and Eight of “The Book of Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest”.

Now, if much of people’s income goes toward debts that continually increase, creditors may become wealthier while households lose purchasing power, businesses face fewer customers, and opportunities for production and investment decline.

In such circumstances, one group’s wealth has increased, but society’s economic capacity has been eroded.

“Maḥq” should therefore not be restricted to meaning that every consumer of riba will necessarily lose all their assets in this world. Someone may accumulate great wealth while their gain is accompanied by eroding trust, others’ dependence, fewer economic opportunities, and harm to society’s life.

Conversely, the apparent durability of wealth does not itself signify its value or acceptability with God. For a fuller examination, see the study of the Quranic root M Ḥ Q.

By contrast, the Quran says:

“وَيُرْبِي الصَّدَقَاتِ”

“And causes charities to grow.”

“يُرْبِي,” from R B W in Form IV, refers to causing growth, increasing, and nurturing.

The striking point of this contrast is that the Quran speaks of destruction concerning riba, which outwardly increases wealth, but of growth and increase concerning charity, which outwardly removes wealth from a person’s possessions.

This is the truth expressed in various ways in the preceding verses on giving. In Al-Baqarah 2:261, giving was compared to a grain producing seven ears, each bearing a hundred grains. In Al-Baqarah 2:265, a fruitful garden provided another example of giving’s growth and fruitfulness.

To understand this growth, we should not look only at the amount the giver paid.

Suppose someone pays for vocational training for a young person in need. The money leaves the giver’s possessions, but the young person learns a skill, finds work and income, supports a family, and may later create employment opportunities for others.

Or someone pays for a sick person’s treatment so they can regain health and the ability to work. This gift can prevent a family from falling into poverty and restore a person’s lost capability.

In such examples, charity has reduced the giver’s possessions, yet its value and fruits in people’s lives have multiplied.

This growth can appear in reduced poverty, increased knowledge and skills, health, employment, purchasing power, and economic cooperation. Beyond these worldly effects, the Quran also promises reward with the Lord for giving and charities; their growth should therefore not be restricted to financial profit and measurable economic outcomes.

The Connection among Charities, Giving, and Upright Trade

The principle of growth and using blessings rightly is not limited to directly giving money. In “The Principle of Giving and Using Blessings”, we examined how people can use their material and nonmaterial assets for good, from capital and resources to knowledge, skills, experience, and life’s opportunities.

Sound buying, selling, and trade can also follow this path.

For example, someone invests capital in producing goods people need, creates jobs for workers, buys raw materials, supplies a useful product, and earns legitimate profit in return.

In this activity, capital circulates, new value is created, and various people benefit from its fruits.

In charity, a person helps another without demanding direct compensation; in sound trade, both parties exchange things of value by consent and with respect for each other’s rights. Both can contribute to society’s growth and flourishing through the proper use of blessings.

Charity and trade are therefore not the same concept, but each can serve healthy social growth in its own way. For the criteria of proper transactions, see the principle of consent and fairness in trade.

In Ar-Rum 30:39, the Quran also speaks of wealth given to increase within people’s wealth but which does not grow with God; by contrast, zakat given to seek God’s countenance is connected with multiplication.

In Al-Hashr 59:7, regarding the distribution of public wealth, its circulation exclusively among the rich is also prevented.

These connections show that society’s true wealth should not be measured only by the amount of money and accumulated assets. People’s capacity to live, produce, exchange, maintain economic independence, and benefit from blessings also matters.

This subject is examined more fully in “The Economy of Dependence: When Human Need Becomes an Instrument of Power” and Chapters Six and Seven of the Book of Riba.

Ingratitude and Sin in the Use of Wealth

The verse ends with this expression:

“وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ كَفَّارٍ أَثِيمٍ”

“And The God does not love any ungrateful sinner.”

“كَفَّار,” from K F R, is an intensive form emphasizing ingratitude and concealing truth in this context. “أَثِيم,” from ʾ TH M, refers to someone characterized by sin.

The placement of these two descriptions at the verse’s end connects riba and charities with human moral responsibility.

Wealth and possessions are blessings that people can use for good or turn into instruments of oppression and corruption. Someone who, despite knowing the truth and their responsibility, uses blessings and economic power to exploit others’ needs disregards truth and responsibility instead of showing practical gratitude.

These descriptions, however, should not be applied to every borrower, depositor, or person living within today’s economic system without considering their knowledge, freedom of choice, and actual role. Being caught in difficult economic circumstances is not the same as knowingly profiting from others’ desperation.

In Ibrahim 14:7, the Quran connects gratitude with increased blessing and ingratitude with a divine warning. This verse likewise reminds us that people’s treatment of the blessings at their disposal is inseparable from their faith and conduct.

Gratitude for a blessing is not merely verbal: using it rightly, respecting others’ rights, and putting it into circulation for good are also signs of practical gratitude.

In the next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:277, the Quran brings together faith, righteous action, establishing salah, and giving zakat.

The message of this verse is that a numerical increase in money and assets does not necessarily mean real growth in wealth or flourishing. Riba can increase one group’s wealth while eroding people’s economic capacity and driving society toward weakness and dependence. By contrast, charity, giving, and the proper use of capital and blessings can increase human capabilities, create new opportunities, and enable healthy social growth. Not every loan is riba, nor is every profit a sign of exploitation; what matters is understanding the reality of the economic relationship and respecting human rights. God eradicates riba and causes charities to grow, for a blessing’s value lies not only in possessing and accumulating it, but in the good and fruits produced by using it rightly.
