Al-Baqarah 2:206

ۚ ۚ

And when he is told, “Be mindful of The God,” pride drives him into sin. Hell is sufficient for him—and what an evil resting place!

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:204, The God described people who win public trust through attractive and deceptive speech. In Al-Baqarah 2:205, He spoke of corruption and the destruction of cultivation and offspring as signs of their conduct. He now describes another trait of these same people:

“وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُ اتَّقِ اللَّهَ أَخَذَتْهُ الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ”

“And when he is told, ‘Be mindful of The God,’ pride drives him into sin.”

This trait is the prejudice and pride that prevent a person from accepting truth and drive them to persist in sin.

Such a person may use fine words to present themselves as concerned for people’s welfare. But when someone points out their error, instead of examining the evidence and correcting their conduct, they become caught in prejudice and a sense of superiority. They do not want to accept that their belief, command, or decision might be wrong.

“الْعِزَّةُ,” from the root ع ز ز, concerns strength, capability, and dignity. Dignity is not objectionable in itself, but when joined to “بِالْإِثْمِ,” it becomes pride and stubbornness in sin. A person becomes so attached to power, position, inherited belief, or social standing that even a call to mindfulness seems an insult to their character and authority.

This behavior can be observed among rulers, religious authorities, political leaders, and their fanatical followers.

For example, if they are told: Nowruz is not forbidden; music is not forbidden; do not decide what is lawful and unlawful for people in place of the Lord; do not destroy a generation of women, and let the doors of learning and education open to them; give people the right to freedom and choice; be mindful of The God! How do they respond?

Do they examine the Qur’anic evidence for these prohibitions? Are they willing to accept that their inherited rulings and decisions might not accord with God’s command? Or do religious prejudice, political pride, and attachment to power drive them to insist still more strongly on the same behavior?

The Qur’an clearly warns in An-Nahl 16:116:

“وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ”

“And do not say, on account of the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and that is forbidden,’ so as to invent lies about The God.”

Declaring something religiously forbidden without evidence from God is itself a serious matter. No one may impose personal preferences, religious customs, or their leaders’ interpretations on others as God’s definitive ruling.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 also says, “لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ”—there is no compulsion in religion. Depriving people of freedom of belief and choice in religion’s name is therefore incompatible with this Qur’anic principle. This subject is examined in detail in “No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey”.

Excluding girls and women from school and university does more than deprive a few individuals of education: such a decision affects a generation’s future. Their abilities remain undeveloped, opportunities for life and work are restricted, and society is deprived of the knowledge and talent of a large part of its population.

This issue is not confined to one country or government. Anywhere in the world, when political or religious power turns to suppressing freedom, erasing cultures, denying education, or imposing belief instead of hearing criticism and examining evidence, the same danger of prejudice and pride against truth arises.

In such circumstances, someone may even call their conduct a defense of religion, national dignity, preservation of tradition, or social reform. But commendable names do not turn sin into goodness.

The dangerous sign of this character is that the clearer their error becomes, the more they insist upon it instead of correcting it, because preserving authority and reputation has become more important than accepting truth.

By contrast, taqwa, from the root و ق ي, concerns conscious care and self-guarding. A mindful person must be able to question even their own long-held beliefs and decisions, examine them, and turn away from them if they are wrong.

Taqwa means guarding oneself against oppression, pride, prejudice, and attributing unsupported rulings to God. This concept is examined in “What Is Taqwa in the Qur’an?”.

At the end, The God says:

“فَحَسْبُهُ جَهَنَّمُ وَلَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ”

“Hell is sufficient for him—and what an evil resting place!”

“حَسْبُهُ” means sufficient for him, and “الْمِهَادُ” refers to a bed or place of rest. Yet what a terrible resting place Hell is for someone who, through pride and prejudice in this world, has prevented themselves from accepting truth.

This warning shows that persisting in sin, especially when a person knowingly refuses criticism and correction, is no small matter.

In these three verses, The God presents three stages in recognizing the character of those who spread corruption. First, in Al-Baqarah 2:204, we see their outward appearance: appealing words, grand and deceptive promises, and calling God to witness their intentions. Their tools at this stage are speech and deception. Then, in Al-Baqarah 2:205, when they leave public view or gain the power to act, the results of their conduct appear: corruption in the land and the destruction of cultivation and offspring, from the devastation of vital resources and the environment to harm to culture, education, and human life. Their tools are destructive policies and actions, even if they call themselves reformers. Finally, in Al-Baqarah 2:206, when called to mindfulness and confronted with the truth of their conduct, pride and prejudice drive them to persist in sin instead of reflecting and correcting themselves. To maintain this course, they resort to obstinacy, justifying wrongdoing, and sometimes suppressing critics. To recognize those who spread corruption, then, we must consider all three signs together: deceptive speech, corrupt conduct, and resistance to correction.
