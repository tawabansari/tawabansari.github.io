Al-Baqarah 2:195

ۛ ۛ

And spend in the way of The God, and do not cast yourselves into destruction by your own hands; and do good; indeed, The God loves those who do good.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verses, The God spoke of fighting in His way, confronting oppressors, observing the bounds of justice, and taqwa. He now turns to another fundamental human responsibility:

“وَأَنْفِقُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ”

“And spend in the way of The God.”

“Infaq”, from the root ن ف ق, is connected with going out and bringing out. In financial usage, it refers to spending and releasing property from one’s possession for use and giving. In a broader reading of the Quran, infaq means putting the blessings and resources The God has given a person to use, rather than gathering and keeping them all exclusively for oneself.

This command is not new in the Quran. At the very beginning of Al-Baqarah, one characteristic of those who practice taqwa is described as follows:

“وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ” (Al-Baqarah 2:3)

“And they spend from what We have provided for them.”

The God does not say that they give only money, gold, or silver; rather, they give from what He has provided. A person can use their wealth, resources, knowledge, abilities, skills, and opportunities for good. In this verse, however, given the context and the use of “أَنْفِقُوا”, spending property to support The God’s way has a direct place.

Infaq stands against self-centeredness, miserliness, and hoarding. A miser wants property only to add to their own possessions, whereas a generous person knows that The God’s blessings can also affect other people’s lives.

In Al-Humazah 104:2, the Quran speaks of someone who “جَمَعَ مَالًا وَعَدَّدَهُ”—gathered wealth and repeatedly counted it. When a person’s entire thinking becomes confined to accumulating, counting, and increasing property, wealth turns from a means of living and serving into life’s purpose. Such attachment can cultivate miserliness and stinginess.

In At-Tawbah 9:34, The God also condemns those who store up gold and silver and do not spend it in His way. The issue is therefore not merely possessing wealth; the issue is wealth being confined to accumulation and personal power instead of flowing toward good.

Infaq brings a person out of this enclosure. It fosters generosity, attention to others’ needs, and responsible use of blessings, and can prevent unhealthy concentrations of property and the formation of extreme economic disparities.

To understand the social effect of this, let us compare two imaginary cities.

In the first city, ninety percent of the property is held by only ten percent of the population. Much of this wealth remains concentrated in their accounts and accumulated assets, with little reaching production, employment, or improvements in other citizens’ lives. Other people face a shortage of capital to start businesses, limited employment opportunities, and economic dependency.

In the second city, ninety percent of the capital circulates in productive activities and useful services. New workshops and businesses emerge, people are employed and receive salaries and wages, and the benefits of economic activity reach more people’s lives through work, production, and exchange.

Which city has greater capacity for growth, progress, and general welfare?

The first city has abundant property, but its benefits remain confined to a small part of society. In the second, capital serves work, production, and people’s lives.

Of course, merely keeping money in a bank does not mean removing it from economic circulation; a bank may also make deposits available for economic activities. The essential difference between these examples is wealth being locked away and its benefits concentrated, as opposed to capital circulating in useful activities and benefiting people more widely.

This example shows that a society’s wealth should not be assessed solely by its total assets. We must also ask how those assets are used, what opportunities they create, and who benefits from them.

In Al-Hashr 59:7, in the context of distributing fay property, the Quran states an important principle:

“كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنْكُمْ”

“So that it does not circulate only among the wealthy among you.”

Although this phrase occurs in a specific ruling on distributing fay, the wisdom it expresses has broad social significance: property must not remain solely within a closed circle of the wealthy while others are deprived of its opportunities and benefits.

This idea is examined in detail in two Forqan studies, “The Book of Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest” and “Zakat and Its Historical Distortion”, from the perspectives of wealth circulation, financial responsibility, capital concentration, and social justice. Riba and zakat have their own distinct places, and their rulings should not all be merged into the concept of infaq.

The God then says:

“وَلَا تُلْقُوا بِأَيْدِيكُمْ إِلَى التَّهْلُكَةِ”

“And do not cast yourselves into destruction by your own hands.”

“Tahlukah”, from the root ه ل ك, is connected with destruction and perishing. Read together with the command to spend, this verse contains an important warning: people must not, by abandoning responsibility and refusing to use necessary resources in the cause of right, create the conditions for their own destruction and that of their society.

This statement follows verses about confronting aggression. A society that allocates no resources to preserving security, supporting the oppressed, and sustaining its shared responsibilities may itself create the conditions for its weakness and destruction.

In economic life too, resources being locked away, work opportunities disappearing, and poverty and dependency spreading can erode society’s strength. A society with abundant wealth that does not use it to meet real needs and develop people’s abilities wastes part of its capacity for life.

Nevertheless, the command to spend does not mean that a person should use up all their property recklessly and leave their family in need. In Al-Furqan 25:67, the Quran praises those who, when spending, are neither extravagant nor miserly, but take a middle course. In Al-Baqarah 2:219, when asked what should be spent, it also states “الْعَفْوَ”.

Infaq must therefore be conscious and responsible. Keeping property for life’s real needs is not the same as harmful hoarding and excessive attachment to accumulating wealth. Just as miserliness can harm individuals and society, imprudent spending can also leave a person in hardship.

At the end, The God says:

“وَأَحْسِنُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ”

“And do good; indeed, The God loves those who do good.”

The God has connected the command to spend with ihsan. “Ihsan”, from the root ح س ن, means goodness and doing something well. Infaq is not merely releasing property from one’s hands; it must be done in a way that results in goodness, reform, and improved lives.

Someone may spend great wealth yet use it for deception, corruption, buying influence, or spreading oppression. Simply because money is spent, such expenditure is not infaq in The God’s way or ihsan. The criterion is how the property is used and the effect it has on human lives.

In Al-Baqarah 2:262, The God also praises those who do not follow their giving with reminders of their generosity or harm to others. Doing good must therefore preserve human dignity, rather than humiliate people or make them dependent.

Infaq releases blessings from the confines of selfishness and lets them flow toward good. Wealth reveals its social value when, instead of fruitless accumulation and concentrated power, it serves work, production, meeting needs, and improving people’s lives. But the goal is not merely the circulation of money; it is ihsan. The God loves those who do good, not those who merely accumulate more property or spend it without regard for right and justice.
