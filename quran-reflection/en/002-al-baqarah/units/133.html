Al-Baqarah 2:133

Were you present when death came to Jacob? When he said to his children, “What will you worship after me?” they said, “We will worship your God and the God of your forefathers, Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—one God, and to Him we have surrendered.”

Our Reflection

The previous verse said that Jacob instructed his children not to die except in a state of surrender. Now the Qur’an takes us to the moment of his death:

“Were you present when death came to Jacob?”

This question challenges later claims about Jacob’s religion. The Qur’an itself recounts Jacob’s words and his children’s answer. At such a moment, his question concerns neither the name of a people nor a religious label:

“What will you worship after me?”

What matters to Jacob as death approaches is the focus of his children’s worship and the direction of their devotion. Their answer is clear:

“We will worship your God and the God of your forefathers, Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac.”

The mention of Ishmael alongside Isaac matters here. Jacob belongs to Isaac’s branch, and the Children of Israel take their name from him. Yet in making this commitment, his children do not separate Ishmael from the Abrahamic family. Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac are all brought together under one truth:

“One God.”

A few verses later, this connection returns even more explicitly:

“And what was sent down to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the tribes … We make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we have surrendered.”
Al-Baqarah 2:136

Jacob’s inheritance therefore cannot be built on discrimination between Abraham’s branches. The very children through whom the generations of the Children of Israel continued pledged before their father to worship the God of Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac as one God.

Elsewhere, this same meaning becomes a principle of faith:

“We make no distinction between any of His messengers.” And they said, “We hear and obey.”
Al-Baqarah 2:285

When people later accept some prophets and set others aside on the basis of their ethnic or religious identity, we can therefore ask: have they not moved away from that original commitment? In An-Nisa 4:150, the Qur’an condemns those who say that they believe in some and reject others.

The answer of Jacob’s children ends with the same theme running through the preceding verses:

“And to Him we have surrendered.”

Abraham said, “I have surrendered to the Lord of all worlds.” Jacob instructed his children, “So do not die except in a state of surrender.” Now his children say, “And to Him we have surrendered.” The thread joining these three verses is clear: Abraham’s way begins with surrender, is entrusted to the next generation, and Jacob’s children commit themselves to that same surrender.

The lesson for us is clear as well. Belonging to a family, a people, a denomination, or a religious history cannot replace this commitment. If we, too, divide God’s prophets according to the interests of our own identities, or carry the name “Muslim” without examining our surrender to God, the same question comes back to us. The standard remains the same: one God, no discrimination between His prophets, and surrender to Him.

The meaning of Islam as surrender and its distinction from religious identity are explored more fully in Dīn, Islām, and Sharīʿah in the Qur’an.
