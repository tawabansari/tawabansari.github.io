Al-Baqarah 2:115

ۚ ۚ

And to The God belong the east and the west; wherever you turn, there is the face of The God. Indeed, The God is all-encompassing, knowing.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse spoke of people who close off places of worship and prevent remembrance of the name of The God. Now the answer reaches beyond the walls of a place of worship:

“To The God belong the east and the west.”

A person may be able to close the door of a place of worship, but cannot close the way to The God. They may take possession of a place, but they do not own the horizon.

The verse then moves beyond even these two sides of the horizon:

“Wherever you turn, there is the face of The God.”

The key to the sentence’s inclusiveness is “wherever”: in any place, in any direction. “East and west” are not two entries in a geographical list from which north and south have been omitted. The verse begins with two edges of the horizon, then opens every possible direction through “wherever.”

Why “East and West”?

This is the first occurrence of the Arabic words for east and west in the Qur’an’s present textual order. Their selection therefore deserves closer attention.

If the intended meaning were merely the four familiar geographical directions, we might ask why north and south are not mentioned. But the difficulty begins with that very assumption of four directions.

The four cardinal directions are a useful human system for navigation; existence itself is not divided into four directions. A person in three-dimensional space does not face only left and right, or north and south. There is also above and below, with countless other directions between them. From any point, one can turn along infinitely many possible lines.

If the Qur’an were to name every direction individually, no short list would suffice.

But that is not what the verse does. It names two perceptible edges of the horizon and then embraces all directions in a single word:

“Wherever.”

This difference matters. Human geographical systems—north, south, east, and west—help us locate ourselves on earth. But the speech of The God does not confine itself within these human divisions that have come into being. It speaks in relation to the existence He Himself created: a world in which possible directions are not limited to four arrows on a map.

The absence of north and south is therefore not a deficiency in the sentence. Even if both were added, above, below, and countless other lines would remain. “Wherever” accomplishes what no list of directions can.

Later, the Qur’an expresses this breadth in another way:

“The Lord of the east and the west and what is between them.” Ash-Shu‘ara 26:28.

In such a construction, east and west are two edges of the horizon, not two boundaries enclosing the presence of The God.

Why These Two Edges?

The Arabic words rendered “east” and “west” are not merely conventional direction names. Both arise from an event in the world: rising and setting.

The east is the place of rising (mashriq), and the west the place of setting (maghrib). A person sees both on the horizon each day: light emerges, travels across the sky, and sets at the other edge. These words are therefore also connected with movement, night and day, and the passage of time.

North and south matter for navigation, but do not carry this daily event within their meaning. Nothing rises because it is north or sets because it is south.

From this perspective, east and west offer a more living image than two marks on a compass:

From where light rises to where it sets, all belongs to Him.

The Qur’an later goes beyond a single east and a single west:

“The Lord of the two easts and the Lord of the two wests.” Ar-Rahman 55:17.

And:

“The Lord of the easts and the wests.” Al-Ma‘arij 70:40.

Al-A‘raf 7:137 likewise speaks of “the eastern and western regions of the land.”

In Qur’anic language, then, east and west are not always two fixed, absolute points. There can be multiple easts and wests: horizons differ, and the place of rising or setting is not one fixed point for the entire world.

This language accords with an important reality: direction acquires meaning in relation to an observer’s position. A person at one point on earth has their own horizon; when their location changes, their spatial relationships change as well.

But the Creator of existence is not subject to the coordinate system a creature has devised to locate itself.

The same point appears in the sequence of the sentence:

“To The God belong the east and the west.” The two edges of the horizon belong to Him.

“Wherever you turn.” Whatever direction you face.

“There is the face of The God.” His face is there.

Human directions help us understand our own position; they are not the boundaries of The God.

The Human Face and “The Face of The God”

A few verses earlier, we read:

“Whoever surrenders their whole orientation to The God.” Al-Baqarah 2:112. The Arabic expresses this through surrendering one’s face (wajh).

Now we read:

“There is the face of The God.”

This proximity in wording matters. In 2:112, a person surrenders their own face or orientation to Him. In 2:115, it becomes clear that His face is not confined to one geographical direction.

Other Qur’anic uses likewise prevent us from reducing “the face of The God” to a physical face at a point in space. The Qur’an speaks of “seeking the face of The God”—acting in pursuit of Him (Al-Baqarah 2:272; Ar-Ra‘d 13:22)—and of “desiring the face of The God” (Ar-Rum 30:38–39). Al-Qasas 28:88 also says:

“Everything is perishable except His face.”

Within this field of meaning, “face” is connected with Him Himself, seeking Him, and turning toward Him—not locating a bodily face in some corner of the world.

Al-Hadid 57:4 also says:

“He is with you wherever you are.”

Geography changes a person’s location; it does not sever their relationship with The God.

The Direction of Prayer Is His Command, Not His Address

Nor should this verse be detached from the question of the direction of prayer (qiblah), since the same expression appears again in Al-Baqarah 2:142, where that subject is addressed directly:

“Say: To The God belong the east and the west.”

One distinction is therefore clear from the outset:

The direction of prayer is His command, not His address.

A command to face a particular direction does not mean that The God is confined to that direction.

There is also a broader reading of the prayer direction that deserves independent study. On this reading, it is not merely a geographical line toward the Kaaba building; it is also an orientation toward the meaning the Sacred House should represent.

Surah Al-Hajj provides substantial material for this reading. It describes the Sacred Mosque as “for the people,” with residents and visitors from outside alike (22:25). The call to pilgrimage addresses people (22:27). It recognizes different rites for different communities, yet affirms one God and calls for surrender to Him (22:34 and 22:67). The same surah defends monasteries, churches, synagogues, and places of prostration where His name is remembered (22:40).

Other verses describe the House as guidance for all peoples, a place of safety, and a means of sustaining people (Aal Imran 3:96–97; Al-Ma’idah 5:97).

Taken together, these passages allow us to test the proposition that turning toward the House is not merely rotating the body toward Mecca’s coordinates. It should also orient a person toward the values for which that House was established: divine oneness, safety, remembrance of The God, openness to people, the coming together of those who believe, and striving to excel in good.

This reading still requires an independent examination of all the verses concerning the direction of prayer. Yet it already raises an important question:

If someone stands physically facing the Kaaba with complete precision, but behaves in a way opposed to its meaning, have they truly turned toward the direction of prayer?

If they regard the mosque as their sect’s property; if they prevent a Jew, Christian, or another Muslim from remembering The God merely because of their religious name; if a house intended for people and their safety becomes an instrument of privilege, exclusion, and superiority; if the building is adorned with gold, stone, and cloth while its spirit of divine oneness, justice, safety, and openness is emptied out—the body may face the Kaaba while the person’s spiritual orientation points in the opposite direction.

In that case, the outward house has been preserved, but the meaning toward which it should orient people has been covered over.

Al-Baqarah 2:177 later recalls the same danger:

“Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east and the west.”

Bodily direction can be a command of worship. But if faith, giving, faithfulness to covenants, patience, and the other realities of serving Him are detached from it, turning the body alone does not bring a person to righteousness.

“All-Encompassing, Knowing”

The ending of the verse now accords with the entire discussion:

“Indeed, The God is all-encompassing, knowing.”

The Arabic adjective expressing breadth and capacity (wāsiʿ) comes from W S ʿ. It should not be reduced to spatial size alone. The same pairing, “all-encompassing, knowing,” appears elsewhere in this surah—in 2:247, 2:261, and 2:268—in contexts of sovereignty, bounty, reward, and forgiveness.

His vastness therefore extends far beyond bodily dimensions.

Here, after east and west and “wherever you turn,” this description clearly accords with the point that human spatial systems and directions cannot limit Him.

Yet alongside “all-encompassing” stands “knowing.”

His vastness does not make Him remote from people or unaware of them. Wherever a person is and whichever way they turn, the truth of their orientation does not escape the knowledge of The God.

He is both all-encompassing and knowing.

Verses 2:114 and 2:115 together form a clear picture. In one, a person tries to close the door of a place of worship; in the other, the Qur’an opens the entire horizon. People can build walls, draw boundaries, and designate directions, but they do not confine The God within any of them.

This verse is also a warning to Muslims. The Kaaba, the Sacred Mosque, and the direction of prayer have their Qur’anic standing, but none is a deed of Muslim ownership over The God. If prayer direction and religious law become instruments of inherent superiority over others, the body’s direction may be correct while the direction of servanthood is wholly reversed. For the distinction between religion itself and historical forms of religious law, see Religion, Surrender, and Religious Law in the Qur’an; for a critique of exclusive claims to salvation, see Paradise Is Not the Monopoly of One Sect or One Religious Law.

East and west, places of worship, and the direction of prayer all lie within the existence that belongs to Him; but The God is confined within none of them.
