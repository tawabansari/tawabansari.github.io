Al-Baqarah 2:97

Say: Whoever is an enemy to Gabriel—indeed, he has brought it down upon your heart by permission of The God, confirming what preceded it, and as guidance and good news for the believers.

Our Reflection

For several verses now, the Qur’an has repeatedly broken down a mental boundary: The God and His guidance are not the exclusive possession of any people or group. They said, “We believe in what was sent down to us”; we believe only in what was revealed to our own people. The Qur’an showed that they had not always submitted even to that same Moses and that same covenant. Then came the claim to a special standing in the Hereafter; yet those very claimants to salvation were described as the people most eager to remain in this world.

Now the discussion turns to Gabriel:

“Say: Whoever is an enemy to Gabriel”; say, whoever holds such hostility toward Gabriel…

Gabriel’s entry into this discussion is not accidental. A few verses earlier, the issue was why The God should send down His bounty upon whichever of His servants He wills. Here the same question appears from another angle: if we are displeased with His choice, can we direct our objection against the one who delivered His message?

The verse itself does not explain the historical cause of this hostility, and we should not fill the Qur’an’s silence with stories. Nor can we conclude from this wording that all Jews were hostile to Gabriel. The Qur’an says “whoever”: anyone who holds such hostility is given this answer:

“For he brought it down upon your heart by permission of The God.”

Gabriel brought this message down upon your heart by permission of The God.

The main weight of the answer rests on “by permission of The God.” Gabriel did not bring the message on his own initiative, nor did he distribute prophethood among peoples according to his own wishes. If the Qur’an came down upon Muhammad’s heart, it did so by His permission. Hostility toward the bearer of the message does not change the sender’s authority to choose.

This is the same truth expressed in different words in Al-Baqarah 2:90:

“That The God should send down of His bounty upon whichever of His servants He wills.”

The God sends down of His bounty upon whichever of His servants He wills.

Someone who acknowledges this authority of The God cannot then blame the bearer of the message because revelation did not descend upon the person or people they had expected.

At this point, an important confusion in reasoning becomes apparent—an error confined neither to this verse nor to one people. Sometimes, instead of assessing a statement by its own evidence, we assess it by the person who brought it. If we dislike the speaker, we imagine that their statement must also be false; or because we do not approve of the intermediary, we set aside the message itself.

The logical problem with this method is straightforward: the truth of a statement and the character or popularity of its speaker are two separate matters. A person we dislike may say something true, just as someone we admire may say something false. Attacking the bearer of a message is not an answer to the message itself. This confusion arises when, instead of answering “What has been said?”, we shift the discussion to “Who said it?”

In this verse, the matter is even clearer, because Gabriel is not even the independent originator of the message: he acts “by permission of The God.”

Jewish sources likewise do not allow us to present hostility toward Gabriel as a general Jewish belief. In the Book of Daniel, Gabriel appears as a messenger and an interpreter: he is commissioned to explain the vision to Daniel and comes to impart understanding. In Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, too, Michael is placed on the right and Gabriel on the left, alongside other angels who serve The God. Differences in roles, or positions on the “right” and “left,” therefore do not mean that angels are divided into the friends and enemies of a people.

This caution matters: the verse speaks of hostility toward Gabriel, but we must not turn that into hostility on the part of all Jews toward Gabriel. What the Qur’an itself has considered necessary for its argument is sufficient.

“He brought it down upon your heart” also clarifies the place of each party. Gabriel brings the message down, the Prophet’s heart receives it, and the permission belongs to The God.

The same picture appears in greater detail in Ash-Shu‘ara 26:192–194:

“And indeed, it is a revelation sent down from the Lord of all worlds. The trustworthy spirit brought it down upon your heart, so that you might be one of the warners.”

The message comes from the Lord of all worlds and has descended upon the Prophet’s heart. The heart here is the place that receives revelation, not the source that produces it.

In An-Nahl 16:102, too, the holy spirit is connected with bringing the message down from the Lord in truth. Gabriel’s precise relationship with the “holy spirit” and the “trustworthy spirit,” and his identity and role throughout the Qur’an, require a separate discussion and are better examined in a dedicated article. In this verse, what is explicit is this: Gabriel brought the message down upon the Prophet’s heart by permission of The God.

But the Qur’an does not merely defend the bearer of the message; it also describes the message itself:

“Confirming what was before it”; confirming what preceded it.

This expression draws the thread of the preceding verses together again. In Al-Baqarah 2:89, a Book came “confirming what was with them.” In 2:91, that same message was called “the truth, confirming what was with them.” Now, too, the Qur’an is presented as confirming what preceded it.

The issue, then, is not an entirely alien message that has come to deny earlier truth at its roots. It confirms the very truth in which the addressees claimed to believe. If such a message is rejected solely because of the route by which it arrived, the problem is no longer its content; it lies in the boundary we have constructed around our acceptance of truth.

And this message does more than confirm the past:

“And as guidance and good news for the believers”; guidance and glad tidings for those who believe.

Why should something that brings guidance and good news to a believer be rejected because of its bearer’s name? The answer lies in the meaning of faith itself. A believer does not accept truth because it has come from their own people, family, or circle; they assess it and, if it is true, find security and trust in relation to it.

The history of Muslim writings about sects also contains a reported example of this same logic. In Al-Farq bayn al-Firaq, ‘Abd al-Qahir al-Baghdadi attributes to a group called the Ghurabiyya the belief that Gabriel was commissioned to deliver revelation to Ali but delivered it to Muhammad instead. This is a report about a particular group in a work on religious sects; it must not be attributed to Twelver Shi‘ism or to all Shi‘a Muslims.

More important than the report itself is the logic behind it: first we decide who should be entitled to the truth; then, when reality does not match our expectation, we accuse the intermediary instead of correcting our expectation. In such a case, truth is no longer our criterion; our preferences and attachments have become the criterion.

This error is far closer to us than something to be sought only in ancient religious disputes. We may calmly listen to a verse, an argument, or a truth spoken by someone we like, yet reject the very same words when they come from a critic of ours. Instead of answering the evidence, we may say that the speaker belongs to a certain denomination, has a certain past, has bad intentions, uses an unsuitable tone, or does not belong to our group.

Sometimes these criticisms of the person are actually correct; even then, however, the statement itself has still not been examined. A speaker’s bad character does not invalidate a reasoned argument, just as a speaker’s good character does not make a claim true.

Here the verse’s lesson in reasoning comes alive: assess the message by its evidence, not by your affection or hatred for the messenger.

The next verse makes this artificial separation still harder to sustain, placing The God, the angels, the messengers, Gabriel, and Michael in a single sequence. One cannot claim loyalty to the sender yet regard an agent who acted by that very sender’s permission as an enemy because one is displeased with the outcome of His choice.

If the message has arrived by permission of The God, hostility toward its bearer takes nothing away from its truth. And if, instead of assessing what is said, we target the speaker, perhaps truth is no longer our concern; perhaps we simply do not want truth to come from a place we did not choose ourselves.
