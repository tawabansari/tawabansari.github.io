Al-Baqarah 2:229

ۖ ۗ ۖ ۗ ۚ

Divorce is twice; then retaining honorably or releasing with kindness. And it is not lawful for you to take back anything of what you have given them, unless the two fear that they will not uphold the limits of The God. Then if you fear that the two will not uphold the limits of The God, there is no blame upon either of them concerning what the woman gives to obtain her release. These are the limits of The God, so do not transgress them; and whoever transgresses the limits of The God—those are the wrongdoers.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:226, God established a four-month waiting period for men who swear to withdraw from their wives. In Al-Baqarah 2:227, He recognized a firm decision to divorce, and in Al-Baqarah 2:228, He addressed the waiting period, the possibility of returning, family responsibility, and the mutual rights of women and men.

The Quran now clarifies the limits of divorce, the possibility of continuing together or separating, women’s financial rights, and a way out of a bond whose continuation may lead to oppression and the violation of rights.

“الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ”

“Divorce is twice.”

“مَرَّتَانِ” speaks of two times or two occasions. This expression matters because the Quran places divorce within an actual process of separation, waiting, the possibility of returning, and decision-making.

In some traditional juristic interpretations, reports and inherited rulings have so displaced the Quranic text that a practice called “triple divorce” has become widespread—as though a man could utter the word divorce three times in one sitting and, through that repetition, carry out three occasions of divorce at once.

But does saying a word three times mean that a process for which the Quran establishes limits and time requirements has occurred three times?

In the preceding verse, God specified three periods of waiting for divorced women and also addressed returning and repairing the relationship during that time. In At-Talaq 65:1, He likewise commands that the waiting period be counted and its limits observed.

These rulings show that divorce is more than uttering a word; it is a decision connected with responsibility, time, and people’s rights.

Suppose a man, in a fit of anger, tells his wife: “I divorce you, divorce you, divorce you!”

Have three waiting periods passed through these three sentences? Have three actual opportunities to reflect, return, and repair the relationship arisen? Have the woman and man separated three times and returned to married life after each separation?

Clearly, none of this has happened.

Repeating the word divorce three times in one sitting does not create three independent occasions of divorce. Counting words in a single sitting to produce a double or triple divorce is inconsistent with the process the Quran describes for divorce.

Each occasion of divorce must be understood as an actual, independent event, with the relevant rulings on waiting, returning, or ending the relationship observed. Of course, if a return occurs before the waiting period ends, there is no need for the entire period to elapse. The length of the waiting period is also determined by the woman’s circumstances and the Quran’s rulings, rather than by converting every case into three calendar months.

The Quran’s rulings should not be altered through reports, fatwas, and inherited customs in ways that render the clear structure of the verses ineffective.

“فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ”

“Then retaining honorably or releasing with kindness.”

This expression states one of the Quran’s most important principles concerning married life and separation.

“إمساك,” from the root م س ك, is connected with taking hold, retaining, and preserving. In contrast, “تسريح,” from the root س ر ح, refers to releasing and letting go freely.

God places two paths before the woman and man: either continue married life honorably or separate with kindness.

Put simply: hold on fully, or let go fully!

If a man wishes to continue married life, he must accept its responsibilities as well: treat his wife properly, respect her rights, and strive to establish affection and tranquility.

He cannot retain her as his wife in name while, in practice, depriving her of affection, companionship, security, rights, and a clear future.

On the other hand, if he has decided to separate, he must allow a way out rather than leave the woman suspended in a situation in which she has neither an actual shared life nor the ability to build a new future.

Sometimes a man becomes estranged from his wife and leaves her in uncertainty for months or even years. He may arrange another life for himself while the woman remains waiting for his decision.

Such conduct is inconsistent with honorable treatment and kindness.

Being held fully is sweet; being held halfway is bitter. Retaining a spouse without fulfilling the responsibilities of married life can be more harmful than a clear and respectful separation.

This is the same principle that mattered in Al-Baqarah 2:226: a person must not keep their spouse waiting indefinitely because of estrangement, an oath, or a failure to decide.

But “تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ” does not mean merely announcing a divorce; the manner of separation and the conduct of the woman and man after married life ends also matter.

After years of marriage, a couple may recognize that continuing the relationship serves neither of them. They can separate without turning to permanent hostility.

Why should a man speak ill of his former wife among friends and relatives after divorce? Why should a woman disclose private details of their shared life to humiliate and discredit her former husband?

They may no longer be able to live together, but this does not mean they must also destroy respect for all the years that have passed.

Kindness in separation does not end when the marriage contract ends. After separating, the woman and man must still respect each other’s human dignity.

For example, if a woman decides to marry another man after the relationship has ended and the prescribed period has passed, her former husband must not interfere in her new life out of jealousy and resentment, speak ill of her, or try to disrupt her new relationship.

The same principle applies to the man. If he remarries after separation, his former wife must not seek to destroy his new life through jealousy, threats, disclosing private matters, or inciting others.

After years of marriage, seeing a former spouse with someone else may be emotionally difficult. But this feeling gives no one the right to destroy another person’s freedom, peace, and future.

Actual separation means accepting that the marital relationship has ended and that both people have the right to build a new future.

This becomes even more important when the woman and man have children.

Sometimes parents turn their children into instruments for pressuring one another after divorce.

A man may refuse to pay his children’s necessary expenses to take revenge on his former wife, or use them to threaten their mother. A woman, too, may turn the children against their father because of conflict with her former husband, or use their relationship with him to impose her personal demands.

Sometimes one parent continually speaks ill of the other in the child’s presence and tries to destroy the child’s affection for their father or mother. They may even ask the child to choose between them.

Must a child who had no part in the parents’ conflict bear the burden of their resentment and revenge?

A child is not an instrument of revenge. Divorce can end the marital relationship, but it does not remove the father’s and mother’s responsibilities toward their child.

In Al-Baqarah 2:233, God addresses parents’ responsibilities toward their child and explicitly states:

“لَا تُضَارَّ وَالِدَةٌ بِوَلَدِهَا وَلَا مَوْلُودٌ لَهُ بِوَلَدِهِ”

“Neither a mother should be harmed on account of her child, nor a father on account of his child.”

This command shows that even in family disputes, the child must not become a means for the father and mother to harm one another.

A woman and man who have separated can still cooperate in their child’s upbringing, education, health, and provision. They need not become husband and wife again to fulfill these responsibilities; they need only keep their personal disagreement separate from their child’s rights.

There is, of course, a difference between vindictive slander and telling the truth to seek justice. Kindness does not mean concealing oppression, remaining silent about violence, or being forced to relinquish rights.

Separating with kindness means ending the relationship without revenge, humiliation, needless exposure of private matters, interference in each other’s new lives, obstruction, or using children to hurt the other person.

After the bond has ended and the prescribed period has been observed, the woman, too, may decide her future and begin a new life with someone she chooses, within the Quran’s rulings. Her former husband or the families must not block her path out of prejudice and hostility. This issue will be examined more clearly in Al-Baqarah 2:232.

Nor should “ma‘ruf” and “ihsan” be reduced simply to social customs. A prevalent custom does not become proper merely by being common if it involves oppression and humiliation. Ma‘ruf must accord with right and proper conduct, and ihsan calls people to kindness even when bringing a dispute to an end.

Thus, the Quran neither regards the forced continuation of a harmful life together as a virtue nor accepts separation accompanied by hostility and revenge.

“وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَكُمْ أَنْ تَأْخُذُوا مِمَّا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ شَيْئًا”

“And it is not lawful for you to take back anything of what you have given them.”

After setting out the two paths of continuing together or separating, the Quran turns directly to the woman’s financial rights.

The basic ruling is clear: a man has no right to reclaim what he has given the woman because of divorce.

This is especially important concerning the mahr, the marital gift due to the woman.

In marriage, mahr is the woman’s financial right and a sign of the man’s commitment. In An-Nisa 4:4, God commands that women be given their mahr and allows taking something of it only when the women willingly relinquish it themselves.

In An-Nisa 4:20, taking it back is likewise forbidden even if the man has given his wife a great amount of wealth.

A man therefore cannot say after deciding to divorce: “Since you are no longer my wife, you must return everything I have given you.”

Nor can he force the woman to surrender her financial rights to escape the marriage by threatening or humiliating her, withholding his responsibilities, or prolonging the conflict.

God also states in An-Nisa 4:19:

“وَلَا تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ لِتَذْهَبُوا بِبَعْضِ مَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ”

“And do not put them under pressure in order to take away part of what you have given them.”

The same verse goes on to state an exception concerning “فَاحِشَةٍ مُبَيِّنَةٍ”; however, this specific exception cannot be extended to every family disagreement or dissatisfaction and made a tool for financial pressure on the woman.

These verses make clear that a man has no right to force the woman to surrender her financial rights by mistreating her and making her life difficult.

Yet Al-Baqarah 2:229 immediately states an exception.

“إِلَّا أَنْ يَخَافَا أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ”

“Unless the two fear that they will not uphold the limits of The God.”

The discussion here concerns a situation in which the woman and man fear that continuing their relationship will lead them to fail to observe the divine limits.

The disagreement may have escalated so much that they can no longer respect each other’s rights. Perhaps the woman no longer wishes to continue the marriage, or one party has failed to honor their mutual commitments. Sometimes the incompatibility becomes so severe that both fear continuing hostility, oppression, and violations of one another’s rights.

The expression “يَخَافَا” deserves attention: it speaks of their fear and concern, without necessarily making a specific betrayal or sin by the woman a condition.

This exception should therefore not be confined to women who have committed a wrong. A woman may, without committing any sin, no longer be able or willing to continue married life and fear that continuing this bond will lead to hostility and the violation of rights.

In such a situation, the Quran provides a way to reach an agreement and obtain release.

“فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ”

“Then if you fear that the two will not uphold the limits of The God, there is no blame upon either of them concerning what the woman gives to obtain her release.”

Initially, the verse spoke of “يَخَافَا,” the fear of the woman and her husband. Now, however, it addresses a plural audience: “خِفْتُمْ,” if you fear.

This shift may indicate that examining the disagreement is not confined to the spouses themselves and that others may also play a role in understanding the situation and helping resolve the dispute—for example, relatives, family arbiters, or those who have accepted responsibility for addressing the disagreement.

In An-Nisa 4:35, the Quran likewise speaks of arbitration involving the families of the woman and man to resolve discord.

Nevertheless, the expression “خِفْتُمْ” itself does not name any particular authority or institution.

It then states that there is no blame upon either of them concerning what the woman gives to obtain her release.

“افتدت,” from the root ف د ي, is connected with obtaining release through the payment of compensation.

For example, a woman may conclude that life with her husband is no longer bearable and that continuing it will cause further conflict and harm. To end the bond, she may willingly relinquish all or part of her mahr or offer other compensation.

In later juristic terminology, such a separation came to be called “khul‘ divorce.”

The important point, however, is that the Quran presents this path as a means of escaping an impasse and preventing transgression of the divine limits, not as a way for a man to extract wealth from his wife through pressure and mistreatment.

A woman must not be forced to submit to the man’s imposed financial demands in order to escape his oppression and abuse. Such exploitation is inconsistent with the very honorable treatment, kindness, and prohibition of transgression stated in the verse.

On the other hand, if the woman freely and willingly reaches a financial agreement to end the relationship, such an agreement should not be regarded as sinful in every circumstance.

This ruling shows that alongside protecting the woman’s financial rights, the Quran also leaves open a way out of a relationship whose continuation has reached an impasse.

The importance of this solution becomes clearer when we consider certain customs concerning the determination of mahr.

In some societies, mahr, which should signify commitment and responsibility, has become an instrument of boasting, bargaining between families, or imposing crushing obligations.

For example, some families set a one-million-dollar mahr for a young employee whose monthly income does not even reach a hundred dollars!

Can someone who may never acquire such wealth in their entire life knowingly and responsibly accept an obligation to pay it?

Does this demonstrate respect for the woman and strengthen the marriage, or create a commitment far removed from the man’s actual means from the outset?

Sometimes families go even further.

Suppose a father has died, leaving his house and courtyard to his four children. All four have a share in this property. Yet when one son marries, the entire house and courtyard are specified as his wife’s mahr, as though all the property belonged to that one son!

Can one of four heirs pledge the entire inherited house as his mahr obligation without the other three heirs’ consent and authorization?

Does a marriage contract give anyone the right to transact with other people’s property?

If done without the consent and authorization of those entitled to the property, this is more than setting a heavy mahr; it also involves violating others’ financial rights.

In An-Nisa 4:29, the Quran forbids consuming and appropriating one another’s wealth wrongfully. No one can manufacture a right to dispose of others’ property in the name of marriage and mahr.

The problem with these customs is that many people do not consider the actual consequences of their obligations when entering the marriage contract.

Weddings and marriage ceremonies are usually accompanied by joy and excitement. Families sometimes accept commitments without seriously examining whether they can be fulfilled, in order to maintain appearances, display wealth, compete with others, or finalize an agreement.

The man may accept any amount in haste or to prevent the marriage from falling through. The woman’s family may likewise regard a heavy mahr as a sign of their daughter’s worth and the respect shown to her.

But how can a person’s worth be measured by the amount of mahr?

Is a young woman with a smaller mahr worth less than one for whom a million-dollar mahr has been specified?

Mahr should be an actual right and a sign of responsibility, not a competition to name the largest number.

In An-Nisa 4:21, God describes marriage as “مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا,” a firm covenant.

A covenant intended to form the foundation of a shared life must not become a transaction for displaying wealth, imposing unrealistic obligations, or obtaining financial advantage.

The problem becomes more evident when married life encounters conflict.

Consider a couple who, after several years together and despite efforts to reconcile, recognize that continuing the relationship serves neither of them.

Yet the man now owes a mahr so heavy that he cannot pay it. The woman, for her part, does not wish to lose her right. Families enter the dispute, threats and hostility increase, and each side tries to pressure the other.

As a result, a relationship that should either continue honorably or end with kindness becomes a battleground of financial conflict and revenge.

Sometimes a man refuses to divorce because he cannot pay the mahr—not because he seeks reconciliation, but because he fears the financial obligation. Meanwhile, the woman may remain waiting for the relationship to end for years.

Should mahr, which ought to support commitment and women’s rights, become a chain that closes the path of a proper separation to both?

Here the wisdom of the Quranic exception becomes clearer.

On the one hand, the Quran does not allow a man to reclaim wrongfully what he has given the woman. On the other, it leaves open a path to agreement through which the woman can freely relinquish all or part of her entitlement, or pay compensation, to obtain release from the relationship.

Thus, if the woman and man recognize that continued disagreement over mahr and other financial rights is drawing them into hostility and transgression of the divine limits, they can facilitate separation through a fair agreement.

The woman may relinquish part of her mahr, or the two may agree on how to bring their obligations to an end. When accompanied by genuine freedom and consent, such an agreement can prevent continued hostility and hardship.

Of course, this solution does not mean that any man may unilaterally reduce the mahr because it is heavy or force the woman to give up her right.

Nor is the size of the mahr alone evidence that it is forbidden. In An-Nisa 4:20, the Quran prohibits reclaiming even a great amount of wealth given to the woman.

The important distinction is this: an obligation knowingly and consensually accepted, involving lawful property and respecting others’ rights, is not the same as one built on deception, pressure, others’ property, or disregard for actual financial capacity.

The woman’s right must remain protected, but a right must not become a tool of revenge and oppression; likewise, the man’s financial responsibility must not be turned into an insoluble crisis from the beginning of married life through irrational customs and fabricated obligations.

Mahr is examined in the study “Quranic Marriage” as a sign of economic maturity, commitment, and responsibility. In that study, we explain that mahr must not become a means of boasting, class pressure, and burdensome bargaining, because such conduct can make forming a family difficult and, when disagreement arises, obstruct a just separation.

This verse likewise shows that, alongside safeguarding women’s financial rights, God has provided a way to obtain release and bring disputes to an end.

“تِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ فَلَا تَعْتَدُوهَا”

“These are the limits of The God, so do not transgress them.”

After explaining the occasions of divorce, honorable treatment, kindness, protection of the woman’s financial rights, and the possibility of obtaining release through agreement, God warns that these are His limits.

“حدود,” from the root ح د د, is connected with boundaries, limits, and separating domains. Here it concerns limits within which human conduct in marriage and at separation must remain.

These limits cannot be confined to the amount of mahr. Respect for rights, honesty, fulfilling commitments, avoiding harm, continuing life honorably, and separating with kindness all matter in understanding the responsibilities of the woman and man.

Sometimes people themselves create conditions for violating others’ rights through improper customs and obligations, then turn to those same customs and inherited rulings to solve the problems they have created.

For example, when families set a mahr beyond the man’s actual means, or make others’ property the subject of an obligation without its owners’ authorization, they cannot evade examination of justice and people’s rights simply by calling it a “marriage custom” or a “mahr entitlement.”

However, it is not always the case that the man lacked the ability to pay the mahr from the outset.

Sometimes a man is wealthy when he marries but later loses his assets through life’s difficulties.

For example, imagine a man who has a successful business when entering the marriage contract and accepts a large mahr for his wife in keeping with his financial means. A few years later, however, he suffers heavy business losses, loses his assets, and becomes bankrupt.

He can now no longer pay the earlier heavy mahr and also struggles economically to meet everyday expenses and repay his debts.

His wife, meanwhile, no longer wishes to continue living with him and has decided to separate. Yet she insists on receiving the entire earlier mahr immediately—an amount the man has no actual ability to pay in his present circumstances.

In such circumstances, is insisting on immediate payment of money that the man can no longer provide consistent with honorable treatment and kindness?

Can genuine inability be turned into ability through threats, pressure, and hostility?

Here the issue concerns more than the woman’s financial right; it also concerns the actual circumstances of two people and how justice and kindness are to be observed.

The man accepted an obligation when entering the contract, and bankruptcy alone does not automatically erase it. Yet the woman, too, can consider his changed circumstances and accept a fair way to resolve the disagreement instead of insisting on something now beyond his means.

In Al-Baqarah 2:280, God states concerning a debtor in hardship:

“وَإِنْ كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ”

“And if someone is in hardship, then a postponement until a time of ease.”

He then states:

“وَأَنْ تَصَدَّقُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ”

“And if you remit it as charity, that is better for you, if you know.”

These two expressions clarify an important distinction: granting time to a debtor in hardship is one matter, and voluntarily forgiving all or part of a financial entitlement is another.

The man’s bankruptcy therefore does not, by itself, remove the woman’s right to mahr. Yet the debtor’s actual inability must not be ignored by demanding immediate payment of an amount he cannot provide in his present circumstances.

The woman can allow time for payment of the mahr or agree to payment in installments. She can also willingly forgive part or all of it; however, this forgiveness is a voluntary act of kindness and must not be turned into a compulsory duty.

The man, too, must remain faithful to his undertaking and, if he gains the financial means in the future, pay the woman’s remaining entitlements in accordance with the commitment and agreement.

Such conduct recognizes the woman’s financial right while also taking account of the man’s actual circumstances.

Honorable treatment and kindness do not matter only when everything goes as we wish; their value becomes especially evident when disagreement, inability, and difficulty arise.

Likewise, a man who forces a woman to relinquish her mahr through pressure and abuse cannot justify his conduct by invoking the exception “فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ.”

Conversely, if the woman uses her financial right solely to seek revenge and cause harm, her conduct must be assessed by the same standard of justice and avoidance of transgression.

Neither the woman nor the man has the right to use divine rulings as a means of violating the other’s rights.

“وَمَنْ يَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ”

“And whoever transgresses the limits of The God—those are the wrongdoers.”

The ending of the verse is very explicit.

God does not say that whoever violates these limits has merely made a family-related mistake; rather, He identifies such people as wrongdoers.

Oppression is not confined to killing, theft, or overt encroachment upon others’ property. A person can also be an oppressor within the most private family relationships.

A man who leaves his wife in uncertainty, forcibly reclaims her financial rights, or uses divorce for revenge has transgressed the limits of justice.

A woman who knowingly violates others’ rights or harms someone through deception and financial exploitation likewise falls outside the standard of justice.

Families that create unrealistic and contentious obligations at marriage instead of providing the conditions for a healthy life must answer for the consequences of their conduct, especially when they also involve other people’s rights in such agreements.

In this verse, the Quran brings together two important principles: protecting rights and opening a way to release.

One cannot abolish women’s rights in the name of separation, nor can one trap people forever in a relationship filled with hostility, pressure, and oppression in the name of preserving marriage or financial obligations.

The marriage covenant must be entered knowingly and responsibly. If continuing life together is possible and proper, it should be preserved through honorable conduct and respect for mutual rights. But if reconciliation is no longer possible, separation must also take place with kindness and without revenge.

The verse’s message is clear: divorce is not a verbal game accomplished by repeating a word twice or three times in one sitting; it is a responsible process with limits, an opportunity to return, and mutual rights. A woman must not be left in uncertainty, nor should a man be trapped by irrational obligations and claims beyond his capacity and authority.

Mahr should signify commitment and support a right, not serve as an instrument of bargaining, boasting, and revenge. The Quran both prevents the wrongful reclamation of women’s entitlements and opens a path to agreement and release when continuing the relationship would lead to a failure to observe the divine limits.

The God asks people to respect one another’s rights both when beginning married life and when bringing it to an end.

Either retain honorably or release with kindness; but never transgress the limits of The God, because transgressing these limits is oppression.
