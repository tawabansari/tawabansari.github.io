Al-Baqarah 2:80

ۚ ۖ

And they said: The Fire will not touch us except for a few numbered days. Say: Have you obtained a covenant from The God (Allah)? Then The God will never break His covenant; or do you say about The God what you do not know?

Our Reflection

This verse is another link in the chain the Qur'an has opened in the preceding verses. One group distorted the word of The God after understanding it through reason and knowledge. Another worried about the disclosure of what The God had opened to them. Another did not know the Book and relied on recitations, wishes, and personal notions in place of knowledge. And another wrote something with their own hands and then said, “This is from The God,” to receive a small price in return.

Now verse 80 shows one of the beliefs that can take shape in such an environment:

The Fire will not touch us except for a few numbered days.

The Fire will reach us only for a few limited days.

They do not deny the Fire itself. They do not say that there is no accounting or no punishment. They even accept that the Fire may reach them. But they have determined its end in advance: whatever we have done, the Fire is not our ultimate destiny; it lasts a few limited days and then passes.

Numbered (ma‘dudah) comes from the root ʿ D D, meaning to count. The days they claim are counted and limited, something whose end can be envisaged in advance. In Arabic usage, being counted sometimes also suggests fewness, but the primary meaning is being limited and countable. They do not see their punishment as infinite and unending; they have imagined a limited period for themselves.

This very idea can change a person's relationship with responsibility. To evade a warning, one need not deny Hell. It is enough to be certain that, whatever happens, one's own final outcome will be salvation. Punishment, however severe, then becomes a temporary stage that will eventually pass.

This condition is also connected with the weaknesses the Qur'an identifies in human beings. Humans are hasty, were created weak, and can prefer what is immediate and present to what will come in the future. Today's benefit is visible, while tomorrow's consequence seems distant. Only a few verses earlier, the subject was a small price: an immediate return for which human speech was attributed to The God. If this is accompanied by confidence that punishment will not be final, the person's inner restraint against such a transaction becomes weaker still.

But the Qur'an does not begin calculating the number of days. It does not say that they have underestimated the duration of punishment or that the actual number of days is something else. It moves the question from the duration of punishment to the foundation of the claim itself:

Say: Have you obtained a covenant from The God?

Say: Have you secured a covenant with The God?

Here the center of the verse becomes clear. The issue is not whether The God has the power to forgive. The Qur'an repeatedly speaks of His mercy and forgiveness. The issue is how these people know that His mercy will necessarily operate in precisely this way for them.

Hope in mercy is not the same as a guarantee of salvation.

A person can hope in the mercy of The God, repent, return, and seek His forgiveness. The Qur'an even tells those who have transgressed against themselves not to despair of His mercy, but the same passage calls them to return, submit, and follow what has been sent down. Mercy opens the path of return; it is not meant to become a reassurance that keeps a person on the same mistaken course.

If someone says, “Whatever happens, my final outcome is salvation,” the verse asks for just one thing: show the covenant supporting that guarantee.

And if a covenant truly comes from Him, the verse itself makes the consequence clear:

Then The God will never break His covenant.

The God will never fail to fulfill His own covenant.

The problem is not His faithfulness. If He has made a promise, His promise is true. The question is whether a person really has such a promise from Him, or has accepted as a divine promise something they wish came from The God.

For this reason, the ending of the verse sets two possibilities against one another:

Or do you say about The God what you do not know?

Or are you making a statement about The God without knowledge of it?

Either there is a covenant, or there is a claim without knowledge.

This ending also connects closely with the preceding verses. In verse 78, there were people who did not know the Book and relied on supposition. In verse 79, others presented their own writing as coming from The God. Now a categorical statement is made about the Hereafter and people's ultimate fate, and the Qur'an brings the same boundary to life again: is this knowledge, or a statement about The God for which you have no knowledge?

The issue is therefore not merely an error in estimating the duration of punishment. It is constructing a guarantee about the Hereafter and attributing it to The God without knowledge.

The Qur'an repeats this claim of a few days in the Fire in Ali 'Imran, but there it also states the effect of the belief:

That is because they said: The Fire will not touch us except for a few numbered days; and what they used to fabricate deceived them in their religion.

This was because they claimed that the Fire would reach them only for a few limited days, and the falsehoods they fabricated misled them within their religion.

Here the connection between such a guarantee and the weakening of human responsibility is no longer merely a psychological possibility. The Qur'an itself says that what they falsely fabricated and attributed deceived them in their religion.

A person may deny neither The God nor the Hereafter, yet construct an image of Him that makes the consequence of sin predetermined, limited, and bearable for themselves. They may say: The God is merciful, so my final outcome is secure; He is forgiving, so whatever happens I will ultimately be saved; or my belonging to the right group is enough to ensure that the Fire is not my final destiny.

But the mercy of The God cannot be turned into a personal guarantee without His covenant.

Nor does this warning remain specific to the Children of Israel. The Qur'an itself says in An-Nisa 4:123:

It is not according to your wishes, nor the wishes of the People of the Book. Whoever does wrong will be recompensed for it.

The matter is determined neither by your wishes and notions nor by those of the People of the Book; whoever does evil receives its recompense.

This verse does not allow readers of the Qur'an to condemn the People of the Book's notions of salvation and then construct the same arrangement for themselves under a new name. The issue is not simply which people or religious school claims salvation; it is the covenant and evidence on which that claim rests.

Al-Baqarah 2:111 shows the same boundary from another angle. When Paradise is claimed exclusively for a particular religious identity, the Qur'an calls that claim their wishes and notions (amani), and then says:

Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful.

If you are telling the truth, produce your evidence.

The standard therefore does not change. Concerning punishment, a covenant is requested; concerning exclusive possession of Paradise, proof is requested. A person's religious name cannot take the place of either.

The same danger can recur among followers of the Qur'an. In later Muslim theological traditions, different forms of the belief developed that a sinful monotheist may be punished, but will eventually leave the Fire if they die with monotheism or faith as defined by that theological system. This is a broad subject. A detailed examination of the reports cited, Sunni and Imami Shiite positions, intercession, divine will, and the relationship of this belief to Qur'anic verses has been undertaken separately in the article Blind to Ourselves, Quick to Judge Others: A Critique of the Double Standard of Advocates of Opinion and Hadith on Salvation, which readers can consult for a closer study. Here, the verse's own question is enough for us: if the ultimate salvation of a category of people is regarded as certain, where is the covenant establishing that certainty?

An-Nisa 4:48 and 4:116 are among the most important verses cited in this discussion:

And He forgives what is less than that for whomever He wills.

What falls short of that, He forgives for whomever He chooses.

The decisive expression is “for whomever He wills.” The door of forgiveness is open, but the choice remains with The God. The verse does not say that everyone belonging to a particular doctrinal category will necessarily be forgiven in the end.

“For whomever He wills” and “this entire group will certainly be saved in the end” are not the same proposition.

This distinction becomes still more important alongside other Qur'anic warnings of punishment. An-Nisa 4:93 speaks of Hell, abiding therein, wrath, a curse, and a great punishment for someone who intentionally kills a believer. In Al-Furqan 25:68–71, associating partners with The God, killing, and unlawful sexual intercourse appear together, and after the severe warning the Qur'an itself explicitly states the path of return: repentance, faith, and righteous action.

Mercy, then, is not closed. Even for grave sins, there is a path of return. But where the Qur'an states an exception and a way to salvation, it states them itself.

This is not the same as constructing a general guarantee for someone who dies persisting in sin without repentance.

On the other hand, this same verse does not allow us to invent categorical judgments in the opposite direction either. Just as we have no right to guarantee salvation for ourselves, we have no right to determine the certain fate of other individuals without a clear covenant and proof. Final judgment has not been placed in human hands.

Al-Ma'idah 5:18 returns this authority to its rightful possessor:

He forgives whomever He wills and punishes whomever He wills.

Whomever He chooses, He forgives; and whomever He chooses, He punishes.

Neither a claim to being beloved by The God, nor ethnic belonging, nor a religious name removes the authority to judge from Him.

The covenant in verse 80 is therefore not something a person can derive from wishes, tradition, religious affiliation, or personal confidence. It must truly come from The God. If He has promised, His promise is true. If He has set a condition, that very condition stands. If He has left something dependent on His will, a human being has no right to declare the outcome certain on His behalf.

The ending of the verse asks us to uphold a simple but demanding responsibility in matters of knowledge: where we lack knowledge, we must not make categorical statements about The God.

Human beings would like to possess a complete map of the Hereafter: to know who will certainly be saved, who will certainly perish, how long each person will be punished, and which group has immunity in advance. But the Qur'an demands evidence for such certainty.

Have you obtained a covenant?

If so, The God does not break His covenant.

And if not, we must beware of putting our wishes and notions in the place of knowledge and then attributing them to Him.

The following verses will continue the answer. Verse 81 begins by contradicting the claim of limited punishment with an emphatic affirmation (bala), returning attention to what a person has earned and the wrongdoing that has encompassed them. Verse 82 then places faith and righteous action on the other side. The Qur'an's response to this claim of salvation therefore does not end in verse 80; verses 80–82 form a closely connected sequence.

The lesson of this verse itself, however, is clear:

Hope in mercy is not the same as a guarantee of salvation. If we attribute a categorical judgment about our own fate or that of others to The God, we must show the covenant and knowledge supporting it; otherwise, we have said about Him what we do not know.
