Al-Baqarah 2:263

ۗ

A decent word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury; and The God is free of need and forbearing.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:262, the Quran spoke of those who do not follow their giving with reproach or injury. Now it goes a step further, explaining the value of treating people well even in comparison with giving wealth.

“قَوْلٌ مَعْرُوفٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ”

“A decent word and forgiveness.”

“Maʿrūf,” from the root AYN R F, is connected with knowing and recognizing. The Quran has not listed every instance of maʿrūf individually, but has presented principles, limits, and foundational verses for evaluating conduct, customs, and the rules of life. Thus, not everything prevalent in society or accepted in the name of religion is necessarily maʿrūf; the criteria for judging it are Quranic principles and the verses themselves in their interconnection. For an examination of these criteria, see Forqan’s Quranic Principles section.

In this verse, a decent word is speech that preserves a needy person’s respect and dignity, even when financial help is not possible.

But the Quran does not stop at good speech; it places “مَغْفِرَة” alongside it.

“Maghfirah,” from the root GH F R, denotes forgiveness and covering a fault. In human relationships, it also refers to overlooking a lapse and refraining from reproaching or exposing another person.

In Al-Isra 17:28, the Quran likewise teaches that even when a person cannot help those in need, they should speak gently and kindly to them. Ad-Duha 93:10 also forbids driving away someone who asks.

The Quran then clarifies the value of these two forms of conduct:

“خَيْرٌ مِنْ صَدَقَةٍ يَتْبَعُهَا أَذًى”

“Better than charity followed by injury.”

This comparison is deeply meaningful. Giving charity is a good deed, but when it is accompanied by injury, its ethical value is damaged. By contrast, decent speech accompanied by forgiveness can preserve a needy person’s dignity even without a monetary gift.

The Quran does not make the amount of wealth given the measure of goodness; it also attends to a person’s conduct and its effect on others.

For example, declining someone’s request for help with decent speech and forgiveness is better than building homes and shelter even for a thousand needy people, only to reproach and hurt them afterward.

Likewise, someone unable to provide financial help should not accompany that inability with harshness and disrespect. Someone able to give should not suppose that paying money permits them to treat the recipient however they please.

The verse finally ends with two divine attributes:

“وَاللَّهُ غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ”

“And The God is free of need and forbearing.”

“Ghanī,” from the root GH N Y, denotes freedom from need, while “ḥalīm,” from the root H L M, denotes forbearance and self-restraint.

God has no need of human wealth or charity. Our giving does not meet a need of God; rather, it is an opportunity to do good, meet others’ needs, and develop our own ethical character.

And God is “ḥalīm”: despite His power and freedom from need, He shows forbearance toward His servants’ faults and does not hasten to punish them.

The placement of these two attributes at the end of the verse recalls an important truth: if The God is forbearing despite His freedom from need and absolute power, how can a person consider themselves superior because of wealth given to another, and treat a needy person with harshness and humiliation?
