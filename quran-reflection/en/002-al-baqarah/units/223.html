Al-Baqarah 2:223

ۖ ۚ ۗ

Your wives are a field of cultivation for you; so come to your field of cultivation however you wish, and send ahead for yourselves; and be mindful of The God, and know that you will meet Him; and give good news to the believers.

Our Reflection

“نِسَاؤُكُمْ حَرْثٌ لَكُمْ”

“Your wives are a field of cultivation for you.”

Some patriarchal readings have used this verse as though a woman were an instrument of male desire and a husband could treat her however he wished. But this reading overlooks both the meaning of “harth” and the commands of taqwa and responsibility that follow in the same verse. The Quran speaks of cultivation and its yield, not of a man’s ownership of a woman or permission to exploit her.

The preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:222, set out the limits of sexual relations during menstruation. Now, through the expression “حَرْث”, the Quran directs our attention to fertility and the bringing forth and nurturing of offspring.

The root ح ر ث concerns cultivating, cultivated land, and the yield of cultivation. Just as a farmer sows seed and waits for it to grow and bear fruit, in a marital union a fertilized egg can develop in a woman’s womb and lead to the birth of a child.

In Al-Baqarah 2:205, “الْحَرْثَ وَالنَّسْلَ” also appear together, while in Ash-Shura 42:20, “harth” is used for the yield and fruit of human endeavor.

The comparison of a woman to cultivated land thus points to her role in fertility and nurturing life; it does not mean that she is her husband’s property, like land or possessions.

Just as cultivated land does not produce a good yield without water, care, and the farmer’s attention, married life does not flourish without affection, compassion, and attention to a woman’s needs. A man cannot expect his wife to bear and nurture offspring while neglecting her comfort, emotional care, sustenance, and financial support. The Quran commands honorable treatment of women in An-Nisa 4:19; in Ar-Rum 30:21, it names affection and mercy among the foundations of tranquility between spouses; in Al-Baqarah 2:233, it places responsibility for providing mothers with food and clothing honorably upon the father; and in At-Talaq 65:7, it emphasizes providing support according to one’s means. A man therefore has more than rights and wishes in relation to his wife; he is also responsible for care, affection, meeting her needs, and her comfort.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, the Quran calls husband and wife garments for one another, and in Al-Baqarah 2:228 it says:

“وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ”

“And they have rights corresponding honorably to their obligations.”

These mutual rights are incompatible with the idea of a man’s unilateral dominion over a woman’s body. Both woman and man are human beings with agency who have shared responsibilities and rights within the marriage covenant.

“فَأْتُوا حَرْثَكُمْ أَنَّىٰ شِئْتُمْ”

“So come to your field of cultivation however you wish.”

“أَنَّىٰ شِئْتُمْ” indicates openness and choice in the manner of the relationship, but this choice is expressed in connection with “harth”. The subject is marital relations and fertility, not unlimited permission for whatever behavior a man desires.

A man’s desire alone does not take the place of a woman’s consent. Marital relations are a relationship between two human beings and must involve agreement, respect for each other’s rights, and avoidance of harm.

Another openness can also be seen in this expression. The God speaks of fertility and its outcome without restricting the means of attaining it to a single form.

Today, some couples cannot have children through the usual means because of physical or genetic problems. Medical science has provided possibilities through which a man’s sperm and a woman’s egg are combined in a laboratory and the embryo is then transferred to the womb.

From the connection between “harth” and “أَنَّىٰ شِئْتُمْ”, permission to use various methods of achieving fertility, including in vitro fertilization, can be inferred. These methods help couples facing infertility to have children and do not, in principle, conflict with the Quranic command concerning cultivation and nurturing offspring.

In some cases, an embryo formed from a couple’s sperm and egg develops in another woman’s womb. This method also raises the important question of the bond of the womb and family rights.

In An-Nisa 4:1, after recalling the creation of human beings and the spread of their descendants, The God draws attention to “وَالْأَرْحَامَ”. The womb is not merely where an embryo develops; it is also connected with kinship and people’s responsibilities toward one another.

If a couple’s embryo develops in another woman’s womb, the gestational carrier’s role in pregnancy and the child’s birth cannot be disregarded. The agreement and consent of all concerned, protection of the child’s rights, and clarity about family relationships are important.

Using medical possibilities for fertility can therefore accord with the choice and openness expressed in the verse, but this choice must be accompanied by respect for women’s dignity, children’s rights, and family ties.

The God continues:

“وَقَدِّمُوا لِأَنْفُسِكُمْ”

“And send ahead for yourselves.”

After recalling cultivation and fertility, the Quran turns human attention to the outcome of conduct and to the future.

A farmer does not go to the land merely to scatter seed; the farmer also thinks about its growth and eventual yield. In married life, too, people should not look only to their immediate desires, but should consider the outcome of this union and the responsibilities that follow.

Among the most important fruits of marriage is bringing forth and nurturing worthy children—children who grow in affection, awareness, and responsibility. In Al-Furqan 25:74, the Quran conveys this prayer in the words of God’s servants:

“رَبَّنَا هَبْ لَنَا مِنْ أَزْوَاجِنَا وَذُرِّيَّاتِنَا قُرَّةَ أَعْيُنٍ”

“Our Lord, grant us joy of the eyes through our spouses and offspring.”

Nevertheless, marriage is not solely for reproduction, and this verse does not prohibit marital intimacy without the intention of having children. Affection, tranquility, faithfulness, and companionship between husband and wife are also valuable fruits of shared life, as Ar-Rum 30:21 emphasizes tranquility, affection, and mercy between spouses.

Even couples who do not have children can have a fruitful life together.

The purpose of marriage and the mutual responsibilities of wife and husband are examined in detail in the article “Quranic Marriage”.

“وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّكُمْ مُلَاقُوهُ”

“And be mindful of The God, and know that you will meet Him.”

After describing choice in marital relations, The God immediately commands taqwa.

This connection matters: choice is not permission to act irresponsibly.

A man who mistreats his wife, disregards her consent, or coerces her under the pretext of marital rights cannot justify his behavior through “أَنَّىٰ شِئْتُمْ”. The same Quran that speaks of choice also commands respect for mutual rights and taqwa.

Taqwa here means attending to what is right even in the most private part of life: a spouse’s rights, children’s rights, and the responsibilities arising from one’s decisions.

This principle also applies to modern fertility methods. In decisions about having children, woman and man must respect each other’s consent, the health and rights of those concerned, and the child’s future.

The reminder of meeting The God also shows that this responsibility is not only toward other people. A person will also be answerable to The God for their conduct.

The meaning of taqwa and its connection with conscious care and justice are examined in the article “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

“وَبَشِّرِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ”

“And give good news to the believers.”

The verse ends with good news for believers. After speaking of marital relations, fertility, the future, and taqwa, this ending reminds us that shared life can also be a sphere of faith and good conduct.

A woman and man who live together with affection and agreement, uphold each other’s rights, and work responsibly to nurture offspring and the family’s future strengthen their union through faith and taqwa.

The verse’s message is clear: a woman is not a man’s property, and comparing her to “harth” does not authorize indulgence in desire or domination over her. Harth speaks of fertility and the bringing forth and nurturing of offspring, while “أَنَّىٰ شِئْتُمْ” indicates openness in its means—an openness from which permission for modern fertility methods can also be inferred. All these choices, however, must be accompanied by agreement, respect for mutual rights, preservation of family ties, and taqwa.
