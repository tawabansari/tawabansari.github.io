Al-Baqarah 2:49

And when We rescued you from the people of Pharaoh: they were driving you into the worst punishment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women alive; and in that there was a great trial and revealing test (bala') from your Lord (Rabb).

Our Reflection

This verse comes immediately after verse 48, the verse that closed all escape routes from accounting and declared decisively that no nafs fills the place of another nafs, no substitute shafa'ah is accepted, no compensation will be taken, and no external force can pull the human being outside the law of truth. Now the Qur'an returns the Children of Israel to one of their greatest historical ni'mahs: rescue from the people of Pharaoh.

This remembrance is, in reality, a continuation of the command udhkurū ni'matī, remember My ni'mah. The ni'mah of the Rabb was not limited to having the Book, prophets, and historical fadl. One of the clearest forms of this ni'mah was being rescued from a system built on istid'af, division, exploitation, and the breaking of human capacity. In this verse, the Qur'an does not treat rescue as a passing historical incident only. It turns it into an ethical, social, and political memory, because a people rescued from such an oppressive system should not again fall into forgetfulness and indifference toward truth, justice, and responsibility.

The phrase najjaynākum min āli fir'awn makes clear that rescue is only from God. The Children of Israel did not rescue themselves; God delivered them from the people of Pharaoh. In this context, āl fir'awn is not only Pharaoh himself or his private family. Āl means the apparatus, dependents, officials, forces, and network whose command and benefit return to the Pharaonic center of power. Without the people of Pharaoh, Pharaoh is only an individual; with his āl, he becomes a system. Oppression becomes stable when ruler, command, army, officials, economy, obedience, and dependent institutions act together in one coordinated network.

In the Qur'an, Pharaoh is introduced with a specific policy. In Surah Al-Qasas, Pharaoh exalted himself in the land, made its people into groups, weakened one group among them, slaughtered their sons, and kept their women alive. So the Qur'an does not make Pharaoh's dream or outside reports the cause of this policy. Such reports do appear in classical tafsir, but the criterion of ta'wil here is the Qur'an itself, which roots the issue in exaltation in the land, division-making, weakening one group, and systemic corruption. From here it becomes clear that the killing of sons was not only a separate act of savagery. It was part of the engineering of domination. Pharaoh divided the people of the land into groups so that no practical alignment and coalition against the center of power could form; then he weakened one group so that they would be emptied of the ability to defend, decide, move, organize, and build a future. In this structure, slaughtering sons and keeping women alive were two complementary parts of one policy.

The expression yasūmūnakum sū'a al-'adhāb shows that this punishment was not only a moment of torture. The root S W M is tied to marking, driving, directing, and continually entering someone into a path. In its material image, animals are driven toward a pasture or a defined route, and in this verse the people of Pharaoh treated the Children of Israel like a marked and powerless population: they turned them through fields of pain, labor, humiliation, fear, and powerlessness, and drove them toward an imposed condition. Sū'a al-'adhāb means the worst or ugliest kind of punishment, a punishment that does not strike only the body but breaks dignity, family, future, and collective capacity. When a society becomes a forced labor class, a dominated population, or a group without rights, punishment flows inside the structure of life itself: in the fear of the mother, in the futurelessness of the father, in the killing of the child, in the humiliation of the woman, in the breaking of the family, and in the blocking of the path of resistance.

Then the verse states the concrete form of this ugly punishment through the phrase yudhabbiḥūna abnā'akum. Dhabh is tied to cutting the throat and severing the path of life. The Qur'an does not merely say that they were killing them. It says they were slaughtering them, in order to show the open and terror-producing violence of the Pharaonic system. The aim was not only the removal of a few individuals; it was cutting off the force of the future, breaking the generation, severing defensive capacity, and terrifying the society. The word abnā'akum must also be understood carefully. The Qur'an in this verse does not say only male infants; it says your sons. Although the story of Musa's mother in Surah Al-Qasas shows that infants were also in danger under this policy, and God inspired Musa's mother that when she feared for him she should cast him into the river, verse 49 itself uses a broader word. So it is better not to restrict this policy only to infants. It is the killing of sons and the future-making, defensive, and building force of the society, with infants included within it.

This meaning becomes clearer through a verse in Surah Ghafir. There, when Musa brought them the truth from God, they said: kill the sons of those who have iman with him and keep their women alive. This shows that the policy of killing sons was not only an event before the birth of Musa. After Musa came with truth, the Pharaonic system activated the same tool again, and when it fell short in the field of argument and truth, it turned toward pressure on the people. In Surah Ghafir, the target is not only the Children of Israel as an ethnic people; the verse speaks of those who had iman with Musa. This point is very important: killing sons at that stage becomes a tool for suppressing iman, breaking the network of Musa's companions, and placing psychological pressure on Musa himself. Pharaoh makes Musa's call costly, so that whoever comes close to Musa knows that his family and future will come under attack. This is a kind of war against truth through taking people hostage.

From here we can understand that the Pharaonic system was not only fighting Musa. It was fighting the possibility that a freer, more faithful, and more truth-aligned society might form. If people had iman in Musa, the Children of Israel and other weakened groups would no longer be raw labor power only; they would become a community with direction, hope, leadership, meaning, and the possibility of resistance. The killing of sons was, in reality, cutting off this very possibility.

Then the verse points to another part of this process with the phrase wa-yastaḥyūna nisā'akum. The outward meaning is that they were keeping your women alive. But next to the slaughtering of sons, this keeping alive was not mercy. It was another part of the same system of domination. They kept the women alive so that, on one side, the society would lose its defensive and future-making force, and on the other side, the remaining society would be held in a condition of control, humiliation, exploitation, and dependency. Here life does not mean dignity. Keeping someone alive can itself become an instrument of domination. The structure of the verse is therefore dual: they killed the sons and kept the women alive. One is the cutting off of life; the other is the control of life. In the first, the defensive and generational future of society is severed; in the second, the remaining society is kept in a restrained condition. This policy shows that Pharaoh was not only killing people; he was redesigning society so that it would never become a free and resistant force.

At the end of the verse, He says wa-fī dhālikum balā'un min rabbikum ʿaẓīm. In the Qur'an, bala' does not mean only calamity. It means test, wearing down, engagement with a hard reality, and the revealing of the true measure of a human being and society. Here the bala' was both in the Pharaonic punishment itself and in rescue from it. In this field, the Children of Israel saw how far a Pharaonic system can break the human being, and they also saw how the Rabb can open a path of rescue from inside that darkness. This bala' is called from rabbikum, your Rabb. Rabb is the Lord who nurtures and trains; that is, even inside painful history and great liberation, there is a system that trains and reveals. God does not approve Pharaoh's injustice, because Pharaoh is one of the corruptors. But in passing through that system, the Children of Israel faced great truths: the weakness of the human being under domination, the corruption of absolute power, the value of rescue, the meaning of tawakkul, and the weight of responsibility after liberation.

The word ʿaẓīm also shows that this bala' was not small. Their punishment was not ordinary, and neither was their rescue. They were brought through an immense system of suppression. Therefore, after rescue, their forgetfulness is not smaller than the forgetfulness of others. Whoever has seen such a great liberation with his own eyes, if he covers truth, sells the signs, commands people to goodness while forgetting himself, has in reality betrayed the memory of his own liberation. From this angle, verse 49 does not return the Children of Israel only to historical pain. It returns them to the root of their responsibility. God rescued them from a system that classified, weakened, suppressed, and consumed human beings. Therefore, those rescued from Pharaoh must not themselves become carriers of the Pharaonic logic in a religious form. Whoever has been freed from istid'af, if he conceals truth and turns his fadl into a shield, has not understood the lesson of rescue.

As a result, verse 49 is part of the chain of remembering ni'mah, but a ni'mah accompanied by pain, blood, fear, test, and liberation. Through this remembrance, the Qur'an says: you were not only bearers of Book; you were rescued from inside a Pharaonic system. That rescue should make you more humble, more grateful, more just, and more faithful to truth, not turn into an ethnic memory without responsibility.
