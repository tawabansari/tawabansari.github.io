Al-Baqarah 2:258

ۖ ۗ

Have you not seen the one who disputed with Abraham about his Lord because The God had given him sovereignty? When Abraham said, “My Lord is the one who gives life and causes death,” he said, “I give life and cause death.” Abraham said, “Then indeed, The God brings the sun from the east; bring it from the west.” So the one who had denied was confounded; and The God does not guide the wrongdoing people.

Our Reflection

The verse begins with a question that invites reflection:

«أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِي حَاجَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ فِي رَبِّهِ»

“Have you not seen the one who disputed with Abraham about his Lord?”

This question draws the reader’s attention to Abraham’s encounter with a man who possessed power and sovereignty and entered into a dispute with him about God’s lordship.

Muhajjah, from the root H J J, means an argumentative encounter and the exchange of arguments between two sides, although presenting an argument does not itself guarantee that the speaker’s claim is correct.

In Al-Baqarah 2:256, the Quran spoke of freedom of faith and rejecting taghut; in Al-Baqarah 2:257, it contrasted divine guardianship with the guardianship of taghut. Now we encounter a man who possesses power and sovereignty, yet that very power has led him to dispute God’s lordship.

«أَنْ آتَاهُ اللَّهُ الْمُلْكَ»

“Because The God had given him sovereignty.”

This expression is highly meaningful. His sovereignty was ultimately not outside the domain of divine power and creation. Nevertheless, his possession of power had become the basis of his pride and claim.

In Aal Imran 3:26, the Quran also identifies God as “مَالِكَ الْمُلْكِ” and attributes the granting and removal of sovereignty to Him. Possessing sovereignty, however, does not establish its holder’s rightness or moral worthiness. Power is an opportunity accompanied by responsibility, not permission to put human will in the place of God’s will.

The dispute concerns “رَبِّهِ”: the Lord and possessor of true sovereignty. Abraham responds:

«رَبِّيَ الَّذِي يُحْيِي وَيُمِيتُ»

“My Lord is the one who gives life and causes death.”

But the ruler replies:

«أَنَا أُحْيِي وَأُمِيتُ»

“I give life and cause death.”

A striking feature of this debate is the use of two corresponding verbs. Abraham describes giving life and causing death as attributes of his Lord, and the ruler attributes those same acts to himself.

This is the boundary Ayat al-Kursi draws between The God, “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ”, and any inferior, dependent deity: possessing limited abilities does not make any being equal to the creator and absolute sovereign of life.

A person can save another’s life through treatment or end it through oppression and killing. A ruler may also have the authority to order someone’s death or preservation. But are such abilities the same as The God’s giving life and causing death?

A human being is themselves living, limited, and dependent. They are neither the creator of life’s fundamental laws nor an independent authority over life and death. Even the power they use to preserve or end another’s life depends on causes and conditions they did not create.

Sharing the expressions “giving life” and “causing death” does not mean that the reality, scope, and source of these abilities are the same in God and in human beings.

As the Quran states in Ash-Shura 42:11:

“لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ” — Ash-Shura 42:11

“Nothing is like Him.”

This principle does not deny people’s real abilities; it clarifies their place and limits. People can affect the world, but their limited effects do not place them in the position of its creator and Lord.

To make this distinction clear, Abraham turns the argument to a phenomenon the ruler witnesses every day:

«فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْتِي بِالشَّمْسِ مِنَ الْمَشْرِقِ فَأْتِ بِهَا مِنَ الْمَغْرِبِ»

“Then indeed, The God brings the sun from the east; bring it from the west.”

This argument is very clear and decisive.

The ruler may govern many people, and his commands may be obeyed by soldiers and officials. But is the sun also subject to his command? Can he, merely by his own will and command, alter the order of creation against its known course?

Abraham does not ask him merely to voice another claim, but to demonstrate his actual ability before the order over which he claims sovereignty.

This argument reveals the boundary between possessing power within the world and possessing absolute authority over the world.

It also reminds us that the order of creation rests on laws and patterns to which human power is subject. People can understand and use these laws, but that understanding and use do not make them the laws’ creator or independent sovereign.

The Quran states the outcome of the debate briefly and decisively:

«فَبُهِتَ الَّذِي كَفَرَ»

“So the one who had denied was confounded.”

The ruler who claimed to give life and cause death found no answer to a clear argument. His political power remained, but the inadequacy of his claim was exposed.

This scene shows that power, wealth, and social standing do not take the place of evidence and truth. Someone may command others yet be unable to answer a clear argument.

This is the danger examined in Taghut in the Quran: from Self-Worship to Domination over People: a person, because of power or a desire for superiority, exceeds their limits and puts their own will and authority in the place of truth.

Abraham, by contrast, neither relied on political power nor resorted to force in answering the ruler. Instead, he exposed the falsehood of the ruler’s claim through a clear argument. This too is an example of movement from the darkness of baseless claims toward the light of understanding and truth.

The verse ends with this statement:

«وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ»

“And The God does not guide the wrongdoing people.”

Wrongdoing here is connected with exceeding the bounds of right and placing human power in the position of lordship. Someone who puts their own will and authority before truth sets themselves against guidance. Possessing power, or even encountering a clear argument, does not by itself mean accepting truth; a person must abandon pride and denial and choose the right path.

This verse does not report a change of belief or repentance by the ruler; it only describes his being confounded by Abraham’s argument.

Al-Baqarah 2:258 thus provides a clear example of the theology of divine transcendence in the arena of life and power. Through two simple arguments, Abraham shows that however powerful people may be, they cannot equate their limited, dependent abilities with the unlimited power and lordship of The God.

Sovereignty over people is not sovereignty over existence, and possessing power does not make anyone the possessor of truth.

With advances in knowledge and technology, people today have created artificial intelligence and developed genetic manipulation and complex methods of embryo cultivation. Perhaps one day they will be able to nurture a human embryo from its beginning to birth outside the mother’s womb, or even use genetic material—deoxyribonucleic acid—remaining from a deceased person to produce an individual with a similar genetic structure. But would that individual be the same person who died? Could reconstructing genetic material also restore their memories, experiences, knowledge, abilities, and entire identity? Could even the exact pattern of their fingerprints be recreated from genetic material alone? In Al-Qiyamah 75:4, the Quran speaks of God’s ability to recreate a person’s fingertips:

“بَلَىٰ قَادِرِينَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ نُسَوِّيَ بَنَانَهُ” — Al-Qiyamah 75:4

However far human knowledge advances and whatever extraordinary new abilities people acquire, this question remains: does understanding and using the laws of creation make people the independent creators of those laws and absolute masters of life and death? This is the boundary Abraham made clear against the ruler’s claim: using the possibilities within creation is not the same as lordship and sovereignty over existence itself.

In the next two verses, Al-Baqarah 2:259 and Al-Baqarah 2:260, the discussion of life, death, and bringing back to life continues, presenting further dimensions of divine power over life and re-creation.
