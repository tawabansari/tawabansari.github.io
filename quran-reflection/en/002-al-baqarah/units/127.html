Al-Baqarah 2:127

ۖ

And when Abraham and Ishmael were raising the foundations of the House: Our Lord, accept from us; indeed, You Yourself are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

Our Reflection

The preceding verses established what this House is: a place of return for people, a secure sanctuary, free of associating others with God, and open for service to The God alone. Now that meaning rises in the form of an actual building. Abraham and Ishmael are raising the House’s foundations:

“When Abraham was raising the foundations of the House, together with Ishmael.”

“Foundations” means the bases on which the building stands. In An-Nahl 16:26, the Qur’an uses the same Arabic word alongside “building” and “roof.” The scene is clear: Abraham and Ishmael are engaged in erecting the House.

Yet after the preceding verses, the image of stone foundations also brings another meaning to mind. This House rests on more than stone. Before its walls rise, the Qur’an has already shown the principles on which it rests: worshipping God alone, safety, the House being for people, freedom from associating others with God, and service to Him. If the stones remain sound but these foundations collapse, the building still stands, yet the way for which Abraham stood has been damaged.

One can maintain the House, expand its building, and add to its outward splendor. But its true flourishing is not measured by stone and architecture alone. A House established for people must remain for people; a House established as a place of safety must not become a place of fear; and a House that must remain pure for God’s service must not become an instrument of monopoly, of associating others with God, or of domination over His servants.

Widely circulated commentaries say that Adam built this House before Abraham, and that Abraham later rebuilt it on the surviving foundations. The Qur’an itself gives no such account. Even within the transmitted reports, there is no sound, reliable hadith from the Prophet establishing that Adam built the first Kaaba. This is one of those places where a gap in the information given by the Qur’an has been filled with unsupported reports, narratives drawn from earlier Jewish and Christian traditions, or exegetical conjecture. These have then been repeated over time as though they were part of the Qur’an’s own story. For a fuller examination of this method, see When Qur’anic Silence Is Filled with Reports.

The Qur’an says as much as is needed for this scene: Abraham and Ishmael were raising the foundations of the House. Yet the verse’s beauty lies less in the building’s history than in the words they speak as they build:

“Our Lord, accept from us.”

They are not doing something small and obscure. They are erecting a House toward which many generations will come. Even so, the greatness of their work is not, for them, proof that it has been accepted. They do not say: because we have built the House, our standing with God is assured. While their hands are occupied with building, their hearts await His acceptance.

There is a distance between performing an act and having it accepted.

Neither a title, fame, nor the outward scale of the work closes that distance. Someone may build an enormous mosque, spend great wealth on religion, serve pilgrims, or devote years of effort to a religious cause. None of these, by itself, is God’s seal of acceptance upon their work. If Abraham and Ishmael say “accept from us” while raising the House, who else can rely solely on the magnitude of their own religious work?

This very prayer quiets religious pride. Working for God does not make God indebted to us.

“You Yourself are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.”

He hears their prayer and knows the truth of their work. People see what has been built outwardly; God also knows what has taken place within a person. Everyone can see the wall, but He knows the builder’s sincerity.

This may be one of the most beautiful images of Abraham’s legacy: hands raising the House and hearts still saying, “Accept from us.”

From here, the verse’s question for our own day takes shape. Abraham’s legacy is not only walls passed down from one generation to the next. If worshipping God alone, safety, the House being for people, and sincere service to God are forgotten, preserving the House’s stones alone does not mean preserving Abraham’s way.

Abraham and Ishmael raised the foundations of the House; our question is whether we have also upheld the principles for which that House was established.

The broader discussion of the House, the foundations of pilgrimage, the rites, and Abraham’s way appears in Hajj in the Qur’an.
