Al-Baqarah 2:25

And give glad news to those who have faith and do works of repair: for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow. Whenever they are provided from them with fruit as provision, they say, “This is what we were provided before,” and they are brought it in resemblance. And for them therein are purified companions, and they abide therein.

Our Reflection

After the warning of the previous verse, this verse restores the structural balance of Qur’anic speech. In verse 24, the speech was about a Fire whose fuel is people and stones: a consequence fed by rigidity, hardness, and covering truth. In verse 25, opposite that structure, an order of life, flow, purity, and continuity appears. If Fire is the burning manifestation of denial and rigidity, the garden is a covered, living, protected enclosure in which the flow of life runs beneath the structures.

The foundational point at the beginning of the verse is the link between “those who have faith” and “do works of repair.” Faith here is not merely identity affiliation or mental claim. It is an existential orientation based on trust, security, and acceptance of truth. But this faith must appear at the level of action as repair. Righteous action is faith embodied and made active in reality: when faith affects the world, it becomes repair, order, restoration, and good.

This point redefines many exclusive and identity-centered views of salvation. The verse does not say that mere attachment to a name, group, or faith-claim brings a human being to this outcome. The structure of the verse is twofold: faith and righteous action. Therefore, if faith is separated from reparative action, within this Qur’anic logic it has not yet reached its full fruit. Just as kufr in the previous verse was not merely not knowing, but covering truth after it had become clear, faith is not merely saying or belonging. It must appear in sound action.

The image then appears: “gardens beneath which rivers flow.” Jannat, from the root J N N, is connected with covering, protected enclosure, and a hidden, preserved garden. This image stands opposite an-nar, which is connected with manifestation, light with heat, and becoming exposed. Fire is the burning exposure of what the human being covered in worldly life through kufr. The garden, however, is a protected and living enclosure preserved from impurity, rigidity, and disorder. So the contrast between verses 24 and 25 is not only a contrast between punishment and blessing; it is a contrast between two existential orders: the burning exposure of denial, and the secure, living coveredness of faith and repair.

“Beneath which rivers flow” shows that this life is not static. Rivers run beneath it; life flows at the foundation of this order. In contrast to Fire, which remains active through fuel, the garden is alive through the flow of life. The previous verse said that the fuel of the Fire is people and stones. This verse shows that the order of reward is also of the same kind as the human path. Whoever turned faith into righteous action in the world meets, in the Hereafter, a structure made of repair, flow, and purity.

The phrase “whenever they are provided from it with fruit as provision” presents provision as a repeated and living event. Provision here is not merely a possession or an object to be owned. It is the continuous flow of receiving from the source of giving. They are provided each time; the relationship of receiving and being given remains alive and ongoing. At this level, provision is not a static condition, but the continuing existential dependence on giving and outpouring.

When they say, “This is what we were provided before,” the speech points to a kind of perceptive familiarity. But the verse immediately says: “and they are brought it in resemblance.” This expression carries a subtle point about recognition. If the blessings of that world were completely alien, the human being would face a break in perception; and if they were merely a full repetition of this world, boredom and habit would arise. Mutashabihan gathers both sides: familiarity in form, freshness in reality; a link with known experience, but without worn repetition and worldly limitation.

Then “for them therein are purified companions” describes the structure of relationship in that order. Azwaj, from the root Z W J, originally means pair, counterpart, and structural complement. It should not be limited only to a gendered or possessive frame. The verse speaks of a relation in which completion, companionship, and correspondence exist, but with the qualification “purified,” cleansed from the impurities of worldly relations.

This purity is not only bodily or outward. It is the purification of relationship from tension, envy, possession, competition, anxiety, harm, and psychological and social impurity. “Purified companions” means companionship and completion within a structure purified from worldly disorders. For this reason, the verse speaks of relationships in a broad and purified way, not as a simple reproduction of worldly family or gender structures.

Finally, “and they abide therein” expresses the continuity of this condition. Abiding here is not only a long duration of time. It is stability within an order of life: a life made of flow, provision, purity, and security. With this ending, the verse shows that the reward of those who have faith and do works of repair is not a passing moment, but entry into a lasting and living order.

Cross-References
  • Al-Baqarah 2:24— The direct contrast between the Fire whose fuel is people and stones, and the garden known by flow, provision, and purity.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:2— “Guidance for the mindful”; guidance is connected to those who realize faith and righteous action on the path of taqwa.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:62— A broader statement that salvation in Qur’anic logic is not merely nominal belonging, but faith in the One God and the Last Day joined with righteous action.
  • An-Nisa 4:124— Faith and righteous action are joined, with no gendered or identity-based monopoly over reward.
  • Al-Kahf 18:30-31— Those who have faith and do righteous action do not lose their reward, and for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow.
  • Muhammad 47:15— A broader description of rivers and provision in the garden, helping explain the symbol of flow and outpouring in the order of reward.
  • Al-Insan 76:5-22— A broad image of the reward of those marked by loyalty, self-giving, and righteous action; showing that reward corresponds to the structure of human conduct.
