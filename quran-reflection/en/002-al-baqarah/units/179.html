Al-Baqarah 2:179

And in qisas there is life for you, O people of understanding, so that you may be mindful.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, The God prescribed qisas and, within the same ruling, opened the way to forgiveness. He now calls people of understanding to reflect on its wisdom: “And in qisas there is life for you.” This statement is better understood when we do not restrict qisas to killing the murderer, but recognize its relationship with justice, forgiveness, and the preservation of life in society.

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, after opening the way to forgiveness, The God commands that the pursuit of a claim be proper and its discharge be with kindness. This command should not be restricted to receiving and paying blood money, although financial compensation can be one instance of it. Murder does not merely take a human life: it leaves a family bereaved and may leave lasting hostility and resentment between two families or groups. If humiliation, harm, and revenge continue after forgiveness, or the person forgiven fails to honor their obligations, forgiveness will not bring genuine reconciliation. Proper pursuit and kind discharge call both parties to good conduct and respect for each other’s rights. The Quran also connects forgiveness with putting things right in Ash-Shura 42:40.

Forgiveness does not benefit only the person forgiven; it may also bring peace of mind to the person who forgives. Someone who knowingly and freely relinquishes their right may find relief from the burden of anger, resentment, and continual thoughts of revenge. Forgiveness may reduce the emotional suffering caused by continuing hostility and give a person the opportunity to carry on with life without remaining trapped in conflict. The person forgiven, in turn, may find an opportunity for remorse, reform, and making amends for their conduct. Forgiveness can therefore benefit both the one who forgives and the one forgiven, without meaning that the crime is forgotten or that forgiveness is imposed on the person wronged.

But why does The God locate life in qisas, rather than in forgiveness, which He has called relief and mercy? This difficulty arises from an incomplete understanding that sets qisas and forgiveness against one another, as though qisas meant only killing the murderer and forgiveness meant refraining from doing so. Yet the two, as we examined in the reflection on the preceding verse, complement one another.

In this legal reading, qisas is not confined to the stage of carrying out punishment. Investigating the crime, establishing the truth, identifying who is actually responsible, examining people’s intent and freedom of choice, and determining a proportionate response are all necessary for justice. Once the crime and the right have been established, the person entitled to the claim may seek punishment or knowingly waive it. Forgiveness therefore does not invalidate the principle of qisas; it opens the way to pardon after the right has been recognized.

If The God had not prescribed qisas and had only encouraged forgiveness, the rights of the victim and the wronged would have been disregarded. A family that has lost a loved one has a right to have the truth of the crime established and the responsible person held accountable. Forgiveness cannot be expected of the wronged when no path exists for seeking justice and securing their rights. Relinquishing a claim is a conscious virtue when someone gives up a recognized right, rather than being deprived of access to justice from the outset.

In a society where crimes are not investigated, investigative authorities and fair courts do not exist, or the rights of the wronged are disregarded, people may feel forced either to endure injustice or to seek revenge themselves. In such circumstances, a single murder can lead to prolonged hostility and reciprocal killing. But when law is upheld, the truth of a crime is examined, and each person answers for their actual share of responsibility, the conditions for private revenge diminish and public trust in justice grows. Someone contemplating a crime also knows that their action will not go uninvestigated.

For this reason, life in qisas should not be restricted to a murderer’s fear of capital punishment. Investigating crimes, establishing investigative authorities and fair courts, and carrying out judgments through an orderly process are social requirements for realizing justice, although the word qisas alone does not name all these institutions. When grounded in truth and justice, this legal system can safeguard human lives, prevent the punishment of the innocent, and restrain the spread of crime.

In Al-Isra 17:33, the Quran both recognizes the right of the murdered person’s representative and forbids excess in killing. In Al-Maidah 5:32, the sanctity of one human life is also connected with the importance of all human life. Protecting life is therefore not limited to keeping one person alive; preventing injustice, blood vengeance, and insecurity also contributes to preserving life in society.

Toward the end, The God addresses people of understanding with “يَـٰٓأُو۟لِى ٱلْأَلْبَـٰبِ”. Albab is the plural of lubb, from the root ل ب ب, denoting the core, kernel, or purest part of something, and from this meaning it is also used for understanding. People of understanding are not merely those who possess abundant information: they reflect, weigh matters, and look beyond appearances. In Az-Zumar 39:18, the Quran describes them as those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it; in Aal Imran 3:190 and Aal Imran 3:191, it presents them as people who reflect on creation. Such understanding can recognize that the wisdom of qisas lies not only in the fate of one murderer but also in the consequences of justice and injustice for everyone’s life.

The verse ends with “لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ”. The aim of this ruling is restraint from violating human life and rights. A society that avoids unlawful bloodshed, punishment of the innocent, abuse of power, and trampling on the rights of the wronged moves toward security and life.

There is life in qisas because investigating crime and establishing justice safeguard human lives; and forgiveness, after the right has been established, leaves open the path to pardon, reconciliation, and reform. Qisas and forgiveness are not opposing rulings: they complement one another in preserving rights, mercy, and human life.

Cross-references
  • Al-Baqarah 2:178 — Prescribing qisas, opening the way to forgiveness, proper pursuit, and kind discharge of a claim.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:194 — Proportionality in responding to aggression and avoiding excess.
  • An-Nisa 4:58 — The command to judge fairly between people.
  • An-Nisa 4:92 — Accidental killing, compensation, and the possibility of remission.
  • Al-Maidah 5:8 — Upholding justice even toward people one opposes.
  • Al-Maidah 5:32 — The sanctity of human life and the connection of killing or saving one life with collective life.
  • Al-Maidah 5:45 — Qisas concerning life and injuries, and the possibility of relinquishing a claim.
  • Al-Anam 6:164 — Individual responsibility: no one bears another’s burden of sin.
  • Al-Isra 17:33 — The prohibition of unlawful killing, the victim’s representative’s right, and the prohibition of excessive blood vengeance.
  • Ash-Shura 42:40 — A proportionate response to wrongdoing and the virtue of forgiveness joined with reform.
  • Aal Imran 3:134 — Restraining anger, forgiving people, and kindness.
  • Aal Imran 3:190 — Signs in creation for people of understanding.
  • Aal Imran 3:191 — People of understanding reflecting on the creation of the heavens and earth.
  • Az-Zumar 39:18 — Listening to what is said and following the best of it as a quality of people of understanding.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:269 — The connection between wisdom, taking heed, and people of understanding.
