Al-Baqarah 2:142

ۚ ۚ

The foolish among the people will say: “What turned them away from the qiblah they used to face?” Say: “East and west belong to The God; He guides whomever He wills to a straight path.”

Our Reflection

Establishing salat did not begin with the appearance of Muhammad. The Quran also speaks of salat among earlier prophets and the Children of Israel. Jerusalem held a special place in the worship tradition of the Children of Israel, and the Quran mentions al-Masjid al-Aqsa and its blessed surroundings in Al-Isra 17:1, and the holy land in the story of Moses in Al-Maidah 5:21. On the other side stood the Sacred House: the house whose foundations Abraham and Ishmael raised, and which God made a place of return and security for people (Al-Baqarah 2:125 and Al-Baqarah 2:127). Both centers were connected with the religious legacy of the prophets, although the Quran does not explicitly name Muhammad’s former qiblah.

Geographically, in Mecca Muhammad could stand in a suitable place so that both the Kaaba and Jerusalem lay along the direction he faced. After the migration to Yathrib, however, this was no longer possible: the Kaaba lay south of Medina, while Jerusalem lay to its northwest. Facing one therefore required turning away from the direction of the other. This situation can clarify the practical importance of determining the qiblah. The Quran itself confirms the existence of the former qiblah in Al-Baqarah 2:143 and gives the command to face al-Masjid al-Haram in Al-Baqarah 2:144.

The objection to this change appears in the question “مَا وَلَّىٰهُمْ عَن قِبْلَتِهِمُ”: what turned them away from their former qiblah? The Quran calls the objectors “sufaha,” but does not restrict this description to a particular people or religion. Earlier, in Al-Baqarah 2:130, turning away from the way of Abraham was also associated with foolishness. Asking why a change has occurred is not, by itself, a sign of foolishness. Foolishness arises when a person becomes so attached to a form and direction that they cannot accept its change in the course of guidance.

The Quran’s answer makes the criterion of judgment clear: “قُل لِّلَّهِ ٱلْمَشْرِقُ وَٱلْمَغْرِبُ”; east and west belong to The God. No geographical direction is God’s exclusive location. The qiblah is a real direction for worship, but its authority comes from God’s command, not from the independent sacredness of a geographical direction. This truth had already been stated in Al-Baqarah 2:115. Al-Baqarah 2:177 also makes clear that righteousness is not confined to merely facing east or west, but is connected with faith, generosity, and human conduct.

For this reason, the answer ends with “يَهْدِى مَن يَشَآءُ إِلَىٰ صِرَٰطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ”. The main destination is the straight path toward which The God guides. Changing the qiblah does not mean that past worship has become worthless; as the following verse emphasizes, God does not let the believers’ faith go to waste. Yet when a new direction is appointed, following guidance requires a person to move beyond attachment to the former direction.

The qiblah is a sign for orientation on the path of servitude; the sign itself must not be put in place of the destination of worship.

For a broader examination of this subject, see Qiblah, Wajh, and Hanif in the Quran.
