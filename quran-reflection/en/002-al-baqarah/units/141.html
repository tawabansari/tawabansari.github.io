Al-Baqarah 2:141

ۖ ۖ

That was a community that has passed away. What they earned is theirs, and what you have earned is yours; and you will not be questioned about what they used to do.

Our Reflection

This verse repeats exactly what was said in Al-Baqarah 2:134. Before entering the debate about Abraham, Jews, Christians, and their heritage, the Qur’an declared: That community has passed away; their deeds belong to them, and yours belong to you. Now, after seven verses discussing religious exclusivity, Abraham’s way, discrimination among prophets, the coloring of The God, sincere devotion, and the concealment of testimony, it returns to the same statement.

It is as though the Qur’an opens and closes this historical discussion with the same sentence: you have come to know the past; now return to your own time.

“That was a community that has passed away.”

An unhealthy attachment to the past can take two very different forms. Sometimes people become so attached to their ancestors, revered figures, and religious heritage that they refuse to accept a truth that challenges those predecessors’ understanding. The righteousness of earlier generations becomes an asset of identity, and respect for them gradually takes the place of examining what they said.

But the past can imprison us in another way as well. We may devote all our attention to the mistakes of other people’s ancestors and revered figures: what a certain people did, what a denomination said, where Jews went wrong, or where Christians went astray. History ceases to be a means of understanding and becomes a means of elevating ourselves above others.

One person clings to their own past to avoid seeing today’s truth; another clings to other people’s past mistakes to escape seeing their own mistakes today.

The Qur’an closes off both paths with one sentence:

“What they earned is theirs, and what you have earned is yours.”

Abraham’s devotion is not credited to our account, nor does the wrongdoing of someone who lived a thousand years ago add to our burden. Lineage, titles, history, and heritage cannot take the place of what a person does today.

This repetition gains further meaning immediately after the verse about concealing testimony. If a truth has become clear to us today, we cannot conceal it simply because our predecessors understood it differently. They were responsible for their understanding and actions; we are responsible for what we see, understand, and choose today.

Respecting earlier generations is not the same as submitting to them. They can be our teachers, but they cannot think, believe, or take responsibility for our choices on our behalf.

The same principle applies in the opposite direction. We will not be called to account for the mistakes of other people’s predecessors:

“You will not be questioned about what they used to do.”

Criticism of history therefore has value when it leads us toward a better understanding of truth and reform in the present. If we merely repeat the mistakes of other people’s past to present ourselves as purer, superior, or better guided, we have turned history into a tool of the very pursuit of superiority criticized in the preceding verses.

In An-Najm 53:39, the Qur’an expresses the same principle from another angle: a person has only the outcome of their own striving. Elsewhere, it repeatedly emphasizes that no one carries another’s burden. Responsibility is ultimately personal.

The lesson is very simple, but living by it is difficult: know the past, learn from it, and even criticize it; but neither hide behind ancestral glories nor spend your life putting other people’s ancestors on trial. The past should illuminate understanding, not imprison thought or serve as an instrument of self-superiority.

They were their own community, and what they earned belongs to them. We, too, will be judged in relation to the truth before us today.
