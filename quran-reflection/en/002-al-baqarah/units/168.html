Al-Baqarah 2:168

ۚ

O people! Eat of what is on earth, lawful and wholesome, and do not follow Satan’s footsteps; indeed, he is a manifest enemy to you.

Our Reflection

Lawful and Wholesome Food: Responsible Enjoyment and Avoiding Satan’s Footsteps

In the preceding verses, the Quran spoke of the fate of people who had placed others in God’s position in their love and following, until the relationship between their leaders and followers fell apart. It now addresses all people and raises a familiar matter of everyday life: human food. This change of subject is not separate from the discussion of following in the previous verses, for what people put on their tables can also be an arena for weighing God’s command against human customs and invented rulings. Yet the verse begins, above all, with an invitation to partake: “كُلُوا مِمَّا فِي الْأَرْضِ حَلَالًا طَيِّبًا.”

In this invitation, God addresses everyone, not the followers of a particular people, religious school, or revealed law. People live in different lands and differ in culture, needs, tastes, resources, and food supplies. Nevertheless, the divine command to benefit from the earth’s blessings comes with two important descriptions: “حَلَالًا” and “طَيِّبًا.” Halal refers to something whose use is open under divine ruling, while tayyib concerns the purity, pleasantness, goodness, and suitability of what is consumed. These concepts are connected but are not identical. For example, in An-Nisa 4:160, God speaks of good things that were forbidden to a group of the Children of Israel because of their wrongdoing. Thus, the goodness and suitability of something should not be equated with its status as lawful or forbidden.

To understand the range of “tayyib,” we should consider where else the Quran uses this description. In Al-Araf 7:58, fertile land ready for growth is described as tayyib, in contrast to poor land that yields little. In Yunus 10:22, a favorable wind that facilitates a ship’s movement is called tayyib. In Ibrahim 14:24 and Ibrahim 14:26, a good and pure word is contrasted with an impure word. These uses show that tayyib is not confined to delicious taste or personal preference; it refers to a kind of purity, goodness, and desirable quality understood in relation to each thing’s nature and function. Tayyib land is suitable for growth, a tayyib wind favors sailing, and a tayyib word is fitting in its meaning and effect. In food, too, purity, pleasantness, wholesomeness, and suitability matter. This semantic range is examined in detail in the independent studies of the root Ṭ Y B and the root KH B TH.

This difference in meaning has an important consequence for people’s lives. A food may be disagreeable to one person or culture while being ordinary and accepted in another land. No one is compelled to eat food they dislike, just as a food’s permissibility does not make eating it necessary. But does our dislike of something authorize us to forbid it to others in God’s name? In Yunus 10:59, the Quran asks those who divide divine provision into lawful and forbidden whether God has authorized them or they are inventing falsehood against Him. An-Nahl 16:116 likewise warns against attributing declarations of lawful and forbidden to God without evidence. We must therefore distinguish personal dislike, recognition of actual harm, and divine ruling. This issue, and the boundary between human choice and God’s right to legislate, is examined in detail in the independent study “Lawful and Forbidden in the Quran: The Divine Right to Legislate and the Danger of Ascribing Partners in Religion.”

But food’s suitability cannot be assessed only at the moment of eating. Before reaching our tables, food has traveled through soil and water, fields and livestock farms, labor and trade. Can we attend to its purity and desirability while remaining indifferent to harm inflicted on the earth, animals, and other people during its production? In Al-Baqarah 2:205, the Quran speaks of corruption on earth and connects it with destroying crops and offspring; in Al-Anam 6:38, it describes animals and birds as communities like human beings; and in Al-Anam 6:141, it joins enjoying the earth’s produce with giving what is due and avoiding excess. These verses show that benefiting from the earth’s blessings is inseparable from responsibility toward the earth, living beings, and others’ rights.

From this perspective, people today can use the reason, innate disposition, and knowledge God has given them to consider matters beyond food’s taste and appearance when choosing tayyib food. Someone with the necessary ability and resources can choose food whose production has avoided pollution and environmental destruction as far as possible; whose water and soil have not been polluted by harmful practices; whose livestock raising and animal care have observed compassion, kindness, and avoidance of needless harm; whose plant cultivation and animal feeding have avoided harmful synthetic chemicals and excessive use of damaging substances; and which is itself suitable and safe for human health. It is also fitting that its production and acquisition have not violated workers’ or other people’s rights or involved waste and corruption. Such a choice can express mindfulness of God and responsible enjoyment of the Lord’s blessings.

People do not, however, live with equal resources and circumstances. Someone may be able to buy products raised under the best health and environmental practices, while another family struggles to secure its daily food. Some lands have advanced food-production and storage facilities, while other regions face shortages of water, fertile soil, or food resources. The God who created people also knows these differences and limitations. In Al-Baqarah 2:286, He says that He burdens no one beyond their capacity, and in At-Taghabun 64:16, He calls people to be mindful of Him to the extent of their ability. Thus, choosing better food when one can afford to do so must not become a standard for blaming someone who lacks those resources.

In this connection, the limited number of principal food prohibitions in the Quran also invites reflection. In Al-Baqarah 2:173 and Al-Anam 6:145, God names specific prohibitions and provides relief even for someone compelled by necessity to consume forbidden food. In Al-Baqarah 2:173, this relief is accompanied by a reminder of divine forgiveness and mercy. Perhaps one wisdom behind the limited scope of these prohibitions lies in God’s consideration of His servants’ different needs, living conditions, and capacities. If everything not produced under better conditions, or every food disliked by one culture, were presented as divinely forbidden, life would become difficult and constrained for many groups of people. God invites people to partake of lawful and tayyib food, but does not close the way to better choices by imposing groundless prohibitions on others. The desirability of one choice is not the same as the prohibition of another.

After this invitation to responsible enjoyment, the verse continues with another important warning: “وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا خُطُوَاتِ الشَّيْطَانِ.” Why is following Satan’s footsteps mentioned alongside food? To understand this connection, we should first recognize this enemy through the Quran’s interconnected verses. In Al-Baqarah 2:34, Iblis is identified as the being who disobeyed God’s command and refused to prostrate; then, in Al-Baqarah 2:36, Adam and his spouse’s stumbling is attributed to Satan. In Al-Araf 7:27, God also warns the children of Adam against the same Satan who caused their parents to leave the Garden. These connections show that “الشَّيْطَانِ” here recalls Iblis, humanity’s known enemy. The Quran is not speaking here of devils in the plural, but refers to that manifest enemy in the singular and definite form.

“Khutuwat” means steps or footsteps. Following Satan’s footsteps means pursuing a path toward which he draws people. In Al-Anam 6:142, the Quran repeats this same warning after inviting people to eat of divine provision. In the following verses, Al-Anam 6:143 and Al-Anam 6:144, those who attribute prohibitions concerning livestock to God face questions about their evidence and knowledge. This juxtaposition shows that inventing groundless rulings about food and attributing them to God is one danger raised alongside the warning against following Satan, although Satan’s footsteps in the Quran are not confined to this subject.

The next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:169, makes the connection clearer. There God says that Satan commands people to evil, indecency, and saying about God what they do not know. Al-Baqarah 2:170 then speaks of people who, when invited to follow what God has sent down, take refuge in their ancestors’ way, even if those ancestors lacked understanding and guidance. Thus, the theme of following raised in Al-Baqarah 2:166 and Al-Baqarah 2:167 concerning leaders and followers continues in these verses. The danger is that, instead of assessing God’s command, people surrender their will and judgment to inherited customs, beloved leaders, or misleading temptations.

This warning is also clear for life today. When someone says they do not eat a food because of health, personal preference, or their living circumstances, they are speaking of their own choice. But when that same person claims that God has forbidden everyone to eat it, the statement enters another domain and requires divine evidence and warrant. What must be avoided is not only eating unsuitable food, but also attributing groundless rulings to God and entrusting the understanding of religion to people whose statements we accept without weighing them against revelation.

In this way, the verse connects blessing, choice, and responsibility. People benefit from the earth’s provision, and the greater their awareness and ability, the more attention they can give to health, others’ rights, animals, and the environment in choosing food; but they must not turn their own preferences and choices into divine commands for others. God has opened the way to enjoyment and called people to choose what is tayyib. Loyalty to this guidance is expressed both in protecting His blessings and in refraining from inventing declarations of lawful and forbidden that He has not ordained.
