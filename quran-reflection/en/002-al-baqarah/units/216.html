Al-Baqarah 2:216

ۖ ۖ ۗ

Fighting has been prescribed for you, though it is unwelcome to you; you may dislike something that is good for you, and you may love something that is bad for you; and The God knows, while you do not know.

Our Reflection

“كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْقِتَالُ وَهُوَ كُرْهٌ لَكُمْ”

“Fighting has been prescribed for you, though it is unwelcome to you.”

This verse begins with a command to fight, but its limits have been made clear in earlier verses. Al-Baqarah 2:190 commands fighting against those who fight you and explicitly forbids transgression; Al-Baqarah 2:193 likewise addresses fighting to end persecution and ceasing hostilities once the enemy desists. This command therefore does not authorize warmongering, aggression against others, or bloodshed in God’s name.

Fighting on a battlefield is unwelcome even when it becomes necessary for defense and resisting oppression. People recoil from being killed, losing loved ones, the destruction of homes, and the suffering of war. Yet sometimes refusing to resist aggression can create the conditions for greater oppression and harm.

After describing this situation, God continues with a broader expression:

“وَعَسَىٰ أَنْ تَكْرَهُوا شَيْئًا وَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ”

“And you may dislike something that is good for you.”

The important word here is “shay’an”—something. God does not say that you may dislike fighting while it is good for you; He says that you may dislike something that is good for you. Although the discussion begins with fighting, the meaning of this part is therefore not confined to it and can encompass many human choices and experiences.

We do not always enjoy what benefits us. Learning a skill, abandoning a harmful habit, or accepting necessary treatment may sometimes be difficult and unwelcome, yet the outcome may improve our lives.

The same applies to human relationships. For example, a man or woman may not particularly match our preferences in looks and appearance, yet be honest, kind, and faithful: someone who does not lie or betray, avoids oppression and corruption, and can be a good partner in life and raising children.

Conversely, God says:

“وَعَسَىٰ أَنْ تُحِبُّوا شَيْئًا وَهُوَ شَرٌّ لَكُمْ”

“And you may love something that is bad for you.”

Loving something does not, by itself, prove that it is good.

For example, a young person may be captivated by a man’s or woman’s beauty and become so infatuated that they overlook the person’s actual character and conduct. But if that person is dishonest, unfaithful, oppressive, or irresponsible, beauty cannot protect a shared life from the harm such behavior causes.

Physical beauty is, of course, no fault, and an unappealing face is no sign of virtue. The point is that appearance alone does not reveal the truth of a person’s character. To recognize a worthy spouse, we must also consider honesty, faithfulness, respect, conduct, and responsibility.

We have similar experiences with food. We may dislike a food because of its taste even though it benefits our health, or be very fond of a food whose excessive consumption harms our bodies.

This difference appears in many other decisions as well. Sometimes something is difficult at first but brings a good outcome; at other times, something appears very pleasing, yet its harm becomes evident later.

The word “‘asa” matters in both expressions. God does not say that everything you dislike is necessarily good, or that everything you love is necessarily bad. The statement concerns the possibility that human likes and dislikes may not coincide with actual good and harm.

We should therefore neither flee from everything difficult nor trust everything pleasant. Nor can we impose every suffering or harm on ourselves or others on the pretext that there might be some good in it.

At the end, God says:

“وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ”

“And The God knows, while you do not know.”

Human knowledge is limited. Sometimes we know only the appearance of something or our present feelings about it, while remaining unaware of many of its characteristics and consequences. What we consider pleasant today may harm us tomorrow, or an action we find difficult now may bear good fruit in the future.

This limitation does not mean setting aside reason and understanding. On the contrary, before choosing, people should examine the truth as far as they can, draw on experience and knowledge, and assess the consequences of their decisions. Nor can anyone claim, in the name of God’s knowledge, that their personal wishes and decisions must be good for others.

This verse expresses a broad truth about human life: feelings of pleasure or displeasure are not a definitive measure of good and harm. What we love may harm us, and what we avoid may prove beneficial. We must therefore think beyond appearances, habits, and momentary feelings, understand the truth and consequences of our choices, and remember that complete knowledge belongs to The God.

  • Al-Baqarah 2:192: Ending confrontation after aggressors desist.

  • Al-Anfal 8:61: Inclining toward peace and the command to accept it when the enemy seeks peace.
