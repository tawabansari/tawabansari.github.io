Al-Baqarah 2:4

And those who believe in what has been sent down to you and what was sent down before you, and are certain of the Hereafter.

Our Reflection

At the beginning of Al-Baqarah, the Quran calls itself a book free of doubt and a guide for the mindful, and immediately reveals the reality of mindfulness through three pillars: belief in the unseen, establishing salat, and giving from divine provision. Mindfulness here is not a sectarian label or an inherited identity; it is a quality of life, faith, and action. No one becomes mindful merely by bearing the title Jew, Christian, or Muslim, just as not all seekers outside a historical religious law can be excluded from mindfulness and guidance.

There is a meaningful subtlety in the language of these verses. The third verse begins with “الَّذِينَ” and the fourth with “وَالَّذِينَ”. Although grammatically all these descriptions can refer to one group, the repeated relative pronoun opens a broader horizon: introducing two groups of mindful people with different backgrounds. One group has lived the reality of servitude through belief in the unseen, salat, and giving; another—particularly converts from the People of the Book—knew revelation before Muhammad and now openly embraces the new truth as well (مَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ).

This reading does not rest solely on a grammatical preference; the network of Quranic verses supports it. Al-Imran 3:199 explicitly mentions believers among the People of the Book who believe in both earlier and new revelation. Al-Qasas 28:52, Al-Qasas 28:53, and Al-Qasas 28:54 also speak of people who were already on the path of submission before the Quran was sent down and who, upon hearing the new verses, reflect the same giving and steadfastness.

The gathering of all these descriptions in the fifth verse makes the central message clearer: “أُولَٰئِكَ عَلَىٰ هُدًى مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ” (they are upon guidance from their Lord). The Quran brings people of different revelatory backgrounds and lineages together under one canopy of guidance, showing that no community can claim an ethnic or religious monopoly over mindfulness and salvation.

The Quran sees monotheism as a continuous current in human history, rather than a disconnected event. Differences in religious laws and rites (Al-Maidah 5:48 and Al-Baqarah 2:148) therefore do not mean a plurality of deities or a difference in the foundation of religion. Shariah is the prescribed path for a community, while din is submission to the same One God. Before the names Jewish and Christian arose, Abraham followed the upright path of submission (Al-Imran 3:67), and successive messengers confirmed the earlier path.

Rejecting religious exclusivism does not mean relativism or making action unimportant. In Al-Baqarah 2:111, the Quran challenges claims to exclusive possession of Paradise; in Al-Baqarah 2:112, it replaces group labels with submission accompanied by doing good. In Al-Baqarah 2:62 and Al-Maidah 5:69, it likewise makes the criterion of salvation genuine faith in God and the Last Day, and righteous action, rather than outward names.

The mindful person does not fragment the history of guidance or discriminate among messengers (An-Nisa 4:150 and An-Nisa 4:151). In the light of the truth that has reached them, they honor earlier revelation and do not refuse to submit to newly encountered truth.

This openness to the current of revelation is secured by another anchor: “وَبِالْآخِرَةِ هُمْ يُوقِنُونَ” (and they are certain of the Hereafter). Certainty in the Hereafter goes beyond a mental proposition about a world after death: it grounds human ethics and responsibility in life. Someone who does not see the world as without reckoning or outcome does not treat it as a realm without responsibility. They know that all choices, judgments, powers, and actions have consequences before God.

Thus two pillars of the mindful person’s character emerge: an honest connection with the whole current of revelation throughout history, and an ethically formative certainty in the final outcome before the Lord. The Quran does not register guidance as the property of any community; it draws the qualities of a person receptive to guidance. Mindfulness is not an inherited title, but a reality that must become visible in faith, doing good, submission, and individual responsibility.
