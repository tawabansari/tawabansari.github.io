Al-Baqarah 2:262

ۙ

Those who spend their wealth in the way of The God, then do not follow what they have spent with reproach or injury, have their reward with their Lord; and no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.

Our Reflection

This verse addresses one of the most important ethical principles of giving: a person must not only give wealth for good, but must also preserve the recipient’s dignity afterward and avoid turning the gift into a means of serving personal aims.

“الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ”

“Those who spend their wealth in the way of The God.”

The qualification “فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ” is essential. Someone who spends wealth in God’s way should aim at goodness, justice, meeting needs, and improving people’s lives, rather than buying respect, gaining superiority, or making others dependent on them.

But the Quran does not stop at intention and action at the time of giving; it also directs attention to conduct afterward:

“ثُمَّ لَا يُتْبِعُونَ مَا أَنْفَقُوا مَنًّا وَلَا أَذًى”

“Then do not follow what they have spent with reproach or injury.”

“يُتْبِعُونَ,” from the root T B AYN, denotes following or bringing something after something else. Its combination with “ثُمَّ” shows that the giver’s ethical responsibility does not end when wealth is handed to another person. What the giver does afterward matters too.

In this context, “مَنّ” means flaunting a gift and reminding the recipient of it in a way that burdens them with indebtedness, shame, or a sense of inferiority.

“أَذًى,” from the root A DH Y, denotes injury, suffering, and harm, which may be verbal, psychological, or behavioral.

These two concepts are close, but not identical. A person may humiliate or hurt a recipient through their conduct without directly mentioning the wealth given. They may also repeatedly flaunt their help and expect obedience, praise, or repayment in return.

Someone who uses a gift afterward to impose demands or gain superiority puts an act that should be for God’s sake at the service of personal aims. Even if the intention was good at the time of giving, they must not allow it to become tainted later by selfishness, pride, or the pursuit of personal advantage.

God then says of such givers:

“لَهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ”

“Their reward is with their Lord.”

This expression further clarifies the direction of giving. One reason for reproaching someone over a gift is expecting the person we helped to repay our kindness.

For example, we may support and help someone during hardship, yet when we ourselves become needy, that person does not come to our aid. If we expected repayment from the beginning, we feel hurt and upset by this conduct and may complain loudly and flaunt our past kindness.

But someone who gives for The God should seek the reward for their kindness from Him, not from the person they helped. Giving in God’s way should not become a claim that makes us regard the recipient as owing repayment.

The expression “عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ” also shows that final judgment concerning the value of giving rests with the Lord who knows human intentions and conduct. Others may not know of a person’s gift or may never thank them, but their good deed is not forgotten by God.

The verse ends with this promise:

“وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ”

“And no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.”

“Fear” refers to apprehension and concern about an approaching danger or outcome, while “grief” refers to sorrow and heaviness of heart. The Quran brings the two together to express the breadth of the security and peace promised to such people.

This promise does not mean that those who do good will never encounter fear, grief, or hardship in worldly life. Rather, they can trust in the outcome of their good deeds and the reward preserved with their Lord.

Thus, giving in God’s way is not merely parting with wealth; preserving a person’s dignity after giving is an inseparable part of its goodness. Someone who truly gives for God must not exploit another person’s need to assert superiority or make them dependent. Giving should untie knots in people’s lives, not fasten a new chain around their necks.
