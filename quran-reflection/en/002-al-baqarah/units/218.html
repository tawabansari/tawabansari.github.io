Al-Baqarah 2:218

ۚ

Indeed, those who believed and those who migrated and strove in the path of The God—they hope for the mercy of The God; and The God is Forgiving and Merciful.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:217, God spoke of barring people from His path, expelling the people of the Sacred Mosque, and fighting those who seek to turn people back from their religion. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:218, He places faith, migration, and striving in God’s path alongside one another.

“إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا وَجَاهَدُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ”

“Indeed, those who believed and those who migrated and strove in the path of The God.”

This is the first verse in the Quran’s order to speak explicitly of migration. Hijrah, from the root H J R, relates to leaving and separating. Here it concerns those who left their former surroundings and homeland and strove in God’s path.

Migration in the Quran is not merely moving to find work, income, or better living conditions. When a person lives under pressure and oppression and is deprived of freedom to worship, think, speak, and act upon their beliefs, leaving that environment can be a way to preserve faith, life, and the ability to carry on living.

The importance of migration becomes clearer in An-Nisa 4:97. God speaks of people who, at death, describe themselves as oppressed, yet are asked: Was God’s earth not vast enough for you to migrate in it?

This is a serious question. People cannot always use attachment to their homeland, home, possessions, relatives, or ancestral land as an excuse to remain in an environment that blocks freedom and worship, when they have a real possibility of migrating.

In Al-Ankabut 29:56, God likewise recalls the vastness of the earth and calls His believing servants to worship Him. Loyalty to God must therefore not be sacrificed to attachment to a piece of land.

To clarify this, consider people trapped for years under bombardment and the fire of war. Their homes are destroyed, their children killed, and they have very limited means of defense against the enemy’s destructive weapons and equipment.

In such circumstances, should remaining under bombs and gunfire be regarded as the only sign of courage and loyalty to one’s homeland, even when a safe route to survival exists? Is fighting destructive weapons with stones and sticks, without assessing capabilities and outcomes, always the right decision?

The Quran does not command people to close their eyes to the reality of their capabilities and resources. In Al-Anfal 8:66, when describing the capacity to confront an adversary, God takes account of the believers’ weakness and lightens their burden.

If a family can migrate from a place of killing to somewhere safer, protect their children’s lives, pursue education, learn skills, and build strength for the future, why should they abandon that path out of prejudice or attachment to their soil?

Migration in such a situation does not mean surrendering to an aggressor, relinquishing rights to land, or abandoning the right of return. People can leave danger to preserve their lives and capabilities while continuing to work to end oppression and restore people’s freedom and rights.

The same question arises for people whose homeland offers no opportunity for education, research, freedom of expression, or thought. Why should a girl or boy denied education, a writer forbidden to express ideas, or a person persecuted for belief and questioning confine their entire future to those conditions when migration is possible?

They can go to a land where they can learn, think, work, and grow. There they can gain knowledge and experience, achieve economic independence, and develop abilities that may one day benefit both themselves and the people of their original homeland.

Migration under such conditions is not an escape from responsibility; it can be a way to preserve life, escape repression, and acquire the capacity needed to continue striving.

The Quran, however, gives a different ruling for those who truly cannot migrate. In An-Nisa 4:98, it exempts men, women, and children who have no means and can find no way. An-Nisa 4:99 also speaks of the possibility of their being pardoned.

Someone trapped under siege, without an exit, unable to travel, or unaware of any place of refuge therefore cannot be blamed for not migrating. Their responsibility is not the same as that of someone who has a safe, practical route available.

This also places responsibility on those with greater power and resources. Leaders and others who influence people’s fate must not merely encourage them to stay, resist, and sacrifice; they must also work to save their lives, find shelter, provide routes out, and preserve their future.

In An-Nisa 4:100, God speaks of the openings and places of refuge a migrant can find on earth. Even if someone dies while migrating, their reward is described as secured with God.

Yet migration is not the end of the path.

“وَجَاهَدُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ”

“And strove in the path of The God.”

“Jahadu,” from the root J H D, relates to exerting ability, enduring hardship, and sustained effort. Jihad in the Quran is not limited to warfare; striving with wealth, life, and resources also falls within its scope. The nature of this effort must be understood from its context and purpose.

Migration and striving appear together in this verse. Their pairing carries an important message: someone who has escaped repression and reached a safer land should not imagine that their responsibility ended upon crossing the border.

A migrant first needs security, shelter, work, and a settled life for themselves and their family. After gaining the ability, however, they can use new opportunities for education, personal development, cooperation, and helping others.

For example, those who have migrated from war and repression can study in their new country, become doctors, teachers, engineers, or researchers, establish media and educational institutions, assist deprived people, and speak for those denied freedom of expression.

If migrants become dispersed and each thinks only of personal comfort, possessions, and pleasure, they may lose a great opportunity to help people and improve conditions.

Migration should create the conditions for human growth and capability, and striving in God’s path can continue that same journey: an effort to uphold truth, remove oppression, spread knowledge, and secure freedom and justice.

Sometimes this effort leads to returning to the original homeland; at other times, people serve their communities from their new country. Returning home is not a condition this verse prescribes for every migrant. Yet a connection with truth and responsibility toward others must not be forgotten amid the comforts of a new life.

An important example appears in the lives of Muhammad’s earliest followers. A group of believers sought refuge in Abyssinia to escape persecution, and Muhammad and his companions migrated from Mecca to Medina.

Their migration did not end with leaving homes and possessions. In their new surroundings, they cooperated, built a community, and developed social and economic capacity. Al-Anfal 8:72 recalls migrants who strove with their wealth and lives in God’s path, and those who gave them shelter and support. Al-Hashr 59:8 and Al-Hashr 59:9 likewise present migrants and those who welcomed them alongside one another.

This experience shows that migration is not solely the migrant’s responsibility; the receiving community also has a role in offering refuge, support, and the conditions for a new life.

Eventually, the way back to Mecca also opened. The significance of this experience is that migration can help preserve people and build the capacity needed to end exclusion and return to their original homeland, rather than aim to impose belief on others. The Quranic criteria remain justice, the prohibition of transgression, and the rejection of compulsion in religion.

This connection also appears in An-Nahl 16:110, where God speaks of those who migrated after suffering persecution and then strove and remained patient.

In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:202, we also discussed the relationship between supplication, choice, migration, building capacity, and community formation. The Quran connects a person’s share with what they earn. Those who want freedom and justice must also understand the way to reach them and strive to make them real. Prayer alone, without sound choices and use of available resources, does not replace action.

“أُولَٰئِكَ يَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَ اللَّهِ”

“They hope for the mercy of The God.”

God does not say that those who migrated and strove have become assured possessors of His mercy; He says that they hope for it.

“Yarjuna” speaks of hope. People may endure great hardships to preserve their faith and freedom, give up their homes and possessions, and strive for truth and justice for years. Yet they must not make these deeds a source of pride or a claim of certain superiority over others.

Nor is every migration a migration in God’s path merely because it involves moving from one place to another; not every effort counts as striving in His path simply because it entails hardship and sacrifice. Purpose, method, and conduct matter. Someone who escapes oppression must not become an oppressor after gaining power.

“وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ”

“And The God is Forgiving and Merciful.”

The verse ends with forgiveness and mercy. The path of faith, migration, and effort is not always easy. People may experience fear, doubt, or error in their choices and encounter unforeseen difficulties. Yet the way of return, reform, and hope in God’s mercy is not closed.

In this verse, faith is joined to movement and effort. When people are trapped in repression and have a way to escape, they should not place attachment to land or prejudice above preserving life, freedom to worship, and the opportunity to grow. After migrating, however, they should not seek new freedom and resources solely for personal comfort.

Migration can take people out of an environment that destroys their capabilities; striving in God’s path means putting those capabilities to use for truth, freedom, justice, and helping others. By preserving life and gaining knowledge and abilities, people can continue opposing oppression more effectively, without themselves resorting to oppression or imposing belief.
