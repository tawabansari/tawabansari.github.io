Al-Baqarah 2:62

Indeed, those who believed, and those who became Jews, and the Nasara, and the Sabi'in; whoever believes in The God (Allah) and the Last Day, and does righteous action, then their reward is with their Lord (Rabb); no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve.

Our Reflection

In the context of the preceding verses, the Qur'an repeatedly spoke about the errors of a group from the Children of Israel: breaking covenant, committing kufr toward the signs, distorting the word of truth, fisq, corruption, ingratitude for ni'mah, entitlement, rebellion, crossing limits, and even killing prophets without any right. This verse comes after those hard and explicit critiques to show that the Qur'an's critique is not racial or ethnic. It is a critique of conduct, kufr, wrongdoing, and deviation.

Therefore, exactly at the moment when this verse exposes the conduct of those who erred, it does not bury the good and righteous under the heavy weight of a collective judgment. The Qur'an does not allow the slips of a group from the Children of Israel to become a condemnation of everyone known by the name Jew, Christian, Sabi'i, or even believer. In the presence of The God, the criterion of salvation is not collective names and labels, but the reality of faith and righteous action under the canopy of oneness.

Here the scale of Qur'anic justice becomes clear. The Qur'an neither covers over historical errors nor condemns human beings as one block merely because they carry a name or group relation. If the preceding verses spoke about people who were committing kufr toward the signs of The God and killing prophets without any right, here it declares that whoever believes in The God and the Last Day, and does good and worthy action, has his reward preserved with his Lord. This means that condemnation in the Qur'anic view moves around wrongdoing and transgression, not around blood, race, tribe, or religious titles.

The subtler point is that the verse begins with the expression “those who believed” (alladhina amanu), an expression that in this setting can refer to a group who, in the space of the Messenger's mission, saw themselves as part of the circle of believers. But the Qur'an places this very group right beside “those who became Jews” (alladhina hadu), the Nasara, and the Sabi'in, and then reminds all of them of the real criterion again with the phrase “whoever believes” (man amana). This structure of expression is deeply meaningful. Even the title “those who believed,” if it remains only at the level of social identity, outward membership, or verbal claim, is not enough for salvation. Every name and sign must pass again through the refining place of real faith, belief in the Hereafter, and righteous action.

Therefore, the arrow of the verse is not aimed only at Jews, Christians, and Sabi'in. It includes all names and claims. Even someone who outwardly and officially stands among the people of faith is not saved merely by leaning on that name. Rather, he must have a truthful faith in The God, take the Last Day seriously, and do worthy action. In Qur'anic logic, a believer is not merely the name of a member inside a religious society. A believer is someone in whom the reality of faith has awakened in the heart, and this awakening shows itself in the mirror of action, justice, truthfulness, gratitude, fidelity, mercy, and responsibility. If faith is reduced to an identity label and does not end in soundness and repair (salah) in a person's conduct, it has fallen far away from its Qur'anic reality. This is why, after listing the names of different groups, the verse returns again to the same foundational criterion: faith in The God, belief in the Day of Recompense, and doing righteous action.

The expression “those who became Jews” (alladhina hadu) also needs a careful reading. This expression is not merely an ethnic label. From the angle of root structure, the word is connected with H W D and the meaning of returning, and it should not be confused with guidance (hidayah) from the root H D Y. In the Qur'an, Musa says on behalf of his people: “inna hudna ilayk,” meaning: we have returned to You. So “those who became Jews” (alladhina hadu), beyond pointing to the recognized community of the people of Musa, also carries a deeper layer of returning, repentance, and becoming soft before truth. If this spirit of being able to return disappears, the historical name alone will not untie anything.

The word Nasara is also not only an external and person-centered name. It is striking that the Qur'an does not use the word Christian, but deliberately speaks of the Nasara, a word tied to the meaning of support (nasr) and helping, and recalling those who, in their companionship with Isa, called themselves helpers in the way of The God. But here too the rule is the same: an empty name is not enough. If this claim of helping the truth remains only a slogan and does not lead to truthful faith in The God, belief in the Hereafter, and righteous action, no name by itself is saving.

The Sabi'in also have a deeply thought-provoking place in this verse. The Qur'an brings them alongside recognized religious groups, but gives no explanation of their race, language, land, legal branches, or historical details. This meaningful silence is itself a major methodological lesson. The message the Qur'an highlights for salvation is not being trapped in ethnic curiosity and polemical historical debates. Rather, the one condition for passing through is the same: faith in The God, belief in the Last Day, and doing righteous action.

As for exactly which people or religious tradition the Sabi'in were, history and tafsir differ. Some have connected them with the Mandaeans, but the Qur'an itself does not enter these details. From the perspective of the verse, knowing every historical detail about the Sabi'in is not a condition for understanding the criterion of salvation. The Qur'an mentions their name in order to take the horizon of guidance outside closed religious borders and return the criterion again to the reality of faith and righteous action.

The root SAD B A is connected with coming out and moving from one state to another. In Arabic usage, Sabi'i can be said of someone who has come out of the common religion of his people. For this reason, the polytheists sometimes used a name of this kind for those who left their inherited religion. But the Qur'an does not use this word as an insult. It places it beside other religious names and places all of them under one criterion. This point is important: coming out of an inherited mold is not enough by itself. It must reach faith in The God, the Last Day, and righteous action.

This same name also reveals another issue. If the later apparatus of reports and tafsir were truly always the complete explanation of the Qur'an, then a clear and reliable knowledge of the Sabi'in should have remained in the general memory of Muslims. But that did not happen. In many places, external reports filled the silence of the Qur'an with abundant details. Yet on subjects such as the Sabi'in, the disjointed letters, and Dhu al-Kifl, that same report-based apparatus became weak and scattered. For a fuller discussion, see the article: “When Qur'anic Silence Is Filled with Reports.”

But this verse must not be turned into religious measurelessness. The Qur'an does not say that every name, every belief, and every path are equal without testing and without truth. The verse makes the criterion clear: faith in The God, faith in the Last Day, and righteous action. So the verse accepts neither religious exclusivism nor boundaryless relativism. Every person, from every name and community, must be measured by these three principles.

Faith in The God means turning toward the Lord (Rabb) and the one worshiped, not surrendering to tribe, clergy, monarchy, report, race, the market of religion, or human lords. Faith in the Last Day means not seeing life as unaccountable and irresponsible; it means knowing that human action is not lost and that final judgment is with the Lord. Righteous action means faith taking effect on the earth of life: in justice, mercy, repair, fidelity, avoiding wrongdoing, and building good.

Here the link between the root of faith (iman) and the end of the verse is also beautiful. Faith comes from the semantic field of safety, trust, and confidence. A human being who believes in The God and the Last Day leans, at the center of final judgment, on a place of security. He does not see the future as ownerless or the past as lost. If righteous action accompanies faith, this inner security takes shape in outward life. That is why the end of the verse says: no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve.

Fear and grief are two great pains of the human being. Fear is anxiety about the future, danger, judgment, destruction, and what lies ahead. Grief is sorrow over loss, failure, the past, absence, and regret. The Qur'an does not say that such human beings see no hardship in life. Rather, it says that in their final relation with their Lord, they are placed in true security. Their reward is with their Lord (Rabb), and when the reward is with the Rabb, the future and the past are not lost in His judgment.

Here we must also return to the connection between this verse and verse 61. In verse 61, the Children of Israel, after great ni'mahs, fell into entitlement and ingratitude. They exchanged good for what was lower; they descended; and humiliation, helplessness, and anger came upon them. In verse 62, the Qur'an again shows the path out of fear and grief: faith in The God, the Last Day, and righteous action. It is as if after the social descent of the Children of Israel, the same prescription that was given after Adam's descent is being recalled once more.

In Al-Baqarah 2:38, after Adam's descent, the promise came that if guidance from The God arrives, then whoever follows His guidance will have no fear upon them, nor will they grieve. Here too, after the descent of the Children of Israel in verse 61, verse 62 opens the same horizon again: no fear and no grief belong to the one who takes the reality of faith and righteous action, not merely the name and relation.

This verse is also repeated in Surat al-Ma'idah with very close wording. In Al-Ma'idah 5:69, the same groups appear with a slightly different order: those who believed, those who became Jews, the Sabi'un, and the Nasara; and again the criterion is the same: faith in The God, the Last Day, and righteous action. This repetition shows that we are not facing a passing sentence. We are facing a Qur'anic principle.

In Surat al-Hajj 22:17, the circle of names becomes wider: those who believed, those who became Jews, the Sabi'in, the Nasara, the Magians, and those who associated partners. But there the sentence of salvation does not appear. Instead, final judgment is entrusted to The God on the Day of Resurrection. This difference is important. Al-Baqarah and Al-Ma'idah state the criterion of salvation; Al-Hajj reminds us that the final separation and complete judgment among all these groups belongs to The God Himself on the Day of Resurrection. No human being, jurist, exegete, people, or religious institution has the right to sit in His place.

So verse 62 breaks the pride of the People of the Book, and it also breaks the pride of later Muslims. Being Jewish is not salvation by itself; nor being Nasrani, nor being Sabi'i, nor even carrying the name believer or Muslim. Any name, if emptied of faith in The God, the Last Day, and righteous action, is a shell. And any human being who turns truthfully toward The God, the final account, and righteous action has his judgment with his Lord.

Elsewhere, the Qur'an breaks this same illusion of exclusivity even more openly: they said that none would enter Paradise except one who was a Jew or a Nasrani. The Qur'an calls this their amani, meaning baseless wishes and group claims. This warning is not only for the past. Whenever a Muslim, believer, Jew, Nasrani, or any other group imagines that his name alone is a certificate of salvation, the same identity disease has been repeated.

This verse also does not allow the Qur'an's preceding critiques against part of the Children of Israel to become ethnic hatred. The Qur'an criticizes wrongdoers, not blood and race. The same Book that condemns kufr, rebellion, transgression, and killing prophets immediately opens the way for every believing and righteous human being. This is the justice of the Qur'an: neither blind sanctification nor blind condemnation, but measurement by truth.

The lesson of the verse is this: see the names, but do not stop at the names. Know history, but do not bury human beings under historical labels. Understand the Qur'an through the Qur'an, not through stories that later cast a shadow over it. The criterion of The God is clear: faith in Him, faith in the Last Day, and righteous action. Whoever takes this reality, his reward is with his Lord (Rabb); no fear is upon him, nor does he grieve.
