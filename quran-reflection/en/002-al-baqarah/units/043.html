Al-Baqarah 2:43

And establish salat, and give zakat, and bow with those who bow.

Our Reflection

This verse comes after three weighty warnings: do not be the first kafir toward the confirming revelation; do not trade God's signs for a small price; and do not cover truth with falsehood or conceal truth while you know. So verse 43 is not an isolated ritual command detached from the surrounding passage. It shows the path of repair: returning to a living relation with the Lord, fulfilling responsibility toward creation, and practically joining the row of those who humble themselves before God.

First it says: wa-aqimu as-salah. Establishing salat is not merely performing a ritual act. Establishing means setting upright, making firm, and keeping something in its proper place. Just as the entire tent is harmed if its central pole is crooked, if the human being's relation with God becomes crooked, his understanding, ethics, judgment, and social responsibility also fall out of balance.

If salat is reduced only to form and custom, it can coexist with the same concealment and mixing of truth with falsehood. But establishing salat means that the human being's relation with his Lord becomes so upright in his life that it pulls him out of bargaining with the signs, covering truth, religious pride, and fear of people. Established salat straightens the human being again before the source of truth.

Salat in this verse shows the human being's right relation before the Lord; not because God needs human worship, but because the human being needs connection, remembrance, direction, and return to God. When salat is established, the human being regulates himself before The One worshiped. He can no longer hide truth for ethnic or political interest and still claim that his relation with God is upright. A salat that does not return the human being to truth remains movement and wording; but a salat that is established makes the human being upright before God.

Then it says: wa-atu az-zakah. If salat establishes the human being's relation with the Lord, zakat makes visible the human being's responsibility toward creation and society. In social life, the human being does not have only an individual relation with God; he also has an ethical and practical relation with God's creation. Zakat means that something leaves the human being in a way that brings purification, growth, repair, and flourishing to society.

Zakat should not be limited only to wealth, even though wealth is one of its clearest fields. The root of zakat is tied to purity, growth, and increase. In its natural image, healthy growth can come with purification: like a tree that, when dry and excess branches are cut away, gains the possibility of new budding, or a fire that burns more clearly when smoke and pollution are reduced. So zakat is not merely the outward decrease of property. It is giving out something that makes the human being and society cleaner, more alive, and more capable of growth.

From this angle, every blessing that a person has the ability to give can enter the field of zakat: wealth, knowledge, power, time, just judgment, support for the weak, meeting people's needs, and even the proper use of social position. When what is in a person's possession becomes a cause of purification and growth for society, the meaning of zakat becomes alive.

This point is very important for the Children of Israel in this context. Earlier, the Qur'an asked them to remember God's favor. If a favor is merely held, it becomes privilege and pride. But if it enters the path of zakat, it leads to purification and growth in society. Someone who has Book, knowledge, rank, or social power does not fulfill his zakat only by paying money. His zakat is also that he not conceal knowledge, not cover truth, and use his position for the repair of society.

But the essence of the verse comes at its end: warka'u ma'a ar-raki'in. The Qur'an does not only say, “bow.” It says: bow with those who bow. This ma'a, “with,” is very important. The subject is not isolated, detached worship. It is joining a shared direction. The Children of Israel are invited, before God, to step out of ethnic isolation and hard identity boundaries, and to bow together with those who bow for God.

Ruku' outwardly is bending. But in meaning, it is the breaking of the posture of pride before God. In its natural image, a branch full of fruit bends downward; a posture full of fruit does not remain dry, stiff, and arrogant. So ruku' is not only a bodily motion. It is the body's language before truth. The Qur'an asks the Children of Israel to perform this humility not in separation and superiority, but in companionship with those who bow.

In the Medinan setting, this meaning is very alive. A new community of God-conscious people is being formed: a community that seeks to be built on tawhid, truthfulness, security, responsibility, and humility before God. In such a setting, the People of the Book are invited not to separate themselves from this movement through opposition, concealment, political bargaining, and preserving hard identity distance. If they are truly People of the Book and people of God, then they should be able to bow with other God-conscious people before the same Lord.

This verse shows that the Qur'an does not call the Children of Israel to erase their scriptural identity completely. Rather, it calls them to a shared truth that they should also recognize in their own Book: relation with God, responsibility toward creation, and practical humility before The One worshiped. The question, then, is not what ethnic or religious label a person carries. The question is whether he covers truth or makes it clear, whether he turns favor into responsibility or privilege, and whether he joins those who humble themselves before God or takes a stance against them.

Here the difference between living religion and closed identity becomes clear. Living religion carries the human being toward salat, zakat, and bowing with those who bow. Closed identity may imprison a person in name, people, history, and group boundaries. In this verse, the Qur'an does not close the doors of shared servanthood; it invites the Children of Israel into the row of those who bow.

From this angle, verse 43 is weighty for our time as well. Today many religious systems, places of worship, and houses attributed to God are closed according to identity and sectarian boundaries. But in this verse, the Qur'an calls the Children of Israel to bow with those who bow. This invitation shows that the horizon of the Qur'an, at this layer, is not the exclusion of God-conscious people from the space of servanthood, but their joining in shared humility before God.

For a wider understanding of this view, the article “Zakat and Its Historical Distortion: From Qur'anic Tazkiyah to Religious Tax, Takfir, and Political Authority” can provide a deeper background, because it shows that zakat in the Qur'an is not merely a religious tax or an instrument of political authority, but is tied to tazkiyah, growth, social responsibility, and the purification of the human being's relation with society.

So verse 43 is the practical answer to the previous verses. If you do not cover truth with falsehood, if you do not conceal truth, if you do not sell God's signs for a small price, the result must become visible in your life: establish salat, give zakat, and bow with those who bow for God. These three commands build together the human being's relation with God, his responsibility toward society, and his place among God-conscious people.
