Al-Baqarah 2:255

ۚ ۚ ۗ ۚ ۖ ۚ ۖ ۚ

The God; there is no deity except Him, the Living, the Self-Subsisting Sustainer. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth belongs to Him. Who can intercede before Him except by His permission? He knows what is between their hands and what is behind them, and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His Kursi encompasses the heavens and the earth, and preserving the two does not burden Him; and He is the Most High, the Magnificent.

Our Reflection

Ayat al-Kursi is among those verses whose greatness and wisdom cannot be fully done justice, however much we write about them. In Al-Kahf 18:109 and Luqman 31:27, the Quran reminds us that even if the seas were ink and the trees pens, the words of The God would not come to an end. How, then, could a few pages of human reflection encompass the truth and greatness of such a verse?

Nevertheless, we strive to learn from this verse according to our knowledge and ability. Ayat al-Kursi alone offers a school of monotheistic philosophy, connecting knowledge of God, creation, sovereignty, knowledge, permission, intercession, and humanity’s place before The God.

The verse begins with “اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ”: there is no deity except The God. This statement does not merely deny the worship of idols; it denies every independent claim to divinity and every attempt to place a being in His position.

Then come two fundamental attributes: “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ”. God is living, but not in the sense of the life experienced by living creatures, which depends on food, environment, energy, and various conditions. He is al-Qayyum: self-subsisting and the sustainer of existence. His existence does not depend on another, whereas other beings need causes and conditions both to come into being and to continue existing.

“لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ”: neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. Both the slightest drowsiness and full sleep are denied of Him. The verse removes not only the limitation of sleep but also the need for rest and replenishment from our conception of God.

“لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ”: whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him. His sovereignty is not limited to a portion of existence, and no being or domain lies outside His ownership.

It then asks: “مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ”: who can intercede before Him except by His permission? This question denies the independence of every intercessor and intermediary. No one can use their standing or influence to impose anything upon the sovereignty of The God.

It then states: “يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ”: He knows what is between their hands and what is behind them. His knowledge is not confined by the boundaries of observation, time, and experience.

Against this encompassing knowledge, the limits of creatures’ knowledge become clear: “وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ”: they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills.

It then states: “وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ”: His Kursi encompasses the heavens and the earth. Here the Kursi symbolizes sovereignty, authority, and the seat of power of The God: the scope of His rule and sovereignty encompasses all the heavens and the earth.

“وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا”: preserving the heavens and the earth does not burden Him. Sustaining existence does not leave God weary or helpless.

Finally: “وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ”: He is the Most High, the Magnificent. Just as the verse begins with God’s oneness and self-subsisting sustenance, it ends by declaring His supremacy and greatness.

Yet the significance of Ayat al-Kursi is not limited to explaining these attributes separately. When we read the whole verse together and connect it with other Quranic verses, several greater truths emerge, each deserving a discussion of its own.

We will examine seven dimensions of this verse’s significance: first, its philosophical place in understanding God and rejecting the idea of an inferior deity; second, the self-subsistence and sustaining power of The God; third, the verse’s ring composition and remarkable linguistic order; fourth, the theology of divine transcendence and the limits of creatures’ knowledge; fifth, divine permission and its relationship with the patterns of creation; sixth, intercession; and finally, the connection between God’s absolute sovereignty and the freedom of faith introduced in the following verse.

1. The philosophical significance of Ayat al-Kursi: recognizing the true God and rejecting an inferior deity

One of humanity’s longstanding questions has been who created the world, and why suffering, corruption, limitation, and ignorance exist within it.

Some Gnostic schools explain this by distinguishing between the supreme divine reality and the creator of the material world. In some such accounts, an inferior, ignorant being, sometimes called the “Demiurge,” brings the material world into existence and imagines himself to hold divine status. Human liberation is connected with a kind of inward knowledge that reveals the deception of this world and its inferior creator.

In modern times, new narratives have also developed around such ideas. For example, some claim that unknown beings draw upon human suffering, fear, emotion, or energy, and that human life is caught in a cycle on which they feed or which they use for amusement. These modern conceptions should not be equated with all historical Gnostic beliefs. Yet both raise the question of beings who dominate humanity or claim divine status.

Ayat al-Kursi provides a fundamental criterion for evaluating such claims.

«اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ»

If a being needs human energy, nourishment, emotions, worship, or attention to continue existing, how could it be The God?

If a being is ignorant of some truth, does not know the future, needs sleep and rest, or has its abilities limited by a higher power, how could it be that same “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ”?

And if a being claims independent creation or sovereignty over part of the world, how does it confront this Quranic statement: “لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ”: whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him?

In Al-Anam 6:102, God also says: “لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ خَالِقُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ”: there is no deity except Him, the creator of everything.

In Al-Anbiya 21:29, even beings whose standing and nearness were mentioned in the preceding verses receive this warning:

«وَمَنْ يَقُلْ مِنْهُمْ إِنِّي إِلَٰهٌ مِنْ دُونِهِ فَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِيهِ جَهَنَّمَ»

“And whoever among them says, ‘I am a deity besides Him,’ We will recompense that one with Hell.”

This statement follows a verse about God’s encompassing knowledge and intercession. Thus, even possessing special standing, extraordinary abilities, or permission to perform an act does not place any being in the position of divinity.

These verses leave no room for an independent creator alongside The God. Even if unknown beings exist with abilities beyond present human understanding, their power, knowledge, and existence still cannot be independent of God.

The existence of corruption and suffering in the world does not, by itself, prove an imperfect creator. In Ar-Rum 30:41, the Quran speaks of corruption appearing on land and sea because of human deeds. Al-Baqarah 2:251 also described restraining corruption through some people opposing others. At least part of the disorder and ruin in human life is therefore connected with people’s own conduct, choices, and responsibility, rather than the supposition of an independent, inferior creator.

One of the greatest gains of monotheistic understanding is having a criterion for assessing claims to divinity, unknown powers, and extraordinary phenomena.

Whether in a dream, while awake, or in any other unfamiliar experience, a person encountering a being’s claim to divinity should not equate the wonder of that experience with the reality of God. Any being that is limited, needy, ignorant, or dependent differs from the attributes of The God from the outset.

Not every power, however extraordinary, is divine power; and a being does not become The God merely by claiming divinity.

This understanding prevents people from setting up equals to God and surrendering to claimants of divinity. For a broader examination, see the principle of the oneness of The God and Andad in the Quran.

2. Self-subsistence and sustaining existence: did God Himself come into being?

One of the deepest concepts of Ayat al-Kursi lies in the word “الْقَيُّومُ”.

Qayyum is connected with standing, establishment, firmness, and sustaining. Here the subject is not a being that merely rules over something, but the deity who subsists through Himself and upon whom the existence of others depends.

To understand the importance of this issue, we must distinguish two questions:

Who created the world?

And did that creator also come into being, or does its existence depend upon something else?

This distinction matters greatly when examining certain ancient creation accounts.

For example, in some ancient Egyptian accounts, Atum emerges as a primordial creator from an initial state or primordial waters, after which other gods and elements of the world are said to arise from him. In some Hindu traditions, Brahma, associated with the creation of the world, himself emerges from a cosmic egg or a lotus connected with Vishnu.

In Hindu philosophy, however, Brahma as a creator deity must be distinguished from Brahman as ultimate reality in certain schools. Hindu traditions themselves also contain diverse views of supreme reality, creation, and the world’s relationship with that reality.

Nevertheless, such accounts raise the fundamental question of self-subsistence: is a being that itself arises through a process the same reality on which the existence of everything depends?

In Ayat al-Kursi, The God is presented neither as the product of a process of creation nor as a being that took over the administration of the world after it came into existence.

He is “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ”; and Al-Hadid 57:3 also states:

«هُوَ الْأَوَّلُ وَالْآخِرُ»

“He is the First and the Last.”

Al-Ikhlas 112:2 also states: “اللَّهُ الصَّمَدُ”: The God is free of need, and others depend upon Him.

These verses present a reality that does not receive its beginning or existence from anything else.

Another subject is theories that regard God and creation as one reality.

In some monistic and nondual approaches, the distinction between ultimate reality and the world is understood in such a way that the multiplicity of beings ultimately returns to one reality. These approaches are not all alike: some speak of complete unity, while others describe more complex relationships between the world and the creator.

In Quranic reasoning, however, although the world depends upon God, God is not identified with the sum of the world and its beings.

The Quran states:

“لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ” — Ash-Shura 42:11

“Nothing is like Him.”

And Al-Araf 7:54 states:

«أَلَا لَهُ الْخَلْقُ وَالْأَمْرُ»

“Indeed, creation and command belong to Him.”

If we identify God with this material world and the totality of its beings, questions arise: must change, deterioration, dependence, and the destruction of phenomena also be attributed to God’s reality? Is a being that experiences sleep, weakness, and ignorance part of the same reality from which the Quran denies all sleep and limitation of knowledge?

By presenting God’s self-subsistence, freedom from need, and encompassing knowledge, Ayat al-Kursi clarifies the boundary between His reality and dependent beings.

There is another important point: God is not merely the world’s initial originator who left it to itself after creating it.

«وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا»

Preserving the heavens and the earth does not burden Him.

In Qaf 50:38, God also says of creating the heavens and the earth:

«وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِنْ لُغُوبٍ»

“And no weariness touched Us.”

Together, these two verses reject two conceptions of limited power: creation does not weary God, and preserving creation does not burden Him.

The same deity who creates existence also preserves it. He needs neither rest, replenishment, nor the help of an independent power to sustain the world.

Self-subsisting sustenance therefore rejects the idea of a deity that came into being and is dependent, the idea of a deity unable to preserve creation, and every claim that creation forms a domain independent of God’s sovereignty.

3. Textual and rhetorical significance: the ring composition of Ayat al-Kursi

One of the remarkable features of Ayat al-Kursi is its internal order and the precise connection between its clauses.

The verse is not a collection of scattered attributes placed beside one another without connection. When divided into nine units of meaning, a ring structure can be discerned: the opening and closing units illuminate one another in corresponding pairs, with one concept at the center.

In this structure, the first unit is connected with the ninth. The verse begins with God’s name and fundamental attributes and ends by declaring His exalted standing and greatness.

The second and eighth units also correspond: at the beginning, sleep and drowsiness are denied of God, and at the end, the burden of preserving the heavens and the earth.

The third and seventh units both mention the heavens and the earth. First, ownership of all that is within them is stated, then the extent of the divine seat of power and sovereignty.

The fourth and sixth units concern the limits of creatures’ authority and abilities. No intercession is independent of God’s permission, and no encompassing of His knowledge occurs except by His will.

At the heart of this structure lies the encompassing knowledge of The God:

«يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ»

This central position has particular significance. A verse discussing power, sovereignty, intercession, knowledge, and the preservation of existence connects all these concepts around God’s knowledge.

The verse’s verbal precision also invites reflection.

Its opening, “لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ”, denies every other deity and affirms only The God.

“سِنَةٌ” and “نَوْمٌ” together deny both drowsiness and sleep.

In the form of a question, “مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي” invites the reader to reflect on the powerlessness of any independent intercessor.

“إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ” and “إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ” express two fundamental limitations on either side of divine knowledge.

The repetition of “السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ” in two corresponding units moves from ownership to the encompassing reach of the Kursi.

The ending, “الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ”, brings the reader’s mind back to the reality introduced at the beginning.

This harmony between wording, meaning, and the verse’s structure can be understood as a manifestation of the Quran’s miraculous expression: an order that is not limited to beautiful speech but also helps us understand and connect its concepts.

4. The theology of divine transcendence: God’s unlimited knowledge and the boundary of creatures’ understanding

One of the deepest foundations of the theology of divine transcendence can be recognized in this expression from Ayat al-Kursi:

«وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ»

“And they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills.”

With this statement, God establishes a fundamental boundary between His knowledge and creatures’ understanding. However much people learn, research, and discover, their grasp of divine knowledge is neither independent nor unlimited. Compared with the reality and extent of the knowledge of The God, what they know is limited to what God has made it possible for them to understand.

This limitation is not simply about being unaware of certain secrets of the world; it also fundamentally affects knowledge of God Himself and His attributes. How can someone who does not even encompass God’s knowledge claim to have fully understood and grasped the reality of The God through their own limited concepts and criteria?

In Ash-Shura 42:11, the Quran states:

«لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ»

“Nothing is like Him.”

This expression clarifies a fundamental principle of knowing God: His reality cannot be equated with the beings of the world. The words people use to understand and describe attributes arise from their experience, observation, and limited knowledge, but the reality and perfection of divine attributes do not fit within those limits.

For example, when we speak of knowledge, we usually understand knowledge acquired through learning, observation, experience, memory, and reasoning. God’s knowledge, however, does not depend on such processes. He does not need instruction to know; nothing is hidden from His knowledge, and His knowing is not subject to forgetfulness or ignorance.

The same difference applies to life, power, mercy, and other divine attributes.

We know life through beings dependent on various causes and conditions, but God is “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ”. We experience kindness through human behavior and emotions, but the mercy of The God cannot be confined within fluctuating emotions and human needs.

Here the theology of divine transcendence becomes important. Tanzih means regarding God as beyond resemblance to creatures and their limitations. This principle does not deny His attributes; it means not reducing their reality to the measure of human experience and understanding.

In the same verse, Ash-Shura 42:11, which states “لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ”, we immediately read:

«وَهُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ»

“And He is the Hearing, the Seeing.”

The Quran denies God’s resemblance to any other being while identifying Him through real attributes. God is therefore not absolutely unknowable; rather, our knowledge of Him does not mean complete comprehension of His reality.

Ta-Ha 20:110 expresses this distinction in another way:

«وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِهِ عِلْمًا»

“And they do not encompass Him in knowledge.”

This expression states creatures’ inability to encompass God Himself in knowledge, whereas Ayat al-Kursi clarifies the limits of their grasp of His knowledge. Together with the principle “لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ”, these two statements provide a firm foundation for understanding the transcendence of The God.

In Allah, Khodavand, Parvardegar, and the Theology of Divine Transcendence (Persian), we have used the logic of the Persian prefix “fara” (“beyond”) to express this truth. Expressions such as “Fara-Rahman” and “Fara-Rahim” seek to show that God’s mercy and kindness, though understandable to people through language, go beyond the limits and measures of human mercy and kindness.

This principle is not merely a philosophical discussion of divine attributes; it also matters for how people know and speak about God.

Sometimes a person becomes so certain of their understanding of God and His attributes that it is as though the unlimited reality of The God were entirely contained within their mind, definition, or school of thought. Yet Ayat al-Kursi reminds us that human knowledge is limited from the outset, and that people encompass nothing of God’s knowledge except by His will.

God should be known through the attributes He has revealed, but His reality must not be reduced to the measure of limited human conceptions and experiences.

This understanding calls people to learn more while restraining them from claiming complete comprehension of the reality of The God.

For a detailed examination, see Allah, Khodavand, Parvardegar, and the Theology of Divine Transcendence (Persian).

5. Divine permission and the order of creation

Concerning intercession, Ayat al-Kursi states:

«مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ»

“Who can intercede before Him except by His permission?”

This expression denies the independence of every intercessor. No being possesses independent power or authority before the sovereignty of The God, such that it could influence His knowledge, judgment, or justice by its own will.

But how is divine permission to be understood?

In Divine Permission in the Quran, we have explained that permission concerning the occurrence and effect of events can be understood in relation to the effective laws, patterns, and conditions God has established in creation.

God has created a world in which events and the effects of human deeds are related to causes and conditions. Plants grow in favorable conditions, ships move when the necessary means are present, and human effort, preparation, decisions, and conduct affect outcomes. None of these causes or abilities lies outside the divine order of creation or is independent of His sovereignty.

In Al-Araf 7:58, the Quran states:

«وَالْبَلَدُ الطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ»

“And the good land brings forth its vegetation by the permission of its Lord.”

A plant’s growth by God’s permission is not unrelated to the soil’s conditions and the laws of its growth. Divine permission should therefore not be imagined merely as ceremonial authorization or an arbitrary command issued independently of all the causes and conditions of creation.

This principle also matters in human life. In Al-Baqarah 2:249, a small group’s victory over a larger group is connected with God’s permission, but the same verse also speaks of patience and steadfastness. Divine permission does not render human effort, preparation, or responsibility ineffective.

Another important point is that the possibility of an act occurring within the order of creation does not mean God morally approves of it. In Al-Baqarah 2:102, the occurrence of harm is made dependent on God’s permission, while the harmful conduct itself is condemned. No one can therefore justify corruption or oppression by claiming that, because they were able to do it, God must have been pleased with it.

In Ayat al-Kursi too, intercession does not lie outside God’s sovereignty. His permission does not mean handing independent power to creatures; rather, it places any intercession within the limits of authority and the order He Himself has established.

Divine permission therefore neither removes human choice and responsibility nor gives beings independent power before God. Every ability and effect ultimately occurs within the sovereignty of The God.

For a broader examination of this concept, Quranic examples, and its relationship with guidance, choice, causes, and the patterns of creation, see Divine Permission in the Quran: the Order of Creation, Guidance, and Human Choice.

6. Intercession: can anyone influence God’s knowledge and judgment?

In the preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:254, the Quran calls believers to spend before a day arrives of which it says:

«لَا بَيْعٌ فِيهِ وَلَا خُلَّةٌ وَلَا شَفَاعَةٌ»

“In it there is neither trade, nor friendship, nor intercession.”

Now Ayat al-Kursi states:

«مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ»

“Who can intercede before Him except by His permission?”

The first verse warns people against relying on bargaining, friendship, and intercession on the day of account; the second makes any intercession before God dependent on His permission. The possibility of authorized intercession cannot therefore be turned into a guarantee of salvation or a way to evade responsibility for one’s deeds.

This question in Ayat al-Kursi presents two important truths.

First, no being independently owns intercession.

Az-Zumar 39:44 also states:

«قُلْ لِلَّهِ الشَّفَاعَةُ جَمِيعًا»

“Say: all intercession belongs to The God.”

Second, immediately after stating the condition of permission, Ayat al-Kursi speaks of God’s encompassing knowledge.

This connection is very important.

In human relationships, someone may escape punishment, wrongfully obtain an entitlement, or change a judgment in their favor by bringing in an influential intermediary. This is sometimes possible because the judge does not know the whole truth, comes under pressure, or gives weight to personal connections.

But can any of these conditions be imagined concerning God?

God knows everything; no one can reveal a truth He does not know. Nor can any power impose something contrary to His knowledge and sovereignty.

This connection between intercession and divine knowledge is also clear in other verses.

In Ta-Ha 20:109, the benefit of intercession depends on the permission of the Entirely Merciful and His approval of the speech. Immediately afterward, Ta-Ha 20:110 states God’s encompassing knowledge and creatures’ inability to encompass Him in knowledge; and in Ta-Ha 20:111, faces bow before “الْحَيِّ الْقَيُّومِ”.

These three verses place together the same three truths found in Ayat al-Kursi: intercession dependent on permission, God’s encompassing knowledge, and His self-subsisting sustenance.

Al-Anbiya 21:28 also states:

«يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يَشْفَعُونَ إِلَّا لِمَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ»

“He knows what is between their hands and what is behind them, and they intercede only for one whom He approves.”

This verse likewise places intercession beside God’s encompassing knowledge and approval. An intercessor, if granted intercession, neither knows some truth hidden from God nor possesses independent authority against His judgment.

Intercession before The God therefore cannot resemble bargaining, personal recommendations, or influence over human judgment.

This does not deny every form of authorized intercession; rather, it denies an intercessor’s independence and arbitrary authority.

Nor has the Quran commanded people to seek intermediaries who guarantee their salvation. In Al-Baqarah 2:186, God presents Himself as near and responsive to His servants’ prayers. In Ghafir 40:60, He directly calls them to invoke Him.

The condition of permission for intercession therefore does not authorize constructing a system of necessary intermediaries between people and God.

In our separate study Intercession in the Quran, we have examined this subject in detail through the word’s root, good and bad intercession, permission, knowledge, right, individual responsibility, and divine patterns.

For understanding Ayat al-Kursi here, this truth is sufficient: no intercessor can possess independent power before the knowledge, justice, and sovereignty of The God.

7. God’s absolute sovereignty: a foundation for understanding freedom of faith

Ayat al-Kursi presents a comprehensive picture of the absolute sovereignty of The God. He is living and self-subsisting, sustaining all; everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him; no power or intercession is independent of His permission; His knowledge encompasses all; and preserving the heavens and the earth does not burden Him.

God’s absolute sovereignty does not, however, mean all creatures must lack choice and will. Within His sovereignty, God can give people the possibility of choice and responsibility without that choice creating an independent or rival power before Him.

This point becomes particularly important in the relationship between Ayat al-Kursi and the following verses.

Immediately after presenting such great power and sovereignty, the Quran states in Al-Baqarah 2:256:

«لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ»

“There is no compulsion in religion.”

The placement of these two verses beside each other shows that human freedom of choice does not lie outside God’s sovereignty; rather, it is discussed within the very domain of which The God is the creator and absolute sovereign.

A God who needs neither the faith nor the worship of His creatures also needs no forced profession of faith to prove His power. His sovereignty is neither limited by human choice nor abolished by human denial.

The absolute power of The God does not conflict with the possibility of real human choice; people can have choice and bear responsibility for their conduct within that same divine sovereignty.

Here Ayat al-Kursi provides a fundamental basis for understanding the two verses that follow it. Al-Baqarah 2:256 speaks of rejecting compulsion in religion, the clarity of sound guidance as distinct from error, and the choice between faith in God and taghut; Al-Baqarah 2:257 places divine guardianship and the guardianship of taghut in contrast.

The connection between freedom of faith, the possibility of rebellion, independent thought, will, and divine testing should be examined in the reflections on those two verses.

Here Ayat al-Kursi has clarified the foundation of that discussion: all power and sovereignty belong to The God, and human choice has meaning within this world and the domain of His sovereignty.

For the related Quranic principle, see the principle of no compulsion in religion.
