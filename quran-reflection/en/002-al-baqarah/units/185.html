Al-Baqarah 2:185

ۚ ۖ ۗ

The month of Ramadan, in which the Quran was sent down: guidance for people and clear proofs of guidance and discernment. Whoever among you witnesses the month, let them practice siyam during it; and whoever is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. The God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you, so that you may complete the prescribed count and magnify The God for the guidance He has given you, and so that you may give thanks.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:183, The God prescribed siyam for believers and identified attaining taqwa as its purpose. In Al-Baqarah 2:184, He spoke of “أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ” and set out the rulings concerning illness, travel, human endurance, and fidyah. Now, through “شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ”, He makes clear the period of siyam.

The numbered days of the preceding verse are identified here with Ramadan. Lunar months may have twenty-nine or thirty days, and the number of days of siyam depends on the duration of that particular month. Rather than setting a fixed number for every year, The God has made Ramadan itself the standard. The meaning of ma‘dudat, the variation in the number of days in lunar months, and a critique of interpretations restricting it to only a few days are discussed in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:184.

The God then says:

“فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ”

“Whoever among you witnesses the month, let them practice siyam during it.”

Shahida, from the root ش ه د, is connected with presence, observation, and witnessing. The God has connected the command of siyam with witnessing the month. Ramadan is an actual phenomenon in the cycle of the lunar month, and recognizing it is connected with the moon’s signs. Al-Baqarah 2:189 also identifies lunar crescents as time markers for people. The command therefore rests on recognizing and witnessing the month, rather than following the conventional timetables of other lands or commands people have added to divine rulings on their own authority.

But why does The God introduce Ramadan through the revelation of the Quran?

“شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ هُدًى لِلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَاتٍ مِنَ الْهُدَىٰ وَالْفُرْقَانِ”

“The month of Ramadan, in which the Quran was sent down: guidance for people and clear proofs of guidance and discernment.”

The Quran is a book of guidance for people. This guidance is not exclusive to a particular people, land, or religious school. Bayyinat, from the root ب ي ن, is connected with the truth becoming apparent and being made clear. The God sent down the Quran with clear proofs so that people could recognize the truth, reflect on its verses, and distinguish the right path from the wrong one.

Furqan is also one of the Quran’s distinguishing attributes. By His will, The God can grant people a furqan through which they distinguish truth from falsehood and good from evil, as He states in connection with taqwa in Al-Anfal 8:29.

A Book that The God describes as guidance and as containing clear proofs must therefore also be the standard for understanding religious rulings. People cannot first make their inherited understanding the standard and then, to preserve it, regard Quranic verses as incomplete, contradictory, or dependent on external rulings. Guidance guides a person when The God’s words govern their judgments, rather than human judgment ruling over His words.

After prescribing siyam during Ramadan, The God again addresses the circumstances of the sick and travelers:

“وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ”

“And whoever is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days.”

As clearly as The God prescribes siyam, He also states how to make it up in circumstances of illness and travel. The wisdom of this command becomes clearer in the continuation of the verse:

“يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ”

“The God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you, so that you may complete the prescribed count.”

This expression must be understood as a connected whole. The God wills ease and rejects hardship so that people can complete the prescribed count of siyam. Completing the count must be possible and practicable, rather than leaving people overwhelmed by an unbearable obligation. Providing ease opens the way to fulfilling the command; unjustified severity closes that same way.

Here the difference between the wisdom of The God’s command and the consequences of human interpretations becomes apparent.

In the third reading of Al-Baqarah 2:184, the command of siyam is consistent with people’s abilities and actual circumstances. Those able to do so practice siyam; sick people or travelers who can make it up do so on other days; and those unable to perform it because of chronic illness, enduring limitations, or overwhelming hardship benefit from fidyah. Such limitations are not confined to illness or old age. Someone who must work long hours in a coal mine or steelworks to support their family may not have the same capacity as someone in a cool and comfortable environment. People living in regions with very long days and nights or unusual climatic conditions also face genuine difficulties.

This is the provision of ease that makes fulfilling an obligation practicable. The God does not burden people beyond their ability or trap them between harming their bodies and disobeying a divine command. Al-Baqarah 2:286 also says, “لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا”—The God does not burden any soul beyond its capacity.

The first two readings of Al-Baqarah 2:184, however, face problems whose consequences run directly against this ease. When fidyah becomes an additional obligation for able people, or is first interpreted as a choice between siyam and feeding and then set aside through a claim of abrogation, no clear path remains for actual incapacity and overwhelming hardship.

Some proponents of juristic opinion and hadith, instead of reconsidering their interpretation and submitting to the solution stated in the Quran, have produced new rulings and fatwas from their own opinions to resolve the impasses caused by that interpretation. Sometimes they have said that people in northern regions should align their hours of siyam with Mecca or a neighboring country; sometimes they have required enduring very long days.

But where is the authority for these commands? In which verse has The God said to adjust the timing of siyam to the clocks of Mecca or a neighboring country? Why should we set aside the means of compensation stated in the Quran itself through a claim of abrogation or a restrictive interpretation, and then construct a parallel religious law to solve the resulting problems?

These difficulties are not merely the result of disagreement over one ruling. They return to a larger question: the place of The God’s knowledge and wisdom in relation to human opinion.

Was The God, who created the earth, the movement of the moon and sun, the changing seasons, the lengths of days and nights, and the climatic conditions throughout the world, unaware of His servants’ lives in different lands? Are we to imagine that He did not know the circumstances of the chronically ill, people with limited capacity, workers enduring hardship, and inhabitants of polar regions?

Has the Quran, which The God sent down as guidance with clear proofs, remained incomplete in explaining the solution to such circumstances, leaving proponents of opinion and transmitted reports to complete it with their fatwas? And must we create contradictions between The God’s verses and then resort to the theory of abrogating and abrogated rulings in order to defend a human interpretation?

What kind of magnifying of The God is this, which diminishes His knowledge and wisdom and puts a human-made ruling in place of His command?

The God is knowing and fully aware. He knows His servants’ circumstances and has stated siyam itself, the means of making it up, and the ruling on fidyah in this very passage. Hardship and impasses arise when people set some of His commands aside in favor of their inherited interpretation instead of understanding them together.

Thus, “وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ” is the outcome of that very provision of ease: The God wants fulfillment of the command to be possible so that people can complete the prescribed count, rather than be left unable to do so through illness, necessity, weakness, or physical harm. This is the fundamental difference between the easy and practicable path stated in the Quran and severity arising from inconsistent interpretations. A detailed critique of the three readings, claims of abrogation, human-made fatwas, and people’s actual difficulties appears in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:184.

The God then says:

“وَلِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ”

“And so that you may magnify The God for the guidance He has given you.”

Takbir, from the root ك ب ر, is connected with greatness and magnifying. In this verse, magnifying The God is connected with His guidance. The same expression appears in Al-Hajj 22:37, where, after stating that neither the meat nor the blood of sacrifice reaches Him, but rather people’s taqwa, The God speaks of magnifying Him for His guidance.

Magnifying The God is not merely uttering words about His greatness. Those who magnify Him should also give His knowledge, wisdom, justice, and command precedence over their own desires and opinions. We cannot speak of The God’s greatness while behaving as though He were unaware of His servants’ circumstances or had fallen short in explaining their rulings.

Truly magnifying The God means making His guidance the standard, recognizing the wisdom of His commands, and following the path He Himself has stated instead of adding obligations without evidence.

At the end of the verse, He says:

“وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ”

“And so that you may give thanks.”

Shukr, from the root ش ك ر, is not confined to saying “thanks be to God.” Gratitude means recognizing the value of a blessing received and making the effect of that recognition apparent in one’s conduct.

Guidance is a great blessing, but gratitude for it becomes meaningful when people follow it. When The God opens a path of ease and compensation alongside a command of worship, gratitude for this guidance means accepting that wisdom and mercy, rather than disregarding it by imposing unjustified hardship on ourselves and others. The connection between gratitude, recognizing blessings, and practical human response is examined in detail in the article “Gratitude in the Quran: From Saying ‘Thanks Be to God’ to Responding Responsibly to Blessings”.

Thus, the beginning and end of this passage are connected. In Al-Baqarah 2:183, siyam was prescribed for attaining taqwa; in Al-Baqarah 2:184, the means of compensation and consideration for human endurance were stated; and now, in Al-Baqarah 2:185, The God identifies Ramadan, presents the Quran as guidance with clear proofs and discernment, wills ease so that completing the prescribed count is possible, and calls people to magnify Him and give thanks for His guidance.

Siyam in the Quran is a path to taqwa, not a competition in enduring suffering and harm. The God, who knows all His servants’ circumstances, has opened a path of ease and compensation alongside the command of worship so that fulfilling the obligation is possible. Magnifying Him means giving His command precedence over human opinion; gratitude for His guidance means recognizing the truth made clear, following it, and not concealing His wisdom and mercy beneath rulings of our own making.
