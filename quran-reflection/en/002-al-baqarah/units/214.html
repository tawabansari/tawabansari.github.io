Al-Baqarah 2:214

ۖ ۗ

Did you suppose that you would enter the Garden while the like of what befell those before you has not yet come upon you? Hardship and suffering befell them, and they were so shaken that the messenger and those who believed with him said: When will the help of The God arrive? Be aware that the help of The God is near.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:213, people differed; God sent the prophets and the Book to judge in truth, yet some recipients of the Book differed over it out of transgression even after clear evidence had reached them. By contrast, those who believed were guided, by God’s permission, to the truth under dispute. Now He addresses the believers themselves:

“أَمْ حَسِبْتُمْ أَنْ تَدْخُلُوا الْجَنَّةَ”

“Did you suppose that you would enter the Garden?”

This question breaks down the notion of guaranteed salvation through religious affiliation. Muhammad’s followers may reproach Jews and Christians for disagreeing over the Book, concealing truth, and blindly following leaders; but are they themselves exempt from the same test?

The Quran does not recount the history of earlier people to taunt them; it exposes their errors so that we do not take the same path.

In Al-Baqarah 2:111, the claim that the Garden belongs exclusively to Jews or Christians was called “amani”—wishful hopes—and in Al-Baqarah 2:112, the criterion returned to submission to God and doing good. An-Nisa 4:123 also states plainly: “لَيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ وَلَا أَمَانِيِّ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ”—neither your wishful hopes nor those of the People of the Book.

This same double standard is examined in the study “Blind to Our Own Faults, Keenly Aware of Others’”.

“وَلَمَّا يَأْتِكُمْ مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ خَلَوْا مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ”

“While the like of what befell those before you has not yet come upon you.”

The test faced by earlier people was not merely receiving the Book and hearing words of truth; they also had to choose their path amid disagreements and pressures.

The same test confronts Muhammad’s followers. If the Quran’s clear words conflict with their inherited beliefs, religious prejudices, or interests, are they willing to accept the truth and turn back from their error?

In the preceding verse, the believers’ guidance was “bi-idhnihi”—by His permission. We have examined this permission in the separate study “Divine Permission in the Quran: The Laws and Established Ways Governing Creation”. Guidance within the framework of divine ways is connected with reason, fairness, abandoning discrimination and arrogance, accepting truth, and submitting to The God, rather than with a denomination’s name or a claim to faith.

How can someone who sets truth aside to preserve power, reputation, or ancestral beliefs reach guidance through the very path they themselves resist?

“مَسَّتْهُمُ الْبَأْسَاءُ وَالضَّرَّاءُ وَزُلْزِلُوا”

“Hardship and suffering befell them, and they were severely shaken.”

“Ba’sa” speaks of severe hardship and distress, “darra” of suffering and harm, and “zulzilu” of a shaking that disrupts a person’s stability and peace.

Earlier people did not face intellectual disagreement alone. Accepting and holding to truth sometimes brought hostility, deprivation, threats, and danger to their lives. In Al-Ahzab 33:11, the Quran likewise speaks of the believers being severely tested and shaken in the face of threats.

“حَتَّىٰ يَقُولَ الرَّسُولُ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَعَهُ مَتَىٰ نَصْرُ اللَّهِ”

“Until the messenger and those who believed with him said: When will the help of The God arrive?”

The pressure was so heavy that even the messenger and the believers with him asked when God’s help would arrive. The Quran does not conceal the difficulty of their path. Faith does not make people immune to suffering and anxiety.

“أَلَا إِنَّ نَصْرَ اللَّهِ قَرِيبٌ”

“Be aware that the help of The God is near.”

This answer keeps hope alive amid hardship. A difficult path does not mean that God has abandoned a person.

The message of these two verses together is clear: the Book came to judge in truth. Some differed out of transgression after receiving clear evidence, while others were guided by accepting truth. Muhammad’s followers are measured by this same criterion, not by their name and claims.

The Quran is a mirror through which we may see the errors of earlier people in our own conduct and correct them. The Garden is not guaranteed by pride in a Book or membership in a community. What matters is a person’s response to truth: do they set aside pride and prejudice and submit to it, or repeat the very errors for which they have reproached others?
