Al-Baqarah 2:45

ۚ

And seek help through sabr and salat; and indeed it is great and weighty, except upon the khashi'in.

Our Reflection

This verse comes directly after the verse that exposed the gap between speech and action: you command people to birr, but forget yourselves while you recite the Book. So verse 45 is the healing response to that same crisis. The Qur'an does not only show the illness; it also shows the path out of it: seek help through sabr and salat.

The first word is isti'anah, seeking help. It means that the human being admits that relying on the self, position, religious memory, pulpit, rank, people, knowledge, or social power is not enough on its own. But the verse specifies the path of this help-seeking: bis-sabri was-salah, through sabr and salat. Help is fundamentally from God, as the Fatihah has already made clear: iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in, You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help. So sabr and salat are not independent sources of help. They are the Qur'anic path and channel for receiving help from The One worshiped.

This point is very important. The Qur'an does not refer the human being to fabricated intercessors, sanctified mediators, scholars and monks made into religious classes, the dead, people of rank, or religious hierarchies. The human being does not transfer his responsibility to others. Isti'anah is from God, but the human being must stand in the field of sabr and salat in order to receive this help. So the verse calls neither to self-sufficiency nor to mediator-worship. It calls to tawhidic help-seeking through sabr and salat.

Sabr in the Qur'an is not merely silent endurance of pain. In its root and material image, sabr is tied to holding, restraining, controlling, and confining the self in a position. In this context, sabr means holding the fleeing and interest-seeking self at the point of truth. The one who commands people to birr but has forgotten himself must restrain his own self before reforming others. Sabr means restraining oneself from escape, justification, superiority, bargaining with the signs, and forgetting responsibility.

This sabr is not silence before oppression or passivity. Sabr means firmness in truth. It means that when truth comes against one's own interest, the person does not flee from it. When he recites a Book that first addresses himself, he does not throw it only toward people. When the verse exposes the gap between his speech and his action, he stands before it and does not flee from reforming the self.

Alongside sabr, the Qur'an places as-salah. In verse 43, the command came to establish salat; here salat enters the path of isti'anah. Salat is not only an outward duty. It is the field of return, being made upright, and standing again before the Lord. In one layer of the root-image, salat may also be reflected upon through the image of straightening and being placed in a field of correction, like a crooked piece of wood becoming straight under heat. In this view, salat places the self that has bent away from truth before the heat of revelation, remembrance, and God's presence so that it may become straight again.

This connection with verse 44 is clear. In verse 44, the problem was that a human being recites the Book, commands people to birr, but forgets himself. That means a gap has appeared between Book and self, speech and action, recitation and responsibility. Salat, if it is living and established, exposes this very gap. Salat returns the human being to the presence of God; and in that presence, the human being cannot easily place himself outside the judgment of the Book.

The combination of sabr and salat in this verse is very precise. Sabr holds the self at the point of truth, and salat straightens that self before God. Sabr without salat may become dry pressure, moral pride, or directionless endurance. Salat without sabr may become movement and wording without effect. Sabr is the field of holding; salat is the field of return and becoming upright before the Lord.

Then the verse says: wa-innaha lakabirah. This phrase requires precision. The pronoun ha is feminine singular. Grammatically, it may return to salat, because salat is feminine; and it may return to the whole process of isti'anah, meaning this path of seeking help through sabr and salat. Therefore, we should not carelessly say that the Qur'an describes both words with a dual form. The subtlety of the verse is precisely that it expresses the weight of this path, or the weight of real salat, with a feminine singular expression.

God has not said that this path is only “hard” or “impossible.” He says kabirah. The root K B R is tied to greatness, magnitude, weight, and heaviness. So the issue is not only practical difficulty. It is existential weight. Real salat, or the process of isti'anah through sabr and salat, is great because it brings the human being down from slogans, religious memory, and commanding others, and makes him face his own self.

For the self that sees itself as great, this path feels great and heavy. Someone who is used to commanding people while placing himself outside the command finds real salat heavy, because he must come down before God. Someone who recites the Book for others but does not want the Book to judge him finds salat great, because it transfers recitation from the tongue to the self. Someone who has used rank, pulpit, power, or the name of religion to protect himself finds isti'anah through sabr and salat great, because he can no longer hide behind his position.

Here the difference between being great and being impossible becomes clear. Every great matter does not close the path; rather, it asks capacity from the human being. Salat and Qur'anic isti'anah are great because they deal with the truth of the human being. This path does not take the self lightly. It compels the human being to see whether he is truly seeking help from God, or using the name of God to preserve his own position.

The end of the verse says: illa 'ala al-khashi'in, except upon the khashi'in. Khushu' is not only fear. Khushu' means inward lowering, softening of the heart, breaking of self-magnification, and becoming still before the greatness of truth. A khashi' is someone who has inwardly come down before God. He does not see himself as the center, does not use the Book to control people, and does not stand before truth in resistance and arrogance.

For the non-khashi', salat and this path of isti'anah are great and heavy because he must bring his self down from the center. But for the khashi', this same path becomes a means of help. The khashi' does not see sabr as humiliation; he understands it as holding the self in truth. The khashi' does not see salat as a heavy custom; he understands it as standing in the field of divine correction. Because the khashi' has already softened before God, salat does not break him; it straightens him.

From this angle, verse 45 is the natural continuation of the criticism in verse 44. The one who commands people to birr and forgets himself is not healed by speaking more, commanding more, or strengthening his position. His healing is to seek help from God, but through the path that the Qur'an has shown: sabr and salat. Sabr restrains the self, salat straightens the self before God, and khushu' makes that weight bearable.

So verse 45 shows the path of reform for elites and for all human beings. Isti'anah is from God, not from fabricated mediators. Its path is sabr and salat, not escape from responsibility. And this path is great and weighty except upon the khashi'in, because only the one who has lowered himself before God can see the weight of self-reform as a path of salvation, not as a threat to his pride.

Cross-References
  • Al-Fatihah 1:5— “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help”; the tawhidic foundation of isti'anah in the Qur'an.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:44— The direct background of the verse: the gap between commanding people to birr and forgetting oneself while reciting the Book.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:46— The explanation of the khashi'in: those who hold that they will meet their Lord and return to Him.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:153— Repeating the command to seek help through sabr and salat; God is with the sabirin.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:177— The connection between birr, faith, giving, salat, zakat, fulfilling covenant, and sabr; a wider context for understanding sabr in Qur'anic ethics.
  • Aal Imran 3:200— The command to have sabr, stand firm, remain stationed, and have taqwa; showing sabr as a force of individual and collective steadfastness.
  • Hud 11:114-115— Establishing salat at the two ends of the day and part of the night, and the command to have sabr; a direct connection between salat and sabr in human reform.
  • Al-Ankabut 29:45— Salat restrains the human being from indecency and what is rejected; showing salat as a force of practical reform.
  • Luqman 31:17— Establishing salat, commanding what is recognized as right, forbidding what is rejected as wrong, and having sabr over what comes; linking salat, social responsibility, and sabr.
  • Al-Mu'minun 23:1-2— The success of the believers and their khushu' in salat; an important witness for understanding “the khashi'in.”
  • Al-Hadid 57:16— Has the time not come for the hearts of those who have faith to become khashi' before God's remembrance and the truth that has been sent down?
