Al-Baqarah 2:177

ۖ ۗ ۖ

Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward east and west; rather, righteousness is in one who believes in The God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets, and gives wealth, despite loving it, to relatives, orphans, those in need, stranded travelers, those who ask, and for freeing people in bondage, and establishes salat and gives zakat; and those who fulfill their covenant whenever they make one, and the steadfast in poverty, suffering, and battle. Those are the ones who have been truthful, and those are the mindful of God.

Our Reflection

What makes a person righteous: the direction they face in worship, or the way their faith becomes visible in their life and conduct? Following verses about the qiblah and the orientation of religious communities, the Qur’an states the criterion of righteousness clearly: “لَيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ”. Righteousness does not consist merely in turning one’s face toward east or west. A person may observe the direction of worship correctly while neglecting others’ rights. Outward orientation alone does not bring the reality of righteousness into a person’s life.

This connects with Al-Baqarah 2:148, where the Qur’an, after mentioning different orientations, calls people to compete in good deeds. It now describes the righteous person’s qualities. “Birr,” from the root B R R, refers to goodness and upright conduct and encompasses a range of human beliefs and actions in this verse. Righteousness is not merely one good deed or one act of worship; it is a reality that should become visible in different aspects of life.

There is also a subtle point in the verse’s structure. After “وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ”, the Qur’an does not simply say that righteousness consists of performing several particular acts. It says “مَنْ آمَنَ”: one who believes. Righteousness is thus presented through a person who realizes it in life. Faith, generosity, worship, fidelity to commitments, and steadfastness are not separate, unrelated qualities; together they form the righteous person’s character.

Belief in The God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets forms the foundation of this righteousness. Through belief in God, a person recognizes responsibility before the Lord; through belief in the Last Day, they do not regard life and conduct as without accountability or ultimate consequences. Belief in angels, the Book, and prophets also expresses acceptance of divine guidance beyond one’s personal knowledge and experience. The Qur’an speaks of “the Book” and “the prophets,” without restricting faith to the name of one prophet or one community. The same principle appears in Al-Baqarah 2:136 and Al-Baqarah 2:285, which describe faith in the divine messengers without distinguishing between them.

Such faith should affect conduct. For this reason, giving wealth is mentioned alongside belief: “وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ”. People love wealth because it enables them to meet their own and their family’s needs and plan for the future. Yet righteousness becomes apparent when attachment to possessions does not prevent them from seeing others’ needs. Someone who gives from beloved wealth shows that possessions do not rule their conscience and responsibility.

This connection is also apparent in Ali Imran 3:92, where the Qur’an connects attaining birr with spending from what one loves. In Al-Insan 76:8, feeding the needy, the orphan, and the captive is likewise accompanied by “عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ”. Giving reveals its value when a person relinquishes something desirable and valuable to themselves so that another’s need may be met. Righteousness is not only giving what is left over in our lives; sometimes it means parting with something we too love.

The Qur’an also specifies the recipients: relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask, and people in bondage. This list extends responsibility from the closest family relationships to people one may not know personally. A relative in need, an orphaned child, someone whose livelihood is insufficient, a traveler needing help along the way, and someone forced to make their need known all fall within the righteous person’s concern.

“وَفِي الرِّقَابِ” is particularly significant here. Riqab is the plural of raqabah, neck, and in this usage refers to people held in slavery. Helping to free them is not merely providing food or money; releasing a human being from bondage is itself included among acts of righteousness. Moral responsibility therefore extends beyond immediate needs: the freedom of someone deprived of it also has value and importance.

Generosity in this verse is not a passing emotion but conduct that should respond to people’s actual needs. Someone who uses wealth to display magnanimity while neglecting orphans and the needy should reconsider the reality of their righteousness. Surah Al-Maun also condemns driving away the orphan and indifference to feeding the needy alongside neglect of salat, ostentation, and withholding help. This connection shows that treatment of those in need cannot be separated from claims of faith and worship.

Establishing salat and giving zakat then appear alongside these responsibilities: “وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ”. Worship and social beneficence are not separate in this verse. A person who stands in worship before God is also responsible in their relations with His servants. Salat and zakat have their place, but neither replaces generosity, care for those in need, or fidelity to commitments.

The separate mention of “وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ” and “وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ” is also noteworthy. The Qur’an describes giving wealth to specified groups and names zakat independently. This distinction indicates that responsibility toward others’ needs should not be reduced to making one specified payment. Giving from beloved wealth and giving zakat are both qualities of the righteous, and each should be understood in its own place.

Righteousness in the Qur’an is not solely an individual responsibility either. In Al-Maidah 5:2, God commands people to cooperate in birr and mindfulness of Him. Someone attentive to others’ needs can also work with others to alleviate deprivation, support those harmed, and establish just relationships. Such a society does not emerge through shared declarations of faith alone; its members’ righteous conduct must affect people’s lives.

Fidelity to commitments is inseparable from this reality: “وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا”. A person may be generous yet deceive in transactions, or worship yet break an accepted commitment. The Qur’an does not judge such a person solely by their giving or worship. A covenant is a responsibility that one undertakes toward another by accepting it. Family commitments, financial contracts, entrusted property, and social covenants are all situations in which truthfulness and fidelity become apparent.

If someone makes a promise in comfort but refuses to fulfill it in difficulty, what is that promise worth? If someone speaks in religion’s name but violates the rights of a partner, worker, or person who trusted them, how can they claim righteousness through acts of worship alone? Fidelity to commitments is a pillar of trust between people. A society in which covenants lose their value moves away from security and justice, even amid fine words about morality.

Fidelity becomes more apparent in difficult times. The Qur’an speaks of “وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ”: those steadfast in poverty, suffering, and battle. Ba’sa is associated with hardship and straitened circumstances, darra with harm and suffering, and hina al-ba’s with intense conflict and battle. These are situations in which a person may experience fear, loss, or pressure, precisely when commitment to faith, covenants, and responsibility becomes more difficult.

Patience in these circumstances means remaining steady on the right path. Someone who helps those in need during abundance faces a different test when they themselves experience poverty. Someone faithful to a covenant may suffer loss in keeping it. A person facing aggression and danger must also not turn away from rights and responsibilities in the most difficult circumstances. Righteousness remains firm when a person is faithful to sound principles in both comfort and hardship.

In the Arabic structure, “وَالْمُوفُونَ” is nominative, while “وَالصَّابِرِينَ” is accusative. In Arabic grammar, this change can be understood as the accusative of praise, a construction used to highlight a quality. Steadfastness in hardship thereby receives particular prominence at the end of the righteous person’s qualities without being detached from the others.

The verse’s conclusion gathers all these qualities into two short statements: “أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا” and “أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ”.

Why does the Qur’an call these people truthful? Truthfulness is not merely saying something that corresponds to fact; the truth of a person’s claim is also known through conduct. Someone who believes in God and expresses this faith through generosity, worship, fidelity, and steadfastness is sincere in that claim. What they have said has also become visible in their life. In Al-Maidah 5:119, the Qur’an likewise speaks of the truthful benefiting from their truthfulness on the Day of Judgment, when the validity of a person’s claim will not be separated from the reality of their deeds.

After truthfulness comes the description “al-muttaqun.” Mindfulness of God in this verse is not limited to avoiding a few prohibited acts or observing outward forms of worship. The Qur’an identifies as mindful those whose faith is joined with responsibility, who give others what they love, do not overlook the rights of those in need, establish salat, give zakat, keep their commitments, and do not abandon the right path in hardship.

These two concluding judgments illuminate the meaning of the entire verse. The Qur’an begins by rejecting the restriction of righteousness to turning one’s face and ends by identifying truthful and mindful people. Between this beginning and end, it depicts a life in which faith becomes visible in relations with God, wealth, family, those in need, society, covenants, and life’s trials.

Righteousness is not merely turning one’s face in a direction; it is faith proving true in one’s actions and conduct. Someone who worships before the Lord but turns away from those in need, breaks commitments, or abandons right in hardship should reflect on the relationship between their claim to faith and their actual behavior. The Qur’an has made the criterion clear: those whose faith and conduct are in harmony are the truthful, and they are the mindful.
