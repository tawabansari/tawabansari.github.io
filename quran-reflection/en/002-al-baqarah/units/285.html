Al-Baqarah 2:285

ۚ ۚ ۖ

The Messenger has believed in what was sent down to him from his Lord, and so have the believers; all have believed in The God, His angels, His Books, and His Messengers; we make no distinction among any of His Messengers. And they said: We have heard and obeyed; we seek Your forgiveness, our Lord, and to You is the return.

Our Reflection

In the closing verses of Surah Al-Baqarah, after many commands concerning faith, justice, human rights, and responsibility before God, the Quran recalls the foundations of faith. This verse is one of the Quran’s clearest statements about believing in all divine Messengers and avoiding discrimination among them.

The faith of the Messenger and the believers in divine revelation

“آمَنَ الرَّسُولُ بِمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ رَبِّهِ وَالْمُؤْمِنُونَ”

“The Messenger has believed in what was sent down to him from his Lord, and so have the believers.”

The Quran first states the Messenger’s own faith. Muhammad is not merely commissioned to convey revelation to others; he himself believes in what has been sent down to him from his Lord. The believers are then placed alongside him, because they too must accept the same truth in which the Messenger believes.

This connects with Al-Baqarah 2:4, which identifies belief in what was sent down to Muhammad and what was sent down before him as a characteristic of believers. Quranic faith is therefore not confined to accepting the revelation of one era or one community; it also encompasses the continuity of divine guidance.

“كُلٌّ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَمَلَائِكَتِهِ وَكُتُبِهِ وَرُسُلِهِ”

“All have believed in The God, His angels, His Books, and His Messengers.”

“كُلٌّ” emphasizes the inclusiveness of this faith. The Messenger and the believers all believe in God, His angels, His Books, and His Messengers.

The plural forms “كُتُبِهِ” and “رُسُلِهِ” deserve attention. Belief in divine revelation is not confined to one Book and one Messenger. The Books and Messengers are different, but the source of their missions is one: The God.

For a broader discussion, see “What Is Iman in the Quran, and How Was Its Meaning Distorted?”.

Not discriminating among the Messengers: a foundation of faith

“لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْ رُسُلِهِ”

“We make no distinction among any of His Messengers.”

“نُفَرِّقُ,” from the root F R Q in Form II, refers to separating and making distinctions. In this negative construction, “أَحَدٍ” leaves none of the Messengers outside this principle.

The Quran does not merely command acceptance of the missions of all divine Messengers; it also restrains believers from treating them discriminatorily.

But does this mean that all Messengers are equal in every respect—in rank, ability, and distinction?

No. In Al-Baqarah 2:253, God explicitly says:

“تِلْكَ الرُّسُلُ فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ”

“Those Messengers—We favored some of them over others.”

In that verse, we examined how God granted particular Messengers special distinctions, abilities, and responsibilities, from speaking with Moses to the clear proofs given to Jesus and his special support through the Holy Spirit. God Himself has stated these differences.

Yet God’s bestowal of distinction is not the same as human discrimination. Determining the Messengers’ standing and distinction belongs to God, not to their followers. We have no right, on the basis of affection, ethnic prejudice, or religious affiliation, to invent ranks for our favored Messenger that God has not attributed to him, or to declare him superior to all other Messengers in a human competition.

For example, the Quran presents Abraham as a leader for humankind (Al-Baqarah 2:124), tells of God speaking with Moses (An-Nisa 4:164), describes Jesus as receiving signs and special support (Al-Baqarah 2:253), and calls Muhammad’s mission a mercy to the worlds (Al-Anbiya 21:107). We accept all these distinctions because God has stated them. But these verses do not allow us to construct a complete table ranking all the Messengers according to our own choices.

This principle is examined in detail in “The Principle of Believing in All Messengers without Discrimination” and is also among the foundations presented in Forqan’s “Introduction to the Quran”.

How did discrimination among the Messengers lead to division among their followers?

This command matters not only for respecting the Messengers, but also for preventing a major source of religious division and hostility.

In Al-Baqarah 2:91, the Quran speaks of people who, when invited to believe in divine revelation, answered:

“نُؤْمِنُ بِمَا أُنْزِلَ عَلَيْنَا”

“We believe in what was sent down to us.”

They made their attachment to earlier revelation a reason to reject another revelation. The problem was not their respect for Moses and the divine Book, but their confinement of truth to their own ethnic and religious affiliation. In the same surah, the Quran also speaks of earlier groups denying and even killing some Messengers (Al-Baqarah 2:87).

Among some followers of Jesus, love and belief in his special standing likewise expanded into attributing divinity to him—an attribution criticized by the Quran in Al-Maidah 5:72.

But the more pressing question concerns us, the followers of Muhammad.

We have often criticized those who preferred their own Messenger over others, treated revelation as their community’s monopoly, or became prejudiced concerning other Messengers. But have we not fallen into the same conduct ourselves?

Among some Muslims, Muhammad is described by titles such as “master of the Prophets,” “the greatest Prophet,” or even “the purpose of the creation of the world,” although the Quran does not state these titles for him. Sometimes love for Muhammad becomes such an exclusive measure of faith and respect that other Messengers, despite their divine missions, do not in practice receive the same attention and respect.

If we criticize followers of Moses or Jesus for claiming superiority for their Messenger, yet repeat the same claims for Muhammad on the basis of reports, religious habits, or personal affection, what difference remains between our conduct and the conduct we condemn in others?

This verse should not simply be read and passed over; it must be practiced in our lives. We habitually celebrate Muhammad’s birth as “Mawlid,” hold Quran-recitation gatherings, give charity, and arrange commemorations, yet at the same time reject and declare forbidden Christmas, associated with the birth of Jesus Christ. If Muhammad and Jesus are both Messengers of The God, why do we regard commemorating one birth as good and commemorating the other as forbidden? Is it fitting for someone enthusiastically to hold recitation gatherings, give charity, and arrange commemorations for Mawlid, yet be unwilling to do something similar with the same enthusiasm for Christmas, and even condemn its celebration? Is this not the very discrimination from which the Quran restrains us through “لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْ رُسُلِهِ”?

Respect and love for Muhammad do not conflict with respect for Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and God’s other Messengers. Each should be recognized in the position God has stated for him—neither less nor more.

The consequences of these prejudices have not been limited to differences of opinion. When followers turn the Messengers’ missions into symbols of their own people’s and community’s superiority, acceptance of truth may give way to religious rivalry. In such an atmosphere, one group regards itself as the sole true heir of guidance and belittles or excludes another. These divisions can lead to hostility, social discrimination, and even war.

In Al-Baqarah 2:253 itself, after describing the Messengers’ distinctions, the Quran recalls that people after them disagreed and fought one another despite receiving clear proofs.

Likewise, in Ash-Shura 42:13, God speaks of the one religion enjoined upon Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad, commanding them to establish it and not divide within it.

Thus, differences in the Messengers’ names, the times of their missions, and certain religious practices must not become hostility toward the followers of other divine Messengers. Guidance has one source, although the missions were conveyed at different times and under different circumstances.

This principle connects directly with avoiding sectarian division in religion and the unity of religion and diversity of prescribed ways.

This criticism concerns discriminatory conduct; it does not treat all Jews, Christians, or Muslims as sharing one outlook and manner of acting. Each person is responsible for the truth they have recognized and the conduct they have chosen, not merely the name of the community to which they belong.

Hearing, obeying, and seeking forgiveness

“وَقَالُوا سَمِعْنَا وَأَطَعْنَا”

“And they said: We have heard and obeyed.”

“سَمِعْنَا,” from the root S M ʿ, concerns hearing, and “أَطَعْنَا,” from Ṭ W ʿ, concerns obeying.

Faith is not merely accepting the name of God and His Messengers; it must also become evident in a person’s response to the divine command.

This expression clearly contrasts with Al-Baqarah 2:93, where the Quran quotes a group saying, “سَمِعْنَا وَعَصَيْنَا”—we have heard and disobeyed.

In this verse, the believers say that they have heard and obeyed. A person therefore cannot claim faith in all the Messengers while knowingly acting against God’s clear command concerning nondiscrimination.

It then says:

“غُفْرَانَكَ رَبَّنَا وَإِلَيْكَ الْمَصِيرُ”

“We seek Your forgiveness, our Lord, and to You is the return.”

Despite their faith and declaration of obedience, the believers still ask their Lord for forgiveness. This reminder matters because faith does not free a person from error, stumbling, or the need for reform. A true believer should not become so proud of their faith that they belittle others or consider themselves beyond the need to reconsider their beliefs and conduct.

“الْمَصِيرُ” also recalls return and final outcome. People’s ultimate destiny belongs to God, not to the followers of one Messenger, religious leaders, or a community that imagines itself superior to others.

The message of this verse is that faith in God also encompasses faith in all His Messengers, and no believer has the right to discriminate among them out of ethnic or religious prejudice. We accept the distinctions God has granted the Messengers, but inventing new ranks and ordering them according to our preferences is not ours to do. If we repeat for our own Messenger the same claims of superiority that we condemn in other Messengers’ followers, we have departed from the principle “لَا نُفَرِّقُ.” True faith should draw a person toward accepting truth, avoiding division, obeying God, and humility before Him.
