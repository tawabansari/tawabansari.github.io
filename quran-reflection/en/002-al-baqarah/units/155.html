Al-Baqarah 2:155

ۗ

We will certainly test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and crops; and give good news to those who remain steadfast.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:153, God called believers to seek help through patience and prayer and promised to be with the patient. The next verse spoke of those killed in God’s way, whose existence is not confined to bodily life. Now the Qur’an describes trials encountered in this worldly life: fear, hunger, and losses of wealth, lives, and crops. Together, these verses show that God’s companionship does not make the patient immune to difficulty, but neither does difficulty determine the whole reality of their life and final outcome.

The verse opens emphatically: “We will certainly test you.” The root B L W concerns testing and bringing the quality of human action and response to light. Life is more than a field for receiving favors; it is also a field of choice and responsibility. Al-Mulk 67:2 connects the creation of death and life with testing the goodness of human conduct. Al-Anbiya 21:35 speaks of testing people through both adversity and good. Divine testing therefore occurs not only in poverty, illness, and calamity: wealth, health, power, and ease can also be tests. See The Qur’anic Root B L W for closer examination.

One subtle feature is the difference between “something of fear and hunger” and “a loss of wealth, lives, and crops.” First comes some measure of fear and hunger, then a decrease in wealth, lives, and produce. Grammatically, “loss” may be read in connection with “something” or with the preceding terms, so the meaning should not be confined to one syntactic possibility. Yet the difference between the subjects is real and worth considering: fear and hunger are conditions, while loss concerns a reduction in something that existed.

Fear can subside when danger passes and security returns. Hunger can end when food and sustenance arrive. If prolonged or severe, either condition can of course cause harm that is difficult or even impossible to repair. In themselves, however, fear and hunger are conditions capable of ending or changing. Loss of wealth, lives, and crops has another dimension. Destroyed property, a ruined harvest, or a person who has died is not the same as a feeling of fear or hunger. Something itself may have been lost.

We recognize this distinction in life. Someone may fear the future for a time and later feel safe, or be hungry and then eat. But if an orchard burns, that season’s harvest does not return when the fire ends. If a person dies, another person’s arrival does not replace that individual in the survivors’ lives. Wealth may be acquired again, another orchard may bear fruit, or God may grant something better, but compensation does not always mean the return of the very thing lost. Even where the same property can be recovered, the distinction between ending a condition and compensating for a loss remains significant. We should not make unsupported declarations about how or when God compensates someone. God knows best.

“Something of” also deserves attention. It connects the trial with some of the named things rather than depicting the entirety of human life as fear, hunger, and deprivation. But it does not establish that every calamity is slight in intensity or duration. For someone losing a loved one, or a community facing widespread hunger, suffering can be immense. The verse does not license minimizing others’ pain. Life involves trials, but the Qur’an does not reduce all life to suffering and deprivation.

Fear and hunger concern security and livelihood, two basic human needs. The verse then mentions losses of wealth, lives, and fruits. Wealth includes property and financial resources; lives here refers to people whose lives may be lost; fruits concerns yields and produce, especially plant and agricultural products. Reduced harvests can endanger a community’s food security and economic life. See The Qur’anic Root TH M R for the word’s semantic field.

But is every fear, hunger, or shortage caused by the sufferer’s personal sin? If a righteous person falls into poverty, illness, or calamity, must we conclude that God is displeased with them? The Qur’an gives us no such simplistic rule. Humans are social beings living in a world where others’ actions affect their fate. Someone may be righteous yet suffer from corruption in the society around them.

Suppose a group clears vast forests for profit. In the same society, someone has spent years warning about forest protection, clean air and water, and future generations’ rights. They have written, protested, and tried to prevent destruction, but the destroyers’ economic and political power has prevailed. Eventually forests disappear, soil is damaged, air quality declines, and people and animals are endangered. That righteous person breathes the same air and suffers the consequences.

Should pollution have no effect on their body because they are righteous? Or does their illness establish that God is punishing a sin? Both conclusions are mistaken. When pollution enters the respiratory system, the material world’s laws do not distinguish the forest’s destroyer from its defender. Moral responsibility belongs to those causing the destruction, but its material consequences can also affect people who played no part in it.

Ar-Rum 30:41 connects corruption appearing on land and sea with what human hands have done. Al-Baqarah 2:205 places the destruction of crops and offspring within corruption on earth. Al-Anfal 8:25 warns of a trial whose harm is not confined to wrongdoers. These verses show that conduct has real consequences, and social harm can extend beyond its perpetrators. See Why Is Human Corruption Only on Earth? for further discussion.

A fundamental question follows: if many losses result from human conduct, why does God say “We test you”? Does attributing the trial to God remove the oppressor’s responsibility?

Understanding this requires attention to divine ways and the relationship between human choice and its consequences. The Qur’an does not present the world as a collection of lawless events. God establishes creation through measures, causes, and laws, within which people live and choose. Fatir 35:43 speaks of the constancy of God’s way, while Ar-Ra‘d 13:11 connects changes in a people’s condition with changes within themselves. These ways concern more than nature: conduct, choice, and social consequences also belong within the divine order.

At-Taghabun 64:11 connects calamity with God’s permission. But permission for an event to occur is not identical to moral approval of its perpetrator. God created a world where fire burns, behavior has effects, and wrongful choices can harm others. Someone who deliberately burns another’s house cannot claim innocence because fire burns according to creation’s laws and nothing occurs outside divine permission. Harm occurring within the created order does not legitimize injustice.

The same distinction matters in Al-Baqarah 2:102, where a harmful practice can have an effect by God’s permission while the practice itself is condemned. Something may occur within the laws and possibilities God established yet remain morally wrong. People can use creation’s possibilities to build or to destroy. God’s will in establishing the created order does not authorize human oppression.

This is examined in Intercession in the Qur’an: Divine Permission, Human Responsibility, and a Direct Relationship with God. That study distinguishes permission within the created order, moral approval, human choice, and effective divine ways. People choose their actions but do not independently create every condition and consequence. Their conduct takes effect within a world encompassed by God. We can therefore neither place an event outside His knowledge and governance nor use its occurrence to absolve an oppressor.

There is no contradiction between these truths: some may choose to cause war, hunger, poverty, or environmental destruction, while others face a real trial within that situation. The perpetrator is responsible for the injustice, while someone involuntarily exposed to its consequences is also tested in how they respond. They may turn toward despair and wrongful conduct, or resist corruption, help those harmed, and continue seeking reform. Nor should all calamities be reduced to oppression: illness, natural events, and other difficulties can have different causes. The verse establishes testing, not one identical cause for every calamity.

This perspective increases responsibility rather than diminishing it. Those making life difficult through warmongering, hoarding, economic corruption, or environmental destruction cannot hide behind “a divine test.” Making life a field of testing does not authorize people to manufacture suffering and impose it on others. We cannot justify oppression as a test and then demand the victim’s silence as proof of patience.

Conversely, we should not regard every calamity as punishment of its sufferer. Al-Fajr 89:15 describes someone treating prosperity as proof of being honored. In Al-Fajr 89:16, the same person regards restricted provision as humiliation. Al-Fajr 89:17 rejects this judgment. Neither wealth alone proves God’s approval nor poverty and difficulty a person’s low standing before Him. Prosperity can test people just as deprivation and suffering can.

Here a common religious interpretation must be addressed directly. A saying attributed to the prophet describes the world as “the believer’s prison and the unbeliever’s paradise.” If taken to mean that believers are condemned to suffering and deprivation while unbelievers enjoy ease and favors, this interpretation contradicts the Qur’an’s explicit promises. No report, however famous or respected, has authority to nullify God’s Qur’anic promise. Misery cannot be declared the believer’s predetermined fate in the name of piety or asceticism, with every effort toward better living, justice, and prosperity dismissed as worldliness.

An-Nahl 16:97 promises a good, wholesome life to believers who act righteously. Al-A‘raf 7:96 connects communities’ faith and mindfulness of God with blessings from heaven and earth. Yunus 10:64 speaks of good news for believers in worldly life and the hereafter. Al-Baqarah 2:201 places seeking good in this world alongside seeking good in the next. God has not withheld good worldly life from believers or made suffering a permanent condition of religion. Interpretations denying promises of worldly good must answer to the Qur’an itself.

A wholesome life does not necessarily mean that every individual becomes wealthy or immune to illness, loss, and others’ oppression. Nor should promised blessings for a community embodying faith and mindfulness become an unconditional personal guarantee for every member. A society destroying its resources, violating rights, and spreading corruption cannot expect every fruit of prosperity merely by uttering the name of faith. Divine ways connect conduct with consequences; a religious label does not replace right action.

The ending now gains greater significance: “Give good news to those who remain steadfast.” After fear, hunger, and loss, God’s words do not end in despair. Good news means that testing and suffering do not cover the whole horizon of life and its outcome. Nor should this good news be reduced to one form of compensation. Security may return, hunger end, other wealth be acquired, or a new orchard bear fruit. But the loss of that particular life or crop cannot be denied through a promise the Qur’an has not specified.

Al-Baqarah 2:156 describes the patient recalling their belonging to God and return to Him when calamity strikes. Al-Baqarah 2:157 speaks of their Lord’s blessings and mercy upon them and identifies them as guided. In its immediate context, this verse’s good news is thus explained through divine mercy and guidance—a favor whose value is not confined to recovering the identical property or harvest lost.

Ash-Sharh 94:5 speaks of ease accompanying hardship, and Ash-Sharh 94:6 repeats it. Elsewhere the Qur’an also announces rescue, relief, and blessing in worldly life. These promises should not be ignored or all transferred to the hereafter. Yet neither should we turn them into our preferred timetable, guaranteeing every patient person full recovery of every loss by a particular worldly date. The Qur’an gives no such individual, scheduled guarantee. Divine good news is real, but its reality must be understood through the verses themselves, not our expectations about the form of compensation.

Patience here does not mean humiliating submission to oppression. A patient person is not someone who merely sits watching their country’s forests destroyed and says to be patient. People working to defend rights and reform society may face repeated setbacks; their perseverance in the right path is itself patience. The righteous defender of forests, even after failing to prevent destruction, can continue protecting what remains, helping those harmed, reforming laws, and informing others. Such patience does not accept corruption; it refuses to abandon truth before corruption’s power. See Patience in the Qur’an: The Meaning of Steadfastness and the Criterion of Its Value.

Worldly life is neither one community’s guaranteed paradise nor another’s predetermined hell. Favor and difficulty can both be tests. Human behavior can spread flourishing and good or corruption and destruction through collective life. Divine ways do not cancel choice and responsibility; they situate conduct in a real world with consequences. God calls people to gratitude in prosperity, patience in hardship, and responsibility toward truth—not to sanctifying poverty, justifying oppression, or closing their eyes to the possibility of good in this life.

The verse begins with testing but ends with good news. Fear and hunger can end; lost wealth and crops may be compensated, though not necessarily by the return of the identical things. Losing loved ones is also a reality that unsupported promises must not trivialize. Yet the Qur’an does not regard the human path as ending in deprivation and calamity. The verse’s final word concerning the patient is not suffering, but good news.

God knows best.
