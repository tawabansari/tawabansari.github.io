Al-Baqarah 2:247

ۚ ۚ ۖ ۚ

And their prophet said to them: Indeed, The God has raised up Talut as king for you. They said: How can he have kingship over us when we are more entitled to kingship than he is, and he has not been given abundant wealth? He said: Indeed, The God has chosen him over you and increased him in knowledge and physique; and The God gives His kingship to whomever He wills; and The God is the Giver of Abundance, Knowing.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:246, the leaders of the Children of Israel asked their prophet to appoint a king for them so that they could fight to reclaim their rights. Now the response to their request is stated.

Lexically, “nabī” derives from the root “nabaʾ,” meaning news, and differs from “rasūl.” In Quranic usage, a nabī is someone who receives news from the unseen and communicates it to people. The word “rasūl” denotes one who is sent and carries a message; its Persian equivalent is payāmbar. A nabī can also be a rasūl, and a rasūl can also bear the responsibility of prophethood. What matters is that where the Quran uses “nabī,” we should not translate it as “payāmbar” or “rasūl.” There must be wisdom in the choice of these words within the contexts of the verses of The God’s message.

“وَقَالَ لَهُمْ نَبِيُّهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ قَدْ بَعَثَ لَكُمْ طَالُوتَ مَلِكًا”

“And their prophet said to them: Indeed, The God has raised up Talut as king for you.”

In this account, the prophet and the king occupy two separate positions: the prophet informs the people of the divine choice, while Talut is chosen to govern and lead them.

“قَالُوا أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لَهُ الْمُلْكُ عَلَيْنَا وَنَحْنُ أَحَقُّ بِالْمُلْكِ مِنْهُ وَلَمْ يُؤْتَ سَعَةً مِنَ الْمَالِ”

“They said: How can he have kingship over us when we are more entitled to kingship than he is, and he has not been given abundant wealth?”

They had themselves requested the appointment of a leader, yet when God’s choice went against their expectations, they objected. It was as though they expected someone whom they considered worthy according to their own wishes and criteria to be chosen as king.

Their two grounds for objection are clear: first, they regarded themselves as more entitled than Talut; second, they did not see him as possessing great wealth.

In effect, they were measuring fitness for leadership by status and possessions. This is the danger of making one’s wishes and assumptions the standard of judgment instead of seeking the truth, and of accepting even a choice one has requested from God only when it accords with one’s own desires.

We see a similar example in the account of Joseph’s brothers. Relying on their numbers and collective strength, they said, “وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ”; and because Jacob’s love for Joseph and his brother did not accord with their expectations, they said of their father, “إِنَّ أَبَانَا لَفِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ”—our father is clearly astray (Yusuf 12:8). This example shows how people may regard their own understanding as so superior that they deem even a prophet’s judgment wrong when it contradicts their expectations.

“قَالَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ اصْطَفَاهُ عَلَيْكُمْ وَزَادَهُ بَسْطَةً فِي الْعِلْمِ وَالْجِسْمِ”

“He said: Indeed, The God has chosen him over you and increased him in knowledge and physique.”

The prophet’s response sets different criteria before them: knowledge and physical ability.

Knowledge is essential for understanding circumstances, making sound decisions, planning, and leading. Physical ability also matters in difficult circumstances, especially when leading a struggle. God has disregarded neither, but He does not present great wealth as a condition of Talut’s suitability.

It is striking that the people objected to the absence of “سَعَةً مِنَ الْمَالِ,” while God spoke of “بَسْطَةً فِي الْعِلْمِ وَالْجِسْمِ”: He highlighted an abundance of knowledge and ability rather than an abundance of wealth.

“وَاللَّهُ يُؤْتِي مُلْكَهُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ”

“And The God gives His kingship to whomever He wills; and The God is the Giver of Abundance, Knowing.”

The final choice in this account rests with God’s will, but the ending of the verse reminds us that He is “وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ.” Divine choice therefore rests on knowledge and wisdom that are not dependent on limited human assumptions and material criteria.

The message of the verse is clear: fitness for leadership should not be judged solely by wealth, social standing, or claims of prior entitlement. Knowledge, ability, and actual competence matter. People must also be careful not to give their personal wishes precedence over the truth, especially when the truth emerges contrary to their expectations.

Cross-references

  • Al-Baqarah 2:248: The sign of Talut’s kingship.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:249: The testing of Talut’s troops and the importance of obedience and steadfastness.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:251: Granting kingship and wisdom to David.

  • Yusuf 12:8: Reliance on numbers and collective strength and accusing Jacob of being clearly astray.
  • Yusuf 12:94: Jacob’s statement about finding Joseph’s scent.
  • Yusuf 12:95: Accusing Jacob of his old error.
  • Yusuf 12:55: Presenting ability and knowledge as qualifications for responsibility.
  • Maryam 19:51: The use of both titles, messenger and prophet, for Moses.
  • Al-Qasas 28:26: Ability and trustworthiness in selecting a person for responsibility.
  • Sad 38:26: David’s rule and the responsibility to judge according to truth.
