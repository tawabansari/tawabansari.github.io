Al-Baqarah 2:283

ۖ ۗ ۚ ۗ

And if you are on a journey and find no scribe, then pledges taken into possession; and if one of you trusts another, let the one who has been trusted fulfill his trust and be mindful of The God, his Lord. And do not conceal testimony; whoever conceals it—indeed, his heart is sinful; and The God knows what you do.

Our Reflection

This verse follows Al-Baqarah 2:282, in which the Quran commanded the recording of financial obligations with deferred terms, writing with justice, taking witnesses, and protecting both parties’ rights. It now addresses circumstances in which the usual means of recording an obligation are unavailable. Alongside a means of securing the creditor’s right, it emphasizes trustworthiness and not concealing testimony.

Collateral when writing is not possible

“وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ”

“And if you are on a journey and find no scribe, then pledges taken into possession.”

Here the Quran emphasizes not finding a scribe, rather than the absence of a witness. Travel in the past could make access to a scribe, writing materials, and facilities for recording and preserving documents difficult. The parties might also be far apart in the future, making access to witnesses or proof of the agreement difficult. Yet the condition stated by the verse itself is travel together with not finding a scribe.

In these circumstances, the Quran presents “رِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ.” “رِهَان” is the plural of “رَهْن,” from the root R H N, and here refers to property pledged to secure an obligation. “مَقْبُوضَةٌ,” from the root Q B Ḍ, describes something received and taken into possession.

Thus, the verse concerns more than a promise to provide collateral: it envisages collateral actually delivered, so that the creditor’s right remains protected.

For example, if someone lends property to another while traveling and a written document cannot be prepared, they can receive property as a pledge, providing clear security for the other party’s obligation until the debt is repaid. The purpose of this pledge is not to appropriate another’s property or gain an additional benefit, but to protect the right established between the two parties.

Today, technological advances have removed many earlier difficulties. Electronic contracts, bank receipts, digital signatures, and remote communication can make it possible to record and follow up obligations even during travel. The scribe and the parties no longer necessarily need to be in the same place, and reliable documents and information can often be examined from afar.

Technology has not, of course, eliminated every problem: forgery, alteration, technical failure, and disputes over documents’ validity remain possible. But the means of achieving the verse’s purpose have expanded, and the principle of clarifying and recording financial obligations retains its importance.

Mutual trust and the responsibility to fulfill a trust

“فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا فَلْيُؤَدِّ الَّذِي اؤْتُمِنَ أَمَانَتَهُ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ”

“And if one of you trusts another, let the one who has been trusted fulfill his trust and be mindful of The God, his Lord.”

After introducing collateral, the Quran also leaves room for mutual trust. If one party trusts the other, they may accept the transaction or financial obligation without taking a pledge. That trust, however, does not remove the debt or responsibility.

Three words in this expression share a root: “أَمِنَ,” “اؤْتُمِنَ,” and “أَمَانَتَهُ” all come from A M N. First, someone trusts another; next, the other person is entrusted; finally, that person is commanded to fulfill the trust.

This connection shows that trust is not merely a feeling or optimism about another person; it places a responsibility upon the person trusted.

For example, if someone lends property to a friend and trusts them without obtaining a document or collateral, the debtor cannot later exploit that very trust to deny the debt. On the contrary, others’ trust should encourage greater honesty and responsibility in fulfilling one’s obligation.

“فَلْيُؤَدِّ,” from the root A D Y, means fulfilling an obligation and delivering a right to its holder. The Quran states this same principle in An-Nisa 4:58, commanding the return of trusts to their owners. Aal Imran 3:75 also discusses differences in people’s trustworthiness: one person may return substantial wealth to its owner, while another fails to return even a small amount.

The measure of trustworthiness is therefore not merely the amount of property or the existence of an official document; it is a person’s honesty in fulfilling the right they have undertaken. For further discussion, see the principle of trustworthiness and the principle of keeping covenants and agreements.

This command also connects with Al-Baqarah 2:280. There, the Quran commanded the creditor to give time to a debtor in hardship; here, it calls on the person trusted to fulfill the trust. These responsibilities do not conflict: the debtor must be honest in fulfilling others’ rights, and the creditor must respect the actual circumstances of a debtor in hardship.

Concealing testimony: a betrayal of truth

“وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ”

“And do not conceal testimony.”

After discussing collateral and trust, the Quran gives another command with importance in its own right: testimony must not be concealed.

“تَكْتُمُوا,” from the root K T M, means concealing something, and “الشَّهَادَةَ,” from SH H D, concerns witness and testimony.

Someone may know of a transaction or financial obligation and know which party is in the right, yet refuse to disclose the truth because of friendship, kinship, fear, personal gain, or hostility. Such conduct can cause someone to lose their right or another person to be wrongly held responsible.

For example, two people have made a transaction without an official document, and a third person was present when they agreed. Later, one party denies the transaction. If the witness knows the truth but remains silent out of friendship with the person denying it, that silence is no longer a private matter between two friends: by concealing the truth, the witness has helped violate another person’s right.

An-Nisa 4:135 also commands just testimony, even against oneself, one’s parents, or close relatives. Family ties, friendship, wealth, and social standing must therefore not lead to altering or concealing testimony.

The Quran then warns:

“وَمَنْ يَكْتُمْهَا فَإِنَّهُ آثِمٌ قَلْبُهُ”

“And whoever conceals it—indeed, his heart is sinful.”

This expression is particularly striking. The Quran does not merely say that the person is sinful; it attributes the sin to “قَلْبُهُ”—his heart.

“آثِمٌ,” from the root A TH M, refers to being sinful. “Qalb,” from Q L B, also relates in Quranic usage to a person’s inner life, understanding, intention, and inclinations.

Here there is an important difference between not knowing the truth and knowingly concealing it. Someone who truly has not seen something, does not know about it, or has forgotten the details is not in the same situation as someone who knows the truth and deliberately hides it.

Deliberately concealing testimony may arise from fear, greed, prejudice, or attachment to one side of a dispute. Thus, even if someone remains outwardly silent and utters no lie, intentionally concealing the truth can make them responsible for the violation of another’s right.

A person does not betray truth only by lying; sometimes, through deliberate silence where testimony is due, they also assist injustice.

For a broader study of this subject, see the principle of truthful and impartial testimony.

The verse ends with:

“وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ عَلِيمٌ”

“And The God knows what you do.”

Someone may deny a debt, exploit another’s trust, or conceal testimony and thereby hide the truth from other people; but their conduct is not hidden from God’s knowledge.

Protecting people’s rights, this verse teaches, does not depend solely on a document, witness, or collateral; people’s trustworthiness and honesty are also essential. In difficult circumstances, the Quran provides a way to secure financial rights, recognizes mutual trust, commands the fulfillment of trusts, and counts deliberate concealment of testimony as a sin. Tools for recording and establishing rights may advance over time, but no technology replaces human responsibility to respect others’ rights and remain faithful to truth.
