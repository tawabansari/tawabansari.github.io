Al-Baqarah 2:225

ۗ

The God does not hold you accountable for your idle oaths, but He holds you accountable for what your hearts have earned; and The God is Forgiving, Forbearing.

Our Reflection

“لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ”

“The God does not hold you accountable for your idle oaths.”

In the preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:224, The God commanded us not to make our oaths an obstacle to goodness, taqwa, and reconciliation between people. He now clarifies that not every utterance spoken in His name is necessarily an oath for which a person is held accountable.

“Laghw”, from the root ل غ و, refers to idle and fruitless speech or action. People sometimes say something out of habit, joy, anger, or excitement without consciously intending to bind themselves to a serious commitment.

For example, someone is so delighted at the birth of their brother’s child that they swear: “By God, when our children grow up, we will marry them to each other!” But does this excited utterance determine the fate of two children? Must they marry years later against their own wishes because of their fathers’ oath? Clearly not.

Or someone says in anger: “By God, I will never care for another orphan!” But once calm, they realize that their words arose from anger and that children without parental care should not be made victims of their personal hurt.

Sometimes, too, a wife and husband exchange harsh words during an argument, and one swears never to speak to the other again. If, after calming down, both want reconciliation, why should they make that hasty utterance an obstacle to repairing their life together?

The God knows about slips of the tongue and passing emotions. But the verse is not only about words; it goes on to state a more important criterion.

“وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ”

“But He holds you accountable for what your hearts have earned.”

Here the distinction between unintentional speech and a conscious decision becomes clear.

The human heart is the seat of intention, will, and decision. Someone may have a heart full of goodwill and affection yet utter something thoughtless in a moment of anger. Another person may knowingly decide, out of resentment, to harm someone and swear an oath to reinforce that decision.

These two are not the same.

The Quran says in Al-Ahzab 33:5:

“وَلَٰكِنْ مَا تَعَمَّدَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ”

“Rather, what your hearts have deliberately intended.”

The outward form of speech is therefore not the only criterion; the intention and decision a person has consciously cultivated in their heart also matter.

If someone utters an oath without serious intent, they should not impose it upon themselves and others as though it were a conscious commitment. But if they swear with clear intent and awareness, their responsibility cannot be removed merely by calling the words idle.

The God makes the same distinction in Al-Maidah 5:89:

“وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ”

“But He holds you accountable for the oaths you have firmly bound.”

The same verse also explains the means of expiation for broken oaths.

Therefore, if someone has consciously sworn an oath that now obstructs goodness and reconciliation, they should not persist in wrongdoing. A means of making amends exists; but oaths must not become a plaything of the tongue.

The God ends the verse as follows:

“وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ”

“And The God is Forgiving, Forbearing.”

“Ghafur” speaks of abundant forgiveness, and “Halim” of forbearance and not hastening to hold someone to account.

A person may say something in excitement and later regret it. The God knows their intention and does not withhold the opportunity to return and make things right.

The verse’s message is this: the weight of an oath lies not in the name we utter, but in the intention and commitment we consciously cultivate in our hearts. Hasty speech must not obstruct goodness and reconciliation; nor should a deliberate oath become a means of lying, resentment, and violating the rights of others.

Cross-references

  • Aal Imran 3:134: Restraining anger and pardoning people.
  • An-Nisa 4:114: The value of reconciliation between people.
  • Al-Maidah 5:89: The distinction between idle and deliberate oaths, and the explanation of expiation.

  • An-Nur 24:22: Prohibiting oaths to withhold assistance and calling for pardon and forgiveness.
