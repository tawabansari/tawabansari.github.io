Al-Baqarah 2:221

ۚ ۗ ۚ ۗ ۖ ۖ

And do not marry women who associate partners with God until they believe; a believing enslaved woman is better than a woman who associates partners, even if she pleases you. And do not give women in marriage to men who associate partners until they believe; a believing enslaved man is better than a man who associates partners, even if he pleases you. They invite to the Fire, and The God invites, by His permission, to the Garden and forgiveness, and makes His signs clear to people, so that they may remember.

Our Reflection

“وَلَا تَنْكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكَاتِ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنَّ”

“And do not marry women who associate partners with God until they believe.”

In this verse, God prohibits marriage to women who associate partners with Him. Yet in Al-Maidah 5:5, He permits marriage to chaste women from the People of the Book.

Let us place these two rulings alongside one another: if all women from the People of the Book associated partners with God, how could God prohibit marriage to such women in one verse and permit marriage to chaste women from the People of the Book in another? Such a reading creates a contradiction between the Quran’s own rulings.

The People of the Book therefore cannot be considered polytheists simply because they are Jewish or Christian. This is one of the clearest Quranic evidences for the distinction between the People of the Book and those who associate partners with God.

Other verses confirm this distinction. In Al-Baqarah 2:105 and Al-Hajj 22:17, the People of the Book and those who associate partners are named under separate designations. In Aal Imran 3:113, God says that the People of the Book are not all alike, and in Aal Imran 3:114, He describes a group among them as believing and righteous. Aal Imran 3:199 also speaks of faith among a group of the People of the Book. In Al-Ankabut 29:46, dialogue with the People of the Book emphasizes the oneness of the God worshipped.

These evidences show that the designation “People of the Book” is not identical to “polytheist,” although some individuals among the People of the Book, like other human beings, may associate partners with God through their beliefs or conduct.

This distinction also matters today: some classify all the People of the Book as polytheists, extend the ruling of At-Tawbah 9:28 to them, and exclude them from the Sacred Mosque and Hajj. This issue is examined in “Hajj in the Quran: Abraham’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights”.

The broader discussion of faith and the standing of the People of the Book appears in “Mumin or Muslim?”.

“وَلَأَمَةٌ مُؤْمِنَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِنْ مُشْرِكَةٍ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ”

“And a believing enslaved woman is better than a woman who associates partners with God, even if she pleases you.”

Why does God use the example of a believing woman in slavery to demonstrate the value of faith?

In a society where people were valued according to freedom, slavery, ancestry, and social standing, an enslaved woman lacked many rights and advantages enjoyed by a free woman. Yet the Quran says that even such a woman, if she believes, is better than a woman who associates partners with God and appeals to you.

The value of faith is so great that even the subordinate social position of slavery does not erase it.

The verse does not explicitly describe the other woman as free. Yet the choice of “amatun mu’minah”—a believing enslaved woman—shows that the believer’s social position does not change the superiority of faith over associating partners.

“Even if she pleases you” also makes clear that human attraction and preference must not override this criterion.

The issue here is not whether every polytheist’s personal character is good or bad. God compares faith with associating partners, not honesty with dishonesty or their other individual qualities.

“وَلَا تُنْكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنُوا”

“And do not give women in marriage to men who associate partners with God until they believe.”

The same prohibition is now stated concerning men who associate partners with God.

In the first part, “tankihu” refers to marrying; “tunkihu,” however, is Form IV and concerns giving another person in marriage.

God continues:

“وَلَعَبْدٌ مُؤْمِنٌ خَيْرٌ مِنْ مُشْرِكٍ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَكُمْ”

“And a believing enslaved man is better than a man who associates partners with God, even if he pleases you.”

This time, a believing man in slavery is compared with a man who associates partners with God.

The criterion is therefore the same for women and men: faith is superior to associating partners, and neither social standing nor personal attraction changes this truth.

But why does this difference matter so greatly in marriage?

“أُولَٰئِكَ يَدْعُونَ إِلَى النَّارِ”

“They invite to the Fire.”

Marriage is not merely a physical union between two people; it is a covenant for a shared life. A woman and a man build a family together and make decisions about the future, raising children, and their path in life.

If one considers service to The God the foundation of life while the other associates partners with Him or denies His existence altogether, their disagreement is not simply a difference of taste. They differ over a fundamental matter in life: the deity and source of authority they serve.

This difference can affect their shared life and the upbringing of their children.

The Quran’s statement here concerns the direction of faith and worship, not a claim that every polytheist necessarily has bad character and every believer good character.

In contrast with the invitation of those who associate partners, God says:

“وَاللَّهُ يَدْعُو إِلَى الْجَنَّةِ وَالْمَغْفِرَةِ بِإِذْنِهِ”

“And The God invites, by His permission, to the Garden and forgiveness.”

The Quran sets two different directions against one another: an invitation to the Fire and the invitation of The God to the Garden and forgiveness.

For this reason, agreement between a woman and a man on the foundations of faith and worship is an important basis for shared life. Love and affection can strengthen that life when both also share an understanding of its fundamental principles.

The purpose of marriage, spouses’ commitment, and the place of faith in shared life are examined in “Quranic Marriage”. For the meaning of “bi-idhnihi,” see also “Divine Permission in the Quran”.

The verse ends with these words:

“وَيُبَيِّنُ آيَاتِهِ لِلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ”

“And He makes His signs clear to people, so that they may remember.”

God makes His signs clear to people. Here He prohibits marriage to those who associate partners, and in Al-Maidah 5:5 He permits marriage to chaste women from the People of the Book. Elsewhere, too, He has not presented the People of the Book and those who associate partners as identical.

The label “polytheist” must therefore not be used to exclude all the People of the Book from provisions for which the Quran has stated no such exclusion.

This is one of the most important insights of Al-Baqarah 2:221 for life today: recognizing the distinction between the People of the Book and those who associate partners on the basis of Quranic verses, rather than religious labels and inherited judgments.

The verse also reminds us that, in choosing a spouse, faith takes precedence over personal preference and social standing, because marriage joins two people in a shared life and direction.
