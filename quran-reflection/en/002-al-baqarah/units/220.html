Al-Baqarah 2:220

ۗ ۖ ۖ ۚ ۚ ۚ

About this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you about orphans. Say: Improving their circumstances is better; and if you share life with them, they are your brothers. The God distinguishes the corrupter from the reformer; and had The God willed, He would have put you into hardship. Indeed, The God is Invincible and Wise.

Our Reflection

“فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ”

“About this world and the Hereafter.”

The preceding verse ended with an invitation to reflect, and this verse begins by speaking of this world and the Hereafter. Immediately afterward, the question of orphans is raised.

This connection invites reflection. A person may speak at length about the Hereafter, reward, salvation, and doing good, yet remain indifferent in this world to the life of a child who has lost a father and needs support from others.

The Quran establishes a clear connection between goodness and the way people treat orphans. In Al-Baqarah 2:177, giving wealth to orphans is counted among the expressions of righteousness, and in Al-Baqarah 2:215, they are included among those who should benefit from the good people give.

This connection is even clearer in Surah Al-Maun. In Al-Maun 107:1, Al-Maun 107:2, and Al-Maun 107:3, the Quran speaks of someone who denies the religion, then identifies driving away the orphan and failing to encourage feeding the needy as that person’s conduct.

Speaking of religion and the Hereafter is therefore inseparable from responsibility toward others’ lives and rights. Our treatment of those who need support is one of the places where the reality of our goodness and mindfulness becomes apparent.

“وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْيَتَامَىٰ”

“And they ask you about orphans.”

“Yatama” is the plural of yatim, from the root Y T M. In its human usage, it refers to a child who has lost a father.

Losing a father can confront a child with many difficulties. Some orphans possess property and an inheritance but need help protecting and managing it. Others depend on support even for food, clothing, shelter, and education.

Their needs are not only financial. A child needs affection, security, upbringing, respect, companionship, and opportunities to grow. Losing a father should not mean losing these rights.

There is another point in the verse’s wording: God speaks of “al-yatama”—orphans—not believing orphans or Muslim orphans.

No religious or denominational qualification is attached to this expression. The responsibility to care for orphans therefore cannot be restricted without evidence to children of one religion, denomination, or community.

A child who has lost a father must not be deprived of affection, care, education, or support because of the parents’ beliefs. An orphan from a Jewish, Christian, Muslim, or any other family remains a child in need of security and support.

This reading accords with the general command to do good to orphans in Al-Baqarah 2:83 and An-Nisa 4:36, and with the principle of kindness and justice in Al-Mumtahanah 60:8.

Our responsibility toward a vulnerable child does not begin by asking about their religion or denomination; it begins by recognizing their needs and respecting their rights.

God answers:

“قُلْ إِصْلَاحٌ لَهُمْ خَيْرٌ”

“Say: Improving their circumstances is better.”

“Islah,” from the root Ṣ L Ḥ, relates to putting things right, bringing order, and removing corruption and disorder.

God does not say merely to put orphans’ property in order. He says “islahun lahum”—reform and improvement for them themselves.

This distinction matters, because an orphan is not merely the owner of property that needs safeguarding. The child is a human being who should have opportunities for life, growth, and a suitable future.

For a child without shelter, providing a home and security can be reform. For a child deprived of education, help with schooling and learning skills matters. A child deprived of family affection may need compassionate care and companionship more than money.

Good guardianship should gradually prepare a child for an independent and responsible life, rather than keep them dependent and unable forever.

In An-Nisa 4:6, the Quran speaks of orphans reaching marriageable age and assessing their sound judgment before handing over their property. This verse shows that safeguarding property is connected with attention to its owner’s ability and maturity.

An-Nisa 4:127 also addresses fairness toward orphans and the rights of orphaned girls in family matters. Their rights in family relationships and marriage must therefore also be respected.

In Ad-Duha 93:9, God forbids oppressing and humiliating the orphan. In Al-Fajr 89:17, those who fail to honor the orphan are likewise condemned.

These verses show that improving an orphan’s life concerns both their property and resources and their affection, dignity, education, security, and future.

God then says:

“وَإِنْ تُخَالِطُوهُمْ فَإِخْوَانُكُمْ”

“And if you share life with them, they are your brothers.”

“Tukhalituhum,” from the root KH L Ṭ, relates to mixing and joining together.

God does not say merely to mix your property with the property of orphans; He speaks of mixing and associating with the children themselves.

This companionship can encompass family life, food, education, play, and social relationships.

Consider, for example, a family caring for an orphaned child. Why should the child be separated from the other children at meals, in play, or during family activities? Why should the child always feel like a guest or a stranger?

The Quran says: “Fa-ikhwanukum”—they are your brothers.

This expression does not portray orphans as strangers or outsiders; it emphasizes a close human bond with them.

Nor should this brotherhood be restricted to orphans of the same denomination. The verse speaks of “orphans” and does not require a child first to follow a particular religion or denomination to be accepted into a family and society.

A child whose care we have undertaken must not be humiliated because of differences in their family’s beliefs or pressured to change belief in order to receive affection and support.

In social life, orphans should likewise enjoy education, friendship, play, and opportunities to grow like other children. Orphanhood must not become a mark used to discriminate against them or exclude them from others.

Companionship and shared life, however, do not erase an orphan’s independent rights.

If a child has property or an inheritance, it still belongs to that child. A guardian cannot use shared living as a pretext to treat the orphan’s property as their own or disregard the child’s rights.

In An-Nisa 4:2, God forbids wrongfully appropriating orphans’ property and exchanging their good property for inferior property. In An-Nisa 4:10, consuming an orphan’s wealth unjustly carries a grave warning.

Al-Anam 6:152 and Al-Isra 17:34 also command that an orphan’s property not be approached except in the best manner, until the child reaches maturity and capacity.

Thus, do not separate the orphan from others in life and affection, but do not erase their independent rights in property and other matters either.

Welcoming a child into a family as a brother also does not mean changing their actual lineage. In Al-Ahzab 33:4 and Al-Ahzab 33:5, the Quran emphasizes the distinction between adoption and actual parentage and the preservation of family identity.

A child can be welcomed into a family with love and respect without changing their name, lineage, property, or rights.

“وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ الْمُفْسِدَ مِنَ الْمُصْلِحِ”

“And The God distinguishes the corrupter from the reformer.”

Here God distinguishes between two kinds of conduct: corruption and reform.

Someone may present themselves as an orphan’s guardian and well-wisher while, in practice, misusing the child’s property, denying education, treating them with humiliation and coldness, or exploiting their dependence and vulnerability for personal interests.

By contrast, someone with little wealth may improve a child’s life through affection, care, and sound education.

Holding the title of guardian, claiming goodwill, or even spending money therefore does not, by itself, make someone a reformer.

In Al-Baqarah 2:11 and Al-Baqarah 2:12, people also claim to bring reform while the Quran identifies them as corrupters. In Al-Baqarah 2:205, corruption is connected with harming life and destroying crops and offspring.

The same criterion must be applied to the care of orphans.

A reformer works to improve an orphan’s life and preserve their rights, rather than using the names of guardianship and goodness to conceal exploitation.

God knows the truth of human intentions and conduct. Yet we too should not be satisfied with claims of goodwill alone; we must consider the effect of a guardian’s behavior on a child’s security, rights, development, and future.

“وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَأَعْنَتَكُمْ”

“And had The God willed, He would have put you into hardship.”

“A‘natakum,” from the root ʿ N T, relates to suffering, difficulty, and hardship.

This expression follows the discussion of companionship and sharing life with orphans.

If orphans had to be kept separate from others in every aspect of life, and all shared meals, living, and cooperation were forbidden, caring for them would become very difficult.

Such restrictions might discourage families from welcoming orphans and deprive the children themselves of affection and ordinary life.

But instead of forbidding companionship, God has made improving orphans’ circumstances the criterion and has called them your brothers.

This easing does not permit exploitation. Someone who uses shared living to seize a child’s property or violate their rights cannot justify that conduct through this verse.

Protecting an orphan’s rights is essential, but must not become a pretext for separating the child from family and society or devising baseless restrictions.

The verse ends with two divine descriptions:

“إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ”

“Indeed, The God is Invincible and Wise.”

“‘Aziz” relates to power and invincibility, while “hakim” speaks of wisdom and firmness in judgment and governance.

God has the power to place people in hardship, yet here He has not closed the way to living and associating with orphans. He distinguishes the corrupter from the reformer and has placed reform above fruitless severity.

God’s power is inseparable from His wisdom. His ruling serves to preserve orphans’ rights and improve their lives, not to deprive them of affection and family bonds.

This verse does not see an orphan merely as a property owner whose wealth must be guarded, but as a human being who needs affection, security, education, respect, and a suitable future.

Caring for orphans and improving their lives is not limited by their religion or denomination. God has spoken of all orphans, and no child should be deprived of support and rights because of their family’s beliefs.

Orphans should be able to live alongside others, receive affection, grow, and have a worthy place in society, while their property, identity, and independent rights remain protected.

The God distinguishes the corrupter from the reformer. The criterion is therefore not a claim to goodness or the appearance of guardianship; we must consider how our conduct affects orphans’ lives, dignity, and future.
