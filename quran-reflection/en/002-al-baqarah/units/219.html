Al-Baqarah 2:219

ۖ ۗ ۗ

They ask you about khamr and maysir. Say: In both there is great sin and benefits for people, and their sin is greater than their benefit. And they ask you what they should spend. Say: What is beyond need. In this way The God makes the signs clear to you, so that you may reflect.

Our Reflection

“يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ”

“They ask you about khamr and maysir.”

In this verse, God places two phenomena alongside one another: khamr and maysir. Both may bring people benefit or pleasure, yet they can also have serious consequences for individual and social life.

“Khamr,” from the root KH M R, relates to covering. Just as a covering conceals something from sight, khamr is connected with the covering of reason and the loss of awareness and discernment.

Khamr should therefore not be restricted to grape wine. Various intoxicating drinks and substances whose intoxicating effects impair reason, awareness, and decision-making can fall within this concept, whether made from grapes, dates, grains, or other materials.

What matters is not the name of the drink or substance, but its effect on reason and conduct.

In An-Nisa 4:43, God also forbids approaching prayer while intoxicated, until people know what they are saying. This verse makes clear the connection between intoxication and reduced awareness and discernment.

“Maysir,” from the root Y S R, relates to ease and availability. Gambling and betting are clear examples of maysir, but the issue is not merely throwing dice, playing cards, or betting on a contest.

In maysir, a person seeks gain through chance, winning and losing, or a situation in which their profit comes from others’ losses, without corresponding work or creation of value.

This can take various forms. In the past it might involve casting lots and wagering property; today it can also appear in financial games and chance-based transactions in which wealth merely passes from one hand to another and one party’s gain comes chiefly from another’s loss.

To recognize maysir, then, we should not look only at a game’s name or outward form; we must examine how the benefit is obtained.

This subject is examined in detail in the study of the root Y S R and the book “Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest”, particularly in the discussion of speculation and chance-based profit-seeking.

In answering the question about khamr and maysir, God says:

“قُلْ فِيهِمَا إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ”

“Say: In both there is great sin and benefits for people.”

The Quran does not deny that there is benefit. This matters because something having a benefit does not, by itself, prove that it is good.

For example, drinking alcohol may temporarily create feelings of pleasure, relaxation, or warmth. Using cannabis may briefly distance someone from life’s pressures and worries. Other intoxicating substances may also reduce pain or suffering for a time.

Yet these same substances can cause dependence and addiction, impair decision-making, and damage the lives of individuals and their families.

Someone may drive under the influence of alcohol and take several lives. Another person may lose work, possessions, family relationships, and responsibilities through dependence on intoxicating substances. Sometimes the behavior of an intoxicated person leads to violence, conflict, and harm to others.

In maysir, too, someone may win a great deal of money or profit from organizing games and betting. On the other side, however, people may lose their possessions, fall into debt, and endanger their families’ lives. Competition over winning and losing can also create hostility, deception, and discord.

The Quran therefore does not say that khamr and maysir have no benefit. It says:

“وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا”

“And their sin is greater than their benefit.”

Here, “ithm” should not be confined to physical or financial harm. Ithm concerns sin and human moral responsibility, although the harmful consequences of conduct may also reveal the gravity of that sin.

The pairing of “ithm” with “benefits” does not negate its meaning as sin. Conduct may benefit some people while still being a grave sin. In Al-Araf 7:33, the Quran includes “al-ithm” itself among the things God has forbidden.

In Al-Maidah 5:90, God also includes khamr and maysir among impure, satanic practices and commands people to avoid them. In Al-Maidah 5:91, He speaks of hostility, hatred, and being kept from remembering God and from prayer in connection with these two.

The issue is therefore not merely short-term pleasure or profit. We must also consider the consequences of conduct: what happens to reason, choice, family, possessions, human relationships, and society?

The Quran places limited benefit and greater sin before people so that they are not deceived by apparent advantage.

But the verse does not end with this subject. Another question follows:

“وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ مَاذَا يُنْفِقُونَ”

“And they ask you what they should spend.”

We encountered this question a few verses earlier, in Al-Baqarah 2:215. There God said that whatever good people spend should be for parents, close relatives, orphans, the needy, and stranded travelers.

That verse discussed the kind of good and those who benefit from it. People can use their wealth, time, knowledge, skills, and other abilities to help others.

Now the answer is very brief:

“قُلِ الْعَفْوَ”

“Say: What is beyond need.”

“‘Afw,” from the root ʿ F W, relates to relinquishing and letting go, and in this context refers to what is surplus and available to give.

God does not specify a percentage or numerical amount in His answer; He says “al-‘afw,” meaning what is beyond need.

People do not have identical possessions, resources, or needs. What constitutes a large part of one person’s wealth may be a small amount for another. Even a very expensive car may be among a wealthy person’s surplus possessions.

The Quran’s answer should therefore not be confined to a list of particular goods or a fixed percentage. The verse’s criterion is clear: spend from what you have beyond your needs.

This answer also connects with Al-Baqarah 2:215: there the word was “good,” and here it is “al-‘afw.” People can put their surplus wealth, time, knowledge, and ability to good use.

The Quran provides no financial table, universal threshold, or fixed percentage for this answer. Zakat, spending, and their transformation into numerical systems and religious obligations are examined in detail in “Zakat and Its Historical Distortion”.

God concludes the verse with a significant statement:

“كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ”

“In this way The God makes the signs clear to you, so that you may reflect.”

God Himself says that He makes the signs clear. He then explains the purpose of this clarification: that people should reflect.

In this very verse, He has discussed khamr and maysir, acknowledged their benefit and the gravity of their sin, and identified “al-‘afw” in answer to the question about spending.

Why, then, after this clear explanation, should we turn to people who rely on transmitted accounts, fatwas, and their own opinions to impose new rulings upon others in God’s name in order to define the limits of religion?

How can khamr be confined to grape wine, and other intoxicants then excluded from the ruling by changing their names and ingredients? If the criterion is the covering of reason and intoxication, why should names such as nabidh or the names of other intoxicating substances change the reality of their effects?

Likewise, how can the clear answer “quli al-‘afw” be set aside and the percentages and thresholds devised within jurisprudential systems be presented as though God had prescribed those very numbers in the Quran?

This is the danger of parallel legislation: instead of understanding and carrying out God’s command, people devise limits for religion themselves and then attribute their own constructions to Him.

In An-Nahl 16:116, God forbids falsely attributing fabricated declarations of what is lawful and unlawful to Him. In Ash-Shura 42:21, He also speaks of partners who have legislated religious matters for people without His permission.

No scholar, leader, denomination, or collection of transmitted accounts therefore has the right to set itself up as an independent authority determining what is lawful and unlawful against God’s own explanation.

If the Quran has stated something clearly, people’s task is to understand it and act upon it, rather than add conditions God has not stated or devise exceptions absent from His words.

“So that you may reflect” is not an invitation to blind following. God calls people to think about His signs, not to surrender their reason and choice to those who devise rulings for them in religion’s name.

This verse gives three clear answers: despite their benefits, khamr and maysir carry greater sin; the criterion for spending is “al-‘afw”; and divine signs are made clear for human reflection.

Understanding God’s ruling must begin with His own words. Whoever invents new lawful and unlawful rulings in God’s name, or attributes limits He has not stated to religion, must provide evidence for that claim from the Quran itself.
