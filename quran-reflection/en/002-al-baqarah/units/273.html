Al-Baqarah 2:273

ۗ

For the needy who have been constrained in the way of The God and are unable to travel through the land; someone unaware of their condition considers them free of need because of their restraint. You recognize them by their signs; they do not ask people insistently; and whatever good you spend, The God surely knows it.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verses, the Quran spoke of the value of giving, purity of intention, avoiding reproach and injury, and the return of good to the giver. Now it draws our attention to a group of people in need who, because of difficult living conditions, cannot provide for themselves, and whose real needs may be unknown to others.

“لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الَّذِينَ أُحْصِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ”

“For the needy who have been constrained in the way of The God.”

“Fuqarāʾ” is the plural of faqīr and refers to people facing a lack of resources and need. “أُحْصِرُوا,” from the root H Ṣ R in the passive form, refers to being caught in hardship or restriction, or prevented from movement and activity.

The Quran connects this situation with “فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ.” This expression should not be restricted to presence on a battlefield without evidence. People may face deprivation, displacement, and various obstacles while preserving faith, life, and freedom and striving in God’s way.

In At-Tawbah 9:60, the Quran states eight uses of charities: the poor, the needy, charity workers, those whose hearts are reconciled, freeing people from bondage, debtors, God’s way, and stranded travelers. Thus, the people in need described in this verse are not the only group deserving charity. For a detailed examination of these uses, see “Zakat and Its Historical Distortion”.

The Quran then clarifies these people’s situation:

“لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ”

“They are unable to travel through the land.”

“ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ” in Quranic usage refers to movement and travel through the land. In Al-Muzzammil 73:20, the same expression describes those traveling through the land and seeking provision from God’s bounty.

Thus, being unable to travel may prevent someone from seeking work, earning a livelihood, accessing necessities, or moving to a safer place.

This inability does not always have a single cause. Poverty, war, siege, blocked roads, illness, disability, or physical incapacity may prevent someone from moving to earn a livelihood or escape hardship.

For example, consider someone who supported their family through daily work before becoming ill but is now bedridden and unable to go out and work. Or consider a family trapped in a war zone, without travel funds, a safe exit route, or a means of supporting themselves.

Such people cannot be held responsible for all their deprivation merely because they lack work, income, or the ability to migrate. Those with greater resources should recognize their circumstances and work to meet their needs and create paths to relief and greater capability.

The Quran then describes another characteristic of these people in need:

“يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ”

“Someone unaware of their condition considers them free of need because of their restraint.”

Here, “jāhil” means someone unaware of their actual circumstances. “Taʿaffuf,” from the root ʿ F F, refers to self-restraint and holding oneself back from desires and conduct one wishes to avoid.

In this context, the verse concerns people in need who, despite their poverty, do not display it or continually ask others for help.

Someone may appear well-groomed and tasteful, treat others respectfully, and never discuss financial difficulties, yet at home be unable to afford food, medicine, or other necessities.

A person’s dignity and restraint do not prove that they are free of need. Sometimes those most in need of help speak least about their needs.

For this reason, the Quran says:

“تَعْرِفُهُمْ بِسِيمَاهُمْ”

“You recognize them by their signs.”

“Sīmā” refers to a sign or mark by which something or someone is recognized. This verse concerns recognizing people in need through signs of their circumstances and lives.

These signs are not necessarily limited to complexion, clothing, or physical characteristics. Living conditions, limited abilities, difficulties earning a livelihood, or familiarity with someone’s circumstances may reveal their need.

Recognizing such needs does not require prying into people’s private lives or forcing them to prove their poverty publicly. Responsibly understanding people’s circumstances differs from spying, humiliation, and exposing their secrets.

In Al-Baqarah 2:271, the Quran described public charity as good and concealing it when giving to the needy as better. One important wisdom in concealing assistance is preserving the recipient’s reputation and dignity.

Therefore, if someone does not want their need, face, or private life exposed to others, we should not turn assistance into an opportunity for filming, publicity, or displaying our generosity. We can help someone in need without sacrificing their reputation and privacy.

The Quran then says:

“لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا”

“They do not ask people insistently.”

“Ilḥāf,” from the root L Ḥ F, here refers to persistence and insistence in asking.

The Quran describes these people as not asking insistently. We should therefore not conclude that any person in need who is compelled to ask someone for help ceases to deserve assistance.

Nor should their restraint become an excuse for others’ neglect. If someone does not ask despite poverty, our task is not to wait until their need worsens and they are finally forced to ask in embarrassment.

Giving aims not only to answer visible requests but also to recognize real needs and assist those unable to meet them themselves.

In Al-Hashr 59:9, the Quran also recalls those who love the migrants and put them before themselves despite their own need. This example shows that social responsibility is not limited to preserving personal wealth; it also appears in helping people in hardship.

For a broader examination of this responsibility, see “The Principle of Supporting the Vulnerable and Oppressed” and “The Principle of Feeding the Hungry and Caring for Those in Need”.

The verse finally says:

“وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ”

“And whatever good you spend, The God surely knows it.”

“ʿAlīm,” from the root ʿ L M, refers to knowledge. God knows the good a person does, whether others see it or not.

This ending accords with the preceding verses. In Al-Baqarah 2:271, God was also described as aware of human conduct, and Al-Baqarah 2:272 recalled that the good of giving returns to the giver.

Thus, for giving to have value, we need not display our generosity, demand thanks and praise from recipients, or appoint ourselves judges of others’ intentions and conduct by prying into their lives. Proper care for those in need and trustworthiness in using donations nevertheless remain our responsibility.

The message of this verse is that society must not forget people in hardship and need merely because they do not reveal their poverty. Those prevented from supporting themselves by hardship, illness, incapacity, or lack of access and resources deserve attention and assistance. Recognizing their needs must preserve their dignity and reputation, without spying, reproach, or humiliation. The God knows every good we do in this way, even if no human being knows of it.
