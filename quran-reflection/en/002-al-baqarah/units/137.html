Al-Baqarah 2:137

ۖ ۖ ۚ

So if they, too, have faith like yours, they are indeed guided; but if they turn away, they are in division and opposition. The God will suffice for you against them; and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

Our Reflection

The previous verse instructed the community of believers to state their faith clearly: faith in the one God, in the revelation sent down to them, and in what was given to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the tribes, Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets, without discriminating between God’s messengers.

Now it says that if the People of the Book also have faith like this faith, they are guided.

The expression “like” (bi-mithli) matters here. The verse does not say that they are guided if they become members of your community or change their religious name. It speaks of faith like this faith: believing in God, accepting His revelation, and abandoning discrimination between the prophets.

Another point in the previous verse deserves attention. The Qur’an named Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, and Jesus, but did not place Muhammad as a separate name in that same list, although it could easily have done so. Instead, it spoke of the revelation sent down “to us.” The Qur’an does not exclude Muhammad or his revelation, but neither does it turn the condition for the People of the Book’s guidance into abandoning their religious identity and joining a new sect.

What is asked of them is to break with exclusivity: to accept Moses without setting Jesus aside; to accept Isaac without excluding Ishmael from the Abrahamic inheritance; to stop treating God’s revelation as the property of one people; and to surrender before the one God.

This meaning accords with the reality of differing prescribed ways within the Qur’an itself. Al-Ma’idah 5:48 says:

“We sent down to you the Book in truth, confirming the Book that preceded it and standing as a guardian over it. So judge between them by what The God has sent down, and do not follow their desires by turning away from the truth that has come to you. For each of you We have appointed a prescribed way and a path. Had The God willed, He would have made you one community, but He intended to test you in what He has given you. So compete with one another in good deeds. To The God you will all return, and He will then inform you about that in which you differed.”
Al-Ma’idah 5:48

This verse does not erase difference; it recognizes it: for each, a prescribed way and a path. Had God willed, He would have made everyone one community, but He did not. Instead, He called people to compete in good deeds and reserved the final judgment of their differences to Himself.

Unity of faith therefore does not necessarily mean uniformity of prescribed ways. To be guided, the People of the Book have not been compelled to abandon their historical identity and adopt Muhammad’s prescribed way as a new identity. What this sequence of verses addresses is discrimination in accepting truth and prophets, not the existence of differing paths and prescribed ways.

This reading also accords with Al-Baqarah 2:62 and Al-Ma’idah 5:69. There, too, Jews, Christians, and Sabians retain their own names, while the criterion of judgment is faith in God and the Last Day, together with righteous action. Before speaking of their guidance, the Qur’an does not first require them to change their names.

This does not mean that the Qur’anic revelation is unimportant. The previous verse includes what was sent down to Muhammad’s community within the line of revelation. A person cannot deny a truth that has become clear to them simply out of prejudice. Yet what truth has reached a particular person, how clearly it has reached them, how fully they have understood it, and with what intention they have responded are matters no other human being can fully judge from the outside.

If they turn away, the Qur’an does not say that the difference in their prescribed way is itself a rupture. It says that they are in oppositional division (shiqāq). The Arabic term conveys a split and separation: a condition in which difference becomes opposition and estrangement.

The Qur’an itself has accepted differences in prescribed ways. Oppositional division arises when people confine truth within their own community, accept some messengers and set others aside because of ethnic or religious prejudice, and use difference to build a wall between “us” and “them.”

An-Nisa 4:150–152 makes this boundary clear. Rather than repeating those verses at length, it is enough to refer to them here: the Qur’an explicitly rejects selective faith in the messengers.

Even when the verse speaks of such division, however, Muhammad is not commanded to change their beliefs by force. He is told that God will suffice for him against them. His duty is to state the faith, convey the truth, and engage in dialogue—not to take control of people’s consciences.

Al-‘Ankabut 29:46 provides a complementary witness, expressing this approach very clearly:

“Do not dispute with the People of the Book except in the best manner, apart from those among them who have done wrong. And say: We believe in what was sent down to us and what was sent down to you. Our God and your God are one, and to Him we surrender.”
Al-‘Ankabut 29:46

This verse does not say to begin by forcing them to abandon their prescribed way and identity. It places the starting point of dialogue in shared truth: revelation to us and to you, one God, and surrender to Him. Differences remain, but the way to address them is discussion in the best manner, not coercion.

Now the ending of Al-Baqarah 2:137 takes on a deeper meaning. It ends with two descriptions: the All-Hearing (al-Samīʿ) and the All-Knowing (al-ʿAlīm).

He hears everyone’s words and knows what lies behind them. We may hear someone’s claim, observe their conduct, and discuss whether an idea is right or wrong. But complete knowledge of a person’s intention, sincerity, extent of understanding, and reality of faith is not ours.

In religious discussions, a person can easily move from inviting others to judging their hearts. The verse’s ending puts us back in our place: we are not the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. Final judgment about how much truth someone has heard, understood, and acted upon belongs to the one God.

The lesson for life today is clear. Faithfulness to our own prescribed way does not give us the right to claim that guidance belongs exclusively to it. A Jew can remain within their prescribed way, a Christian within theirs, and we within ours. What the Qur’an does not accept within these differences is prejudice, discrimination between the messengers, and turning difference into oppositional division. Our part is to state the truth, engage in good dialogue, and compete in good deeds—not to force others to change their identity. Final judgment belongs to the One who both hears and knows.
