Al-Baqarah 2:174

ۙ

Indeed, those who conceal what The God has sent down of the Book and receive a small price in exchange for it eat nothing but fire into their bellies; and The God will not speak to them on the Day of Resurrection or purify them; and for them is a painful punishment.

Our Reflection

How can a truth that God has revealed to guide people become a means of acquiring wealth and power? After stating food rulings and warning against following inherited traditions, the Qur’an speaks of those who knowingly conceal what has been revealed of the Book and receive a small price in return. They not only turn away from the truth themselves, but also place it beyond the reach of people who need guidance.

“Yaktumun,” from the root K T M, means concealing and keeping hidden. Concealment becomes an issue when a person knows a truth but does not disclose it. In Al-Baqarah 2:159, God speaks of those who conceal clear signs and guidance after they have been explained to people. The distinction between concealment, ignorance, mistaken understanding, and other forms of obscuring or altering the truth is examined in the reflection on that verse. Here, too, the subject is not a simple difference of interpretation; it is the deliberate concealment of what God has revealed of the Book.

This warning is not limited to the scholars of one people or one historical period. “مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ” refers to truth revealed by God: truth intended to guide people, not the exclusive property of a religious class. If those who hold knowledge and religious authority conceal parts of revelation that conflict with their interests or inherited beliefs, they come within this warning. The divine Book should enlighten people. In Al-Maidah 5:16, God speaks of guiding people to paths of peace and bringing them out of darkness into light. Whoever conceals revealed truth deprives people of the benefit of that light.

But why would someone who knows the truth conceal it? The Qur’an speaks of an exchange in which truth is set aside for “thamanan qalilan,” a small price. That price can be money or possessions, but human advantage is not always material. Preserving religious rank or social influence, pleasing those in power, fearing the loss of followers, or protecting the standing of a religious ruling or inherited tradition can also motivate concealment. Even if a person acquires great wealth or considerable power in this way, what they gain is a small price compared with divine truth and life’s ultimate outcome.

In Al-Maidah 5:44, while speaking about the Torah and the responsibility of the Book’s custodians, God warns them against fearing people and forbids trading His signs for a small price. He then emphasizes the necessity of judging by what He has revealed. The connection is clear: when fear of people or personal interest overcomes fidelity to revelation, a person may conceal what they ought to disclose and make human wishes, rather than God’s command, the standard. In Ali Imran 3:187, those given the Book were also bound by a covenant to explain it to people and not conceal it; yet some cast that covenant aside for a small price.

This danger exists today as well. If a scholar knows a clear Qur’anic ruling but conceals it to preserve the authority of a report, legal opinion, or religious school, imposing another ruling on people in God’s name, the issue goes beyond scholarly disagreement and approaches the deliberate concealment of truth. For example, the independent study “Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Ruling Derived from Reports?” examines the substitution of stoning for the punishment stated in An-Nur 24:2. If someone, after recognizing revelation’s clear ruling, conceals it to defend an inherited ruling, their responsibility is not merely a matter of legal disagreement; human life and people’s right to know God’s command are also at stake. Divine truth must not be sacrificed to the reputation of leaders, the interests of religious institutions, or the advantage of those in power.

The Qur’an describes the outcome of such a bargain in a startling image: “أُولَٰئِكَ مَا يَأْكُلُونَ فِي بُطُونِهِمْ إِلَّا النَّارَ”. Someone who obtains wealth and comfort by concealing truth may outwardly enjoy the finest foods, yet the Qur’an describes their gain as fire that they eat into their bellies. What served as a means of pleasure and enjoyment in this world is, in reality, bound to a burning outcome.

The same image occurs in An-Nisa 4:10 concerning those who unjustly consume orphans’ property. There, a person wrongfully appropriates the property of a child whose rights they should protect, and the Qur’an says that such people eat fire into their bellies and will enter a blazing fire. In Al-Baqarah 2:174, too, a person turns truth that should be disclosed for people’s guidance into a means of personal gain, and the outcome of this exchange is likened to eating fire.

The connection between these two verses is instructive. One concerns betrayal of an orphan’s property, the other betrayal of the trust of revelation. In both, a person appropriates for personal benefit something whose rightful claim they should uphold: one violates a vulnerable person’s financial rights, while the other violates people’s right of access to truth and guidance. Both may profit from this conduct in worldly life, but the Qur’an shows that the true value of gain obtained through injustice and betrayal will become apparent in a person’s ultimate outcome.

“Eating fire” is a powerful image of the connection between human conduct and its consequences. Someone who unjustly consumes an orphan’s property or conceals truth for gain acquires something apparently beneficial, but the Qur’an expresses its outcome through fire. This image should not be turned into a theory about the physical conversion of food into fire or the structure of the body in the world to come; the reality and laws of that world are not clear to us. Conversely, the punishment and fire described in the Qur’an should not be reduced to regret and psychological suffering in worldly life alone. The verse plainly warns of a real and painful outcome, even though we do not know its nature.

The gravity of this warning becomes apparent through another deprivation: “وَلَا يُكَلِّمُهُمُ اللَّهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ”. Someone who concealed God’s revealed words in worldly life and withheld truth to please others will face deprivation of divine speech on the Day of Resurrection. This correspondence is morally significant: the person who made God’s words an object of trade is deprived of the Lord’s address on the Day of Account. In Ali Imran 3:77, those who sell the divine covenant and their oaths for a small price face a similar warning about God not speaking to them and their deprivation of purification. In the context of judgment and punishment, this deprivation indicates exclusion from divine words of acceptance and honor; yet the nature of God’s speech in the hereafter cannot be equated with the way human beings speak in this world.

“وَلَا يُزَكِّيهِمْ” is another warning: God will not purify them. Purification is connected with purity, growth, and flourishing. Divine revelation has come to guide and reform human beings, but someone who knowingly conceals it turns the truth that should enable reform into a tool of self-interest. The verse now speaks of their ultimate deprivation of divine purification. Alongside God not speaking to them and painful punishment, this deprivation reveals the gravity of betraying the trust of revelation.

Despite this firm warning, the path of return is not closed to someone who abandons such conduct. In Al-Baqarah 2:160, after condemning those who conceal truth, God identifies the path of repentance, reform, and disclosure. Someone who has concealed truth does not remedy its effects through verbal remorse alone; they must make clear what they hid and, as far as they can, repair the harm done to others. These verses make clear the difference between persisting in concealment and returning with reform.

No religious rank, economic benefit, inherited legal opinion, or political interest can authorize the concealment of revealed truth. Those who teach and explain religion are entrusted with truth; they do not own it. Whenever God’s words are concealed to preserve human power, wealth, fame, or standing, the very exchange whose outcome the Qur’an describes as eating fire takes place. Whatever is gained by selling truth, however valuable it appears in this world, is a small price beside a person’s responsibility before The God.
