Al-Baqarah 2:186

ۖ ۖ

And when My servants ask you about Me, I am near; I answer the caller’s prayer when they call upon Me. So they too must respond to My invitation and believe in Me, so that they may find the right way.

Our Reflection

What distance exists between a person and their Lord that would require an intermediary to speak to Him? In this verse, when His servants ask Muhammad about Him, The God answers directly: “فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ”—I am near. He does not even say, “Tell them that I am near,” but gives the answer immediately in His own voice. It is as though He wants His servants to know that reaching Him does not require passing through anyone else’s position or authority.

He then says: “أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ”—I answer the caller’s prayer when they call upon Me. No condition is set here requiring religious rank, membership in a particular family, approaching a leader, or seeking an intercessor. The God presents Himself as near and responsive. Neither the prophet nor anyone else controls the answering of prayers or holds authority over The God’s mercy and forgiveness.

Yet throughout history people have repeatedly placed intermediaries between themselves and their Lord. In Jewish religious tradition, the Temple and priests of Aaron’s lineage acquired a special place in religious rites; in some Christian traditions, saints also became intercessors whose mediation with God is requested. Jesus called people to serve The God, and Muhammad brought the same call to divine unity. Sadly, however, among their followers these prophets themselves, members of their families, and other sacred figures later became intermediaries for drawing near to God and making requests of Him.

The Quran, however, recounts in Az-Zumar 39:3 the claim of those who said that they worshiped their deities and protectors only to bring them nearer to The God. In Yunus 10:18, it also speaks of people who described other beings as their intercessors with The God. But in Az-Zumar 39:44, The God declares that all intercession belongs to Him. Even if intercession is possible within the scope of divine permission, a command to seek and invoke intermediaries cannot be derived from that possibility. This issue is examined in detail in the article “Intercession in the Quran: Divine Permission, Human Responsibility, and a Direct Relationship with The God”.

The end of the verse further clarifies the relationship of servanthood: “فَلْيَسْتَجِيبُوا لِي وَلْيُؤْمِنُوا بِي لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْشُدُونَ”. The same Lord who answers His servants’ prayers also calls them to respond to His invitation and believe in Him. This relationship is not one-sided: people do not call upon The God merely to have their wishes fulfilled; they must also accept His guidance and respond to it through their conduct. Rushd, from the root ر ش د, is connected with finding the way and attaining what is right and sound. Human prayer is thus connected with faith, acceptance of guidance, and growth in right judgment.

Throughout the Quran, The God has set out various prayers for His servants and taught them how to call upon their Lord. Surah Al-Fatihah may be regarded as the mother of these prayers: in it, a person stands directly before their Lord and says, “إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ”—You alone we serve, and You alone we ask for help (Al-Fatihah 1:5). Immediately afterward, they ask Him directly for guidance: “اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ” (Al-Fatihah 1:6). No intermediary is named, and no other servant is asked to convey the prayer to the Lord.

The same pattern appears in other Quranic prayers. The God’s servants repeatedly address their Lord directly as “رَبَّنَا” or “رَبِّ”. In Al-Baqarah 2:201, they ask Him Himself for good in this world and the Hereafter; in Aal Imran 3:8, they ask Him not to let their hearts deviate after guidance; and in Al-Furqan 25:74, they ask Him for the well-being and righteousness of their families and children. In these prayers, the Lord Himself is addressed, not an intermediary who must carry the request to Him.

Even the magicians at Pharaoh’s court, when the truth became clear to them and they believed in their Lord’s signs, called directly upon The God despite Pharaoh’s severe threats: “رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَتَوَفَّنَا مُسْلِمِينَ”—Our Lord, pour patience upon us and cause us to die in submission to You (Al-Araf 7:126). They did not say, “O Musa, ask your Lord to send patience upon us through your blessing.” They recognized the Lord themselves and turned directly toward Him.

By contrast, earlier verses of this same surah show different behavior among the Children of Israel. In Al-Baqarah 2:61, they said to Musa, “فَادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ”—ask your Lord for us. In Al-Baqarah 2:68, Al-Baqarah 2:69, and Al-Baqarah 2:70, they repeatedly used the same expression, “ادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ”. Instead of turning to The God themselves with the address “our Lord,” they continually asked Musa to make requests of “your Lord” on their behalf.

This behavior appears in the context of verses recounting the Children of Israel’s ingratitude, excuse-making, and disobedience. Asking another person to pray for one’s well-being is not in itself the same as turning that person into a necessary intermediary between servant and God. Nevertheless, the repetition of “your Lord” in such a context, set against the direct invitation in Al-Baqarah 2:186, reveals a contrast worth reflecting upon. Musa conveyed The God’s command; he did not control the path of communication between servants and their Lord.

In the Quran, The God has placed no necessary intermediary between Himself and His servants’ prayers. He Himself is near, hears, and answers. The prophets came to convey guidance, not to become gatekeepers of divine mercy after their departure. Those who know their Lord do not need to make another person’s name, rank, or blessing a condition for their prayers to be heard.

The God has made the way clear: “فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ”. The servant should therefore call upon Him directly, respond to His invitation, and believe in Him, so that they may find the path of right guidance.

Cross-references
  • Al-Fatihah 1:5 — “إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ”: serving The God and seeking His help directly.
  • Al-Fatihah 1:6 — A direct request for guidance to the straight path.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:61 — The Children of Israel’s request to Musa: “فَادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ”.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:68 — Repeating the request that Musa ask his Lord.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:69 — The Children of Israel’s renewed request: “ادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ”.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:70 — Continued excuse-making and asking Musa to question his Lord.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:201 — Direct prayer for good in this world and the Hereafter, addressing “our Lord.”
  • Aal Imran 3:8 — Direct prayer for hearts to remain firm upon guidance.
  • Al-Araf 7:126 — The believing magicians at Pharaoh’s court praying directly for patience and submission.
  • Yunus 10:18 — Criticism of those who describe other deities as their intercessors with The God.
  • Al-Isra 17:57 — The beings invoked are themselves seeking a means of approach to their Lord.
  • Al-Furqan 25:74 — Direct prayer for the well-being and righteousness of families and children.
  • Az-Zumar 39:3 — Criticism of making intermediaries under the pretext of drawing nearer to The God.
  • Az-Zumar 39:44 — All intercession belonging to The God.
  • Ghafir 40:60 — The direct command “ادْعُونِي أَسْتَجِبْ لَكُمْ”: call upon Me and I will answer you.
  • Al-Maidah 5:35 — The command to seek a means of approach to The God, not to make servants independent recipients of prayer.
