Al-Baqarah 2:85

ۚ ۚ ۖ ۗ

Then here you are, killing one another and driving a group of your own people out of their homes, supporting one another against them in sin and aggression. And if they come to you as captives, you pay a ransom for their release, while driving them out is forbidden to you. Do you then believe in part of the Book and reject part? The recompense of whoever among you does this is nothing but disgrace in the life of this world; and on the Day of Resurrection they will be returned to the severest punishment. And The God (Allah) is not unaware of what you do.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse stated the covenant: do not shed one another’s blood, and do not drive one another out of your homes. The addressees had acknowledged it and borne witness to it. Now, with “Then here you are,” their conduct is placed against that commitment. The very community that had accepted the inviolability of its members’ lives and homes was killing one another and driving a group of its own people out of their homes. The issue is the distance between a known covenant and conduct that violates it.

The statement “you kill yourselves” expresses killing one another as killing “yourselves.” It is followed by “a group of you”: a group of your own people. Those targeted for expulsion are not outside the community or the covenant. Disagreement and hostility do not dissolve the bond through which a commitment was made to protect their lives and homes. We cannot count others as our own when claiming unity, then exclude them from our responsibility when violating their rights.

The expression “you support one another against them in sin and aggression” shows that this aggression involved mutual support. Supporting one another (taẓāharūna), from the root Ẓ H R, here means helping and backing one another. The Arabic word for the back (ẓahr) belongs to this root as well; the image of giving one another backing makes the sense of support tangible. Yet the rest of the sentence makes the direction of this support clear: against a group of their own people, in sin and aggression. Collective power was deployed against members of the community instead of protecting them.

Sin (ithm) means wrongdoing, while aggression (ʿudwān) concerns transgressing the boundaries and rights of others. In Al-Ma’idah 5:2, these same two words are set against cooperation in goodness and mindful guarding: “Help one another in goodness and mindful guarding, and do not help one another in sin and aggression.” Solidarity is therefore not a virtue in itself; we must ask what it serves and against whom it is formed. Someone who places their power, wealth, command, or influence at the service of aggression does not escape responsibility for cooperation by claiming not to have carried it out directly.

Against this conduct, the verse presents another action: “And if they come to you as captives, you pay a ransom for their release.” The Arabic word for captives (usārā) is a plural of the word for a captive. The root A S R is associated with binding and taking into captivity; a captive is someone whose freedom has come under another’s control. A ransom (fidyah) is the compensation given for that person’s release. They spent resources to restore their captives’ freedom, yet in another part of their conduct violated the inviolability of the homes and lives of members of that same community.

The Qur’an does not condemn freeing a captive. Alongside it, the Qur’an reminds them: “While driving them out is forbidden to you.” The very person for whose release you felt a religious responsibility already had a right not to be driven from their home. One cannot take one command seriously and invalidate another, then treat the fulfillment of the commendable part as evidence of faithfulness to the whole covenant. The right action of freeing a captive does not make unjust expulsion right.

The verse’s central question arises from this contradiction: “Do you then believe in part of the Book and reject part?” The Book was not unknown to them, nor had they set all of it aside. They accepted and practiced part of it, while in practice invalidating the part that prohibited their conduct. The Qur’an places this selective acceptance against their claim to believe in the Book.

The root K F R is associated with covering. In this sentence, rejection (kufr) stands opposite believing in the Book and concerns refusing to accept part of it. Their conduct shows them setting aside the prohibition on killing and expulsion while accepting responsibility for releasing captives. The issue is not merely a lapse that a person recognizes as wrong and tries to correct. The danger lies in claiming a right to choose, treating one part of the Book as valid and another as invalid. Before this warning becomes a means of judging others, it must bring our own way of dealing with the Book into view.

The Qur’an’s standard does not change in response to interest or hostility. In An-Nisa 4:135, believers are called to stand firmly for justice even against themselves, their parents, or their relatives. In Al-Ma’idah 5:8, hostility toward a group must likewise not lead them into injustice. Closeness to us therefore does not authorize exemption from justice, and hostility toward us does not authorize aggression. The Book that imposes obligations on others also obliges us and those who hold power.

From here, religious conduct today can be assessed. An individual or government may be strict about clothing, food, and rituals, yet show no comparable concern for keeping covenants, respecting life, and observing others’ rights. Enforcing numerous religious regulations does not answer this contradiction. The question is not how much religion has been put on display; it is whether The God’s command remains valid when it restricts our own conduct and interests.

The Taliban government in Afghanistan is one example to which this standard can be applied. An apparatus for promoting virtue and preventing vice has been deployed to regulate people’s clothing and everyday conduct, while cases of unlawful killing, torture, and violations of the declared amnesty have also been documented in the conduct of this government’s own forces. The name “Islamic Emirate” cannot cover this contradiction. A government that demands religious obedience from people must itself answer for the inviolability of life, keeping covenants, and justice. Strictness in what it calls religious law does not authorize it to violate these commitments.

When commands that restrict the public are enforced with power while commands that restrict the ruler are set aside, this selection serves power: people remain bound by obligations, while the holder of power is kept away from the same measure. In such a situation, religion becomes a means of demanding obedience from others without the government considering itself equally obliged to obey. Justice, keeping covenants, and the inviolability of people’s lives and homes are not peripheral matters of religion that can be surrendered to the government’s expediency.

The same contradiction is visible in a community’s conduct. A community may collect aid for displaced Muslims elsewhere while supporting the expulsion of a Muslim family from its own city. It may demand freedom for a captive of the same faith, yet remain silent about the unjust imprisonment of its own opponent or invent justifications for it. When aid and sympathy prevent a person from seeing their own wrongdoing, they conceal the very blind spot this verse has exposed: another person’s suffering matters in one situation, while that same person’s rights are set aside in another.

This is the danger examined in the context of religious judgment in the article Blind to Ourselves, Quick to Judge Others: A Critique of the Double Standard of the Adherents of Religious Opinion and Hadith in the Question of Salvation. We use the verse to condemn the errors of earlier people, but when the same pattern appears in the conduct of our own people, group, or government, we give it another name. The standard is consistent only when we ask ourselves the same question, rather than reading the Book solely against others.

After exposing this contradiction, the verse announces the recompense: “The recompense of whoever among you does this is nothing but disgrace in the life of this world.” Disgrace (khizy) means humiliation and dishonor, an outcome at odds with claims to religious dignity and faithfulness to the Book. A religious name and appearance cannot remove the shame of conduct contrary to that very Book. The expression “whoever among you does this” also ties the recompense to committing this conduct, not to the name of a people.

The statement does not end with this world: “And on the Day of Resurrection they will be returned to the severest punishment.” This warning shows that accepting part of the Book does not create immunity for setting aside another part. The same covenant that respected human lives and homes is not made ineffective by selective conduct, and responsibility for violating it remains.

The end of the verse once again places action before The God’s knowledge: “And The God is not unaware of what you do.” What a person adorns with religious names does not become separate from the reality of their action. A claim to safeguard the Book does not hide killing and displacement from The God’s sight, just as a good deed does not grant permission to continue wrongdoing.

Faith in the Book means accepting its judgment when it restricts and holds us accountable as well. We cannot demand obedience from others while creating exceptions for our own power, prejudice, and interest. The Book must be the standard for our conduct; our preferred conduct must not determine which part of the Book remains valid.
