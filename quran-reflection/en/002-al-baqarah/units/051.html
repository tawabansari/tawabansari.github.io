Al-Baqarah 2:51

And when We appointed a promise (wāʿadnā) with Musa for forty nights (laylah), then after him you adopted the calf (ʿijl), while you were wrongdoers (ẓālimūn).

Our Reflection

This verse comes exactly after the verse in which the Children of Israel were observing the splitting of the sea, their own rescue, and the drowning of the people of Pharaoh. In verse 50, we saw that they were watching. But now in verse 51, it becomes clear that merely watching a sign and a miracle is not enough to remain faithful to the covenant with the One who is worshiped. Divine miracles, if they do not become living remembrance in the heart, real gratitude, khushu', and deep reflection inside the human being, quickly lose their color and collapse at the first period of a leader's absence, in a season of waiting, or under collective pressure.

The verse begins with wa-idh, and when. This means that this event too is part of the same bitter and sweet memory of ni'mahs and failures. The Qur'an does not limit the memory of the Children of Israel to the sweet moment of liberation from Pharaoh. Immediately, it takes them by the hand and brings them to the point where, right after rescue, their faith slipped in the test of tawhid. The arrangement and order of the verses is extremely precise: first rescue from Pharaoh's torture, then the splitting of the sea, then watching the drowning of the people of Pharaoh, and after that Musa's appointed meeting, and finally the making of the calf. This means that their problem was not a shortage of miracles and signs. The problem was that the immense scenes they saw with their own eyes had not yet become a rooted, heartfelt faithfulness.

The phrase wāʿadnā Mūsā shows that Musa was invited to a divine meeting place. Wāʿadnā comes from the root W ʿ D, which carries the meaning of setting an appointment, making a promise, and entering a defined time and path. This separation was not simple disappearance or an ordinary absence. Musa had gone to the appointed meeting of his Lord. So the people were placed in a condition where their constant leader was no longer in front of their eyes, even though the reality of guidance, the sweet taste of rescue, the signs, and the remembrance of God all remained.

This is exactly where the expression arbaʿīna laylah, forty nights, becomes important. The Qur'an does not say that Musa went to the appointment for “forty days.” It says “forty nights.” In the present Torah, in Exodus and Deuteronomy, this appointment appears through the well-known expression “forty days and forty nights,” and Deuteronomy also refers to Musa's bodily hardship, hunger, and thirst, that he did not eat bread or drink water. But the Qur'an does not repeat this formula. In 2:51 it gives the expression “forty nights,” and in 7:142 it opens the details more fully: thirty nights, completed by ten more nights, until the appointment of his Lord reached forty nights.

This subtle difference should not be passed over casually. If our measure is the Qur'anic text, the primary unit in Musa's appointment is night (layl). Of course, this does not mean that Musa returned during the days, or that the appointment only belonged to the darkness of night. The text does not say that at all. Rather, the point is that the Qur'an colors the nature and atmosphere of this divine appointment through the image of night, as if the entire period is placed inside the meaning-vessel of night.

Night in the Qur'an is tied to ideas such as covering, calm, the lessening of sensory noise, retreat, and cutting away from the clamor of the day. Day is the field of work, effort, and outward bustle. Night is the time when voices settle and the heart becomes ready for deeper receiving. When the Qur'an describes Musa's appointment as “forty nights,” it moves our mind away from the material and physical side of time toward the quality of covenant, retreat, patience, and readiness to receive revelation.

From this angle, the difference between the Qur'an and the Torah's narration is very interesting. The Torah, by bringing the expression “forty days and forty nights” and mentioning not eating and not drinking, focuses more on bodily hardship and the length of time. But the Qur'an does not make these dimensions the center. It does not highlight Musa's bodily hardship, and it does not explain the appointment through hunger and thirst. The Qur'an places its hand on “night,” meaning that vessel of time more fitting for retreat, intimate prayer, silence, solitude, and readiness for the divine covenant.

This view is harmonious with the wider language of the Qur'an. The Qur'an also relates its own descent to the Night of Decree (Laylat al-Qadr) and to a blessed night (Laylah Mubārakah). This subtlety shows that in the Qur'anic culture, night is the finest vessel for the descent of mercy, the shaping of decree, retreat, and receiving the message of heaven. Therefore, arbaʿīna laylah is not merely a number on a calendar. It is a delicate way of picturing that appointed meeting.

God also commands the final prophet to stand and pray in the heart of the night as preparation for receiving the weighty revelation of the message:

“يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُزَّمِّلُ”

O you wrapped in a garment

Al-Muzzammil 73:1

“قُمِ اللَّيْلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا”

Stand in the night, except a little of it

Al-Muzzammil 73:2

“نِصْفَهُ أَوِ انقُصْ مِنْهُ قَلِيلًا”

Half of it, or subtract from it a little

Al-Muzzammil 73:3

“أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا”

Or add to it, and recite the Qur'an in measured, careful recitation

Al-Muzzammil 73:4

“إِنَّا سَنُلْقِي عَلَيْكَ قَوْلًا ثَقِيلًا”

Indeed, We will soon cast upon you a weighty speech

Al-Muzzammil 73:5;

This command shows that night is the most fitting expanse for the descent of divine speech, because the noise of the day has become quiet and the mind, in the Qur'an's expression in the next verse, enters greater steadiness and complete focus:

“إِنَّ نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ هِيَ أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا وَأَقْوَمُ قِيلًا”

Surely the rising of the night is firmer in step and more upright in speech

Al-Muzzammil 73:6.

In addition, within the Qur'anic ordering of time, night is introduced as a means of calm, covering, and widespread stillness of the senses: “And We made the night a covering” (An-Naba 78:10), and “By the night when it settles” (Ad-Duha 93:2), describing the calming and soothing darkness of night. In harmony with this silence and calm, the prophet's heavenly journey also takes place in the setting of night: “Glory be to the One who carried His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque...” (Al-Isra 17:1). All of this bears witness to the reality that, in the Qur'anic view, night is the unmatched vessel for cutting away from the noise of the earth and for the manifestation of the unseen realm.

In verse 51, Musa too enters an appointed meeting whose nature is marked by night. Now the people's test changes from “seeing clear signs” to “faithfulness during absence and darkness.”

And exactly here their feet slip. A people who had seen the sea split with their own eyes, as soon as Musa moved a little away from their sight, failed in the test of patience and solitude. It is as if their eyes had seen the miracle, but their hearts had not yet become so tied to the One who is worshiped that they would remain firm even in Musa's absence. When the prophet is no longer before the eyes, only then does it become clear whether the people's faith is in God Himself or merely resting on the physical presence of a leader. Whenever God tells the signs of earlier communities, it is not only to blame the Children of Israel; it is also a reminder and remembrance for us, because after the death of the final prophet, his companions and supporters can also repeat the same basic mistakes. Just as in the time of Abu Bakr, compulsion and coercion entered religion, and purifying social responsibility (zakat) was turned into taxation.

Then the verse says: thumma ittakhadhtum al-ʿijl, then you took the calf. The word thumma, then, brings this sudden turn into view: after all those ni'mahs, liberation, and direct watching, you went and took the calf! The expression ittakhadhtum is extremely important. The Qur'an does not say only that a calf was made. It says, “you took it for yourselves.” Taking here means choosing something as a point of reliance, raising it up, and seating it in a special position. So their calf-worship was not merely a simple mistake or being fascinated by a statue. It was a collective choice to make it the center of attention and the object of worship.

The word al-ʿijl, the calf, also needs to be understood carefully in this context. Elsewhere the Qur'an explains that they made from their gold and ornaments the statue of a calf that had a sound like lowing. This means that the calf was something tangible, visible, pointable, and with an audible effect. After the God who had rescued them through unseen ways, they stretched their hands again toward a material and visible form.

Here the central knot of the story opens. In verse 50, they were observers. In verse 51, when they no longer see Musa, they begin making something they can observe with their eyes. This turn from a divine sign to a handmade statue shows that their problem was not only illiteracy or lack of information. It was an inability to become free from tangible and material things. They had been freed from the political Pharaoh, but they had not yet been freed from the need for a tangible and material deity.

The calf here is not only an animal or a statue. It is a symbol of psychological return to the world of material things, things that can be touched and possessed. The people of Pharaoh had drowned, but the craving for a material, noisy, close, and tangible symbol was still alive deep in the soul of this community. The calf was, in reality, a small religious reconstruction of the same tendency toward materialism: something around which people could gather, something they could look at, show to others, and use to feel a false security.

The expression min baʿdihi, after him, also has an important meaning: after Musa left, after his physical presence was removed from among them. This shows that their failure was not merely a belief-problem on paper. They were deeply dependent on Musa's physical presence. As long as Musa was there, he controlled the situation. But as soon as he left, everything they had hidden inside poured out. Faith that is tied only to the physical presence of a leader collapses when he goes.

Of course, here we must be careful about a common mistake: this verse does not say that the Children of Israel are inherently and forever oppressive or wrongdoers. Just as superiority and favor in the previous verses were not racial and permanent qualities, wrongdoing is not an essential trait either. The Qur'an says wa-antum ẓālimūn, while you were wrongdoers; meaning that in that situation and through that act, you did wrong. In the Qur'anic view, both favor and wrongdoing depend on human choices, actions, and beliefs. A society can become worthy of divine favor through faithfulness to the covenant, and through shirk and calf-worship it can pull itself into wrongdoing.

This point is the key to understanding this whole section. In verse 47, the Children of Israel are faced with the remembrance of their worthiness and God's favor, and in verse 51, those same people are called wrongdoers. If favor were inherited and eternal, this shift into wrongdoing would make no sense. And if wrongdoing were an inherent quality, then remembering ni'mahs and inviting to repentance would be useless. The Qur'an makes both conditions dependent on the human being's own choice: worthiness has meaning when the human being answers ni'mah with gratitude and tawhid, and wrongdoing begins when, despite all these signs, the human being leaves truth and turns toward handmade idols.

Zulm here is not just a small personal mistake. The root Ẓ L M means putting something outside its true place and covering truth. After all that rescue, after seeing the miracle of the sea, after the destruction of the people of Pharaoh, and after Musa's covenant with God, they went toward the calf. This means that they did not see the ni'mahs in their proper place, lost the purpose of the miracles, and bent their hearts away from tawhid toward a material idol. This act is exactly zulm: wrongdoing against God by making a partner for Him, wrongdoing against the self by falling after all that guidance, and wrongdoing against society by damaging the path of freedom.

If we look at the flow of the story, calf-worship immediately after liberation from Pharaoh's grip becomes deeply unsettling. The Pharaonic system was built on material power, outward awe, the whip, and physical enslavement. The Children of Israel were freed from that bondage, but as soon as Musa was absent, the same old need for a material and tangible power woke up inside them. The calf was, in reality, the religious and small-scale version of the same system of captivity: something made by their own hands, visible, touchable, and attractive for gathering the masses.

This verse teaches us a major lesson: being released from Pharaoh's grip is not enough; the inner Pharaoh must also be removed. External rescue, if it is not accompanied by the purification of religious thought and mind, can still leave the human being building idols even after crossing through the middle of the sea. The only difference is that this time Pharaoh's iron chains are replaced by a golden calf, but the result is the same: distance from true freedom and tawhid.

Therefore, verse 51 is not only the narration of a historical failure in the past. It shows us that the greatest danger after every success is that the great experiences of life do not become a lasting and deep conviction. The Children of Israel saw the miracle, crossed, were rescued, but in the first absence of Musa they took refuge in the calf. This means that seeing signs, if it is not joined with reflection, living remembrance in the heart, and khushu', creates no guarantee for the future.

In the end, verse 51 is the natural and logical continuation of verse 50. They saw the miracle, but after that they took the calf. One day they were worthy of divine favor, but through this choice they stood in the position of wrongdoing. This movement back and forth clarifies the Qur'an's main criterion: neither ethnic superiority is permanent, nor is wrongdoing an eternal racial quality. Human beings and societies build their position through their moment-by-moment decisions. Divine favor remains alive when it ends in tawhid and faithfulness; and if its place is given to idol-worship and forgetfulness, that same chosen society will stand among the wrongdoers.
