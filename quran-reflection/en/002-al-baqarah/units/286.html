Al-Baqarah 2:286

ۚ ۗ ۚ ۚ ۖ ۚ

The God does not charge any soul except according to its capacity; for it is what it has earned, and against it is what it has acquired. Our Lord, do not take us to task if we forget or make a mistake. Our Lord, do not lay upon us a heavy burden as You laid upon those before us. Our Lord, do not impose upon us what we have no strength to bear; and pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy upon us. You are our guardian, so help us against the disbelieving people.

Our Reflection

With this verse, Surah Al-Baqarah states one of the most fundamental principles of divine guidance: God does not impose responsibility on any human being beyond their capacity.

In Al-Baqarah 2:284, the Quran spoke of accounting for outward and hidden deeds. In Al-Baqarah 2:285, the Messenger and the believers declared their faith and obedience and sought forgiveness. Now, at the surah’s conclusion, God clarifies the limits of human responsibility and teaches His servants a prayer in which they ask their Lord for pardon, forgiveness, mercy, and help.

“لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا”

“The God does not charge any soul except according to its capacity.”

“يُكَلِّفُ,” from the root K L F, concerns imposing an obligation, while “وُسْع,” from W S ʿ, refers to capacity and ability.

This is not merely advice to be lenient; it is a clear statement about how God imposes obligations. People differ in physical ability, health, knowledge, resources, living environment, and social circumstances. What is easy for one person may be very difficult or even impossible for another.

For example, consider someone born into a poor family who spends most of their time and energy securing food, shelter, and life’s basic necessities. They have neither sufficient resources for education nor adequate opportunities to read, research, and expand their knowledge. Another person, by contrast, grows up in a wealthy family, studies at the best universities, has access to educational and scholarly resources, and enjoys abundant opportunities for intellectual development and recognizing truth. Is it just to hold these two people accountable by an identical measure despite such vast differences in resources, knowledge, and opportunities? God, who charges no one beyond their capacity, knows each person’s circumstances, limitations, opportunities, and actual knowledge. Each person’s responsibility must therefore be assessed according to what they were capable of knowing and doing, not according to resources and abilities they never possessed.

This principle recurs throughout the Quran. In Al-Baqarah 2:185, God desires ease for people, not hardship. In An-Nisa 4:28, He speaks of His will to lighten their burden, and in Al-Hajj 22:78, He denies placing hardship and constraint in religion.

Therefore, no individual, leader, or institution has the right to impose strictures and restrictions that God has not prescribed on others in the name of religious law, especially when those obligations exceed people’s actual capacities.

This subject is examined in “The Principle of Responsibility According to Capacity” and “The Principle of Ease and the Removal of Hardship in Religion”.

Each person is responsible for their own earnings

“لَهَا مَا كَسَبَتْ وَعَلَيْهَا مَا اكْتَسَبَتْ”

“For it is what it has earned, and against it is what it has acquired.”

“كَسَبَتْ” and “اكْتَسَبَتْ” both come from K S B and refer to what a person acquires through their deeds, but appear in two different morphological forms.

In this verse, “لَهَا” emphasizes what benefits a person, while “عَلَيْهَا” emphasizes what returns to their detriment and responsibility. The difference between the two verbs should not lead us to conclude that K S B is always confined to good deeds: the Quran also uses this root for wrongdoing, as in Al-Baqarah 2:81.

A person is therefore responsible for their own deeds. Their goodness returns to their benefit, and they bear the consequences of their wrongdoing. This accords with Al-Anam 6:164, which distinguishes each person’s responsibility for sin from that of others.

Yet the beginning of the verse limits this responsibility to a person’s capacity. Their ability, knowledge, and actual circumstances therefore cannot be disregarded in divine accounting.

Prayer directly to God: distinguishing forgetfulness, error, and deliberate denial

“رَبَّنَا لَا تُؤَاخِذْنَا إِنْ نَسِينَا أَوْ أَخْطَأْنَا”

“Our Lord, do not take us to task if we forget or make a mistake.”

This is the first request in the surah’s closing prayer. Without an intermediary, the believers address their Lord directly as “رَبَّنَا.”

“نَسِينَا,” from N S Y, refers to forgetting, and “أَخْطَأْنَا,” from KH Ṭ A, to making a mistake. These situations should not be equated with consciously denying truth.

A person may err through ignorance, misunderstanding, or forgetfulness. Sometimes, intending to do what is right, they choose a path they later recognize as mistaken. Such a person is not the same as someone who has recognized the truth and consciously conceals it to preserve personal interests, reputation, power, or prejudice.

In Al-Ahzab 33:5, the Quran distinguishes unintended error from what hearts deliberately intend. In Al-Baqarah 2:225, it likewise distinguishes unintentional speech from what the heart consciously earns.

This distinction is particularly important for understanding kufr. Kufr should not be reduced to every instance of not knowing, making a mistake, or differing in understanding. Someone who has not yet properly recognized the truth is not in the same situation as someone who, after recognizing it clearly, persists in denial and concealment.

Even we ourselves may believe an incorrect understanding for years. Once we recognize our error, however, our response matters: do we correct it, or knowingly refuse to accept the truth out of fear of people, loss of standing, or personal interests?

For a broader discussion, see “Who Is a Kafir, and What Is Kufr?”.

Seeking release from heavy burdens and obligations beyond endurance

“رَبَّنَا وَلَا تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا إِصْرًا كَمَا حَمَلْتَهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا”

“Our Lord, do not lay upon us a heavy burden as You laid upon those before us.”

“إِصْر,” from A Ṣ R, refers to a heavy burden or binding obligation that places a person under constraint.

This request connects with Al-Araf 7:157, where the Quran speaks of removing heavy burdens and shackles that had been upon people.

For example, in An-Nisa 4:160, the Quran speaks of certain good things being forbidden to groups among the Children of Israel because of their wrongdoing, and in Al-Anam 6:146, it describes some of their dietary restrictions. These verses illuminate part of the Quranic background to earlier peoples’ heavy burdens without confining “إِصْر” to those restrictions alone.

Heavy burdens can affect human life in different forms. Religious strictures, inherited prejudices, social restrictions, and rulings attributed to God without evidence can entrap people and deprive them of opportunities to grow and reform. These are examples for reflection, however, and the entire meaning of “إِصْر” should not be confined to them.

The prayer then continues:

“رَبَّنَا وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ”

“Our Lord, do not impose upon us what we have no strength to bear.”

“طَاقَة,” from Ṭ W Q, concerns strength and the capacity to bear something. This word has an important connection with the discussion of “يُطِيقُونَهُ” in Al-Baqarah 2:184.

In “Siyam in the Quran: Taqwa, Human Endurance, and the Place of Fidyah”, we examined the difference between the ordinary difficulty of performing an act of worship and a situation in which it becomes exhausting or harmful for a particular person.

Now, at the end of Surah Al-Baqarah, this principle appears again. At the verse’s beginning, God declares that He charges no one beyond their capacity, and then teaches His servants to ask Him not to impose upon them what they cannot bear.

Two matters are distinguished here: the beginning clarifies the limits of human obligation and responsibility before the divine command, while the prayer also includes a request to be spared unbearable burdens, including severe hardships and consequences in life. Praying not to face suffering beyond one’s endurance therefore does not conflict with the principle that a person is not charged beyond their capacity.

How could God, who teaches people this prayer and Himself denies imposing obligations beyond capacity, prescribe a religious command that some servants cannot fulfill under their actual circumstances, and then deem them sinful because of that very inability?

This question matters when assessing many religious interpretations. Whenever a ruling is presented in God’s name that exceeds people’s actual abilities, we should return to the Quran itself and examine whether that hardship comes from God’s command or from interpretations and restrictions people have added to religion.

Religious obligation is not, of course, identical to all life’s suffering and events. The principle of not charging beyond capacity does not, by itself, mean that people will never encounter illness, calamity, or very severe suffering. But none of these situations authorizes people to impose impossible obligations on others in God’s name.

Pardon, forgiveness, and mercy: requests directly to the Lord

“وَاعْفُ عَنَّا وَاغْفِرْ لَنَا وَارْحَمْنَا”

“And pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy upon us.”

This expression brings together three related but distinct requests.

“اعْفُ,” from ʿ F W, refers to pardon and overlooking. “اغْفِرْ,” from GH F R, concerns forgiveness and covering sin, while “ارْحَمْنَا,” from R Ḥ M, asks for mercy and kindness.

The servants do not merely ask to escape being taken to task; they also seek forgiveness and their Lord’s mercy. A person may err, turn back from the error, and strive to reform their conduct, yet still need God’s pardon and mercy.

Another important point is the manner of this prayer. God does not teach believers to make another person a necessary intermediary when seeking pardon, forgiveness, and mercy. From beginning to end, the prayer addresses the Lord directly: “رَبَّنَا”—our Lord.

This is the same direct relationship expressed in Al-Baqarah 2:186, where God recalls His nearness and His response to the caller’s prayer.

For further discussion, see “Intercession in the Quran: Divine Permission, Human Responsibility, and a Direct Relationship with God”.

“أَنْتَ مَوْلَانَا”: The God is our Mawla

“أَنْتَ مَوْلَانَا”

“You are our Mawla.”

“Mawla,” from W L Y, relates to nearness, guardianship, support, and authority. The Quran also uses this word for certain human relationships, but human guardianship should not be equated with the authority and power uniquely belonging to God.

In this Quranic prayer, after asking for pardon, forgiveness, and mercy, believers directly call God their Mawla.

This expression is especially important in supplication. Some followers of religious schools habitually call figures such as Ali, Husayn, or other religious leaders “our Mawla” and turn to them in prayer and requests for their needs.

Yet here the Quran teaches us, in prayer and in seeking forgiveness, mercy, and help, to call The God Himself our Mawla. Why should we place someone else in that position in such supplication and ask them for a need whose ultimate authority is God?

Respecting a person, recognizing their social or religious standing, and asking others for ordinary help do not, in themselves, make them rivals to God. The problem arises when someone assigns a person a position in worship, supplication, or independent divine authority.

This connects directly with “Andad in the Quran: How Do People Set Up Rivals to God?”.

In that study, we examined how someone may recognize God as the sole Creator yet set up a rival to Him in love, worship, following, or attributing uniquely divine authority. Setting up rivals is not confined to worshiping stone idols; sometimes people assign a leader or beloved figure a position that belongs to God alone.

“أَنْتَ مَوْلَانَا” is therefore more than a title; it recalls God’s place in supplication, guardianship, and a person’s ultimate reliance.

Seeking help: first against denial of truth within ourselves, then in society

“فَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ”

“So help us against the disbelieving people.”

“انْصُرْنَا,” from N Ṣ R, refers to helping and supporting, and “الْكَافِرِينَ,” from K F R, concerns those who engage in kufr.

This request for help should not be confined to victory on a battlefield. The struggle between truth and its denial has wider dimensions in human life.

The first field of struggle is within the person.

Before looking for kufr and denial of truth in others, a person must confront their own inner inclinations. They may recognize a truth, yet resist accepting it because of pride, fear, prejudice, greed, or self-interest.

For example, someone knows they have wronged another person but considers admitting the mistake harmful to their reputation. Another knows that a claim they defended for years was mistaken, but refuses to correct it for fear of losing their position. Someone may even read a clear Quranic verse yet try to disregard its truth because it conflicts with an inherited belief or personal desire.

In such situations, a person’s real struggle is against desires that seek to prevent acceptance of truth. This is the great human struggle with one’s own self, a struggle that must begin within oneself before judging others.

In An-Naziat 79:40, the Quran speaks of someone who restrains the self from following its desires. Yusuf 12:53 also warns that the self can command a person to evil unless the Lord’s mercy reaches them.

This struggle does not end, because people continually face choices between personal desire and recognized truth.

For a broader discussion, see the principle of governing one’s desires.

But the struggle between truth and its denial does not occur only within us. Outside us, this confrontation also continues at home, at school, in the workplace, in the marketplace, in politics, in government, and in society.

A student may face lying and cheating; an employee may encounter bribery, corruption, or an unjust order; someone within a family may need to choose between partisan support for relatives and respecting another’s right. In society and politics, people may conceal truth or oppose reform and justice to preserve power and interests.

In all these situations, a person needs God’s help to recognize truth, remain steadfast under pressure, and avoid becoming an instrument of injustice and concealment themselves.

The direct meaning of “الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ” does, however, refer to a group of human beings. The inward struggle against desire must therefore not replace the phrase’s meaning; this reflection instead recalls our responsibility toward the same denial of truth that can also appear in our own conduct.

Nor should every non-Muslim, intellectual opponent, or person with a different understanding of truth be regarded as an enemy to overcome. There is a difference between someone who does not know or sincerely seeks truth and someone who knowingly conceals and opposes it.

In Al-Mumtahanah 60:8, the Quran does not prohibit kindness and justice toward those who do not fight believers because of religion or expel them from their homes.

Seeking God’s help therefore does not authorize hostility toward people on the basis of their religious or sectarian name.

We ask The God to help us first overcome prejudice, pride, and the tendency to conceal truth within ourselves, and then remain steadfast in social life against injustice, deception, and deliberate denial of truth.

This struggle does not end when a war ends or a group is defeated. As long as people possess choice and can choose between truth and falsehood, their responsibility to recognize and uphold truth continues.

The closing message of Surah Al-Baqarah is that God does not charge people beyond their capacity, each person is responsible for their own earnings, and unintended error differs from deliberate denial of truth. God teaches His servants to seek pardon, forgiveness, mercy, and help directly from Him and to recognize Him as their Mawla. This help is not only for confronting external enemies: before all else, a person must overcome prejudice, pride, and the tendency to conceal truth within themselves, and then support truth and justice in society.
