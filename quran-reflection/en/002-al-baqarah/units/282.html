Al-Baqarah 2:282

ۚ ۚ ۚ ۚ ۚ ۖ ۚ ۚ ۚ ۖ ۗ ۚ ۚ ۗ ۖ ۗ

O you who have believed, when you enter into a debt with one another for a specified term, write it down; and let a scribe write between you with justice. Let no scribe refuse to write as The God has taught him; let him write, and let the one upon whom the right is due dictate, and let him be mindful of The God, his Lord, and diminish nothing of it. If the one upon whom the right is due lacks sound judgment, is weak, or cannot dictate himself, let his guardian dictate with justice. And call upon two witnesses from among your men; if there are not two men, then a man and two women from among the witnesses you approve, so that if one of the two errs, the other may remind her. Let the witnesses not refuse when they are called. Do not tire of writing it down, small or large, up to its due date. That is more just before The God, firmer for testimony, and more likely to keep you from doubt; unless it is immediate trade that you conduct among yourselves, in which case there is no blame upon you for not writing it down. And take witnesses when you trade with one another; and let neither a scribe nor a witness be harmed; if you do so, it is disobedience on your part. And be mindful of The God; and The God teaches you; and The God knows all things.

Our Reflection

This verse, the longest in the Quran, follows a series of verses about giving, charities, riba, and people’s financial rights. In Al-Baqarah 2:275, the Quran distinguished trade from riba; in Al-Baqarah 2:279, it addressed the principal and the rejection of injustice on both sides; and in Al-Baqarah 2:280, it commanded that a debtor in hardship be given time until relief. Now, with the direct address “يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا”, the Quran calls believers to observe clear principles in arranging financial obligations. Thus, the discussion moves from giving and charities to riba and its distinction from trade, and then connects with recording financial obligations, protecting both parties’ rights, and preventing disputes.

Recording obligations: a command to protect rights and prevent disputes

“إِذَا تَدَايَنتُم بِدَيْنٍ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى فَاكْتُبُوهُ”

“When you enter into a debt with one another for a specified term, write it down.”

“دَيْن” should not be confined to borrowing money. “تَدَايَنتُم,” from the root D Y N in Form VI, here refers to establishing a debt relationship between parties. Such an obligation may arise from a loan, a credit purchase, an advance sale, or other transactions in which payment or performance is deferred. “أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى” is a specified, agreed deadline.

The essential point is in “فَاكْتُبُوهُ.” God uses an imperative to command that this obligation be written down. This should not be reduced to an insignificant formality or an ordinary suggestion. Recording debt is part of the Quran’s clear command to safeguard people’s rights. The verse itself makes an exception for immediate trade, and the next verse considers travel, the absence of a scribe, and mutual trust; none of these diminishes the importance of the principal command to record obligations with a deferred term.

This divine emphasis on writing raises a further question concerning the preservation and transmission of religious rulings: If God gives such a clear command to write down a financial obligation with a specified term to prevent disputes, how can we accept that He left essential guidance and legislation, on which the fate of future generations depends, outside His Book and entrusted them to human memory and oral transmission from generation to generation, without a clear command to record and preserve them? Human memory may fail, transmitters may make mistakes, and people’s words may undergo alteration, addition, or omission over time. How can such reports become an independent authority for determining what God has made lawful or forbidden?

This reflection connects with verses about the Quran itself. In Al-Anam 6:114, God speaks of a Book sent down in detail; in An-Nahl 16:89, He describes the Quran as a clarification of all things; and in Al-Ankabut 29:51, He recalls the sufficiency of the Book sent down to the Messenger. Thus, just as financial rights should not depend on people’s memories and claims, understanding divine rulings requires returning to the Book God sent down for guidance. The study “The Principle of the Quran’s Completeness and Sufficiency for Religious Guidance” examines this subject in detail.

But the importance of writing is not confined to possible lying or forgetfulness. In everyday life, two friends or brothers may decline to write a contract when lending to one another. One says, “We are not strangers—don’t you trust me?” Afraid of appearing distrustful or ungenerous, the other stops insisting on recording the debt. They may even regard a written document as contrary to friendship, honor, or the local ideal of “kakagi”—loyal, generous conduct.

Yet the Quran does not make the protection of rights depend on such social courtesies. Writing down a debt is not a sign of distrust; it is a means of protecting trust. Two people may sincerely love each other today, but disagree years later about the loan amount, repayment date, or terms. Such disagreement can destroy longstanding friendships, separate brothers, and create hostility between families.

Even if both parties are entirely honest and upright, the future is not under their control. One may die in an accident, leaving heirs unaware of the financial obligation. Another may experience illness, paralysis, brain injury, memory impairment, or forgetfulness. Sometimes no major event occurs; people simply forget details over time. There is also the possibility of lying, denying a debt, forging a document, or deliberate exploitation.

A financial obligation does not come into existence only when it is written down; rights and responsibilities may already exist between two people. But a document records the reality of that obligation clearly and accessibly, so that proving it does not depend solely on the parties’ memory, health, survival, or claims. Even after their deaths, it may serve as evidence of the earlier agreement for their heirs and others whose rights are involved.

Recording an obligation is therefore not merely a way to resolve a dispute after it occurs; it also helps prevent one from arising. This matter is also important in the principle of clarifying and recording financial obligations and the principle of keeping covenants and agreements.

The responsibilities of the scribe, the debtor, and the guardian of someone unable to act

“وَلْيَكْتُبْ بَيْنَكُمْ كَاتِبٌ بِالْعَدْلِ”

“And let a scribe write between you with justice.”

The Quran does not stop at requiring writing; it also makes clear how the writing should be done and who is responsible. The document must be prepared justly: the scribe must not alter the actual agreement, add a right favoring one party, conceal anything, or write terms whose meaning the other party does not understand.

It then says:

“وَلَا يَأْبَ كَاتِبٌ أَنْ يَكْتُبَ كَمَا عَلَّمَهُ اللَّهُ”

“And let no scribe refuse to write as The God has taught him.”

Knowledge and the ability to write are blessings that can protect others’ rights. Someone able to prepare a document correctly should not, without reason, refuse to use that ability in the service of justice. This responsibility must also respect the scribe’s rights, as the verse later prohibits harm to the scribe and witness.

An even more significant point follows:

“وَلْيُمْلِلِ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ الْحَقُّ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ وَلَا يَبْخَسْ مِنْهُ شَيْئًا”

“And let the one upon whom the right is due dictate, and let him be mindful of The God, his Lord, and diminish nothing of it.”

The Quran does not give the creditor sole authority to have whatever they wish written in the document. The person who owes the debt or obligation must also have a direct role in stating it, doing so with taqwa and honesty, without diminishing the other party’s right.

This instruction protects both sides of justice. The creditor must not impose a debt greater than what actually exists, and the debtor must not understate the amount, agreed terms, or their responsibility. This is the principle “لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ” in Al-Baqarah 2:279, now given practical expression in preparing a financial document.

The Quran then considers someone unable to dictate their obligation properly:

“فَإِنْ كَانَ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ الْحَقُّ سَفِيهًا أَوْ ضَعِيفًا أَوْ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُ أَنْ يُمِلَّ هُوَ فَلْيُمْلِلْ وَلِيُّهُ بِالْعَدْلِ”

“If the one upon whom the right is due lacks sound judgment, is weak, or cannot dictate himself, let his guardian dictate with justice.”

“سَفِيهًا,” from the root S F H, refers to deficient judgment and foolishness and should not necessarily be confined to mental illness. “ضَعِيفًا” expresses weakness, while “لَا يَسْتَطِيعُ أَنْ يُمِلَّ” includes those unable to dictate for any reason.

In such a situation, the person’s guardian must state the obligation justly. A person’s inability must therefore not become an opportunity for exploitation. Even when someone cannot properly defend their own right, the responsibility to uphold justice remains.

These commands show that the Quran does not rely on the parties’ good intentions alone. The scribe must be impartial, the debtor must state the actual obligation, the creditor must not demand more than their due, and a person unable to act must not be left without support. Justice is better protected when each person’s responsibility is clear and no one can exploit power, knowledge, or another’s inability to alter the reality of the agreement.

Testimony: protecting truth, not ranking human worth

“وَاسْتَشْهِدُوا شَهِيدَيْنِ مِنْ رِجَالِكُمْ”

“And call upon two witnesses from among your men.”

In addition to a written document, the Quran calls for witnesses. A document may be lost or altered, or disagreement may arise about the circumstances in which it was prepared. The testimony of trustworthy people is therefore another way to protect the truth of the agreement.

It then says:

“فَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُونَا رَجُلَيْنِ فَرَجُلٌ وَامْرَأَتَانِ مِمَّنْ تَرْضَوْنَ مِنَ الشُّهَدَاءِ أَنْ تَضِلَّ إِحْدَاهُمَا فَتُذَكِّرَ إِحْدَاهُمَا الْأُخْرَىٰ”

“If there are not two men, then a man and two women from among the witnesses you approve, so that if one of the two errs, the other may remind her.”

Throughout history, this expression has sometimes been interpreted as though the Quran made a woman’s testimony worth half a man’s in every matter, or described women as inherently having poorer memories. Yet such a general conclusion does not follow from this verse itself.

First, we must preserve the subject of the verse: the Quran is speaking about recording financial obligations with deferred terms and bearing witness to them, not every kind of testimony in every area of life.

Second, the verse itself connects the reason for this arrangement with the possibility of error and reminder through “أَنْ تَضِلَّ إِحْدَاهُمَا فَتُذَكِّرَ إِحْدَاهُمَا الْأُخْرَىٰ.” In this context, “تَضِلَّ” may refer to making a mistake or forgetting the matter witnessed, rather than religious misguidance or an inherent deficiency of intellect.

Third, the social and economic conditions of the verse’s audience may also matter in understanding this arrangement. One possibility worth examining is that differences in women’s and men’s familiarity with transactions and financial documents in that society played a role. Economic dependence and family or social pressure may also have affected the independence of some people’s testimony. However, these historical explanations must not be presented as the definite reason explicitly stated by the verse.

The essential point is that testimony here is a responsibility to protect truth and people’s rights, not a privilege establishing one human being’s inherent superiority over another. This instruction does not establish that every woman, at every time and on every subject, is less capable than every man of knowing the truth or recalling events. At the same time, the specific arrangement of witnesses stated in the verse must not be disregarded.

In An-Nur 24:6, An-Nur 24:7, An-Nur 24:8, and An-Nur 24:9, the Quran sets out a different structure for testimony between spouses. An-Nisa 4:135 also presents the principle of just testimony, even against oneself and close relatives. These verses show that the subject, circumstances, and kind of testimony matter when examining the rulings concerning it.

For a broader understanding of this principle, see the principle of truthful and impartial testimony.

The Quran then says:

“وَلَا يَأْبَ الشُّهَدَاءُ إِذَا مَا دُعُوا”

“And let the witnesses not refuse when they are called.”

Testimony is not merely a title or social privilege; it is a responsibility toward truth and others’ rights. Someone aware of a financial obligation should not, without reason, refuse their role in clarifying the truth. Such refusal may prevent someone from establishing their right or cause another person to be wrongly deemed indebted.

Why must even a small debt be written down?

“وَلَا تَسْأَمُوا أَنْ تَكْتُبُوهُ صَغِيرًا أَوْ كَبِيرًا إِلَىٰ أَجَلِهِ”

“And do not tire of writing it down, small or large, up to its due date.”

The Quran even addresses the excuse that the amount is small. Someone may say that such a minor debt is not worth documenting. But disputes do not always remain proportionate to the amount. A small debt, accompanied by denial, suspicion, or insult, can become a major conflict.

The small size of another person’s right does not make it unimportant. Just as no one should unjustly take another’s substantial property, their small entitlement must also remain protected.

The Quran then explains the reason for these precise commands:

“ذَٰلِكُمْ أَقْسَطُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَأَقْوَمُ لِلشَّهَادَةِ وَأَدْنَىٰ أَلَّا تَرْتَابُوا”

“That is more just before The God, firmer for testimony, and more likely to keep you from doubt.”

Three results appear together: greater justice, firmer testimony, and less doubt.

“أَقْسَطُ,” from the root Q S Ṭ, means more just in this form. “أَقْوَمُ,” from Q W M, concerns firmness, and “تَرْتَابُوا,” from R Y B, points to falling into doubt and suspicion.

Recording a contract therefore does more than provide a document for a future dispute; it also helps prevent disputes. When the debt amount, repayment date, parties’ responsibilities, and witnesses are clear, there is less room for misunderstanding, forgetfulness, changing claims, and suspicion.

This principle remains important today. A written contract, bank receipt, official document, electronic record, and valid digital signature can serve this purpose. These tools do not replace honesty and justice; they help preserve and establish what the parties actually agreed. The means of recording may change over time, but clarity of obligation, firmness of testimony, and preventing violations of rights retain their importance.

The exception for immediate trade and the prohibition of harm

After emphasizing the recording of deferred debt, the Quran distinguishes it from a transaction completed between the parties at that time:

“إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ فَلَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَلَّا تَكْتُبُوهَا”

“Unless it is immediate trade that you conduct among yourselves; then there is no blame upon you for not writing it down.”

For immediate trade in which the exchange occurs on the spot, a written contract is not presented as necessary in the same way as for deferred debt. The expression “فَلَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ” itself makes this exception clear.

But the Quran immediately adds:

“وَأَشْهِدُوا إِذَا تَبَايَعْتُمْ”

“And take witnesses when you trade with one another.”

Permission not to write down immediate trade must therefore not be equated with testimony and the parties’ rights becoming unimportant.

Toward the end it also says:

“وَلَا يُضَارَّ كَاتِبٌ وَلَا شَهِيدٌ”

“And let neither a scribe nor a witness be harmed.”

The Arabic form and reading allow this expression to be examined in two directions: the scribe and witness must not be harmed, and they themselves must not cause harm to others.

For example, no one should threaten a scribe to change a contract in their favor or pressure a witness to conceal the truth. Conversely, a scribe or witness must not violate the parties’ rights through forgery, lies, concealment, or misuse of their position. Justice is not confined to the debtor’s and creditor’s rights; those who help record and establish rights must also be protected from injury and improper pressure.

The Quran then warns:

“وَإِنْ تَفْعَلُوا فَإِنَّهُ فُسُوقٌ بِكُمْ”

“And if you do so, it is disobedience on your part.”

This warning directly follows the prohibition against harming the scribe and witness. “فُسُوق,” from the root F S Q, denotes departing from the command and proper bounds. Harming those who help preserve truth and others’ rights is therefore not merely an administrative or contractual disagreement; it is conduct contrary to the divine command.

The verse ends by recalling taqwa and divine knowledge:

“وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَيُعَلِّمُكُمُ اللَّهُ وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ”

“And be mindful of The God; and The God teaches you; and The God knows all things.”

This conclusion connects all the preceding commands with human responsibility before God. Someone may deceive the other party, alter a document, or conceal the truth from witnesses, but God knows the reality of human conduct. Taqwa in such relations means practical honesty and justice, even when exploitation is possible.

The message of this verse is that faith and taqwa must also become visible in protecting people’s rights. For financial relations with deferred terms, the Quran commands clear recording of obligations, just writing, honesty from the debtor, protection for someone unable to act, trustworthy testimony, and prevention of harm. These commands aim to ensure that friendship, kinship, and people’s trust are not destroyed by forgetfulness, ambiguity, deception, or disputes over property. Justice lies not only in restoring rights after a dispute, but also in preventing their violation.
