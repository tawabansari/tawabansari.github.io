Al-Baqarah 2:170

ۗ

And when they are told, “Follow what God has sent down,” they say, “Rather, we follow what we found our fathers following.” Even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided, would they still follow them?

Our Reflection

People may become so accustomed to inherited beliefs and practices that they regard following them as religion itself, even when those beliefs stand against God’s command. After warning against Satan’s footsteps in Al-Baqarah 2:168 and identifying one of his commands as speaking about God without knowledge in Al-Baqarah 2:169, the Quran presents a clear example of this deviation: people who, when called to follow what God has sent down, reply that they follow their fathers’ way. Two authorities stand opposed here: “مَا أَنزَلَ اللَّهُ,” what God has sent down, and “مَا أَلْفَيْنَا عَلَيْهِ آبَاءَنَا,” what has been inherited from predecessors. The fundamental question is whether people learn their religion from God’s revelation or from the words and conduct of those who lived before them.

“What God has sent down” clearly identifies the standard for following. In Aal Imran 3:3, the Quran also speaks of the revelation of the Torah and Gospel, while Al-Maidah 5:44 and Al-Maidah 5:47 recall revelation given to the People of the Book. Across the Quran, therefore, this expression is not confined to one book; its referent is understood through its audience and context. In Muhammad’s mission, however, the Quran is the Book God sent down to him, described in Al-Maidah 5:48 as confirming and guarding earlier scriptures. Al-Araf 7:3 commands people to follow what has been sent down to them from their Lord and not to follow other protectors instead of Him. The distinction is clear: the source of guidance and final authority in religion is divine revelation, not a statement’s fame, a custom’s antiquity, or the standing of its transmitters.

The opponents’ response begins with “بَلْ”: instead of accepting the first invitation, they prefer another path. They do not claim to have another proof or warrant from God; they say that they found their fathers following this way. It is as though a belief’s age and transmission through generations were sufficient to establish its truth. But does truth arise through the passage of time? Does a statement repeated for centuries become God’s command merely through repetition? Their fathers were also human: they may have understood correctly or erred, been guided or strayed from guidance. A belief’s truth cannot be derived from its history or ancestry.

The Quran itself shows that following predecessors is not inherently blameworthy. In Yusuf 12:38, Joseph speaks of following the faith of his fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; but the value of this following lies in their monotheism and divine path, not merely their fatherhood. If predecessors were right, we should learn the truth from them; if they erred, respect cannot turn error into truth. The Quran does not ask people to deny their past, but to assess it against truth. The problem begins when this standard is reversed: instead of assessing ancestral inheritance against revelation, people make revelation subordinate to that inheritance.

This is not confined to family and ethnic customs. Much of what people today call religion has reached them through leaders’ statements, earlier legal opinions, religious traditions, and reports passed from person to person through chains of transmission. Such reports tell us that one person heard from another, who related it from someone else, until a statement or action is ultimately attributed to the Prophet. However long or famous, such a chain does not in itself bear the seal of “what God has sent down.” The Quran identifies itself as revealed scripture, but nowhere identifies collections such as Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, or other hadith books compiled after the Prophet as books sent down by God. On what grounds, then, should rulings in these human reports occupy revelation’s position and determine what is lawful, forbidden, obligatory, or prohibited for people?

Two questions must be clearly distinguished: whether a report truly comes from the Prophet, and whether its content was a divine command binding on every generation. Even if strong evidence establishes a report’s historical attribution to him, it remains necessary to determine whether the statement expressed God’s permanent ruling or was an explanation, judgment, or decision concerning particular circumstances in people’s lives at that time. Establishing that a statement was heard proves neither its revealed origin nor its ruling’s perpetual scope. If the attribution itself also rests on a chain of human memories and reports, how can it be placed in the position of the Lord’s certain command without addressing these questions?

The distinction between the Messenger and a report attributed to him is fundamental. The Quran explicitly commands obedience to the Messenger and recognizes his responsibility to judge; yet An-Nisa 4:105 connects his judgment with what God has shown him, and Al-Maidah 5:48 and Al-Maidah 5:49 command him to judge by what God has sent down. Obeying the Messenger who bore and conveyed revelation is therefore not identical to accepting as certain every statement attributed to him generations later. Respect for the Prophet does not make historical chains of transmission immune to error or grant later narrators and jurists an independent right to legislate. This distinction is examined in the independent study “Confusing Obedience to the Messenger with Obedience to Hadith and Disparate Narrations.”

In Al-Ankabut 29:51, the Quran even asks whether it is not sufficient for its audience that God has sent down the Book to the Prophet and that this Book is recited to them. Al-Anam 6:114 describes the revealed Book as “مُفَصَّلًا,” and An-Nahl 16:89 speaks of its clarifying role. These verses establish the divine Book’s place as a source of guidance. Anyone claiming that the Quran is deficient for knowing and implementing religious rulings, and that a collection of historical reports must govern it, must first show where God appointed such an independent authority. One cannot first assume a collection of inherited rulings and details to be necessary and divine, and then call the Quran deficient because not all of them appear in it. What people have added to religious structures throughout history is not necessarily what God has made obligatory for His servants.

The danger of this displacement becomes clearer when inherited reports are not merely placed alongside the Quran but govern its clear ruling. If Al-Baqarah 2:256 speaks of no compulsion in religion, on what grounds should a historical report authorize a general ruling of execution for changing belief? If An-Nur 24:2 clearly states the punishment for zina, how can stoning replace it without demonstrable divine warrant? These are not merely disputes about preferring one report over another; the question is whether the revealed Book remains the standard of judgment or whether rulings attributed to predecessors may set its command aside. Every alleged conflict must be established through careful examination of verses, qualifications, and context; but once the Quran’s ruling is clear, a report’s fame alone cannot authorize setting it aside. The independent study “Stoning: A Quranic Ruling or One Derived from Narration?” examines one example.

In Ash-Shura 42:21, this danger connects with a more fundamental issue. God asks about partners who have prescribed religious matters for people without His permission. In Yunus 10:59, those dividing divine provision into lawful and forbidden likewise face the question of whether God authorized them or they are inventing falsehood against Him. Making a religious ruling without divine permission is therefore not merely adding a small instruction to a list; it enters the domain of God’s legislative authority. If a jurist’s opinion, a leader’s statement, or a narrator’s report becomes a binding command from God without clear divine warrant, the danger arises against which the Quran warns concerning partners in legislation.

Surah Ash-Shuara also distinguishes the descent of revelation from the realm of hearsay mixed with falsehood. Ash-Shuara 26:210, Ash-Shuara 26:211, and Ash-Shuara 26:212 deny that devils brought down the Quran and describe them as removed from hearing it. Ash-Shuara 26:221, Ash-Shuara 26:222, and Ash-Shuara 26:223 then speak of devils descending upon sinful fabricators and of things heard that are accompanied by lies. These verses directly concern devils and fabricators; nevertheless, they also offer an important warning for assessing claims based on human hearsay. Hearing and transmitting do not themselves guarantee truth. A historical report may be correct, or its transmission may involve error, forgetting, additions, or distortion. The certainty and standing of divine revelation therefore cannot be granted without evidence to every transmitted statement—especially when it is used to derive God’s ruling concerning people’s lives, property, worship, faith, and destiny.

The closing question of Al-Baqarah 2:170 exposes the root of this deviation: “أَوَلَوْ كَانَ آبَاؤُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ.” Should their fathers still be followed even if they understood nothing and were not guided? The Quran does not merely question tradition’s antiquity; it presents two essential standards: reason and guidance. An inherited legacy is neither accepted because it is famous nor rejected because it is old; it must be assessed for truth and soundness. If the fathers understood nothing and had not found guidance, repeating their path does not turn their error into truth.

Reasoning is not merely possessing mental capacity or gathering information; it is using understanding to comprehend, assess evidence, and recognize truth. In An-Nisa 4:82, God calls people to reflect on the Quran, and in Sad 38:29, He connects the descent of the blessed Book with reflection on its verses and remembrance by people of understanding. Someone who has decided in advance, rather than reflecting on the verses themselves, that the Quran cannot be understood or provide sufficient religious guidance without a body of inherited rulings must explain the basis of that judgment. Was this conclusion reached by reflecting on the Quran, or through requirements already prescribed by their religious system? Making inherited tradition govern the Quran and subordinating God’s word to hearsay and earlier legal opinions can exemplify the failure to reason condemned by this verse.

Al-Anam 6:116 adds another standard: following most people on earth may lead one away from God’s path, because, within this warning’s context, they follow conjecture and resort to guessing. Numbers of followers and transmitters, and the fame of a belief, do not themselves prove its truth. Thousands may repeat a statement inherited from predecessors without adding anything to its original evidence. If a ruling’s source is unclear, multiplying books and chains of transmission does not automatically turn it into revelation. A majority may be right or wrong; a claim’s value is determined by its truth and evidence, not the number who accept it.

In contrast to such unexamined following, Az-Zumar 39:18 describes God’s guided servants and people of understanding as those who listen to speech and follow its best. This verse distinguishes hearing, assessing, and following. Hearing a statement does not mean accepting it unconditionally, and respect for the speaker does not remove the responsibility to discern the best speech. In the same surah, Az-Zumar 39:23, God describes the revealed Book as “أَحْسَنَ الْحَدِيثِ.” This connection is meaningful: people are called to listen and choose the best speech, while the revealed Book itself is described as the best discourse. Why, then, subordinate revealed speech to statements whose origin and authority depend on human transmission? Wisdom is not immediately accepting everything heard in religion’s name; it is assessing speech and preferring truth to conjecture and imitation.

The absence of guidance is placed beside the failure to reason for a reason. How can someone who sets aside the source of guidance and replaces it with inherited commands reach divine guidance through that same path? In Al-Furqan 25:30, the Messenger complains that his people have abandoned this Quran. Abandoning it does not mean only failing to read it or visibly setting it aside. People may recite its verses daily, kiss it, and place it high, yet prioritize another authority over its clear words when determining religious rulings. They may outwardly show great respect while practically removing it from its position of guidance and judgment. A book meant to be the standard for understanding religion becomes a text that must be harmonized with religious rulings already accepted. This, too, can be a practical form of abandoning the Quran.

Luqman 31:21 repeats the warning in closely similar words. People called to follow what God has sent down again choose their fathers’ way; this time the verse asks whether they would do so even if Satan called them toward the punishment of the Fire. This strengthens the connection between Al-Baqarah 2:168, Al-Baqarah 2:169, and Al-Baqarah 2:170. First, following Satan’s footsteps is forbidden; then his commands, including speaking about God without knowledge, are identified; afterward, people take refuge in their ancestors’ way against revelation. Luqman likewise connects this refuge in inherited tradition with the danger of Satan’s call. Sometimes people imagine that loyalty to religious inheritance defends religion, while that very inheritance may have become an obstacle to God’s command.

Al-Maidah 5:104 describes this condition still more plainly: what we found our fathers following is sufficient for us. This shows how a historical inheritance can become an authority deemed sufficient against revelation’s invitation. People then ask not what God commanded, but what their fathers did, their leaders said, and their school taught. When those answers take priority over God’s word, religion is reduced from divine guidance to following predecessors’ authority. The danger is not learning from history; it is placing history and human inheritance in the position of God’s command.

This verse places responsibility for choosing a religious path directly upon each person. In Al-Isra 17:36, God forbids following what one has no knowledge of, while Al-Ahzab 33:67 speaks of people who, at the Resurrection, attribute their misguidance to obeying their chiefs and great ones. No one can substitute the names of fathers, leaders, narrators, or jurists for their own responsibility. Someone consulting another to understand truth must recognize that human speech, however learned and respected its speaker, still requires evidence and assessment. If a leader issues another ruling against God’s clear command, following their rank cannot make that ruling divine. God calls people to reason and follow guidance, not to surrender unrestricted authority over their religion and conscience to others.

The lesson for us today is clear and decisive: neither ancestry, a religious school’s name, a leader’s fame, numerous narrators, nor a book’s antiquity is in itself evidence of divine revelation. If a statement from predecessors is correct, its correctness must be recognized through evidence and truth; if a ruling contradicts God’s clear command, it cannot be imposed in the name of loving the Prophet or loyalty to tradition. God calls people to follow what He has sent down, not to submit to everything inherited. Whenever narration, opinion, and tradition govern the Quran instead of being assessed against it, the condemned following of ancestral inheritance is repeated in religious clothing. Reason and guidance begin when people dare to place their inheritance before truth and, wherever a clear conflict appears, give The God’s command priority over their predecessors’ words.
