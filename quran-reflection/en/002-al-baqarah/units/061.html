Al-Baqarah 2:61

And when you said: O Musa, we will never be patient over one food; so ask your Rabb for us to bring out for us from what the earth grows: its greens, its cucumbers, its fum, its lentils, and its onions. He said: Do you exchange what is lower for what is better? Descend to a city, and you will have what you asked for. And humiliation and helplessness were struck upon them, and they returned carrying anger from God. That was because they kept covering the signs of God and killing the prophets without truth. That was because they rebelled and kept crossing the limits.

Our Reflection

This verse comes after great ni'mahs: rescue from Pharaoh, the shade of the cloud, mann and salwa, water from the stone, twelve springs, and each group knowing its own drinking place. Up to this point, we have repeatedly seen that the One God provided their basic needs so that a people accustomed to Pharaonic humiliation could find an opportunity for growth, gratitude, order, and inward freedom. But verse 61 shows that the presence of ni'mah by itself does not grow the human being. If the eye of gratitude does not open, ni'mah too appears ordinary, small, and tiring.

Their first slip becomes visible in this very sentence: “We will never be patient over one food.” This is not merely a request for dietary variety. Asking for food is not in itself blameworthy; the human being has need, taste, and preference, and the earth with its plants and foods is also among God's signs. But the tone of the verse shows that this is not a calm and grateful need; it is impatience, weariness, and belittling ni'mah. They had seen mann and salwa, water, safety, and the order of the drinking places, but in their language they reduced all of that to “one food.”

Ingratitude begins with not seeing ni'mah. Gratitude, before being a phrase on the tongue, is seeing and understanding the ni'mah. A person who has health often does not know its value until he becomes ill. Someone whose parents are alive may not see the greatness of that ni'mah until loss comes. A person with two healthy feet may dream of a house and a luxury car; but someone without feet may wish only to stand and walk. When the human being ignores what has been given to him, he blocks the path of gratitude from the very beginning.

In this verse, kufr stands directly opposite gratitude and means covering and failing to see ni'mah. Kufr means the human being does not see the clear signs of provision and treats the ni'mah that should have been honored with gratitude as small and worthless. Rescue from Pharaoh, water bursting from within the stone, clear flowing springs, and provision without painful struggle were all great signs from God. But when the human being ignores all these immense gifts and lowers them to the level of one simple and worthless food, ingratitude and darkness have begun.

The manner of their asking is also deeply striking. They do not say: ask our Lord for us. Rather, addressing Musa, they say: ask your Lord for us. This subtle distancing in speech exposes their inner reality. The same people who had received rescue from Pharaoh, cloud shade, flowing springs, and easy provision still had not fully felt God's guardianship and lordly care. In their sight and language, the Lord is Musa's God, not their own God. In addition, the repeated word “for us” shows that their language is not the language of gratitude and thanks; it is the language of excess demand and entitlement. They neither want to see the value of these ni'mahs nor are they willing to step into a new stage through responsibility and effort; they only want Musa to ask his own God so their material desires may be prepared for them without any effort.

The things they ask for are all simple produce of the earth: greens, cucumber, garlic, lentils, and onions. These foods are not bad or impure in themselves, and the Qur'an never despises the earth and what grows from it. The issue is not that plant food is low or blameworthy; the issue is that in that sensitive historical moment, they were exchanging that special and training provision for their old and lower habits. They turned away from a path meant to cleanse the psychology of slavery and the trait of entitlement from within them, and returned again to second-hand tastes and old stomach-driven wants. We can clearly see this entitlement today in backward societies: people who demand everything from the state and government, without even making the effort to stop polluting their own city, or as a small step, to clean the trash in front of their own homes. This outlook is an escape from personal responsibility and a projection of laziness onto structures; the human being sees himself only as deserving to receive, without feeling the smallest commitment to participation and building.

About mann and salwa, one must speak carefully. The Qur'an presents them as a special provision sent upon them, but it never seeks to explain their exact material kind and details. Salwa may refer to a particular bird or a food of that kind, and mann may have been a special and easily available ni'mah; but the heart of the verse is not exhausted by these material details. The main point is that they did not see that training and constructive good, and instead inclined toward what was lower, more accessible, and habitual.

Musa answers: Do you exchange what is lower for what is better? The word adna comes from a root connected with nearness, what is close at hand, and a short horizon; the word dunya belongs to the same family and means something near, immediate, and accessible. In contrast, khayr does not simply mean something more pleasant or tastier; khayr means what is more constructive for the human being's growth, freedom, gratitude, and training. In reality, they were exchanging the high horizon of liberation and self-training for immediate desires and old habits.

Here the deep connection of this verse with the story of Adam becomes visible. Adam was placed in a garden full of ni'mah where eating and benefiting were available, but beside that there was also an ethical boundary. The Children of Israel too, after rescue from Pharaoh's grip, were placed in a space where provision, water, cloud shade, and the order of springs had been prepared for them so they could be freed from the psychology of slavery and captivity and grow. But just as the story of Adam ultimately led to descent, here too the speech reaches descent into a city.

The word misran in the verse has tanwin; for this reason, it is better not to restrict it with certainty to the historical country of Egypt. It can mean any city among cities: a center of earthly life, market, agriculture, and daily routine. The meaning of the verse is that if you desire such things, you must descend from that training space and provision without painful struggle into the crowd of the city, the arena of the market, and material efforts. This descent is not only a change of location; it is a fall from a unique opportunity for spiritual growth in exchange for a return to lower material habits.

The similarity between this verse and the story of Adam is very deep and thought-provoking. In both scenes, the human being or the people are placed in a secure space of ni'mah and ease, where eating and benefiting are abundantly available, but this ni'mah is tied to boundaries and aims for growth. In both stories, the main slip is not simply eating; it is the change in the human being's relation to the command of truth and the weakening of his covenant. In the end, both stories conclude in descent: Adam's descent was his entry into the earth of effort, struggle, and test; the descent of the Children of Israel was also a return to a level of life in which they would have to seek through work, agriculture, the noise of the market, and the heaviness of daily life the very things that had previously been provided for them easily and without anxiety in that training and sacred space.

Then the verse says: humiliation and helplessness were struck upon them. The expression “being struck” here is very heavy and shaking; it is as if humiliation and helplessness descended over them like a tent, or like an unavoidable seal placed upon their collective destiny. This expression does not mean racial humiliation or an eternal and essential condemnation of every individual of this people. Rather, it shows the unavoidable consequence of a particular spirit and disposition. A people who cannot pass beyond the entitled psychology and traits of the age of slavery will find humiliation not merely as an external or political event, but as a heavy shadow over all aspects of collective life.

Humiliation here means the loss of dignity, the breaking of human honor, and being placed under the domination of others. Maskanah, which comes from the root of stillness, indicates helplessness, being grounded, and incapacity to rise. Maskanah is not only material poverty and shortage; it is immobility, spiritual stagnation, and social lethargy. A society that loses the ability for initiative, movement, gratitude, and building, even if outwardly alive, has fallen into a level of ongoing groundedness and weakness.

This bitter outcome is never the simple result of requesting a few material foods; it is the direct consequence of a spirit: not seeing ni'mah, entitlement, treating the Lord as distant, refusing the training program, and preferring immediate wants over true good. A people whose bodies had been freed from Pharaoh's bondage, if they did not cleanse the Pharaonic psychology and the taste of slavery from within themselves, would inevitably go back under humiliation and helplessness. In Egypt, the Children of Israel were whipped and their sons were slaughtered, but they had garlic, lentils, and onions. In the desert, they were freed from Pharaoh's whip, but they had to be patient so a tawhidi civilization could be built. They preferred the onions and lentils of captivity to the hard freedom of the desert.

How often do we, in our personal or social lives, surrender to “modern captivities, low but secure attachments” (adna) in order to escape the heavy responsibility of freedom, intellectual independence, and creativity (khayr)? Are we also willing, for the sake of “onions and garlic,” to descend again into the Pharaonic Egypt of our habits?

The expression “they returned carrying divine anger” also has a deep meaning here. The word ba'u comes from a root connected with returning, settling in a place, and coming back with a burden. They returned with anger from God; that is, they placed themselves at the threshold of unavoidable formative consequences. God's anger should not be understood as human nervous or emotional anger, because the pure essence of the Lord is far above being affected by emotional reactions. Divine anger in such verses means the human being or society entering the path of the necessary consequences of the order of truth; just as fire burns and poison takes life, transgression and ingratitude naturally bring ruin and fall.

This concept becomes clearer through another verse, which says: eat from the wholesome things We provided you, but do not transgress in it, lest My anger descend upon you; and whoever My anger descends upon has fallen. So divine provision always comes with an ethical boundary. If ni'mah does not lead to gratitude, and if it is joined with excess demand, entitlement, and lawlessness, that same ni'mah can become the ground of human fall and ruin.

But the verse suddenly moves from food and descent into a city to a much larger and more fundamental matter: they were covering the signs of God and killing the prophets without truth. Here the Qur'an moves beyond one scene and one event and opens a wider historical file before us. This deviation is no longer limited only to the generation contemporary with Musa. Rather, the verse shows a deep and recurring disease throughout history: refusing to accept signs and truth, stubborn resistance against guidance, rebellion, crossing boundaries, and in the most severe and merciless form, killing God's messengers.

They expected the prophets who came to confirm their assumptions, interests, and inherited position; but when those messengers spoke against their desires, social customs, and inherited beliefs, they denied and killed them. This spirit and historical disease is widely reproduced today among different tendencies and movements. Although there is no prophet among us today, religious societies and sects often treat anyone who speaks against their intellectual habits and inherited doctrines with the tools of takfir, denial, labeling, removal, or full isolation. This blind partisanship reaches the point where even within movements of the same root and legal schools, people declare one another kafir over the smallest theological disagreements and make each other's lives and blood licit. This is exactly the continuation of the logic of prophet-killing: the attempt to destroy every voice that serves as a mirror of truth and awakens the human being from the sleep of inherited habits and traditions.

This connection and common root is not strange at all. The one who does not see the sign-ness and value of ni'mah complains at a lower and material level about his provision; but the same person, at a higher and intellectual level, when a prophet or reformer speaks against his desire and habits, also fights him and declares him outside the faith. The disease is the same in both cases: the human self does not want to submit to the training of truth. Such a spirit today says, we will not be patient over this one food; tomorrow it says, we will not accept this prophet and new speech; and in the end, across history, this same stubbornness reaches the point of killing prophets without truth.

Among wrongdoers from this people, such a crime and offense occurred against other prophets; and in the case of Isa too, there was clear intent, plotting, and action toward killing, although God made it fail and saved him.

The end of the verse gathers the cause of all these deviations: dhalika bima asaw wa-kanu ya'tadun. That was because they rebelled and kept crossing the limits. Rebellion means conscious disobedience and defiance before the command of truth; and i'tida means transgressing the boundary, crossing limits, and stepping beyond one's right. So the root of all these falls is one thing: they did not want to remain within the frame and boundary of divine training. They did not recognize limits in the measure and kind of provision, nor in gratitude, nor in their relation to the Lord, nor in their treatment of God's messengers.

Verse 61 of Al-Baqarah warns all of us that a person may have his body rescued from Pharaoh's grip, while still carrying the Pharaonic psychology and the taste of the age of slavery in his inner world and speech. He may have water, food, safety, and a unique opportunity for growth, but because he does not see God's sign in them, he treats everything as small and worthless. He may say “your Lord” instead of “our Lord”; he may choose entitlement instead of gratitude; and instead of ascent and growth, he may choose descent and falling down.

So the great lesson of the verse is this: see ni'mah rightly before it is lost. Do not treat provision as something ordinary and trivial; count it as a sign from God. Do not seek gratitude only in speech; real gratitude means seeing the ni'mah itself, recognizing the Owner of the ni'mah, using it rightly, and growing through it. If the human being does not see ni'mah, he will inevitably exchange true good for immediate wants; and if this spiritual disease continues, the human being moves from ingratitude for food to kufr toward divine signs, from impatience to rebellion, and from entitlement to crossing the boundaries.
