Al-Baqarah 2:138

ۖ ۖ

The coloring of The God; and who colors better than Him? And we serve Him.

Our Reflection

After several verses about Abraham’s way, surrender to God, breaking religious monopolies, faith in the prophets without discrimination, and entrusting final judgment of people to the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing, the Qur’an gathers this whole path into one brief image:

“The coloring of The God.”

The Arabic word for coloring (ṣibghah) belongs to the root Ṣ B GH, whose semantic field concerns dyeing and taking on color. Something that takes on a color does not merely carry that color’s name; the effect is visible in its appearance. After shaping the character of a faith through several consecutive verses, the Qur’an now gives it an image: God’s coloring.

We must understand this coloring from that context, not from our own imagination.

In the preceding verses, people were called back from blind attachment to Jewish and Christian labels to Abraham’s way. Muhammad, too, was commanded to follow that same way. Faith in Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Jesus, and the other prophets was brought together, and it was said:

“We do not discriminate between any of them.”

Nor were the People of the Book compelled, in order to be guided, to abandon their historical identity and enter a new sect. Differences in prescribed ways were not denied. What was rejected was monopolizing truth, discriminating between messengers, and turning difference into oppositional division. Dialogue with the People of the Book was entrusted to “the best manner,” rather than coercion and humiliation.

Immediately after all this, the Qur’an says:

“The coloring of The God.”

Here the verse’s meaning becomes particularly profound. Freedom from prejudice, the courage to examine our own inheritance, accepting truth without prejudgment, not discriminating between prophets, not imposing our prescribed way on others, and surrendering to the one God are not the lexical meanings of “coloring.” Yet all these appear within the very context that the Qur’an now calls God’s coloring.

Human freedom to seek truth is therefore not something outside faith that was later added to religion for the sake of tolerance. A faith that recoils from questioning, prohibits inquiry, forces people into imitation, and asks them to choose a conclusion before seeing the evidence must ask itself: where did it acquire this coloring?

People readily color religion in their own image. Ethnic allegiance gives it an ethnic cast; a denomination makes it sectarian; power turns it into an instrument of rule; devotion to personalities makes it revolve around human beings. Inherited tradition can color the mind so thoroughly that a person can no longer see a truth outside the framework they inherited.

But the Qur’an does not call for colorlessness or a lack of direction. It asks:

“And who colors better than God?”

The question is not whether we should have no color at all. It is who has colored us.

Has our faith taken on the coloring of the one God, or that of the family and society into which we were born? Is the Qur’an our criterion, or what we have been told since childhood? Are we seeking truth, or merely collecting evidence for a belief we have already chosen? Do we accept God’s prophets, or love each only to the extent that he serves our own religious identity?

These questions are particularly serious for someone who calls themselves “Muslim.” The name Muslim is not, by itself, God’s coloring. Just as being Jewish or Christian was not, by itself, the criterion of guidance, being “Muslim,” if it becomes prejudice, superiority, and blind imitation, cannot take the place of God’s coloring.

The verse’s ending makes the direction of this coloring clear:

“And we serve Him.”

Two verses earlier, it was said:

“And to Him we surrender.”

Now the same expression, “to Him” (lahu), returns: we surrender to Him, and we serve Him.

This direction is crucial. God’s coloring must not turn, along the way, into the coloring of a prophet, jurist, denomination, people, or community. If any of these takes the one God’s place at the center of devotion, a person may still speak God’s name while having given religion a different coloring.

From this perspective, “God’s coloring” is more than a belief held in the mind; it must be visible in how a person sees, judges, and acts. Someone who has taken on God’s coloring does not fear truth, because they do not see truth as faith’s enemy. They do not fear questions, because their faith is not built on coercion. They do not belittle another person merely for belonging to a different prescribed way, because they have learned that final judgment belongs to the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing. Nor do they divide the prophets into rival camps, because they have said, “We do not discriminate between any of them.”

Perhaps the lesson for us begins here: before trying to color others in our own image, we must examine the coloring we ourselves have taken on. Has our faith made us freer to see truth, fairer toward others, and more sincere in serving God? Or have we merely stamped God’s name onto our own ethnic, sectarian, and inherited colors?

“The coloring of The God” is not a call for everyone to become identical. It is a call for the coloring that shapes our inner lives, amid our differences, to belong to God.

And the final criterion remains:

“We serve Him.”
