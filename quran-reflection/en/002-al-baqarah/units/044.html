Al-Baqarah 2:44

۞ ۚ

Do you command people to expansive goodness (birr) and forget yourselves, while you recite the Book? Will you not use reason?

Our Reflection

This verse comes after the command to establish salat, give zakat, and bow with those who bow. Up to this point, the Qur'an has called the Children of Israel to remember the favor, have faith in the confirming revelation, leave off bargaining with the signs, avoid covering truth with falsehood, and return to salat and zakat. Now the question of the verse moves beyond external command and reaches directly into the inner gap between speech and action: do you command people to birr, but forget yourselves?

The first audience of this verse is in the context of the Children of Israel and the bearers of the Book. But the Qur'an does not bring this remembrance as ethnic insult. The Qur'an turns history into a mirror, not an identity weapon. When it names the error of a people, a religious group, or a community carrying a Book, the point is not for others to imagine themselves pure. The point is for every community that has Book, pulpit, power, legal opinion, teaching, recitation, and the name of religion to see itself in this mirror and not step into the same footprint.

The first key word is ta'muruna. Commanding is not merely gentle advice. It is a kind of directing, pressing, ordering, and shaping the behavior of others. The verse speaks to people who stand in a place of influence over others. They are not only hearers of the Book. They speak, teach, call people to goodness, and create standards for society. The danger begins here: when someone creates a standard for others, but places himself outside that same standard.

An-nas, the people, shows that the matter is not only a private relation between one person and himself. People are involved. When bearers of religion, knowledge, power, or pulpit command people toward birr, their speech affects people's lives. People, because of their words, give wealth, give life, make decisions, give up their rights, show patience, fight, forgive, and sometimes sacrifice themselves. So if the one who commands exempts himself from the same birr, the betrayal is not only personal. It is social betrayal.

Bil-birr is also a very wide word. Birr is not merely a small moral goodness. In its root and material image, birr recalls wide, open, firm land: land that, in contrast to the turbulence of the sea, is a place of stability, walking, and safety. For this reason, birr in the Qur'an is a wide field of goodness, truthfulness, faithfulness, justice, ihsan, taqwa, fulfilling people's rights, caring for the needy, fulfilling covenant, and upright conduct. Birr places society on firm moral ground. But when it becomes a tool for commanding others while the commander places himself outside it, that same firm concept becomes a means of controlling people.

Then the verse says: wa-tansawna anfusakum, and you forget yourselves. Forgetting here is not merely mental forgetfulness. Do they not know that they too are responsible? Yes, the end of the verse says they recite the Book. So forgetting here is more an ethical and practical forgetting. The human being places himself outside the circle of the very truth he preaches to others. He sees people, but does not see his own self. He sees the errors of others, but does not see his own account. He makes people's burden heavy, but keeps his own shoulder light.

This forgetting is a kind of leaving the self outside the field of moral accounting. A person becomes so busy reforming others that he removes himself from the same measure. It is as if people must stand under the judgment of the Book, while the commanding self stands outside the judgment of the Book. This is the dangerous making of an exception: the Book is read for others, but the first addressee of it, the reciter himself, steps aside from before it.

This verse is one of the Qur'an's greatest warnings to religious, political, and social elites. The danger appears when a human being uses religion to reform people, but not to reform himself. Such a person may be a preacher, judge, scholar, ruler, advisor, teacher, leader, or person of influence. But if he wants birr for others and forgets himself, in the logic of the Qur'an he has suffered an intellectual and ethical failure.

A ruler who calls the poor and unprotected children of the people toward war, suicidal death, death itself, and sacrifice, while his own child studies in the universities of the very enemy against whom he raises slogans, is an example of this gap. He commands people toward birr, jihad, sacrifice, or religious honor, but keeps himself and his family outside the cost of that same command.

Likewise, the one who sends people's children to the front, prison, deprivation, migration, poverty, and death, while his own child lives with guards, armored vehicles, safe houses, foreign travel, and a calm life of comfort, must read this verse upon himself. Here the issue is not only individual hypocrisy. The issue is a structure in which the children of the poor become fuel for slogans, while the children of those in power inherit security and privilege.

At another level, this verse also reaches many pulpits and religious institutions. When a mullah, Mawlawi, preacher, or mosque functionary constantly calls poor people to zakat, charity, offerings, vows, and help, while he and his religious apparatus always remain in the position of receiver, they must ask themselves: what is our share in birr? Do we only command people to give, or do we also give from what we have to the orphan, widow, needy person, and hungry person?

If in a village or district, the orphan, widow, needy person, and poor worker spend the night hungry, while the food of the mullah, the right of the mosque, the share of the religious institution, and the honor of the pulpit-holder are always protected, this verse must be read in that very mosque. The Qur'an does not ask people only to give to preachers. The Qur'an asks everyone to establish birr. Whoever receives charity from people but does not turn people's pain into real responsibility should fear that he may be commanding people to birr while forgetting himself.

This statement does not mean disrespect toward every scholar, mullah, religious teacher, or mosque servant. Among them there have always been, and still are, sincere, poor, pure, and service-oriented people. The Qur'anic criticism is directed at a position that turns religion into a tool for demanding from people, while exempting itself from responsibility. Whoever stands in this position, whether ruler, scholar, preacher, intellectual, or social activist, is addressed by this verse.

The ending of the verse is decisive: wa-antum tatluna al-kitab, while you recite the Book. The problem is not that they have not heard the Book. They recite the Book. Words of truth, birr, zakat, salat, justice, covenant, ihsan, and taqwa flow on their tongues. This is the point of danger: a person may read the Book, but the Book may not pass below his throat; he may recite the signs, but not place his own self before the signs; he may read the sacred text for people, but not apply it to himself.

Reciting the Book here is not privilege. It is responsibility. Each time the Book flows over the tongue, the person's awareness becomes heavier. The one who recites the Book can no longer claim that he did not know the standard. Recitation, if it does not reach application upon the self, can turn a human being into a preacher who builds others while abandoning himself. Such recitation is dangerous, because it gives the human being an illusion of knowledge while he has not yet passed through himself. He reads the Book, but the Book does not read him. He measures people by the verse, but hides himself from the verse's measure.

At the end it says: afala ta'qilun, will you not use reason? This question shows that the problem is not only unethical behavior; it is also failure of reason. The root of ʿaql is tied to binding, restraining, and holding, like the cord used to bind the leg of an unruly camel so it does not run loose and endanger the herd. Aql should hold the human being back from dispersion, contradiction, and the rebelliousness of the self. If a human being reads the Book, commands people, but places himself outside the same command, his aql has not performed its restraining work.

Aql must connect speech and action. Aql must ask the human being: how do you command people to goodness while forgetting yourself? How do you recite the Book while you are not its first addressee? How do you call people to giving, patience, sacrifice, reform, and taqwa, while you do not bring your own self into the same field?

So verse 44 separates religious reason from religious memory. Religious memory may memorize verses, know terminology, build pulpits, and command people. But religious reason begins with the self. Ta'aqqul means that the human being connects Book and self, recitation and action, commanding birr and reforming oneself. If this connection is cut, religion becomes a tool for commanding others, not a path for making the human being upright.

As a result, this verse is not only a criticism of the Children of Israel in the past. It is a criticism of every human being who, from the position of religion, Book, ethics, politics, or knowledge, commands people while placing himself outside the same command. With this question, the Qur'an stands every owner of pulpit and power before the mirror: do you command people to birr and forget yourselves while you recite the Book? Will you not use reason?
