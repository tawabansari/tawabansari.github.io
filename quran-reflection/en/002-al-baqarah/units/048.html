Al-Baqarah 2:48

And guard yourselves with God-conscious restraint (taqwa) against a Day (yawm) when no self (nafs) will discharge anything on behalf of another self (nafs), no intercession or supporting mediation (shafa'ah) will be accepted from it, no equivalent compensation ('adl) will be taken from it, and they will not be helped.

Our Reflection

This verse comes immediately after the reminder of the ni'mah and fadl of the Children of Israel. In verse 47, we read that God bestowed ni'mah upon the Children of Israel and gave them fadl over the worlds, but verse 48 immediately clarifies the boundary of that fadl. Fadl, ni'mah, Book, historical relation, prophets, righteous fathers, and religious status do not remove the human nafs from the law of accounting. So this verse is the natural continuation of the previous verse. If the previous verse spoke of increased gift and fadl, this verse shows that increased giving does not mean increased immunity. On the contrary, whoever has received more Book, sign, teaching, history of revelation, and experience of rescue has a clearer accountability before that reception, and the carriers of fadl can never turn their fadl into a fortress for escaping accounting.

The verse begins with the command of taqwa and the phrase wa-ittaqū yawman. Taqwa here does not mean mere fear. It means making a shield and protecting oneself, or wiqayah, through regulated conduct. The human being must regulate today for a day on which the usual mechanisms of the world will not work. In the world, a person may sometimes pass through the consequences of his action by putting forward relation, mediation, family, people, rank, wealth, influence, ransom, compensation, or collective force. But the verse introduces a day on which all these paths of escape are closed. The word yawm is not merely a calendar day. It is the appointed field where truth and the result of deeds become manifest. In verse 46, it was said that the khashi'in keep meeting with their Lord and return to Him present in the calculation of life; now verse 48 explains that same horizon from the angle of the law of accounting, where the nafs returns to its personal and non-transferable position.

The first clause of the verse is la tajzī nafsun ʿan nafsin shay'an. The root J Z Y is tied to sufficing, discharging, settling an account, and paying a result or equivalent. The structure of the verse here is very precise: nafsun ʿan nafsin means one nafs cannot discharge anything on behalf of another nafs, fill its place, settle its account, or suffice in its place. This principle returns responsibility from the level of group and lineage to the level of the nafs itself. The verse does not say that only idolaters are like this, or only public sinners or hypocrites. In the near context, even though the address is to the Children of Israel, the law being stated is fully general: no nafs discharges anything on behalf of another nafs, and even relation to prophets, righteous fathers, sacred Book, chosen people, or religious class cannot replace the human nafs. The word shay'an, anything, also closes the way for every small exception: nothing of the account of a nafs is filled by another nafs, not the whole account, not a part of it, and not even a small amount of responsibility. Every nafs becomes present with what it itself has done, itself has known, itself has covered, itself has sold, itself has accepted, or itself has fled from.

This principle is not foreign to the Children of Israel, because the Torah also says that fathers are not put to death because of children, and children are not put to death because of fathers; each person is accountable for his own sin. In the books of the prophets of the Children of Israel, the same meaning appears with greater intensity: the sinful nafs itself is accountable, and even if Noah, Daniel, and Job were among a people, their righteousness would deliver only themselves. Therefore this verse does not impose a foreign principle upon the Children of Israel. It returns them to the same law of personal responsibility for which there are clear signs in their own scriptural tradition.

Then the verse says wa-la yuqbalu minha shafa'ah. Shafa'ah comes from the root SH F ʿ, which means becoming paired, joining, attaching, and adding a supporter to something that remains alone or incomplete. In the world, a person sometimes imagines that he can compensate for the weakness of his own action by attaching himself to a stronger force, such as lineage, fathers, leaders, elders, religious class, intermediaries, recommendations, or collective belonging. In the context of the Children of Israel, this meaning is very important. They could rely on their relation to Ibrahim, Ishaq, Ya'qub, Musa, the prophets, the Book, covenant, and the history of rescue, and imagine that these connections operate like guaranteed shafa'ah. But the verse breaks this imagination. On the Day of accounting, shafa'ah in the sense of a connection that replaces responsibility is not accepted, and no elder, no righteous father, no sacred history, and no scriptural title can fill the place of the truthfulness and action of the nafs.

So the negation of shafa'ah here should not be limited only to idolaters who imagined idols or angels as their shafa'ah-makers with God. That discussion has its own place in other verses, but here the address is still moving within the path of the Children of Israel, and the verse targets the illusion of the People of the Book: the belief that belonging to the people who carry revelation, relation to righteous fathers, or standing in the continuation of the history of prophets can allow the nafs to pass beyond its own accounting. Of course, the Qur'an elsewhere speaks of shafa'ah with the conditions of permission, approval, and covenant. But this verse speaks about the shafa'ah that a human being imagines as a guaranteed support and substitute for responsibility. Such shafa'ah is not accepted at all. If shafa'ah has a real meaning, it is within the order of God and according to His approval, not in the religious marketplace of human beings and not according to lineage, class, people, or manufactured mediation.

Then He says wa-la yu'khadhu minha 'adl. The root ʿ D L is tied to balance, counterweight, equality, equivalent, and a matching load of equal weight. In a material image, 'adl can recall a load whose two sides must be equal so that equilibrium remains. In this verse, 'adl comes with the verb yu'khadhu, meaning that no 'adl is taken from that nafs, and no equal compensation, ransom, substitute, repair, payment, or formal equivalent is accepted. This clause closes the path of transaction. In the world, many losses are replaced by compensation, money, ransom, settlement, sacrifice, gift, fine, or substitute payment; a person gives something so that something may be removed from him. But the verse says that on that day, no 'adl is taken from the nafs. The accounting of the Hereafter is not adjusted by formal compensation, and the scale of that day is not calibrated by lineage, sacrifice, gift, soulless ritual, money, social status, or outward repair. The difference between la tajzī and la yu'khadhu minha 'adl is very important here. First, substitution of person for person is closed: no nafs discharges anything on behalf of another nafs. Then, substitution of thing for thing is closed: even from the nafs itself, no equal compensation is taken. That is, neither another human being stands in place of a human being, nor does another thing fill the place of the truth of his action.

Then comes the final clause: wa-la hum yunṣarūn. Nasr, from the root N Ṣ R, is tied to help, support, defense, and strengthening under pressure. In the world, a person may be helped by his people, party, tribe, state, army, religious class, network of influence, or social alliance. But on that day, such nasr does not work. They are not helped, meaning that no external coalition can pull them outside the law of truth.

The fourfold order of the verse is very precise. First the law of personal responsibility is established: no nafs discharges anything on behalf of another nafs. Then the system of mediation and substitute connection is closed: no shafa'ah is accepted. Then transaction and compensation are closed: no 'adl is taken. Finally, the shelter of power and collective force is closed: they are not helped. These four clauses close four worldly escape routes: personal substitution, religious mediation, compensating payment, and collective reliance on power. With this order, the verse dismantles the marketplace of salvation from the foundation. Salvation is not a commodity purchased by lineage, recommendation, ransom, gift, class, or social shelter. The human nafs must stand before God with its own reality.

In the context of the Children of Israel, this fourfold structure has a very special meaning. The previous verses spoke of ni'mah, fadl, Book, recitation, truth, concealment, salat, zakat, birr, khushu', meeting, and return. Now verse 48 announces that none of these titles, if they do not become truthfulness and responsibility, takes the place of the nafs. The Book, if it is not carried, is not a guaranteed intercessor. Righteous fathers, if they are not followed, do not fill the place of the actions of their children. Fadl, if it does not become shukr and fulfillment of covenant, is not a shield of immunity. This verse also completes verse 46. The khashi' is one who has entered meeting with the Lord and return to Him into the calculation of life, and verse 48 explains why such khushu' is necessary: on that day, the human account cannot be transferred, bought, recommended away, or defended by an outside force. Since this is so, the human being must reform himself before that day, not postpone reform to people, relation, fathers, intermediaries, ransom, or collective support.

Therefore this verse is not only an abstract discussion about the Hereafter, and it is not only a criticism of idolaters. In this context, the address is to the carriers of the Book and the possessors of fadl. The Qur'an says to them that your ni'mah and fadl were real, but your place does not answer for you: no other nafs fills your place, no shafa'ah is accepted from you, no compensation is taken from you, and no force helps you outside the truth. As a result, this verse establishes personal responsibility at the heart of tawhid. The human being lives in the world within family, people, ummah, history, and society, but before God his nafs is not exchangeable. Book, prophet, righteous fathers, fadl, ni'mah, and history, if carried rightly, are paths of guidance. But if they are turned into tools of imaginary immunity, they become a heavier proof against the human being himself.
