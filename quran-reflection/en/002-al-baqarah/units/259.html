Al-Baqarah 2:259

ۖ ۖ ۖ ۖ ۖ ۖ ۚ

Or like the one who passed by a settlement that had collapsed upon its roofs. He said, “How will The God bring this to life after its death?” So The God caused him to die for a hundred years, then raised him. He said, “How long did you remain?” He said, “I remained a day or part of a day.” He said, “Rather, you remained a hundred years. So look at your food and drink: it has not changed. And look at your donkey; and so that We may make you a sign for people. And look at the bones, how We raise them, then clothe them with flesh.” Then, when it became clear to him, he said, “I know that The God has power over all things.”

Our Reflection

The Quran begins the verse with “أَوْ كَالَّذِي مَرَّ عَلَىٰ قَرْيَةٍ”: “Or like the one who passed by a settlement.”

It gives neither the person’s name, nor the location of the settlement, nor the time of the event. Nevertheless, some commentators have identified this passerby as Uzayr and related stories about him. Although Uzayr is named in At-Tawbah 9:30, the Quran establishes no connection between him and the passerby in this verse; nor is this particular event about Ezra reported in the writings of the Old Testament.

This is an example of an error that sometimes occurs in interpretation: filling the Quran’s silence with conjectures and “amānī,” then presenting those guesses as religious knowledge. For a discussion of this issue, see When Quranic Silence Is Filled with Reports.

What matters for understanding this verse is not the person’s identity, but his question, the experience he undergoes, and the truth that eventually becomes clear to him.

“وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا”

“While it had collapsed upon its roofs.”

“Qaryah” means a settlement and a place where people gather, while “khāwiyah” presents an image of emptiness and ruin. The passerby encounters a settlement from which life and habitation have departed and whose buildings have collapsed. The Quran does not state the cause of this destruction; it therefore cannot be attributed without evidence to war, an earthquake, or divine punishment.

On seeing this scene, he asks:

“أَنَّىٰ يُحْيِي هَٰذِهِ اللَّهُ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا”

“How will The God bring this to life after its death?”

His question concerns how something bearing visible signs of death and destruction can be brought to life. Here, “death” is used of the settlement and may refer to the destruction of its life and habitation. Yet the Quran goes on to give the answer through the questioner’s own direct experience of death and being raised.

“فَأَمَاتَهُ اللَّهُ مِائَةَ عَامٍ ثُمَّ بَعَثَهُ”

“So The God caused him to die for a hundred years, then raised him.”

This statement has a clear connection with the preceding verse. In Al-Baqarah 2:258, a ruler claimed: “أَنَا أُحْيِي وَأُمِيتُ”; “I give life and cause death.” Now the Quran attributes directly to God an instance of causing a human being to die and raising him again. This is the distinction, in the theology of divine transcendence, between the limited abilities of created beings and the power of The God that we discussed in the reflections on the preceding verses.

After being raised, he is asked:

“كَمْ لَبِثْتَ”

“How long did you remain?”

He replies:

“لَبِثْتُ يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍ”

“I remained a day or part of a day.”

But he receives the reply:

“بَلْ لَبِثْتَ مِائَةَ عَامٍ”

“Rather, you remained a hundred years.”

Here the difference between the actual passage of time and a person’s perception of it becomes apparent. A hundred years have passed, yet the person thinks it was only a day or part of a day.

Human beings usually perceive the passage of time through awareness, memory, and observation of changes around them. When this continuity of experience is lost, their perception of the elapsed period may also differ from external reality.

In Al-Kahf 18:19, the Companions of the Cave likewise use the expression “لَبِثْنَا يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍ” in answering a question about how long they have remained. This similarity is valuable for understanding the limits of human perception of time, but it does not mean that the events in the two verses occurred in the same way.

The person’s attention is then directed toward tangible signs:

“فَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِكَ وَشَرَابِكَ لَمْ يَتَسَنَّهْ”

“So look at your food and drink: it has not changed.”

Food and drink normally change with the passage of time, yet in this experience they have not changed after a hundred years. This sign shows him that the passage of time and the transformation of things, although they follow an order and laws in the world we know, are not beyond the power of The God.

The Quran does not say what this food was or by what means it was preserved. The central point is that it did not change during a period that human beings regard as very long.

It then says:

“وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ حِمَارِكَ وَلِنَجْعَلَكَ آيَةً لِلنَّاسِ”

“And look at your donkey; and so that We may make you a sign for people.”

“وَانْظُرْ إِلَى الْعِظَامِ كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا”

“And look at the bones, how We raise them, then clothe them with flesh.”

After the food and drink that have not changed, attention turns to the donkey and the bones that are raised and clothed with flesh.

“نُنْشِزُهَا,” from the root N SH Z, is connected with rising and being elevated, while “نَكْسُوهَا,” from the root K S W, means covering. The verse presents an image of bones being raised and covered with flesh.

Given the mention of the donkey in the preceding sentence, the bones may belong to that animal. However, in the expression “إِلَى الْعِظَامِ,” the Quran does not explicitly identify whose bones they are. What is clear is the demonstration of God’s power to reconstruct a bodily structure.

This part connects with the opening question: how can something marked by death and disintegration be raised again?

In Ya-Sin 36:78, the Quran also speaks of someone who points to decayed bones and questions their being brought to life again. The answer in Ya-Sin 36:79 is that the one who brought them into being the first time will bring them to life again.

There is also an important difference between the two verses: in Al-Baqarah 2:259, the passerby’s question is not in itself presented as a sign of denial. He has asked “How?” and is then presented with an experience through which the answer becomes clear.

“وَلِنَجْعَلَكَ آيَةً لِلنَّاسِ”

“And so that We may make you a sign for people.”

This experience is not presented solely to answer one individual’s question; the Quran describes that person as a sign for people. The verse’s message thus goes beyond a personal event and calls human beings to reflect on life, death, time, and the power to recreate.

Finally, it says:

“فَلَمَّا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُ قَالَ أَعْلَمُ أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ”

“Then, when it became clear to him, he said: I know that The God has power over all things.”

The story ends with “تَبَيَّنَ”: the truth becoming clear to the questioner. At first, observing destruction, he asked how life could be restored. In the end, after experiencing and observing signs, he reached a clear understanding of God’s power.

This verse shows that asking how a phenomenon occurs is not necessarily a denial of its reality. A person may ask about something whose manner of occurrence they do not yet understand and, through signs, reach a clearer understanding.

The God, described in Ayat al-Kursi as “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ,” does not merely hold sovereignty over the lives of creatures; causing death, raising, and recreating also lie within His power.

In the next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:260, the same question about how the dead are brought to life is raised, this time by Abraham, with the difference that he explicitly speaks of his faith and wishes his heart to find reassurance.
