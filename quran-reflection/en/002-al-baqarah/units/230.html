Al-Baqarah 2:230

ۗ ۗ

Then if he divorces her, she is not lawful for him thereafter until she marries a husband other than him. Then if he divorces her, there is no blame upon the two if they return to one another, provided they think they can uphold the limits of The God. And these are the limits of The God, which He makes clear for a people who know.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:229, God described two occasions of divorce and stated that married life must continue honorably or end with kindness. He now addresses the consequences of divorce after those two occasions.

“فَإِنْ طَلَّقَهَا فَلَا تَحِلُّ لَهُ مِنْ بَعْدُ حَتَّىٰ تَنْكِحَ زَوْجًا غَيْرَهُ”

“Then if he divorces her, she is not lawful for him thereafter until she marries a husband other than him.”

In the expression “حَتَّىٰ تَنْكِحَ زَوْجًا غَيْرَهُ,” the verb “تَنْكِحَ” is third-person feminine singular and refers to the divorced woman. If, after the two previous occasions, the man divorces his wife a third time, he can no longer marry that same woman directly. The possibility of their remarriage arises when the woman has married another husband and that bond has subsequently ended as well.

There is, however, a difference between the woman and man in this ruling that deserves reflection.

For a man who has divorced his wife three times, the Quran does not stipulate that he must first marry another woman before remarrying the same woman; for the woman, however, it makes marriage to another husband a condition.

What wisdom might be seen in this difference?

One possible wisdom may be to provide the woman with an opportunity to gain distance from her emotional attachment to her first husband, experience married life differently, and decide her future with greater understanding.

For example, imagine a woman who married young and spent many years of her life with only one man. Her first experiences of affection, companionship, marital intimacy, and forming a family may all have been with that husband.

In such circumstances, even after severe disagreements and repeated divorces, the woman may remain attached to the same man—not necessarily because the relationship is suitable, but because of habit, shared memories, emotional attachment, physical needs, or lack of experience of life with another husband.

People sometimes regard what they have experienced for years as the only possible form of life. A person may suffer in a relationship yet, having experienced no alternative, imagine that all marriages are like this.

An actual marriage to another husband can change that perception.

In her new life, the woman may experience a different approach to affection, conversation, companionship, intimacy, and responsibility. She may discover that married life can be calmer, more respectful, and better suited to her needs and personality.

She may build a successful and lasting life with her second husband and never wish to return to her first husband.

But if that marriage also later ends in separation and the woman again considers marrying her first husband, she now has another experience of married life. Her choice this time may rest on broader understanding and comparison of actual experiences, rather than solely on her earlier emotional attachment and physical needs.

From this perspective, the second marriage can provide the woman with an opportunity to discover a different life and relationship outside the shadow of her first husband, and to decide her future with greater awareness and independence.

This becomes more significant in societies where patriarchal structures have governed family relationships.

In such societies, men have generally enjoyed greater freedom and social opportunities to choose a spouse and remarry, while women, because of cultural restrictions, economic dependence, family pressure, or concerns about reputation, have often had fewer opportunities to experience independent life and choose again.

A man may divorce his wife several times and return again, while the woman remains attached to the same relationship, hoping each time that things will now change.

After the third divorce, however, the woman can no longer be kept in the same cycle of leaving and returning. She can begin a new life and build her future outside a relationship that has repeatedly ended in separation.

In considering its possible wisdom, this ruling can also be understood as an opportunity to break a one-sided attachment and open the way to a more informed choice for the woman. Such an experience may matter to some women, although people and their relationships differ, and a second marriage does not necessarily lead to a better life.

In any case, the woman is not compelled to return to her first husband after marrying a second time. She may continue her new life and never wish to return. Her future must not remain forever dependent on the decisions of a man who has ended the marriage three times.

Readers with questions about the differences between rulings concerning women and men, and the issue of multiple spouses, may consult the study “Polygamy in the Light of the Quran”.

“فَإِنْ طَلَّقَهَا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَنْ يَتَرَاجَعَا إِنْ ظَنَّا أَنْ يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ”

“Then if he divorces her, there is no blame upon the two if they return to one another, provided they think they can uphold the limits of The God.”

This part concerns the second marriage ending in divorce. After that, the possibility of the woman and her first husband marrying again arises.

But this return, too, has an important condition:

“إِنْ ظَنَّا أَنْ يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ”

“If they think they can uphold the limits of The God.”

“ظَنَّا” speaks of their expectation and assessment of the future, while “يُقِيمَا” refers to upholding and establishing.

Here the woman and man must consider their ability to observe the divine limits in married life.

Their previous relationship ended in divorce three times. Now, if they intend to marry one another again, they must consider what has changed.

Have the disagreements that caused their earlier separations been resolved? Have their conduct, outlook, and sense of responsibility changed? Can they respect each other’s rights and continue life on the basis of honorable conduct, affection, and justice?

Imagine a man who repeatedly humiliated his wife, disregarded her rights, and drove the relationship to divorce through anger and threats. Now, after the woman’s second marriage has ended, he wants her to return again.

Is expressing regret alone enough? If the same behavior continues, what reason is there to expect that life together will not again end in hostility and divorce?

The woman, for her part, must also decide with an understanding of her past experiences and present circumstances, rather than because of family pressure, fear of loneliness, or attachment to past memories.

Returning can mark the beginning of a healthy life together when both are ready to reform their conduct, respect mutual rights, and uphold the divine limits.

In this expression, “عَلَيْهِمَا” and “ظَنَّا” both refer to the woman and man. The decision to return therefore does not depend solely on the first husband’s wishes.

The woman also has a place in this choice. Having experienced another life, she can decide whether or not to return to the former relationship.

In Al-Baqarah 2:232, the Quran likewise speaks of not preventing women from remarrying when the parties agree honorably.

Thus, even if the obstacle to remarriage has been removed, no one has the right to force the woman to return.

“وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ يُبَيِّنُهَا لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ”

“And these are the limits of The God, which He makes clear for a people who know.”

The verse ends with a reminder of the divine limits.

“Limits” speaks of boundaries that must not be transgressed. After stating the rulings on divorce, the rights of women and men, and the conditions for returning, God recalls these limits once again.

The expression “يُبَيِّنُهَا لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ” also matters. Knowing is more than memorizing words and rulings; people must also understand the reality and consequences of their actions.

Someone who knows the divine limits cannot knowingly circumvent them and then rely on religious appearances to present their conduct as correct.

One clear example of this problem is the practice that has become known as “halala.”

Within some of the four legal traditions associated with the people of opinion and hadith, particularly in parts of Sunni jurisprudence, there are permissions for recognizing a prearranged marriage as valid: after the third divorce, a woman contracts a marriage with another man so that, following marital intercourse, she can divorce him and become lawful again for her first husband. In some fatwas, even keeping this intention concealed when entering the contract has not been regarded as an obstacle to its validity.

Yet the Quran states in Al-Baqarah 2:9:

“يُخَادِعُونَ اللَّهَ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَمَا يَخْدَعُونَ إِلَّا أَنْفُسَهُمْ وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ”

“They seek to deceive The God and those who have believed, while they deceive none but themselves and do not perceive.”

It also warns in Al-Baqarah 2:231:

“وَلَا تَتَّخِذُوا آيَاتِ اللَّهِ هُزُوًا”

“And do not make a mockery of the signs of The God.”

And in An-Nisa 4:21, it describes the marriage bond in these terms:

“وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا”

“And they have taken from you a firm covenant.”

Marriage in the Quran is a “firm covenant,” not a temporary performance to carry out the halala stratagem. Do those who turn this firm covenant into a transaction for circumventing God’s ruling seek to deceive The God, or themselves?

Some who have written religious laws in the name of religion may now have millions of followers and take pride in their rulings, while the very Quran they claim to follow has warned: “Do not make a mockery of the signs of The God,” and “They deceive none but themselves and do not perceive.”
