Al-Baqarah 2:228

ۚ ۚ ۚ ۚ ۗ

And divorced women shall wait for three periods; and if they believe in The God and the Last Day, it is not lawful for them to conceal what The God has created in their wombs. And their husbands have a greater right to take them back during that time, if they desire reconciliation. And women have rights corresponding honorably to their obligations; and men have a degree over them; and The God is Invincible, Wise.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:227, the Quran recognized the decision to divorce. It now addresses the waiting period of divorced women, not concealing pregnancy, the possibility of returning to married life, and the mutual rights of woman and man.

Divorce may end two people’s shared life, but it does not immediately erase all the responsibilities arising from that bond. Especially when pregnancy and the birth of a child are possible, the woman’s and man’s decision can also affect another human being’s life.

“وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ”

“And divorced women shall wait for three periods.”

“يَتَرَبَّصْنَ”, from the root ر ب ص, denotes waiting and pausing. “قُرُوء” is connected with women’s menstrual cycle. In Arabic usage, there is lexical disagreement over whether “قَرْء” refers precisely to a menstrual period or the interval of purity between two menstrual periods. In either reading, however, the subject is periods related to menstruation, not simply counting lunar or solar months.

But why has The God prescribed this duration of waiting?

One understandable wisdom of this ruling is clarifying pregnancy status and preserving lineage and the rights of the child and parents.

Pregnancy is directly connected with the menstrual cycle. During pregnancy, a woman’s usual menstruation stops, and the passage of periods related to this cycle can help clarify whether she is pregnant.

This was especially important in an age without pregnancy tests or genetic methods of establishing paternity.

For example, a woman might marry another man after divorce and later discover that she was pregnant by her former husband. In such a situation, uncertainty over the child’s father could lead to disputes over lineage, guardianship, maintenance, inheritance, and other family rights.

The Quran places great importance on kinship and children’s rights. In An-Nisa 4:1, after recalling human creation and the spread of descendants, it draws our attention to “الْأَرْحَامَ”. In Al-Ahzab 33:5, it also commands that children be attributed to their fathers, describing this as more just before The God.

The importance of pregnancy status is also apparent in the rules of the waiting period. In At-Talaq 65:4, pregnant women’s waiting period is stated to last until childbirth. By contrast, Al-Ahzab 33:49 prescribes no waiting period to be counted for women divorced before marital contact.

Reading these verses together shows that pregnancy status and the possibility of a child coming into being have an important place in the rules of the waiting period.

Today, medical science can detect pregnancy much earlier, and genetic tests also help establish the biological relationship between father and child. These possibilities can prevent disputes and violations of rights, but advances in human knowledge do not, in themselves, abrogate a Quranic ruling.

Nevertheless, we should not claim to know the entire wisdom of this waiting period. The Quran states three periods of waiting, but does not say that detecting pregnancy is its only reason. Preserving lineage and the child’s rights is a wisdom that can be understood by connecting this verse with others; absolute knowledge of all the ruling’s wisdom, however, belongs to The God.

The waiting period can also offer the woman and man an opportunity, after their disagreements subside, to reconsider the relationship’s future and the possibility of returning.

“وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَهُنَّ أَنْ يَكْتُمْنَ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ فِي أَرْحَامِهِنَّ إِنْ كُنَّ يُؤْمِنَّ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ”

“And if they believe in The God and the Last Day, it is not lawful for them to conceal what The God has created in their wombs.”

This passage further clarifies the connection between the waiting period and the condition of the womb and pregnancy.

“يَكْتُمْنَ”, from the root ك ت م, means concealing something one knows about while preventing it from becoming apparent.

A woman usually has more direct knowledge of her bodily condition, the timing of her menstruation, and the possibility of pregnancy. She may sometimes know she is pregnant while her former husband still knows nothing about it.

If a woman knowingly conceals her pregnancy, the rights of the child and its father may be put at risk. Years later, this concealment might cause disputes over lineage, inheritance, guardianship responsibilities, or other family relationships.

There is, of course, a distinction between not knowing and deliberate concealment. A woman who is not yet aware of her pregnancy or is uncertain about it cannot be accused of concealing a truth she herself does not know.

Once the truth has become clear to a person, however, knowingly concealing it is a different matter.

The God connects this command with faith in Him and the Last Day. Why?

Because a person may hide a truth from others and no one may ever discover it. But does keeping a betrayal hidden from people’s eyes remove its moral responsibility?

Faith in the Hereafter reminds people that they do not live only before the gaze and judgment of others; their conduct and intentions are also accountable before The God.

In Al-Baqarah 2:225, The God also distinguished unintentional speech from what the heart consciously earns. Here, too, the issue is deliberate concealment and the responsibility it brings.

Faith should commit a person to honesty and respect for others’ rights even in life’s most private matters. Concealing a truth connected with a child’s lineage and future is not merely a personal matter between wife and husband; it can affect another human being’s destiny.

“وَبُعُولَتُهُنَّ أَحَقُّ بِرَدِّهِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا”

“And their husbands have a greater right to take them back during that time, if they desire reconciliation.”

“بُعُولَتُهُنَّ” refers to their husbands, and “أَحَقُّ” expresses greater entitlement and priority.

During the waiting period, The God states a prior right for former husbands to return to married life, but makes this right dependent on an important condition:

“إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا”—“if they desire reconciliation.”

Returning must therefore not be for maintaining the family’s appearance, asserting male power, preventing the woman’s future marriage, or continuing harm and hostility. The goal must be genuine repair of the relationship.

In At-Talaq 65:1, after stating rules of the waiting period, The God says:

“لَا تَدْرِي لَعَلَّ اللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا”

“You do not know; perhaps The God will bring about something after that.”

These words remind us that people’s circumstances do not always remain fixed. During a disagreement, a wife and husband may see continuing their life together as impossible, yet after some calm and reflection find a new path to understanding.

The question of children has particular importance here as well.

Sometimes a woman and man marry young, when they do not yet have enough experience of life. After a while, they discover that they do not agree about the future, their way of life, responsibilities, or fundamental priorities.

If this couple has no children and, after trying to repair the relationship, concludes that their marriage has no suitable future, they may be able to separate with fewer shared responsibilities and each pursue their own path in life.

But when a child is involved, the situation changes.

It is no longer only the woman’s and man’s wishes and priorities that matter. There is also a child who needs affection, security, care, education, and support to grow. The parents’ decision can affect the child’s entire childhood and future.

In these circumstances, if genuine repair of the marriage is possible, the woman’s and man’s return can benefit the child as well. A child growing up in a healthy family with loving and responsible parents has a valuable opportunity.

For this reason, preserving the possibility of repairing the family and supporting the child’s future can be understood as one wisdom of the opportunity to return. This is particularly so because these words occur in the very context that addresses the waiting period and not concealing what is in the womb.

The verse itself, however, does not limit the right of return to families with children. Its explicit condition is the intention to reconcile; the presence of a child can nevertheless increase the woman’s and man’s moral responsibility to strive for repair.

Yet not every return is reconciliation.

If married life has become an environment full of violence, humiliation, injustice, and constant hostility, merely having father and mother in one house does not mean that a healthy family has been provided for the child.

In Al-Baqarah 2:231, The God explicitly says:

“وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِتَعْتَدُوا”

“And do not retain them to cause harm and transgress.”

In At-Talaq 65:2, He also says:

“فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ”

“Then retain them honorably or part with them honorably.”

Priority in returning is therefore not permission to impose a harmful life together. Genuine reconciliation should improve the relationship, respect both parties’ rights, and provide a healthy environment for the family.

Responsibility toward a child is an important reason to strive for reconciliation, but it cannot become a pretext for continuing injustice and violating the dignity of either woman or man.

The purpose of marriage, spouses’ maturity and responsibilities, the child’s place, and the possibility of reconciliation or fair separation are examined in detail in the article “Quranic Marriage”.

“وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ”

“And women have rights corresponding honorably to their obligations.”

This expression states one of the Quran’s most fundamental principles concerning relations between woman and man.

The God does not speak only of women’s duties; He explicitly mentions their rights as well. Just as a woman has responsibilities in married life, the man is also responsible toward her.

Married life is not the relationship of a ruler and a subordinate; it is a covenant between two human beings, both of whom have rights and responsibilities.

A man who asks his wife for faithfulness, affection, respect, and cooperation must himself be faithful, kind, and responsible toward her. He cannot expect her to bring tranquility while he continually behaves with violence, humiliation, and disregard.

Nor can he ask the woman to shoulder every responsibility of shared life while he evades his own commitments.

For example, a man who expects his wife to attend to the family’s needs cannot regard providing for their livelihood, caring for children, or cooperating through difficulties as solely her duty. Conversely, the woman cannot disregard her husband’s reciprocal rights and insist only on her own wishes.

These mutual rights remain in force during disagreement and separation as well.

If a man has decided to divorce, that decision does not authorize him to violate the woman’s financial rights, humiliate her, or use the children to pressure their mother. Nor should the woman use matters concerning the child or shared property to take revenge and violate others’ rights.

The Quran makes “بِالْمَعْرُوفِ” the criterion for these relationships.

“Maruf”, from the root ع ر ف, is connected with knowing and recognizing, and in ethical and social usage refers to conduct recognized as good and honorable.

But maruf should not be restricted merely to whatever has become customary in a society.

Humiliating women, excluding them from decisions, or disregarding their rights may have become a longstanding habit in a society. Does the prevalence of such behavior make it good and commendable?

Clearly, social custom alone is not the criterion of right and justice.

In An-Nisa 4:19, The God commands honorable treatment of women. In Ar-Rum 30:21, He also presents affection and mercy as signs of the marital bond.

Maruf in family relationships must therefore accord with justice, dignity, affection, and respect for human rights.

A woman is not merely obliged to fulfill a man’s rights; she has rights of her own. Just as the man expects his rights to be respected, he must recognize his wife’s rights as well.

This principle also matters for understanding the verse’s next expression, because men’s “degree” cannot be interpreted in a way that renders meaningless the reciprocal rights of women explicitly stated in this very verse.

“وَلِلرِّجَالِ عَلَيْهِنَّ دَرَجَةٌ”

“And men have a degree over them.”

“Darajah”, from the root د ر ج, is connected with a rank or a step. The wording speaks of a kind of relative rank or advantage for men, but the verse itself does not explicitly explain which particular right or responsibility this degree concerns.

Two possibilities can be examined to understand this expression.

The first possibility connects the degree with the man’s priority and responsibility to initiate reconciliation and return after divorce.

This possibility is closely connected with the verse’s own context, because shortly before it The God said that husbands have a greater right to take their wives back during the waiting period if they desire reconciliation.

“Darajah” may therefore refer to this very priority and responsibility of the man in taking the first step toward reconciliation.

In this reading, if the man wishes to continue the marriage, he is expected to take the first step toward repair: speak with his wife, take the initiative in soothing hurt, and make clear his decision to return.

Sometimes a wife and husband distance themselves from one another after an argument. Both may still love each other, but pride, hurt, or fear of rejection prevents either from taking the initiative. The woman may wait for her husband to take a step toward reconciliation, while the man waits for her to return to him first.

If both persist in waiting and holding on to pride, a relationship that still could be repaired may be lost.

In romantic and emotional relationships today, too, the expectation that the man should take the initiative in making amends, show his affection, and work to resolve hurt is familiar. Many women may also hope that through such an action their husband will show that he still values the relationship.

Women’s physical and emotional condition during menstruation can also help explain the importance of patience and emotional care. In some women, particularly in the days before menstruation begins, hormonal changes are accompanied by mood fluctuations, emotional sensitivity, irritability, or being easily hurt. This condition is not the same in all women, however, and must not be treated as a fixed personality trait or a measure of their ability to make decisions.

Understanding these physical and emotional differences can call a man to greater patience, kindness, and attention to his wife’s condition, just as the woman should also understand her husband’s physical and psychological circumstances.

If the man’s degree in this verse concerns priority in returning, an important wisdom can be seen in it: instead of using his position to impose his wishes, the man takes responsibility for making the first move, soothing hurt, and preparing the ground for reconciliation.

Taking this initiative does not mean that the woman lacks agency. Repairing a relationship only has meaning when both parties are prepared for a healthy life and respect for each other’s rights.

In this reading, the man’s degree is more a responsibility to initiate repair than a privilege to rule, and this meaning clearly accords with the condition “إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا”.

The second possibility connects the degree with qawwamiyyah and the man’s economic responsibility in the family.

The God says in An-Nisa 4:34:

“الرِّجَالُ قَوَّامُونَ عَلَى النِّسَاءِ بِمَا فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَبِمَا أَنْفَقُوا مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ”

In this verse, men’s qawwamiyyah is connected with responsibilities, one of which is spending from their own wealth.

It is therefore possible that the degree mentioned in Al-Baqarah 2:228 is also connected with the man’s responsibility for providing a livelihood, support, and organizing family affairs.

In such a reading, the degree is not a privilege detached from responsibility.

For example, a man who meets all his living expenses from his wife’s income or his parents’ wealth and does not shoulder the family’s economic responsibility cannot, merely because he is male, invoke spending he has not undertaken and demand an absolute privilege for himself.

Financial incapacity, illness, or unemployment do not, in themselves, diminish a man’s human worth. The issue is the basis of a possible responsibility and advantage, not measuring human dignity by income or wealth.

Nevertheless, Al-Baqarah 2:228 itself does not explicitly state that this degree is the economic qawwamiyyah described in An-Nisa 4:34, just as it does not explicitly restrict the degree to the right of return after divorce.

Of these two possibilities, connecting the degree with the man’s taking the initiative in reconciliation and return appears stronger in the verse’s context, since the verse directly addresses the waiting period, the right of return, and the condition of reconciliation. This possibility also accords with responsibility and emotional care within marriage.

Even so, we do not have clear and sufficient evidence to determine the meaning of “darajah” conclusively.

What we know from the verse itself is that The God first states the mutual rights of women and men, then speaks of a degree for men.

This degree therefore cannot be taken as permission for injustice, humiliation, deprivation of agency, or disregarding a woman’s rights. Conversely, disliking a social interpretation should not lead us to deny the presence of this expression itself.

The proper approach is to accept what the Quran states clearly and refrain from claiming certainty about what we lack sufficient evidence to define.

“وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ”

“And The God is Invincible, Wise.”

The verse ends with two divine attributes: Aziz and Hakim.

“Aziz” speaks of power, might, and invincibility, while “Hakim” speaks of wisdom and soundness in judgment and ordering affairs.

After setting out the limits of waiting, the responsibility of honesty, the possibility of reconciliation, and mutual rights, The God recalls His power and wisdom.

A person may not know all the wisdom of a ruling, but this lack of knowledge does not permit using that ruling to justify personal desires and domination over others.

In this very verse, waiting is connected with pregnancy and family rights, return is conditioned on the intention to reconcile, and women’s rights are recognized alongside men’s rights.

Taken together, these matters show that divorce is not merely the ending of a private relationship; the rights of the woman, the man, and any child that may have come from their union are also involved.

The verse’s message is that even during separation, a person’s responsibility toward truth and others’ rights does not end. Three periods of waiting can matter for clarifying pregnancy status, preserving a child’s lineage, and creating an opportunity for reconciliation. Knowingly concealing the truth is incompatible with faith and responsibility, and returning to married life is valuable when undertaken with the intention of genuine repair.

Alongside these rulings, the Quran states the principle of mutual rights between woman and man. Concerning men’s degree, connecting it with taking the initiative in reconciliation and return accords more closely with the verse’s context, but this possibility should not be made the verse’s sole meaning without conclusive evidence.

The God is Invincible and Wise, and His ruling must not become a means of violating the dignity and rights of His servants.
