Al-Baqarah 2:56

Then We raised you again (baʿathnākum) after your death (mawtikum), so that you may respond with responsible gratitude (shukr).

Our Reflection

This verse is short, but very weighty. After the Children of Israel said, “O Musa, we will never believe in you until we see God openly,” the thunderbolt seized them while they were observing. Now the Qur'an says: then We raised you after your death (mawt), so that you may respond with responsible gratitude (shukr).

At first glance, the verse may appear to refer only to bodily death and physical revival. But when we read it alongside the previous verses, a deeper meaning also appears. In verse 54, the speech was about killing the polluted self: the self that had wronged itself by taking the calf. In verse 55, that same self was still alive and this time wanted God openly, jahratan. In verse 56, after the thunderbolt and death, the speech is about being raised again.

So this verse must be read in continuation of the same educational path: the calf, pardon, the Book and the criterion, repentance, killing the polluted self, demanding to see God openly, the thunderbolt, death, and then being raised again for gratitude. The Qur'an shows a people on a path of training: a people who had come out of slavery, but had not yet become free from all the effects of slavery, sensory-worship, and desire for form.

The word mawt in the Qur'an does not always mean absolute nonexistence or the complete end of being. Death can be the stillness of a condition, the stopping of a stage, the disabling of a level of awareness, or passage from one mode of life to another. The Qur'an itself uses this language about sleep: God takes the selves at the time of their death, and those that have not died, during their sleep; then He keeps the one upon whom death has been decreed and sends the other back until an appointed time (Az-Zumar 39:42). So from the Qur'anic view, sleep too is a kind of taking of the self and an experience similar to death, without the human being being annihilated.

From here, we can understand verse 56 more precisely. The thunderbolt may have taken them into unconsciousness, stunning, the stillness of the senses, or a death close to existential collapse. The Qur'an calls this state mawtikum, your death, but immediately speaks of baʿathnākum, We raised you. This means that this death was not the end; it was a stage after which they were raised again.

This meaning also harmonizes with the thunderbolt. They wanted to see God openly. They wanted to bring the absolute unseen into the field of sense. But that very sense, eye, and gaze could not even endure a thunderbolt. The thunderbolt seized them, broke in them the ability to see, stand, and demand, and brought them into stillness. The eye that wanted to see God jahratan itself stopped functioning before one striking sign from God's signs.

Here death is not only death of the body; it is also death of a condition. The death of the sensory-seeking self, the death of that old gaze, the death of the illusion that wanted to place the One who is worshiped before the eye like a visible phenomenon. The thunderbolt brought them to the point of collapse, but God did not leave them there. After that death, raising came.

Baʿathnākum means We raised you, made you rise, brought you out of the state of fallen stillness. Being raised is not only for the Resurrection. In the language of the Qur'an, baʿth can also mean awakening, moving, bringing back into the field, and making something rise after stagnation. In this verse, being raised means that after that collapse, the possibility of life, understanding, gratitude, and continuing the path was given to them again.

This point is very important: God raised them not so they would return to the same demanding posture, but laʿallakum tashkurūn, so that you may give thanks. So the purpose of this death and raising was not destruction; it was awakening. The thunderbolt did not come to end the people; it came to break their illusion. The raising too was to return them to the path of gratitude.

Here the connection of verse 56 with verse 54 becomes clearer. In verse 54, Musa said: kill your selves; meaning, disable the calf-making and polluted self. In verse 55, that same self still wanted God in the form of open seeing. In verse 56, after the thunderbolt, a kind of death and raising takes place. It is as if what they should have killed within the self by choice was now shown to them through a hard blow: this sensory-struck self must die so the human being can rise again for gratitude.

This reading also weakens the blood-filled view of verse 54. If the path of the Qur'an in this section were slaughter and physical elimination, why do the verses one after another speak with pardon, gratitude, guidance, repentance, mercy, and being raised again? In verse 56, even when death comes, it is not the end; God raises them so they may give thanks. This logic is the logic of lordly nurturing and training, not the logic of destroying a people still coming out of intellectual and spiritual captivity.

Here God's justice and mercy are seen together. God does not treat their error as small. Their audacity in demanding to see God does not remain unanswered. The thunderbolt seizes them. But that same God raises them after death so they may give thanks. So God neither makes the error insignificant, nor buries the erring human being inside the error. Justice makes them face the consequence of their demand; mercy leaves the road of return and awakening open.

This verse shows that sometimes the human being must die in order to understand how he must live. Not necessarily bodily death, but the death of a pride, the death of an illusion, the death of a gaze, the death of a polluted self. The Children of Israel wanted to see God openly with their own eyes; but first they had to see how powerless their eye and sense really were. After this breaking, God raised them so that perhaps they would pass beyond mere seeing and arrive at gratitude.

Laʿallakum tashkurūn brings back the same key of verse 52. In verse 52, after calf-worship, God pardoned them so that they might give thanks. In verse 56, after the thunderbolt and death, He raised them so that they might give thanks. It is as if the Qur'an says: pardon was for gratitude, and being raised again is also for gratitude. Every renewed opportunity carries a renewed responsibility.

Gratitude (shukr) here is not merely saying thanks. Gratitude means understanding that renewed life, renewed sight, renewed opportunity, and continuing the path are all ni'mah. Gratitude means not turning the sign into a game again, not constantly questioning the messenger again, not dragging God into the stage of the senses again, and not building a new calf after every rescue, pardon, and raising.

From this angle, verse 56 is one of the most beautiful signs of divine training. God did not abandon a people who had been psychologically and spiritually broken and polluted. He shook them with the thunderbolt, brought them face to face with the death of that condition, and then raised them again so that perhaps they would give thanks. This raising is not merely a return to breathing; it is an invitation to wakefulness.

As a result, verse 56 can be understood in this way: after the thunderbolt struck down that openly-seeing-demanding gaze and sensory-struck self, God raised them from the death and stillness of that state so they might reach gratitude. They had to understand that renewed life after such a fall is a ni'mah; and if ni'mah does not arrive at gratitude, it turns again into ingratitude and collapse.

Cross-References
  • Al-Baqarah 2:52— Pardon after calf-worship with the purpose of gratitude; verse 56 also presents being raised again after death with the purpose of gratitude.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:54— The command to kill the polluted self; verse 56 can be understood in continuation of the death of the sensory-seeking self and being raised again for gratitude.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:55— The thunderbolt after the request to see God openly; verse 56 is the direct continuation of that event.
  • Az-Zumar 39:42— God takes the selves at the time of their death, and those that have not died, during their sleep; an important witness that death in the Qur'an is not only biological annihilation and that sleep is also a kind of taking of the self.
  • Al-A'raf 7:143— The manifestation of the Lord to the mountain, the collapse of the mountain, and Musa falling stunned; an important witness for the inability of creation before divine majesty.
  • Al-A'raf 7:155— Musa chose seventy men from his people for the appointed meeting, and the trembling seized them; useful for understanding the connection between thunderbolt, trembling, appointed meeting, and the training of the people.
  • An-Nisa 4:153— The request to see God openly and their being seized because of their wrongdoing; a direct witness for the connection between verses 55 and 56.
  • Al-An'am 6:122— The one who was dead and God gave him life and appointed for him a light; a clear witness for death and life at the level of guidance, awareness, and coming out of darkness.
  • Al-Hajj 22:5— Dead earth that becomes alive and stirred by the descent of water; a witness that death and raising in the Qur'an can also be used for the stilling and reviving of a condition.
  • Ar-Rum 30:19— God brings the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living, and gives life to the earth after its death; useful for the wider Qur'anic meaning of death and life.
  • Al-Hadid 57:17— God gives life to the earth after its death; a witness for death and life as transformation of state, not only absolute annihilation.
