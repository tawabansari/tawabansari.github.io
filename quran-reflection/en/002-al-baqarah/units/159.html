Al-Baqarah 2:159

ۙ

Indeed, those who conceal what We have sent down of clear signs and guidance, after We have made it clear for people in the Book—The God excludes them from His mercy, and those who curse also curse them.

Our Reflection

This verse speaks of those who knowingly conceal the clear signs and divine guidance after God has explained them for people in the Book. The Quran calls this behavior “kitman,” from K T M, meaning to hide or keep something concealed.

“Bayyinat” is the plural of “bayyinah,” from B Y N, and refers to God’s clear verses and signs: verses that disclose truth and distinguish truth from falsehood. Here, “bayyinat” appears alongside “guidance,” because these verses not only clarify truth but also guide people to the right path.

In Al-Baqarah 2:99, the Quran described those who reject the clear signs as “fasiqun.” Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:159, it speaks of concealing that same clear truth. The distinction matters: rejecting the clear signs means refusing, denying, or setting aside clear truth; concealing them means hiding a truth of which one is aware. Concealment is particularly significant here because the truth has been explained for people, yet some hide it from them. Such conduct can be both rejection and concealment of the clear signs.

Al-Baqarah 2:146 also speaks of a group who knowingly conceal the truth. In Al-Baqarah 2:42, mixing truth with falsehood and concealing truth appear together. These behaviors can be connected, but are not identical: someone may conceal truth, mix it with falsehood, or alter its meaning to prevent people from understanding it correctly.

The phrase “مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا بَيَّنَّاهُ لِلنَّاسِ فِي الْكِتَابِ” adds to the concealers’ responsibility. “Al-kitab” is definite, but its definiteness alone does not determine the scope of its referent. The Quran uses this designation for revelation sent down to Muhammad and for earlier scriptures, including the Torah and the Gospel. In our reading, these usages and the criterion of concealing clear signs and guidance mean that the warning is not confined to those who conceal truth in earlier scriptures; it also encompasses concealment of the Quran’s truth. “Li-l-nas” is grammatically connected to “bayyannahu”: God has made that truth clear for people, not merely for a group of scholars or holders of religious authority. Divine verses are therefore not the private property of any scholar, jurist, school, or religious institution, to conceal at will or to impose a ruling upon people in God’s name contrary to their clear statement. Furthermore, if someone knows the Book’s clear statement but seeks a ruling among scattered sayings and narrations and replaces that statement with it in God’s name, so that the Book’s truth remains hidden from people’s access or attention, this conduct too can constitute concealment. The issue is not merely consulting sayings and narrations, but knowingly covering the Book’s statement beneath the authority of another utterance.

This warning is not confined to the past or a particular group. The verse speaks of “الَّذِينَ يَكْتُمُونَ”: those who conceal revealed truth, not merely bearers of a particular historical title. Today, too, any mufti, religious teacher, hadith scholar, jurist, religious authority, or Quran researcher who recognizes the clear signs and knowingly sets them aside or conceals them to preserve human-made rulings places themselves before this same Quranic criterion. A religious title, social standing, or claim to Quran-centeredness cannot turn rejection or concealment of clear truth into obedience to God.

An evident example against which to assess this criterion is the ruling of stoning. An-Nur 24:1 announces a surah that has been sent down and made obligatory, containing clear signs. An-Nur 24:2 then prescribes one hundred lashes each for the woman and man guilty of zina, without distinguishing between married and unmarried people. An-Nisa 4:25 also describes a punishment that can be halved in a particular case, whereas stoning to death cannot be halved. Yet narration-based jurisprudence prescribes stoning to death for some of these women and men in place of the Quran’s written ruling.

This conflict is not merely a minor disagreement about words. It concerns a ruling whose subject and amount the Quran specifies, but which narration-based jurisprudence sets aside for part of that same category, replacing it with another punishment in God’s name. Many scholarly defenders of stoning know the relevant verses and invoke narrations and the theory of specification to restrict their scope. But knowing the verse and then restricting its clear ruling through another source does not resolve the issue. It raises the question: which verse of God permits an explicit ruling of His Book to be changed by a narration outside it?

If someone knows this clear Quranic ruling but deliberately sets it aside to preserve a narration, fatwa, or religious authority, imposing another punishment upon people in God’s name, the issue is no longer merely a difference of interpretation. A truth made clear in the Book is covered by the authority of another ruling. This is the danger posed by rejecting and concealing the clear signs. The independent study “Stoning: A Quranic Ruling or a Ruling Derived from Narration?” examines this conflict with broader Quranic evidence.

There is, of course, a difference between deliberate concealment and ignorance or an honest mistake in understanding the Quran. Someone who has not yet understood a truth correctly, or is seeking to understand it, is not a concealer merely because their reading differs. This verse concerns knowingly covering what God has made clear for people. Yet this distinction must not become a means of absolving those who know the Quran’s clear ruling but nevertheless impose other rulings upon people’s lives in God’s name.

The end of the verse describes the grave consequence: “أُولَٰئِكَ يَلْعَنُهُمُ اللَّهُ وَيَلْعَنُهُمُ اللَّاعِنُونَ”.

“La‘n,” from L ‘ N, means expulsion and distancing. God’s curse should not be understood like an angry human imprecation. Divine cursing is exclusion from God’s mercy and guidance, a consequence connected with human choices and deeds. Someone who knowingly conceals truth enters a path on which continued recognition and acceptance of truth become harder. As-Saff 61:5 likewise connects the deviation of hearts with a deviation people themselves initiated.

This consequence unfolds within divine patterns: regularities God has established in creation, in human beings, and in their relationship with truth. Someone who repeatedly rejects or conceals clear truth prepares, through their conduct, the conditions for hardness of heart, greater deviation, and distance from guidance. God, the Creator of this order and its governing patterns, attributes this distancing from mercy to Himself. These patterns do not replace God’s action; they are part of the order established by His will. Al-Maidah 5:13 also connects breaking the covenant with divine cursing and hardened hearts.

But why does the verse mention the curse of “al-la‘inun” after God’s curse?

“Al-la‘inun” means those who curse. This verse does not explicitly identify them, but Al-Baqarah 2:161 and Al-Imran 3:87 mention God’s curse alongside that of angels and people. Angels and human beings are therefore among those to whom the Quran attributes cursing.

Their curse, however, should not be equated with independent authority to deprive someone of God’s mercy. Just as guidance and misguidance within God’s creation involve different patterns and agents, cursing can also be understood in connection with that order. Angels and people are not outside it. From this perspective, the curse of “al-la‘inun” may, beyond condemnation and declared disavowal, be connected with the agents and consequences of those same divine patterns, although the verse itself does not explain the precise identity of all who curse or how their curse takes effect.

God’s curse and that of those who curse should therefore not be reduced to two independent verbal imprecations. Both speak of rejection and distancing: the first is attributed to God, and the second to those who themselves exist within His creation. For a broader examination of cursing, human conduct, hardness of heart, and the patterns of guidance and misguidance, see “Cursing in the Quran: Exclusion from Divine Mercy”.

The warning is clear: God has disclosed the clear signs to guide people. Those who knowingly conceal them not only deprive others of knowledge of truth but also place themselves on a path away from guidance. This warning is directed first of all at those with access to the Book and religious knowledge whom people trust to understand God’s rulings.

No turban, fatwa, narration, school, or religious authority can turn concealment of the clear signs into obedience to God. What God has made clear for people in His Book must not be hidden from them in that same God’s name.
