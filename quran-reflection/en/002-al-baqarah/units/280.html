Al-Baqarah 2:280

ۚ ۖ

And if someone is in hardship, then a respite until ease; and that you give in charity is better for you, if you know.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:278, the Quran commanded that outstanding claims to riba be relinquished. In Al-Baqarah 2:279, the creditor’s right to the principal was preserved, and the fundamental principle of neither doing nor suffering injustice was stated.

Now the Quran addresses a debtor unable to repay the debt.

“وَإِنْ كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ”

“And if someone is in hardship, then a respite until ease.”

“عُسْرَة,” from ʿ S R, refers to difficulty and constraint, here describing a debtor unable to pay. “نَظِرَة,” from N Ẓ R, concerns granting time and deferring a claim. “مَيْسَرَة,” from Y S R, refers to ease, relief, and the availability of capacity.

Together, these words express a clear command: if a debtor is in hardship, they must be given time until they become able to pay.

The sentence structure also matters. In “وَإِنْ كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ,” the existence of a debtor in hardship is stated as a condition, and “فَنَظِرَةٌ” is its consequence. Although there is no direct imperative verb here, the nominal sentence answering the condition expresses the requirement to grant time, rather than merely describing it as good.

Elsewhere in the Quran, N Ẓ R also occurs with the meaning of granting respite, including Al-Araf 7:14 and Al-Araf 7:15.

This command reveals an important point about the context of riba. Riba has been prohibited in the preceding verses; here, the debtor’s hardship is presented as a circumstance with a particular ruling. Thus, hardship is not introduced as a condition defining riba, but as one circumstance clarifying the creditor’s responsibility.

The Creditor’s Responsibility and the Debtor’s Circumstances

In financial relations, the parties do not always have equal knowledge, freedom of choice, or economic power.

A professional lender usually understands the contract terms, interest rate, debt calculation, late-payment penalties, and repayment guarantees better. The borrower, however, may accept terms whose consequences they do not fully understand because of necessity, lack of knowledge, or the absence of a suitable alternative.

For example, consider a worker who borrowed for their child’s medical treatment. When taking the loan, they hoped to repay it from their earnings, but later became ill or lost their job.

In such circumstances, should their debt be increased daily, with threats and pressure placing the family in even greater hardship?

The Quran’s answer is clear: give them time until ease.

This command does not merely mean refraining from harsh or humiliating behavior; demanding payment from a person in hardship must be deferred until they become able to pay. Adding financial pressure to someone genuinely unable to pay also conflicts with this command and the principle of neither doing nor suffering injustice in Al-Baqarah 2:279.

In Al-Baqarah 2:275, condemnation was directed at those who consume riba; in Al-Baqarah 2:278, the command to relinquish outstanding claims to riba addressed those holding the claims. Now, too, the responsibility to grant time rests with the creditor.

Someone knowingly profiting from others’ need and incapacity cannot be held equally responsible with someone who has fallen into debt through necessity or lack of knowledge.

Being a professional does not itself indicate consuming riba, just as not every debtor is necessarily uninformed or in hardship. A debtor able to pay who knowingly withholds another’s due is not in the same situation as someone genuinely unable to repay.

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, the Quran connects responsibility with capacity; in At-Talaq 65:7, concerning financial responsibility, it recalls ability and ease after hardship.

Even recognizing people’s real needs is not always easy. In Al-Baqarah 2:273, the Quran described people in need whom others consider free of need because of their restraint. A debtor’s situation must therefore be assessed through genuine understanding of their circumstances, without supposition, humiliation, or prejudice.

The creditor’s right to the principal is preserved, but this right does not authorize pressure on someone genuinely unable to pay.

Forgiving Debt: Goodness beyond Granting Time

The Quran then says:

“وَأَنْ تَصَدَّقُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ”

“And that you give in charity is better for you.”

In the preceding verse, the Quran preserved the creditor’s right to the principal. Now, after commanding time for a debtor in hardship, it speaks of giving in charity.

In this context, forgiving all or part of a claim against a debtor in hardship is a clear instance of charity.

The difference between these two actions matters:

Granting time means that the creditor’s right remains, but claiming it is deferred until ease.

Forgiving debt means that the creditor voluntarily relinquishes all or part of their financial right and exempts the debtor from paying it.

The verse’s structure also shows this distinction: granting time is stated as the consequence of a condition, whereas charity is described as “خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ,” indicating that forgiveness is the greater good.

This use of charity is not unprecedented in the Quran. In An-Nisa 4:92, the deceased’s family relinquishing its right to blood money is described in terms of charity; in Al-Maidah 5:45, relinquishing the right to retribution is connected with charity.

In Al-Baqarah 2:237, concerning financial rights after divorce, relinquishing one’s right is also described as closer to taqwa.

These passages show that charity is not only giving new wealth to another; voluntarily relinquishing one’s legitimate right can also be charity.

There is a subtle point here: relinquishing the increase constituting riba has already been commanded. Merely forgoing a claim that the Quran prohibits is therefore not the same voluntary gift now described as better. But if the creditor also forgoes all or part of the principal, they have shown greater goodness to the debtor in hardship.

For example, if someone forgives part of an impoverished worker’s debt, that family may again be able to meet essential needs and regain its capacity for work and economic independence.

This directly connects with Al-Baqarah 2:261, on the multiplying fruits of giving, and Al-Baqarah 2:276, on growing charities in contrast to eradicating riba.

Instead of making someone’s hardship a means of increasing their debt, forgiveness can bring them out of constraint and restore their ability to live and work.

In At-Tawbah 9:60, “الْغَارِمِينَ,” meaning debtors, are also named among the recipients of charities. Helping a debtor in hardship is therefore not limited to the creditor’s individual decision; society can also contribute to relieving their difficulty through charities and support mechanisms.

If You Know

The verse ends with this expression:

“إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ”

“If you know.”

A creditor may see only the money they do not receive today. But understanding the effects of granting time and forgiveness on the debtor, their family, and society can reveal that sometimes relinquishing part of one’s wealth produces far greater goodness and fruits.

This is the truth recalled in Al-Baqarah 2:272: the good of giving also returns to the giver.

The message of this verse is that the debtor’s hardship is not a condition defining riba, but a circumstance clarifying the creditor’s responsibility. The Quran commands that a debtor in hardship be given time until ease and describes forgiving all or part of the claim as better. Someone knowingly profiting from others’ need does not bear the same responsibility as a person who has fallen into debt through necessity or lack of knowledge. Justice is not only reclaiming one’s due; sometimes exercising a right properly means granting time, forgiving, and helping someone who has lost the ability to rise out of poverty.
