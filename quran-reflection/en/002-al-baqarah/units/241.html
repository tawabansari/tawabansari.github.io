Al-Baqarah 2:241

ۖ

And for divorced women there is honorable provision—a duty upon the mindful.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, God spoke of the right to provision and support for women whose husbands have died. He now addresses the rights of women whose married lives have ended in divorce.

“وَلِلْمُطَلَّقَاتِ مَتَاعٌ”

“And for divorced women there is provision.”

“Mata‘,” from the root م ت ع, refers to benefit and enjoyment. In this verse, it is provision that a woman receives after divorce and can use to put her life in order.

In Al-Baqarah 2:236, the Quran commanded provision for the woman upon divorce before marital contact and before the mahr was determined. In Al-Baqarah 2:237, it stated the right to half the specified mahr upon divorce before marital contact. In the present verse, however, the general expression “لِلْمُطَلَّقَاتِ” appears: divorced women, without restricting the ruling to one of the preceding situations.

The Quran thus states the principle of provision for women at separation in general terms. This provision is not identical to the marital mahr: mahr is a right associated with entering marriage, whereas the provision in this verse concerns supporting the woman when that bond ends.

“بِالْمَعْرُوفِ”

“In an honorable manner.”

“Ma‘ruf,” from the root ع ر ف, means knowing and recognizing. In the Quran’s ethical usage, it refers to good and proper conduct whose goodness can be discerned in light of right, justice, fairness, and sound understanding.

Ma‘ruf is connected with a society’s prevailing customs, but not every custom is ma‘ruf. The prevalence of a behavior does not prove it right, just as society’s disapproval of a behavior does not, in itself, prove it wrong. The criterion is its consistency with right, justice, and the clear principles of the Quran.

For example, if a society customarily expects a woman to leave her husband’s home empty-handed after divorce, with no right to support, that custom is inconsistent with this verse’s command, however common it may be.

Ma‘ruf also matters in determining the amount and manner of provision. In Al-Baqarah 2:236, the Quran states: “عَلَى الْمُوسِعِ قَدَرُهُ وَعَلَى الْمُقْتِرِ قَدَرُهُ”; the affluent according to his means, and the one of limited means according to his means. An amount beyond a poor man’s capacity therefore cannot be demanded of him, just as an affluent man must not evade his responsibility by paying a negligible sum.

The woman’s circumstances also matter in discerning proper conduct. For example, a woman who separates after a few months of marriage may not be in the same situation as one who has devoted twenty years to married life, raising children, and caring for the family without an opportunity to earn an independent income. A woman who also bears responsibility for caring for children after divorce may face greater needs.

Honorable provision must therefore be determined with an understanding of both parties’ actual circumstances: financial ability, living needs, responsibilities, and available resources. A monetary payment may be appropriate in one society, while providing necessities or another form of support may be a more suitable way of fulfilling this right in another.

The Quran establishes the right itself, but its amount and manner of implementation may vary with actual living conditions, time, place, and people’s resources. Custom can help organize these details, but it cannot abolish the Quran’s command or turn oppressive conduct into ma‘ruf.

“حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُتَّقِينَ”

“A duty upon the mindful.”

“حَقًّا” emphasizes that this provision is established and required. Provision for divorced women is therefore not merely an optional gift motivated by sympathy or a man’s personal kindness. The Quran calls it a right, not charity whose payment depends upon the man’s wishes or preference.

“Al-muttaqin,” from the root و ق ي, refers to those who observe divine limits through conscious restraint and care for their conduct. Mentioning the mindful does not exempt men lacking mindfulness from fulfilling women’s rights; rather, respecting this right is a sign of mindfulness and obedience to God.

This command continues Al-Baqarah 2:229 and Al-Baqarah 2:231, which emphasize retaining a spouse honorably or releasing her with kindness and without harm. The Quran permits ending a marriage, but does not make its end a license for revenge, depriving the woman of her rights, or evading financial responsibilities.

Divorce may end married life, but it must not begin oppression and deprivation. A mindful person fulfills his former wife’s rights even when he no longer wishes to live with her, and does not allow anger, resentment, or personal interest to displace justice and God’s command.
