Al-Baqarah 2:243

ۚ

Have you not considered those who left their homes for fear of death, although they numbered thousands? Then The God said to them: Die; then He brought them to life. Indeed, The God is gracious to people, but most people do not give thanks.

Our Reflection

Sometimes the Quran tells a story without placing every detail at the disposal of the adventurous human mind. The names of the people, the location, the time, and many of the preceding circumstances remain unstated. Perhaps there is wisdom even in this silence; perhaps The God wishes to see who recognizes the limits of their knowledge and submits to what God has stated.

We now approach this verse in the same way. The Quran speaks of a large group who left their homes for fear of death, but it does not identify them, their land, or the kind of threat from which they were fleeing. Some commentaries have identified them as the Children of Israel and related stories about illness, war, and other events. Yet none of these details appears in the verse itself.

“أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ خَرَجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ وَهُمْ أُلُوفٌ حَذَرَ الْمَوْتِ”

“Have you not considered those who left their homes for fear of death, although they numbered thousands?”

Here the Quran draws our attention to a group who, despite their great numbers, left their homes for fear of death.

“أُلُوفٌ” is a plural of the number one thousand and indicates a large population. This matters because the passage is not about a few scattered, powerless individuals, but about thousands of people who could have possessed considerable collective strength together.

“دِيَار” also refers to their homes and places of residence. Leaving one’s homes is not merely walking out of a building; it can mean abandoning one’s home, land, property, social ties, and the resources on which a community’s life rests.

“حَذَرَ الْمَوْتِ” states the motive for their departure: fear of death. Ḥadhar, from the root ḥ-dh-r, refers to conscious fear, caution, and action to avoid danger.

Migration to preserve life, dignity, and freedom is among the principles of religion and, in some circumstances, becomes a human responsibility. In Al-Qasas 28:21, Moses also left the city in fear and vigilance to escape a threat to his life. In An-Nisa 4:97, An-Nisa 4:98, An-Nisa 4:99, An-Nisa 4:100, migration is likewise presented as a way out of oppression and toward greater opportunity.

But a person’s responsibility does not end upon reaching safety. In Al-Baqarah 2:218 and An-Nahl 16:110, migration is linked with striving afterward. The important question is what individuals and societies do with their new opportunity after saving their lives and finding security. Do they settle for protecting their own comfort and interests, or use their newfound freedom and resources to rebuild their strength, help others, and oppose oppression?

This subject is examined in Forqan’s independent study, “Hijrah and Migration in the Quran: Preserving Life, Freedom, and Mutual Responsibilities”.

“فَقَالَ لَهُمُ اللَّهُ مُوتُوا ثُمَّ أَحْيَاهُمْ”

“Then The God said to them: Die; then He brought them to life.”

In a direct reading, the Quran attributes that group’s death and subsequent revival to God’s will. Yet in light of the following verses, another layer of the meaning of death and life invites reflection: the death and revival of a society.

The Quran does not use death and life solely for the physical condition of human beings. In Al-Anam 6:122, it speaks of someone who was dead and whom God brought to life, giving him a light by which to walk among people. In Al-Anfal 8:24, people who are already alive are called to that which gives them life.

Life can therefore extend beyond the body’s being alive and be connected with guidance, awareness, and the ability to act. On a social level, too, we can speak of a society that has a population but whose collective life is fading because of fear, fragmentation, oppression, and the loss of its ability to defend its rights.

If we now read Al-Baqarah 2:243, Al-Baqarah 2:244, Al-Baqarah 2:245, Al-Baqarah 2:246, Al-Baqarah 2:247, Al-Baqarah 2:248, Al-Baqarah 2:249, Al-Baqarah 2:250, Al-Baqarah 2:251 together, this possibility becomes clearer.

In the present verse, a group numbering thousands leave their homes themselves for fear of death. Immediately afterward, in Al-Baqarah 2:244, comes the command to fight in God’s way:

“وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ”

“And fight in the way of The God, and know that The God is Hearing, Knowing.”

Here the Quran does not say “اقْتُلُوا (kill),” but “قَاتِلُوا (fight).” Fighting is not identical with killing; the expression concerns combat and confrontation. In the subsequent account of the Children of Israel, this fighting also takes a military form; however, effort and steadfastness against oppression are not limited to military warfare and can also be pursued through legitimate civil, political, economic, and other forms of striving. In Al-Furqan 25:52, the Quran even speaks of a great striving through the Quran itself.

Then in Al-Baqarah 2:246, the Quran introduces another account with the same construction, “أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى”; this time it concerns the leaders of the Children of Israel after Moses, who say to their Prophet:

“وَمَا لَنَا أَلَّا نُقَاتِلَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَقَدْ أُخْرِجْنَا مِنْ دِيَارِنَا وَأَبْنَائِنَا”

“Why should we not fight in the way of The God, when we have been driven from our homes and our children?”

There is an important difference between these two pictures: the first group left their homes themselves for fear of death; the second group had been forcibly driven from their homes and children.

One picture thus concerns departure out of fear, and the other expulsion by others. Both involve the loss of one’s homes and a threat to collective life.

In view of the expression “the Children of Israel after Moses” in Al-Baqarah 2:246, it is possible that the two verses speak of one society’s experience across two generations: a generation that left its homes for fear of death and suffered death, and a subsequent generation that restored the life and dignity of that society through the steadfastness of a small group. In this social reading, the pronoun “هُم” in “ثُمَّ أَحْيَاهُمْ” refers to the same community—a community that can come alive again in later generations, without necessarily meaning the revival of every individual of the first generation.

Al-Maidah 5:21, Al-Maidah 5:22, Al-Maidah 5:23, Al-Maidah 5:24, Al-Maidah 5:25, Al-Maidah 5:26 offer another example of fear and refusal to stand firm in the history of the Children of Israel. In Moses’ time, when commanded to enter the land assigned to them, they refused because a powerful people lived there. They said that Moses and his Lord should go and fight while they remained sitting where they were. The Quran then speaks of their wandering for forty years.

Later in the account in Al-Baqarah, when fighting was prescribed for the Children of Israel, many turned away and only a small group stood firm. Eventually, in Al-Baqarah 2:249, the believers recalled:

“كَمْ مِنْ فِئَةٍ قَلِيلَةٍ غَلَبَتْ فِئَةً كَثِيرَةً بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ”

“How many a small group has overcome a large group by the permission of The God.”

Al-Baqarah 2:251 also speaks of Goliath’s defeat and David’s victory. These verses show that standing against aggression and oppression can be a means of preserving life, security, and freedom, and preventing corruption on earth.

A society that, despite its large population, abandons everything for fear of death and loses the ability to stand firm may suffer the death of its independence and collective life. But a society that, after migration and displacement, uses the opportunity of safety to rebuild its strength and solidarity and stands firm to reclaim its rights can regain its life and dignity.

In the second account, the Quran does not explicitly use the word “revival.” Yet the experience of a group moving from expulsion, fragmentation, and weakness to organization, resistance, and victory offers an intelligible picture of social revival.

Thus, a social truth emerges from this sequence of verses: a large population, without faith, responsibility, solidarity, and the will to stand firm, does not guarantee a society’s life and dignity.

This does not mean that military warfare is necessary in every circumstance, nor that human life is without value. Striving for what is right must take actual circumstances and resources into account and observe justice. In this same context, Al-Baqarah 2:251 presents God’s checking some people by means of others as a barrier to corruption on earth. The focus of this reflection is therefore to uphold rights and prevent oppression, not to praise war for its own sake.

“إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى النَّاسِ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَشْكُرُونَ”

“Indeed, The God is gracious to people, but most people do not give thanks.”

The ending of the verse extends its message beyond one group’s experience to a general truth about human beings.

“Faḍl,” from the root f-ḍ-l, refers to increase and a gift beyond the basic measure. “Shukr” is also not merely giving thanks verbally, but recognizing a blessing and responding appropriately in awareness and action.

This same sentence is repeated verbatim in Ghafir 40:61 and appears in similar wording in Yunus 10:60 and Yusuf 12:38. Divine bounty is not confined to one event or group; life, guidance, security, the ability to choose, and the possibility of rising after defeat are also blessings whose worth human beings should recognize.

It is noteworthy that the account of fighting and David’s victory also ends, in Al-Baqarah 2:251, with:

“وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ ذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى الْعَالَمِينَ”

“But The God is gracious to all worlds.”

This sequence of verses thus begins by recalling divine bounty in death and revival and ends by recalling the same bounty in preventing corruption and preserving collective life.

In this reading, the verse’s message is not simply that thousands fled death and death overtook them. The Quran goes on to place before us the fate of a community some of whose members, despite expulsion from their homes and fear of the enemy, stood firm and opened a path back to strength and social life. Migration can save a person’s life, but their responsibility does not end upon reaching safety. Great numbers without resolve and responsibility do not prevent collapse, just as small numbers, together with faith, patience, and steadfastness in what is right, do not prevent victory. Gratitude for divine bounty means that individuals and societies use the blessings of life, security, freedom, and ability in the service of truth, justice, and the prevention of oppression.
