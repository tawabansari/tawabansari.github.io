Al-Baqarah 2:279

ۖ

So if you do not do so, be aware of a war from The God and His Messenger; and if you repent, your principal sums are yours; you neither do injustice nor suffer injustice.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:278, the Quran commanded believers to relinquish what remained of riba. Now it describes the consequence of defying this command with one of its severest warnings.

“فَإِنْ لَمْ تَفْعَلُوا فَأْذَنُوا بِحَرْبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ”

“So if you do not do so, be aware of a war from The God and His Messenger.”

“فَإِنْ لَمْ تَفْعَلُوا” refers back to the preceding verse’s command: if you do not relinquish the outstanding claims to riba.

“فَأْذَنُوا” comes from ʾ DH N, a root connected with hearing, becoming aware, announcing, and permitting. In this construction and context, however, it means become aware and know well, not giving or seeking permission.

In At-Tawbah 9:3, “أَذَانٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ” likewise refers to an announcement from God and His Messenger. This linguistic connection shows that idhn does not mean only permission; it can also concern conveying news and making an audience aware.

“Ḥarb,” from Ḥ R B, refers to war and severe confrontation. “مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ” also clarifies the source of this warning: a war from The God and His Messenger.

The severity of this expression shows that consuming riba in the Quran is not merely a simple financial disagreement or a question of the amount of profit.

In Al-Baqarah 2:275, the Quran declared riba forbidden; in Al-Baqarah 2:276, it spoke of eradicating riba in contrast to growing charities. Now it confronts those who persist in claims to riba despite receiving these warnings with an announcement of war from God and His Messenger.

This severity can also be understood in light of riba’s consequences. When people’s financial needs become a means of continually increasing others’ wealth, debt can destroy their economic independence, the fruits of their work, and opportunities in life.

Such relations are not confined to the two parties to a contract. If they spread throughout society, they can concentrate wealth and economic power in the hands of small groups and make much of the population dependent on them for necessities.

By contrast, in the verses on giving, the Quran discusses circulating blessings, empowering those in need, and the growth of goodness’s effects. For example, Al-Baqarah 2:261 depicts the multiplying fruits of giving, and Al-Hashr 59:7 prevents public wealth from circulating only among the rich.

From this perspective, consuming riba can conflict with justice, human dignity, and healthy social growth. For a fuller examination of these consequences, see “The Book of Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest” and “The Economy of Dependence: When Human Need Becomes an Instrument of Power”.

But we must distinguish between the severity of the Quran’s warning and how it is implemented in society.

The verse explicitly speaks of war; this expression should therefore not be reduced merely to riba’s ethical undesirability or economic harm. On the other hand, the expression itself does not specify the details of military action, a method of confrontation, or individuals’ authority to use violence. It therefore cannot be made into authorization for vigilante action against people.

What is certain in this verse is the severity of God’s and His Messenger’s opposition to knowingly continuing to consume riba and disregarding the command to relinquish it.

Returning from Riba and Preserving the Principal

Immediately after this severe warning, the Quran sets out the path of return:

“وَإِنْ تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ”

“And if you repent, your principal sums are yours.”

“تُبْتُمْ,” from T W B, refers to returning. Repentance in this context is not merely expressing regret; it accompanies relinquishing a claim to riba.

“رُءُوس” is the plural of raʾs, meaning the head, beginning, or main part of something. In “رُءُوسُ أَمْوَالِكُمْ,” it refers to the principal capital or original wealth.

This expression contains a very important point: by prohibiting riba, the Quran does not extinguish a person’s legitimate ownership of the principal.

For example, if someone provided wealth to another and demanded an illegitimate increase through a relationship involving riba, desisting from riba removes the claim to that increase; the original wealth, however, still belongs to its owner.

Repentance and reform therefore do not mean depriving someone of all their possessions or legitimate rights.

This also connects with Al-Baqarah 2:275, which distinguished what had passed from desisting after receiving admonition.

Al-Baqarah 2:278 commanded relinquishing outstanding claims to riba; now it becomes clear that this command does not deny a person’s right to their principal.

In today’s economy, where credit money and inflation can change purchasing power, assessing the principal’s real value involves further complexities. This expression alone does not determine how to calculate currency depreciation, compensate for inflation, or rule on every contemporary contract.

Such examination requires understanding both the reality of the economic relationship and the principle of justice for both parties that the Quran states immediately afterward. These issues are discussed in detail in “The Book of Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest”.

Neither Do Injustice nor Suffer Injustice

The verse ends by stating a fundamental principle:

“لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ”

“You neither do injustice nor suffer injustice.”

Both verbs come from Ẓ L M, but their grammatical forms differ.

“تَظْلِمُونَ” is active: you do injustice.

“تُظْلَمُونَ” is passive: injustice is done to you.

This distinction shows that the Quran does not merely address preventing one party’s injustice; it considers both parties’ rights.

A creditor has no right to wrong a debtor by demanding riba, just as a debtor has no right to deprive another person wrongfully of their principal.

For example, someone exploiting another’s need and continually increasing their debt may drive the debtor into dependence and hardship.

Conversely, if someone lends wealth to another and the debtor knowingly refuses to return it despite having the ability and a genuine obligation, the lender’s rights are also violated.

The Quran endorses neither injustice.

This principle also accords with An-Nisa 4:29, which prohibits consuming others’ wealth wrongfully and discusses trade by consent.

In An-Nisa 4:135, the Quran also commands upholding justice, even against oneself or one’s relatives.

Economic justice is not merely supporting the debtor or defending the creditor. It means respecting both parties’ rights and preventing the abuse of power, need, or financial position.

This principle is especially important in today’s financial relations: a contract may appear legal yet violate one party’s rights in practice. Conversely, the legitimate rights of those who have made their wealth available to others cannot be disregarded in the name of supporting people in need.

In the next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:280, the Quran also addresses the debtor in hardship. Examining rights and responsibilities under those circumstances complements this same principle of justice for both parties.

For further study, see the principle of justice, the principle of respecting others’ property rights, and the Quranic root Ẓ L M.

The message of this verse is that knowingly continuing to consume riba and persisting in claims to it conflicts so profoundly with God’s command that the Quran warns of war from The God and His Messenger. Nevertheless, the path of repentance and reform remains open. A person who desists from riba does not lose their right to their principal, but has no right to exploit others’ need and incapacity to demand an increase constituting riba. The Quran itself makes the fundamental boundary of these financial relations clear: neither do injustice nor suffer injustice.
