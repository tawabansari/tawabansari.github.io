Al-Baqarah 2:173

ۖ ۚ

He has forbidden you only carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that over which a cry has been raised for someone other than The God; but whoever is compelled by necessity, without seeking it out of defiance or exceeding the limit, incurs no sin. Indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.

Our Reflection

After warning against speaking about God without knowledge and following inherited rulings, the Qur’an itself identifies the principal categories of forbidden food. The verse begins with “innama,” which conveys restriction in this construction. Four categories are stated: carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that over which a cry has been raised for someone other than God. The same categories occur in Al-Anam 6:145 and An-Nahl 16:115, while Al-Maidah 5:3 explains in greater detail different circumstances of an animal’s death, lawful slaughter, and prohibited rites. God has stated the actual boundaries of forbidden food; human beings have no right to add to them through preference, conjecture, or inherited traditions.

Attributing permission and prohibition to God without knowledge and His authorization is a fabrication against God and an encroachment into associating partners with Him in legislation. Al-Maidah 5:103 condemns invented rulings concerning livestock, and Al-Araf 7:32 questions those who forbid God’s adornments and wholesome provisions. For a fuller examination, see “Halal and Haram in the Qur’an: Divine Legislative Authority and the Danger of Associating Partners in Religion”.

The first three categories concern food and its condition: “al-maytah” is carrion; “al-dam” is blood, clarified further in Al-Anam 6:145 by “daman masfuhan,” meaning blood poured out or flowing; and “lahm al-khinzir” is the flesh of swine. In the fourth category, however, the issue is not simply the kind or physical condition of the food, but also the direction of a person’s devotion in a ritual act.

The expression “وَمَا أُهِلَّ بِهِ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ” speaks of something over which a cry has been raised for someone other than God. “Uhilla,” from the root H L L, means raising a cry or making an announcement aloud, an expression also associated in Arabic with proclaiming a name at a sacrifice. To understand the ruling, we must attend to “li-ghayri Allah”: the cry and proclamation were for someone other than God. In such a situation, the animal is dedicated to another object of worship, and the sacrifice departs from the path of serving God. What this expression prohibits is therefore not merely a type of meat, but food connected with a ritual act for someone other than God.

But sacrificing for someone other than God is not the same as not knowing what name was mentioned at slaughter. If someone buys meat at a market and does not know what was said during its preparation, that lack of knowledge does not itself establish that the animal was sacrificed for someone other than God. The Qur’an does, of course, give commands in Al-Anam 6:118 and Al-Anam 6:121 concerning remembrance of God’s name and not eating that over which His name has not been mentioned; these must be taken seriously. Yet “God’s name was not mentioned” and “the animal was dedicated to someone other than God” are not identical in wording or subject. Al-Maidah 5:5 also declares the food of the People of the Book lawful for believers. These verses must therefore be understood together: neither treating ignorance of a spoken expression as conclusive proof of prohibition nor disregarding the commands concerning remembrance of God’s name.

This distinction has clear examples in people’s lives. In some communities, an animal is slaughtered at a bride’s feet when she arrives; elsewhere, a sheep is slaughtered at a pilgrim’s feet on returning from pilgrimage; sometimes an animal is vowed and sacrificed in the name of a spiritual elder, a sheikh, a leader, or a person buried at a shrine. In each case we must ask: for whom and with what intention is the sacrifice performed? If the animal is sacrificed as an act of worship seeking closeness to the bride, pilgrim, elder, or person at the shrine, seeking their sacred favor, or seeking blessing and protection from harm through a power attributed to them, the act falls within dedicating a sacrifice to someone other than God. The title of pilgrim, saint, sheikh, or elder cannot turn such an act into worship of God. But if the animal is slaughtered simply to feed guests, mark an occasion, or feed those in need, the presence of a bride or pilgrim does not itself make it a sacrifice for someone other than God. The central distinction is between feeding human beings and offering sacrifices to them as an act of worship.

Serving The God is not fulfilled merely by uttering His name. In Al-Baqarah 2:172, God connects eating wholesome things with gratitude and sincere worship. A person uses God’s provision to nourish themselves and their family, and expresses gratitude for that blessing through good deeds, helping those in need, respecting others’ rights, and avoiding waste. In Al-Hajj 22:37, God also reminds us that neither the meat nor the blood of sacrifices reaches Him; rather, people’s mindfulness of Him reaches Him. The reality of servitude thus appears in a person’s orientation toward God and good conduct, not in a display of bloodshed or the repetition of a few words.

Immediately after stating the prohibitions, God opens a way of relief for the person in necessity: “فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ”. If a person faces severe hunger, poverty and a genuine shortage of food, illness, or incapacity, and has no suitable means of preserving life and health other than consuming forbidden food, there is no sin upon them. The two qualifications, “ghayra baghin” and “wa-la adin,” make clear that one must not seek to break the ruling out of defiance or exceed the bounds of necessity. These qualifications, however, do not come to make life harder for the person in necessity; their purpose is to open a path of rescue in actual conditions of necessity.

The verse ends with the two descriptions “ghafurun rahim,” revealing divine mercy. The God who has defined the boundaries of prohibition also knows His servants’ weakness, hunger, and real needs. Even in these prohibited categories, He has opened a way for the person in necessity to be free of sin. If God has provided such mercy and relief within His explicit prohibitions, by what right do human beings make His servants’ lives harder by adding prohibitions without evidence? Serving The God means respecting the boundaries He has established, not forging new chains in His name.
