Al-Baqarah 2:202

ۚ

They have a share of what they have earned; and The God is swift in reckoning.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:200, The God spoke of those who seek only this world. In Al-Baqarah 2:201, He presented a prayer encompassing good in this world and the Hereafter. He now says:

“أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ نَصِيبٌ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا”

“They have a share of what they have earned.”

This short statement clarifies an important truth about prayer and its answer. In the preceding verse, The God presented the prayer for good in this world and the Hereafter, but He now connects a person’s share to “مِمَّا كَسَبُوا”—what they themselves have earned.

Neither Al-Baqarah 2:186, where The God says that He answers the supplicant’s prayer, nor the prayer in Al-Baqarah 2:201 should lead us to conclude that every good request necessarily comes true without human effort and in exactly the form requested.

The Qur’an teaches us to pray, but does not say that prayer replaces human choice, action, and effort.

“كَسَبُوا,” from the root ك س ب, means to acquire or earn. Someone seeking knowledge must strive to learn. Someone seeking a better life must work to improve it. A society seeking security, justice, and progress must likewise understand the paths to them and act to bring them about.

The God says in An-Najm 53:39:

“وَأَنْ لَيْسَ لِلْإِنْسَانِ إِلَّا مَا سَعَىٰ”

“And that a person has only what they have striven for.”

In Ar-Rad 13:11, He also connects change in a people’s condition with change in what is within themselves. In Al-Anfal 8:53, the same principle is expressed concerning divine blessings:

“ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّ اللَّهَ لَمْ يَكُ مُغَيِّرًا نِعْمَةً أَنْعَمَهَا عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ حَتَّىٰ يُغَيِّرُوا مَا بِأَنْفُسِهِمْ”

“That is because The God does not change a blessing He has bestowed upon a people until they change what is within themselves.”

Changes in living conditions are thus connected with people’s choices and actions; their responsibility should not be detached from this relationship.

This does not mean that prayer has no effect. Prayer can prepare the way for relief: The God can place new paths, opportunities, and circumstances before a person, making better choices easier and more practicable. Yet using these opportunities and taking responsibility for the choice remain the person’s task.

The God has created human beings in a world with definite laws and established patterns. A person does not choose the circumstances in which their life begins, but their choices within those circumstances can help shape what follows. This relationship between freedom of choice, decree, prayer, and divine patterns is examined in “Divine Decree: Choice or Compulsion?”.

Therefore, a person cannot choose a path contrary to God’s guidance, neglect the necessary means and resources, and then expect the desired outcome merely by repeating prayers.

The Qur’an also makes worldly life a field of testing. In Al-Baqarah 2:155, it speaks of testing through fear, hunger, and losses of wealth and lives, and then gives glad tidings to the steadfast.

But patience does not mean remaining forever in the same situation and waiting without reform or effort. When difficulty continues, alongside patience and prayer we should examine the path we have taken, the choices we have made, and the resources we have used. Commendable patience is steadfastness on the right path, not insistence on every decision we previously made. This distinction is examined in “Patience in the Qur’an: The Meaning of Steadfastness and the Criterion of Its Value”.

At this point, we should also attend to the end of the verse itself:

“وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ”

“And The God is swift in reckoning.”

“Account,” from the root ح س ب, concerns counting, assessing, and examining. God’s reckoning is not subject to human slowness and limitations. Yet we should not confine this statement to the idea that all effects of human actions appear only after death. The accounting and results of human actions need not necessarily be deferred to the Hereafter; people’s choices and actions have consequences in this world as well.

In Ar-Rum 30:41, the Qur’an connects the appearance of corruption on land and sea with what people’s hands have earned. People should therefore recognize the effects of their choices in this life and act to correct them. Of course, swift divine reckoning does not mean every prayer immediately produces the desired result. Rather, it reminds us that human actions do not escape God’s knowledge and accounting, and that their consequences can begin in this world.

We should therefore not defer the entire response to life’s suffering to the Hereafter, telling people simply to endure until they receive their reward after death. The Qur’an speaks of testing in this world, and also of glad tidings, goodness, relief, and people’s responsibility to change their circumstances within it. This subject is examined in detail in “Trials and the Promise of Good in the Qur’an: Is This World the Believer’s Prison and the Disbeliever’s Paradise?”.

To make this lesson clearer, we can consider an example from life today.

This example was inspired by a message from a friend who, referring to the prolonged suffering of the Palestinian people and their prayers, asked why no sign of an answer was yet apparent despite so much prayer.

The following discussion of Palestine is not a political analysis of Palestine or a judgment of the parties involved. It is a practical example for understanding the relationship between prayer, its answer, divine commands, human choice, and the consequences of action.

In this connection, the lives of Muhammad and the believers of Mecca provide an important example within the Qur’an itself.

The believers in Mecca faced persecution, exclusion, and pressure. But their path was not simply to remain in the same circumstances and pray for hostility to end. They emigrated, leaving behind homes, property, and many attachments to begin a new stage of life and worship.

In An-Nisa 4:97, the Qur’an questions those who remained in oppressive circumstances despite having the ability to emigrate:

“أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ اللَّهِ وَاسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا فِيهَا”

“Was The God’s earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate within it?”

Emigration is a major command in the Qur’an and one of religion’s practical principles, not a peripheral recommendation that a person may disregard during hardship. In An-Nisa 4:97, the claim of powerlessness by people able to emigrate is not accepted, and a very grave consequence is stated for their refusal. In An-Nisa 4:98 and An-Nisa 4:99, The God distinguishes those who truly lack the ability and a way out, while An-Nisa 4:100 speaks of the openings an emigrant finds in the earth. In Al-Anfal 8:72, emigration appears alongside faith, striving with wealth and lives, providing refuge, and giving support. Thus, when someone has a real possibility of escaping oppression but refuses to emigrate solely out of attachment to land, property, and ancestral territory, they should ask whether they have followed God’s command or placed their own desires and prejudice before it.

When confronting an enemy far superior in equipment and military capability, sheer stubbornness cannot replace sound judgment. One cannot face guns and aircraft with stones and sticks merely out of prejudice and then expect prayer to change the balance of power without changing course and using appropriate means.

In Al-Anfal 8:65 and Al-Anfal 8:66, the Qur’an itself considers the actual condition of the forces when discussing their ability to confront an opponent. In Al-Anfal 8:66, it says:

“الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ وَعَلِمَ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ ضَعْفًا”

“Now The God has lightened your burden and knows that there is weakness among you.”

It then reduces the ratio for confrontation compared with the preceding verse. This difference shows that even in battle, people’s actual weakness and strength are not disregarded. Steadfastness in truth should therefore not be confused with closing one’s eyes to actual capabilities and resources.

This does not remove responsibility for oppression and crime from the aggressor. It raises another question: what choices have we made concerning the commands and paths God has presented for preserving life, improving conditions, and gaining capability?

Muhammad, as the believers’ leader, did more than call people to emigrate: he worked to prepare for their reception. Historical accounts describe his contacts with the people of Yathrib and the agreements made before emigration as preparing refuge for the emigrants. The Qur’an also speaks of the emigrants and those who sheltered and supported them in Al-Anfal 8:72, Al-Hashr 59:8, and Al-Hashr 59:9. This example places a major responsibility on leaders and commanders. Palestinian leaders and commanders likewise face this question: for people willing and able to emigrate, do they provide safe passage, reception, accommodation, and preservation of collective capacity, or encourage them to remain exposed to killing through slogans about refusing to relinquish property, life, and ancestral land? Leadership means more than calling people to stand firm; providing a way to preserve their lives and preparing for their future are also responsibilities of leadership.

But emigration is not the end of the path either.

After emigrating, the believers of Mecca did not merely scatter in another land so that each could pursue their own life and business. Together with the Helpers, they built a new community with bonds of faith, mutual support, shared responsibility, and social capacity.

In Al-Anfal 8:72, the Qur’an speaks of those who believed, emigrated, and strove with their wealth and lives in God’s way, and those who sheltered and supported them. In Al-Hashr 59:8 and Al-Hashr 59:9, it presents the emigrants driven from their homes and possessions alongside those who received them and preferred others to themselves even when personally in need.

This is community-building after emigration: providing refuge, cooperation, reform, acquiring capability, conveying the message, justice, doing good, and preparing for greater responsibilities.

The question is therefore not only why people should emigrate from a land. The next question matters too: what do they do after emigration? Do they build a united and capable community, or does each person, scattered from the others, pursue personal interests alone and forget the shared purpose?

The journey of Mecca’s emigrants did not end when they left their homes. After emigration, they passed through the difficult stages of building a community, organizing relationships, reform, conveying the message, and developing their capabilities, eventually gaining the ability to return to Mecca.

Movement and leaving familiar territory also have an important place in the story of the Children of Israel. The Qur’an speaks of Jacob’s family coming to Egypt in Yusuf 12:99, the Children of Israel’s departure from Egypt, and then the command to enter the holy land in Al-Maidah 5:21. This journey likewise shows that wishes alone do not bring people to their destination: obedience, patience, movement, and facing trials play a part.

Thus, if we use the Palestinian experience to understand this verse in practice, the main question is not merely how many years people have prayed. It is what paths were available to them, what choices were made, what resources were used, and what steps toward reform, solidarity, and capability followed each choice.

Prayer, patience, emigration, personal reform, community-building, and effort do not replace one another. Each has its own place, and people must accept responsibility for their choices and actions.

This is the point that “مِمَّا كَسَبُوا,” in connection with the preceding verse’s prayer, places before us.

Prayer matters, but it does not replace action. The God can open paths and prepare opportunities for better choices, but a person must choose the right path, relinquish attachments that obstruct obedience, work to improve conditions, and accept the responsibilities of the next stage after each stage is completed.

After teaching the prayer for good in this world and the Hereafter, Al-Baqarah 2:202 brings us back to the truth that a person’s share is connected with what they themselves earn. Alongside asking about the answer to prayer, we should therefore ask ourselves: have we acted according to The God’s guidance, or merely chosen our preferred path and asked Him for a different outcome?
