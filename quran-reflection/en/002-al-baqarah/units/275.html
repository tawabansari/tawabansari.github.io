Al-Baqarah 2:275

ۚ ۗ ۚ ۖ ۖ

Those who consume riba do not rise except as someone whom Satan has thrown into confusion through contact rises. That is because they said: “Trade is just like riba”; yet The God has permitted trade and forbidden riba. So whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists may retain what is past, and his affair rests with The God; and whoever returns—those are the companions of the Fire; they will remain in it.

Our Reflection

Riba is a Quranic concept around which limited and sometimes mistaken interpretations have developed throughout history. In many common interpretations, riba is reduced to interest received on a loan; at times, every kind of profit or financial increase is then called riba without examining its nature. Yet the Quran does not offer such an exclusively numerical definition.

Not every loan is riba, nor can every increase in wealth be called riba without understanding its source, structure, and consequences. To understand this concept, we must first examine its meaning in Quranic language and the economic relations of the period of revelation, then consider how the same principles apply to today’s economic system.

This matters greatly because the economy of the Quran’s time differs fundamentally from the economic system of today’s world.

In that economy, trade took place using goods, gold, and silver, while loans, deferred debts, partnerships, and financial obligations also existed. Today, however, money is not merely coins or banknotes; a large part exists as bank deposits, and banks can create new bank money when extending credit. Inflation may also reduce the real value of people’s assets and purchasing power even if the nominal amount of their money has not changed.

Thus, the difference between past and present economies is not merely the means of payment: the creation of money, circulation of capital, allocation of risk and loss, and possibilities for concentrating economic power have also changed.

Without understanding this transformation, we may focus entirely on the interest rate of a loan while overlooking mechanisms that, without being called riba, lead to unjust transfers of wealth and others’ economic dependence. Conversely, we may declare a transaction forbidden merely because it produces profit or a numerical increase, without examining its actual nature.

We examine these developments in detail in the independent study “The Book of Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest”. Chapter Three is particularly relevant to the economy of the period of revelation; Chapter Four to the emergence of banking and credit money; and Chapter Eight to the limitations of the traditional definition of riba in today’s economy.

The aim is not to change the Quran’s ruling through new economic theories, but to understand the Quranic concept of riba correctly and apply it to today’s different financial relations.

The root of riba, R B W, refers to rising, increasing, and growth. The root itself does not carry a negative value. In Al-Baqarah 2:265, the Quran spoke of a garden on elevated ground, “رَبْوَة,” from the same word family. In Al-Hajj 22:5, the earth’s swelling after receiving water is also expressed through a derivative of this root.

By contrast, ribḥ, from the separate root R B Ḥ, refers to profit and benefit. In Al-Baqarah 2:16, the Quran speaks of failing to profit in a trading metaphor.

Thus, ribḥ and riba do not share one root or one meaning. The root R B W conveys increase itself; the kind of increase, how it is obtained, and its ethical value are determined by usage and context. For a fuller examination, see the studies of the Quranic roots R B W and R B Ḥ.

Nor does the Quran explain riba through only one expression. In Ar-Rum 30:39, the increase of riba in people’s wealth is contrasted with the increase resulting from zakat given for God’s countenance. In Aal Imran 3:130, consuming multiplying riba is forbidden, and in An-Nisa 4:161, taking riba despite its prohibition appears alongside consuming people’s wealth wrongfully.

Now, in Al-Baqarah, after an extensive sequence of verses on giving, the Quran introduces riba. In the following verses, it connects it with charities, repentance, principal capital, injustice, and the situation of a debtor in hardship.

Together, these passages provide a network of evidence for understanding riba. First, we must attend to this verse’s place in its context.

In the preceding verses, the Quran presented giving as a means of putting blessings into circulation, helping those in need, developing people, and enabling society to flourish. In Al-Baqarah 2:261, giving was compared to a grain capable of producing multiplied fruits. In Al-Baqarah 2:265, giving was connected with inner firmness, and Al-Baqarah 2:272 recalled that the good of giving returns to the giver.

This principle is not limited to charitable donations. Using capital, knowledge, skills, and life’s opportunities for production, creating jobs, and empowering people can also enable healthy social growth. This is examined in “The Principle of Giving and Using Blessings”.

By contrast, relations involving riba can concentrate wealth in the hands of small groups possessing the power of capital and control of resources, while much of the population becomes dependent on them to meet living needs and continue economic activity.

For example, when people have suitable employment and income, their purchasing power increases, businesses thrive, and more economic opportunities emerge. But if a large part of household income goes toward debts that continually increase, their ability to buy, save, and invest declines, and their dependence on owners of capital grows.

Along one path, capital helps develop people’s capabilities and expand economic opportunities; along the other, it becomes a means of concentrating wealth and increasing others’ dependence.

Regarding public wealth, Al-Hashr 59:7 also prevents its circulation from being confined to the wealthy. In Al-Baqarah 2:276, the contrast between eradicating riba and increasing charities becomes more explicit. This connection shows that a numerical increase in wealth does not necessarily mean real economic growth or improvement in people’s lives.

The Quran now says of those who consume riba:

“الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ الرِّبَا لَا يَقُومُونَ إِلَّا كَمَا يَقُومُ الَّذِي يَتَخَبَّطُهُ الشَّيْطَانُ مِنَ الْمَسِّ”

“Those who consume riba do not rise except as someone whom Satan has thrown into confusion through contact rises.”

In this context, “يَأْكُلُونَ” refers to consuming and benefiting from wealth obtained through riba. “يَقُومُونَ,” from Q W M, concerns rising and standing upright, while “يَتَخَبَّطُهُ,” from KH B Ṭ, presents an image of confusion, irregular impacts, and loss of balance.

The verse speaks of those who consume riba and benefit from it. This expression does not establish that every person compelled by financial need to borrow has the same moral responsibility.

For example, someone compelled to borrow for their child’s treatment is not in the same position as someone knowingly exploiting that desperation to extract additional wealth.

In narrations cited in common Sunni and Shia jurisprudence, not only the recipient of riba but also its payer is counted among those cursed. Yet the payer may be a person in need compelled to borrow to treat a child, feed a family, or preserve life. This verse, however, condemns “الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ الرِّبَا”; it does not equally condemn the consumer of riba and someone trapped by them under the pressure of need. Such an equation is inconsistent with responsibility according to capacity (Al-Baqarah 2:286), God’s consideration of necessity (Al-Baqarah 2:173), and the command to grant time to a debtor in hardship (Al-Baqarah 2:280). Profiting from others’ desperation and falling into the grip of profiteers are neither the same conduct nor the same responsibility.

Thus, people’s responsibilities cannot be equated without considering freedom of choice, capacity, necessity, and actual conduct. The Quran has not authorized us to condemn everyone caught in financial relations involving riba without regard to their circumstances and roles.

The Quran compares the condition of those who consume riba to the rising of a person thrown into confusion by Satan’s touch. Without Quranic evidence, this image should not be turned into a mental illness, a distortion of personality, or a theory of the physical mechanism of Satan’s contact.

In An-Nisa 4:5, the Quran describes wealth as a means of sustaining people’s lives. This linguistic connection also matters when reflecting on riba’s economic effects, since financial relations can strengthen or weaken people’s ability to stand, maintain independence, and continue living.

On the other hand, rising in the Quran can also relate to the scene of resurrection, as Al-Mutaffifin 83:6 speaks of people rising before the Lord of the worlds. Without sufficient indication, therefore, this verse’s image should not be confined exclusively to a worldly or otherworldly condition.

Given the context of giving and financial relations, the image of confusion can also draw our attention to riba’s ethical and social consequences. Someone who places the increase of their possessions above every other consideration may become so absorbed in profiteering that they fail to see others’ needs, suffering, and rights. At the social level, relations based on debt pressure, unequal transfer of risk, and making people dependent produce inequality and instability rather than healthy growth.

Wealth that increases numerically while depriving others of the ability to stand and live independently cannot be considered a sign of society’s flourishing merely because it increases.

This social reflection does not replace the verse’s original image; it is one consequence that can be examined in light of the context and the network of verses on riba. For a more precise understanding of the linguistic image, see the Quranic root KH B Ṭ.

The Quran then explains the reason for their condition:

“ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّمَا الْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ الرِّبَا”

“That is because they said: Trade is just like riba.”

Here the Quran reports the words of people who made no distinction between trade and riba. Their wording is also noteworthy: they did not say riba is like trade; rather, they described trade as like riba and emphasized this equivalence with “إِنَّمَا.”

They may have made this claim because they regarded both as means of increasing wealth, or saw the similarity in agreement and contracting between two parties, exchanging value, and receiving a benefit in return for time or making capital available. The verse itself, however, does not specify which of these considerations underlay their claim.

The Quran answers:

“وَأَحَلَّ اللَّهُ الْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ الرِّبَا”

“And The God has permitted trade and forbidden riba.”

“Bayʿ,” from B Y ʿ, refers to buying and selling, exchanging something for consideration: the familiar trade among people. But a transaction’s prevalence does not itself prove its correctness. Sound trade rests on genuine consent, fairness, exchange of value, and respect for both parties’ rights: one provides a good or service and the other pays for it. By contrast, one of riba’s clearest manifestations is an owner of capital using the other party’s need and incapacity to increase their own wealth. The difference thus lies not merely in the contract’s name or earning profit, but in the nature of the relationship and how human rights are respected. For the criteria of proper conduct and sound trade, see the principle of encouraging right and the principle of consent and fairness in trade.

A merchant can earn legitimate profit through understanding people’s needs, providing goods, investing, managing, and accepting the risks of trade. The buyer also receives a good or service of value in return for the price paid.

A merchant’s profit is not inherently riba. Sound trade can promote production, circulation of capital, employment, and greater social welfare. In this respect, legitimate trade can also exemplify the principle of giving and using blessings for good: people put their capital and abilities to work and, alongside the benefit they obtain, provide value and economic opportunities for others.

But a quite different relationship may develop in arrangements involving riba.

For example, one person possesses the capital a producer needs. The producer works with that capital, makes a product, and accepts the risk of the activity failing. If the capital owner shares in the actual profit and loss, their benefit is linked to the outcome. But if, solely through financial power, they demand a fixed increase under all circumstances and place the main burden of failure, debt, and loss on the producer, an unequal relationship takes shape.

In such circumstances, one party creates value and bears the risk of the activity, while the other seeks to guarantee an increase in their assets independently of that activity’s actual outcome.

In large financial systems, this problem can extend beyond a relationship between two people. When capital and credit are controlled by small groups, they can use their economic position to transfer gains to themselves and impose much of the risk and loss on others. Those adding value to the economy through work and production may then receive a smaller share of its fruits, while holders of financial power accumulate more wealth without proportionate participation in risk and responsibility.

Nevertheless, receiving a benefit without direct work, or earning a return under a contract with a specified yield, is not by itself sufficient to establish riba. Making capital available can also entail opportunity costs, limited liquidity, and risk for its owner. Nor is every partnership fair, or every transaction called trade necessarily respectful of both parties’ rights.

The distinction should therefore not be sought solely in the contract’s title, the amount of profit, or its legal appearance. The actual structure of the relationship and how the benefit is obtained must be understood.

In An-Nisa 4:29, the Quran contrasts trade by consent with consuming wealth wrongfully; in An-Nisa 4:161, it condemns taking riba alongside wrongfully appropriating people’s wealth. In Al-Baqarah 2:279, neither doing nor suffering injustice is also an important principle in addressing riba.

These verses provide important criteria for assessing economic relations. We must ask: What value has been exchanged or produced? Where has the profit come from? Who bears the risk and loss? Have both parties’ rights been respected, or has one person’s need and incapacity become a means of increasing another’s wealth?

The distinction between trade and riba should not be reduced to two different names or contractual forms; the reality of the economic relationship must be understood. Yet not every unjust relationship automatically constitutes riba merely because it is unjust. Identifying riba requires examining the full Quranic evidence and the transaction’s actual nature.

This subject also has a conceptual connection with maysir. The Quran discusses maysir in Al-Baqarah 2:219 and Al-Maidah 5:90. In the study of the root Y S R and Chapter Five, section 5.6 of the Book of Riba, we examine how some financial activities can rest on speculation, chance, and profiting from others’ losses rather than participation in production and creating value.

Riba and maysir are not one word or one identical structure, nor is every risky transaction maysir. Their connection lies in certain ways of obtaining benefit that depart from creating value and bearing real responsibility.

For a fuller examination of the differences among trade, riba, risk-taking, investment, and speculation, see Chapters Five, Six, and Seven of the Book of Riba.

After declaring riba forbidden, the Quran also leaves the way open for return:

“فَمَنْ جَاءَهُ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ فَانْتَهَىٰ فَلَهُ مَا سَلَفَ وَأَمْرُهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ”

“So whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists may retain what is past, and his affair rests with The God.”

“فَانْتَهَىٰ” refers to ceasing the prohibited conduct, and “مَا سَلَفَ” to what has already passed. For someone who receives divine admonition and desists from riba, the Quran distinguishes between the past and continuation of that conduct, leaving the final disposition of their affair to God.

This statement opens a path to return and reform without yielding an unrestricted rule concerning all past possessions and rights.

The verse, however, ends with a severe warning:

“وَمَنْ عَادَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ”

“And whoever returns—those are the companions of the Fire; they will remain in it.”

“ʿĀda,” from ʿ W D, refers to returning. This expression stands opposite “فَانْتَهَىٰ”: after receiving admonition, a person can desist; but if they return to the same conduct, they face the Quran’s severe warning.

This ending shows that riba in the Quran is not merely a simple error in calculating profit and loss. It concerns human conduct after recognizing the divine command, responsibility toward others, and the consequences of complying with or returning to prohibited conduct.

The message of this verse is that not every financial increase is riba, nor is every profit forbidden simply because wealth increases. The Quran draws an explicit distinction between trade and riba. To understand this distinction today, we must examine the Quranic meanings of the words as well as the actual mechanisms of money, capital, production, risk, and wealth distribution. Sound trade can create value, circulate blessings, and help society grow; relations involving riba, however, can increase a small group’s wealth through others’ pressure, dependence, and loss. Alongside its severe warning, the Quran has also left open the path of desisting and reform.
