Al-Baqarah 2:178

ۖ ۚ ۗ ۗ

O you who believe, qisas concerning those killed has been prescribed for you: the free for the free, the ʿabd for the ʿabd, and the woman for the woman. Whoever has something remitted for him by his brother, pursuit should be proper and discharge to him with kindness. This is relief from your Lord and mercy. Whoever then transgresses after that will have a painful punishment.

Our Reflection

Al-Baqarah 2:178 is a verse that cannot simply be passed over. It raises fundamental questions about justice, human equality, individual responsibility, and the right to forgive. Perhaps The God, by placing such a verse before His servants, intends them to think and question rather than accept inherited interpretations without examination, so that it becomes clear which readings of His command accord with uncorrupted human nature and the high ideals of social justice.

Before drawing conclusions, then, we should honestly raise these questions:

1. What exactly is qisas? Has God prescribed killing in return for killing, or pursuing a rightful claim and a just, proportionate response to a crime? Why has He used the word qisas rather than an explicit command to kill?

2. If the Qur’an seeks justice and human equality, why does it say “the free for the free, the ʿabd for the ʿabd, and the woman for the woman”? Does this expression assign different values to human lives according to sex and social position, or has it come to correct such distinctions? Why are “free” and “ʿabd,” which concern social status and relationships of autonomy and obedience, placed alongside “woman,” a designation of sex? How does this ruling encompass men and women in different social and legal circumstances?

3. If justice rests on individual responsibility, why does the verse not speak directly of the killer and that person’s responsibility? Could someone bear a different responsibility for a killing because of sex or social position? How would such a reading accord with the Qur’anic principle that no one bears another’s burden?

4. Is the Qur’an reforming an earlier legal system? In some Torah rulings concerning human life, distinctions are made between free and enslaved people, yet the Qur’an speaks of “life for life” in Al-Maidah 5:45. What does Al-Baqarah 2:178 affirm or reform in this context?

5. If qisas is prescribed through “كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ”, why does God immediately open the way to pardon? Is qisas a right whose pursuit may be waived, or an obligation that must always be carried out? How can these be reconciled?

1. What is qisas: killing for killing, or investigating crime and establishing justice?

Qisas should not be restricted to killing in return for killing. The fundamental meaning of Q Ṣ Ṣ is connected with following a trace and pursuing something, as in Al-Kahf 18:64 concerning retracing footsteps and Al-Qasas 28:11 concerning following and searching. In legal usage, qisas concerns a response proportionate to the offense, as it is also used concerning injuries in Al-Maidah 5:45 and proportionate response to aggression in Al-Baqarah 2:194. Qisas therefore cannot mean killing in every instance. Investigating the crime, identifying the perpetrator, clarifying the circumstances, and determining a just response are necessary for its proper application. Capital punishment may, when intentional killing is established and its conditions are met, be one outcome of qisas; not every death automatically authorizes killing someone else.

To appreciate the need for this distinction, consider several situations.

First, is every killing intentional? Someone may cause another’s death without intending to kill. Can such a person be punished in the same way as someone who knowingly and deliberately killed a human being? An-Nisa 4:92 gives a specific ruling for accidental killing, while An-Nisa 4:93 separately issues a grave warning about intentionally killing a believer. The occurrence of death alone is therefore insufficient to determine the type of responsibility.

Second, is everyone who kills a human being themselves an aggressor? Suppose someone attacks your life or personal security and dies while you defend yourself. Does merely having caused their death make you a killer deserving the same punishment? Or must it first be established who initiated the aggression, what danger existed, and whether killing was necessary to repel that danger?

Third, who bears responsibility when a killing is carried out on another’s orders? A soldier kills someone at a commander’s order. Does all responsibility fall on the soldier, or is the commander also responsible? If a police officer executes someone under a court judgment and that person is later shown to have been innocent, who is responsible for the death: the executing officer, the judge, or those who supplied the evidence? Did the court make an unintentional error in examining the evidence, or did people knowingly fabricate it and bring about an innocent person’s death? How do these differences affect identifying the crime and assigning responsibility?

Fourth, if an enslaved person kills someone at an owner’s order, who is responsible? Should we consider only the enslaved person’s act, or also the owner’s power, coercion, threats, and the enslaved person’s degree of choice? Does someone under another’s domination, who may be unable to disobey, bear in all circumstances the same responsibility as the person issuing orders with power and freedom of choice?

Fifth, do the circumstances and capacities of both parties have no bearing on establishing the truth of the crime? Were the deceased and the person who caused the death in equivalent circumstances regarding physical strength, financial means, legal autonomy, and social position? Did one dominate the other or exploit their vulnerability? These differences do not change the value of human life, but might they not be decisive in understanding how the offense occurred and the degree of agency and responsibility involved?

These questions show that justice cannot be established through the simple formula “one killing for another.” Before determining a proportionate response, the truth of the crime, intent, agency, circumstances, and people’s actual responsibilities must be established. Someone may directly cause a death while the principal responsibility lies with the person giving the order; or a death may occur without its cause constituting wrongful intentional killing.

Conversely, the existence of someone giving orders does not itself absolve the person carrying them out. Al-Qasas 28:8 counts Pharaoh, Haman, and their soldiers together among those at fault. In Al-Ahzab 33:67 and Al-Ahzab 33:68, following leaders and rulers is not presented as an excuse removing responsibility for all the followers’ actions. An-Nahl 16:106, by contrast, distinguishes voluntary action from coercion. Thus, neither can all responsibility be assigned to an agent without inquiry nor can that agent be declared innocent merely for obeying orders. Each person’s part must be clarified separately.

The legal meaning of qisas should be understood in connection with pursuing a rightful claim and responding proportionately to an offense. The root points to following and pursuing, while legal usage emphasizes equivalence and limits in the response. These points should not be confused: the root meaning alone does not define every stage of investigation and adjudication, but just implementation of qisas is also impossible without establishing truth and responsibility.

Justice is established when the proportionate response is directed at the person actually responsible for the crime, not when another human being is killed merely because someone has been killed.

2. Why does the Qur’an say “the free for the free, the ʿabd for the ʿabd, and the woman for the woman”?

Does this expression seek to value lives according to sex and social position and create a class-based system of punishment? Or does it seek to establish justice with attention to people’s actual situations and degrees of agency and responsibility?

First, hurr, free, does not refer only to a man; a woman may also be free. The expression therefore cannot be taken to divide humanity into three separate groups of free men, enslaved people, and women. This overlap itself shows that these three designations are not a complete, mutually exclusive classification by sex and social class.

The second point concerns ʿabd. In Qur’anic language, ʿ B D is connected with servanthood, worship, service, and obedience. A human being can be God’s servant without that servanthood meaning one person’s ownership of another. For example, Jesus identifies himself as God’s servant in Maryam 19:30. The root’s semantic field therefore cannot be confined from the outset to human slavery.

For the specific relationship of ownership, the Qur’an also uses “مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ”: literally, “what your right hands possess,” and, in its application to people, those held in your ownership and control. This expression emphasizes ownership. In An-Nahl 16:75, too, ʿabd is accompanied by the qualification mamluk, owned. These distinctions matter when examining the word’s semantic range.

An enslaved person is therefore one instance of ʿabd, not its entire semantic field. In a broader reading, someone in another’s service who lacks independent decision-making or whose discretion in carrying out orders is restricted can be examined in terms of servitude and obedience. This extension does not equate all legal situations; it draws attention to the shared aspects of service, dependence, and subordination to another’s will.

For example, a soldier serving under an army commander, a police officer charged with executing a court judgment, an enslaved person under an owner’s domination, or even a hired killer acting at another person’s direction all exemplify relationships of service, obedience, and dependence on another’s will. Their degrees of choice, coercion, and knowledge are, of course, not identical.

Here the question of legal responsibility becomes important. Suppose a soldier, on an army general’s orders, bombs a city and kills innocent people. Does all responsibility lie with the soldier who executed the command, or is the general who ordered the killing also responsible?

Or consider a police officer who executes someone under a court judgment, only for that person’s innocence to be established later. Is the executing officer entirely responsible for the death? Did the judge and court officials make an unintentional error in reviewing evidence, or did someone knowingly fabricate evidence and cause an innocent person’s death?

The same question arises about an enslaved person who kills at an owner’s command. Should only the enslaved person be held responsible, or should the owner’s role, power to command, threats, and the enslaved person’s degree of choice also be examined?

In such situations, justice is not complete merely by identifying who directly performed the killing. It must be established who commanded, who acted, how much knowledge and choice each possessed, and what their actual contribution to the crime was.

But why does the Qur’an also mention women separately?

To understand this, women’s social and legal realities must also be considered. Regrettably, in many patriarchal societies women have lacked economic independence, the ability to defend their rights, and autonomy equal to men’s, and have sometimes even been treated as commodities.

The Qur’an itself exposes such injustices. At-Takwir 81:8 and At-Takwir 81:9 speak of the girl buried alive who is asked for what sin she was killed. An-Nahl 16:58 and An-Nahl 16:59 condemn those ashamed at news of a daughter’s birth. An-Nisa 4:19 also prohibits inheriting women against their will. These examples show that deprivation and injustice against women were real issues in the society addressed by the Qur’an.

Such conduct is not confined to the past. In some communities, daughters are exchanged in reciprocal marriages, so that if one family’s daughter is mistreated in her husband’s home, her family inflicts the same mistreatment on its own daughter-in-law. In the reprehensible practice of baad, women and girls are handed to the opposing family to settle disputes or compensate for men’s crimes. In many social relationships and even courts, women also lack equal opportunity and power to defend their rights.

These discriminatory practices are reprehensible and unjust, but denying their existence does not change the realities of people’s lives.

For this reason, people’s actual living conditions, independence, decision-making power, and capacity to defend themselves cannot be ignored in examining responsibility. Two people may be equal in the value of their lives and human dignity but not in agency and power to act. Justice requires assessing each person’s responsibility in light of actual role, intent, knowledge, choice, and circumstances.

This does not mean that being a woman automatically entails greater or lesser responsibility. A woman who freely and knowingly commits a crime is responsible for her conduct, just as a man acting under genuine coercion must be judged with regard to his circumstances and agency. The issue is not sex as a criterion of worth or punishment, but the person’s actual situation in the offense.

On this basis, “the free for the free, the ʿabd for the ʿabd, and the woman for the woman” can also be examined from another angle: not as superiority of a free person’s life over an ʿabd’s or a man’s over a woman’s, but with attention to differences in legal and social position and their effects on choice and responsibility. In this reading, placing the free and the ʿabd alongside the woman draws our attention to actual situations of power and vulnerability rather than creating three separate classes with different human worth.

Equality in the value of human life does not mean identical circumstances and responsibility in every event. Justice does not mean punishing everyone identified as directly causing a death in the same way without examining the truth. It means clarifying each person’s actual role and responsibility and holding no one responsible beyond what they knowingly and freely did.

This reading shifts the question of the free, the ʿabd, and the woman from valuing human lives toward understanding people’s circumstances and actual responsibilities, the same issue fundamental to understanding qisas and the just investigation of crime.

3. If justice rests on individual responsibility, why does the verse not speak directly of the killer and their responsibility?

The Qur’an is in fact seeking to establish this principle of individual responsibility and legal justice in a society where local and tribal ties, the power of influential people, and patriarchal structures could affect individual rights. In circumstances without organized, independent investigative and judicial institutions in their modern form, social position and connections with powerful people could influence the handling of crime and assignment of responsibility.

The verse addresses the community of believers and speaks of qisas “concerning those killed.” The issue is not simply that one person has killed another; society must also recognize its responsibility toward homicide, loss of rights, and the spread of blood vengeance.

As explained in the first answer, qisas should not be restricted to killing for killing. Pursuing a crime requires establishing truth, identifying those involved, and determining their actual responsibility. The second answer also explained how being free, serving under another’s orders, or living in unequal social and legal conditions can affect people’s agency and responsibility.

Individual responsibility therefore does not mean that the person directly carrying out the killing must always bear all responsibility. The person who orders it, coerces it, or knowingly prepares the crime must also answer for their own conduct. Conversely, someone acting under coercion without genuine choice cannot be equated, without examining their circumstances, with someone who freely and knowingly decided to commit a crime.

The Qur’anic principle in Al-Anam 6:164 recalls this necessity: no one bears another’s burden of sin. Each person’s responsibility must therefore be determined by their own conduct, knowledge, choice, and actual contribution to the crime, not simply sex or social position. Obedience to another person alone also does not eliminate responsibility for one’s knowing actions.

Justice does not mean treating everyone’s circumstances as identical; it means equality in the right to seek justice and assessing each individual’s responsibility according to the truth of their conduct.

4. Is the Qur’an reforming an earlier legal system?

In the Torah, Exodus 21:20, Exodus 21:21, Exodus 21:28, Exodus 21:29, Exodus 21:30, Exodus 21:31, and Exodus 21:32 show legal distinctions between free and enslaved people in some rulings concerning human death. Yet in Al-Maidah 5:45 the Qur’an recalls “life for life” among the Torah’s rulings and also speaks of remission in that same verse.

In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Qur’an describes itself as confirming earlier scriptures and as their guardian and criterion. Examining these legal differences in the light of the Qur’an is therefore appropriate, although it cannot be claimed without evidence that every expression in Al-Baqarah 2:178 was directly intended to reform a particular Torah ruling.

Reform of such relationships in Al-Baqarah 2:178 can be examined through qisas: not assigning different values to lives by sex and social position, but just investigation, recognition of actual responsibility, and prevention of excess in punishment. As explained in the first two answers, justice is established when each person’s circumstances, agency, and actual role are examined, not when people are judged simply by social class or sex.

In this reading, what is reformed is the conversion of differences in power and social position into criteria for the value of human life and punishment. The verse itself does not explicitly say it is reforming a particular Torah ruling; this connection arises from comparing texts and principles of justice in the Qur’an.

5. If qisas is prescribed through “كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ”, why does God immediately open the way to pardon? Is it a right whose pursuit may be waived or an obligation that must always be carried out? How can these be reconciled?

In fact, framing the question this way is unfair, because the Qur’anic text itself has not set qisas and pardon against each other. Nevertheless, including this question in our reflection matters because it arises from mistaken readings and incomplete translation and interpretation of this verse. The greatest mistake is to regard qisas simply as killing the killer and then imagine pardon as an opposing ruling, as though God first commanded killing and immediately abandoned it.

As explained in the first answer, qisas is connected with pursuing the crime, establishing truth, proving the offense, and determining responsibility and a proportionate response. When a killing occurs, it must be established who is responsible, how it occurred, whether it was intentional or accidental, and which factors contributed to it. After examination and clarification of the right, a decision about implementing punishment or granting pardon follows.

“كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ” denotes a prescribed and binding ruling, but the obligation to establish qisas should not be equated with a requirement to execute in every case. Fasting is prescribed with the same expression in this surah, while subsequent verses provide relief and rules appropriate to the circumstances of the sick and travelers (Al-Baqarah 2:183, Al-Baqarah 2:184, and Al-Baqarah 2:185). A prescribed ruling is therefore compatible with relief and consideration of circumstances.

Pardon thus does not stand against qisas, but is a stage following investigation, establishment of responsibility, and clarification of the right. The deceased’s family may seek the prescribed punishment or waive its right. Forgiveness does not deny the crime or disregard the victim’s right; it is the conscious relinquishment of a recognized right.

This remission can help end resentment and hostility, give the killer an opportunity for remorse, repentance, and reform, and bring psychological peace to those who forgive. The deceased’s family may also receive financial compensation or blood money through an agreement. Yet pardon should not be reduced to a financial transaction; its value also lies in opening the way to mercy, reconciliation, and reform.

For this reason, after prescribing qisas, God opens the path of pardon and calls it relief and mercy. Qisas ensures that a crime is not left unpursued and the wronged person’s right is not lost; pardon keeps open a path to remission and an end to hostility after the right has been established.

Qisas and pardon are therefore not opposing rulings but complementary parts of a path of justice: first investigating the crime and establishing the right, then the possibility of implementing punishment or consciously waiving it.

Conclusion of Our Reflection

This verse seeks to protect human life from blind vengeance and judgment based on sex, power, and social position, grounding justice in understanding the crime and people’s actual responsibilities. By prescribing qisas, God recognizes the right to pursue a crime and demand a just response, a right whose proper implementation requires examination of intent and agency, identification of the roles of perpetrators and commanders, and restoration of the right to its holder. In the reading examined here, mention of the free, the ʿabd, and the woman does not assign different values to human lives; it directs attention to differences in legal and social circumstances and degrees of agency in the offense. No one should bear another’s burden or be exempt from accountability because of their position.

The verse does not end with identifying the crime and establishing the right. In speaking of something being remitted “by his brother,” it opens a way beyond hostility and blood vengeance. “فَاتِّبَاعٌ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَأَدَاءٌ إِلَيْهِ بِإِحْسَانٍ” shows that both parties’ conduct still matters after pardon. If a claim or obligation remains, it must be pursued appropriately and discharged with kindness. Ittibaʿ, from T B ʿ, means following and is not the same as qisas from Q Ṣ Ṣ; here it concerns properly pursuing what remains due after remission. Such pursuit may result in financial compensation, but proper conduct and kindness should not be reduced to receiving and paying money. Pardon should be an opportunity to end resentment, respect each other’s rights, and repair relationships.

God calls this opening of the path to forgiveness relief and mercy. Mercy, however, does not authorize fresh aggression. The verse’s final warning, “فَمَنِ ٱعْتَدَىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَلَهُۥ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ”, recalls that once the right is clear and the path to pardon and good conduct is available, neither party may exceed the bounds of justice. The person who forgave must not resume blood vengeance, nor may the forgiven person exploit remission for renewed wrongdoing and crime.

Qisas ensures that the wronged person’s right is not lost, and pardon keeps open the way to mercy, reconciliation, and reform after the right is established. Justice and forgiveness complement each other in this verse.
