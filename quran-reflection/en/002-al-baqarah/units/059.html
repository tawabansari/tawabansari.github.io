Al-Baqarah 2:59

So those who did wrong changed the word into something other than what had been said to them; so We sent down upon those who did wrong a painful disturbance (rijz) from the higher realm (al-samā'), because of the boundary-breaking they kept doing (yafsuqūn).

Our Reflection

Verse 58 explains the law of entry: enter this city and land, benefit from its abundance, but enter its gate with humility and practical submission before the command of truth, and ask for lightening and the falling away of sins. In other words, entering ni'mah, land, home, and comfort must be accompanied by humility, setting down the burden of fault, and respecting the rights of others. Verse 59 shows the breaking of this very law: those who did wrong changed the word into something else.

The Qur'an does not say exactly what word they placed in its place, because the issue is not curiosity about wording and vocabulary. The issue is that they changed the spirit and essence of the command. The aim was for the human being to enter with khushu' and humility, in a state of prostrating submission and by its lawful path, meaning the gate; but they changed this meaning into another behavior and another speech. The command to prostrate meant lawful entry with khushu', not rebellion and arrogance; but they turned this etiquette of entry into an assault with a wrongdoing self. They had been told to enter lightened and lawfully, but they stepped in with the burden of claim and wrongdoing.

This betrayal is also reproduced in our own time in every kind of unethical entry. Imagine a society that, on humanitarian principles, opens its doors to migrants, refugees, or guests and asks them to enter with respect for the law, respect for the host's boundaries, and proper participation; but a group abuses this opportunity. Instead of entering through the gate, meaning the lawful path that fits the etiquette of entry, they enter through smuggling, deception, and bypassing the law. After entering, instead of work, production, respect for public order, and paying a fair share of the costs of society, they exploit public resources and bring into the host's home the very cultures whose harm they had fled: administrative corruption, deceit, lying, interference in the lives of others, and the imposition of religious compulsions. This too is a form of changing the word: benefiting from the table of ni'mah, but without the etiquette of entry; using the host's safety, while behaving in a way that wears down that same society's safety and health from within.

Here the Qur'an uses a very precise expression: “those who did wrong.” So the verse does not condemn all who entered as one undifferentiated mass, but it also does not speak vaguely or colorlessly about the wrongdoers among them. Wrongdoing must be named as wrongdoing. When a group turns the command of humility into the language of possession, when entry into a land becomes the occupation of the homes and land of others, when the rights of the inhabitants are ignored and the new ni'mah is treated as absolute property, that group has in reality changed the command.

This pattern has repeated throughout history. In our own time, political Zionism in Palestine is a clear example of this logic. A people who had themselves been wounded by great historical wrongs, if they do not transform that wound into awareness, justice, humility, and the falling away of claims, but instead turn it into occupation, expulsion, walls, siege, demolition of homes, and killing of another people, fall from the position of victim into the pit of wrongdoing. This critique is not directed at Jews as a religion or a people; it is a critique of political Zionism and every power that tramples land, homes, and human lives. The Qur'anic measure is not name or lineage, but human action.

Punishment and insecurity are also consequences of this changing of truth. Rijz is not merely a legendary punishment; it is trembling, pollution, and instability in a society that has crossed beyond divine limits. A society that changes the language of truth may outwardly build power and slogans, but inwardly it becomes afflicted with anxiety, insecurity, corruption, and moral collapse. This is the bitter consequence that descends from the order of truth upon a law-breaking society.

Fisq means leaving the boundary and going out from the protective covering of the divine command. In this verse, fisq is the trampling of the etiquette of entry. They had been told to enter through the gate with humility and practical submission, but they chose arrogance. They had been told to ask for lightening and forgiveness, but they changed the word. They had been told that the path of doing good was open, but they took the path of wrongdoing.

So verse 59 warns us: the danger is not only failing to hear the command; the greater danger is hearing it and distorting it. When the request to be lightened from fault becomes the language of ownership and domination, and when humility becomes an empty performance or a tool of power-display, that land will no longer be a field of mercy. Ni'mah turns into wrongdoing, and wrongdoing, sooner or later, brings insecurity and ruin.

Here the connection of this punishment with the story of Pharaoh is also worth noticing. Earlier, the people of Pharaoh were afflicted with plagues because of their struggle against the message and their continuing wrongdoing. Now, part of the very people who had been rescued from Pharaoh's grip will not be safe from the consequences if they revive the same spirit of wrongdoing, distortion, and law-breaking within themselves. Rescue from Pharaoh's claws is not enough; the Pharaonic spirit must also be purified from within the human being.

The end of the verse makes the cause of this descending punishment clear: because of their continual departure from the law of truth. Fisq means crossing beyond boundaries, leaving the sanctuary of the command, and being released from the order of truth. Divine commands are like a protective covering for society, preserving the human being from inner ruin, outward transgression, and corruption in relationships. When a society tears that protective covering and breaks out from it, it exposes itself to collapse and insecurity.

Here law-breaking means trampling the etiquette of entry. They had been told to enter with humility, but they chose arrogance; they had been told to set down the burden of fault, but they changed the word; they had been told to do good, but they did wrong. So this painful consequence was not unrelated. God does not punish a people merely because of one verbal slip. This descent of hardship was because of their continuing departure from ethical boundaries, their distortion of commands, and the wrongdoing that flowed through their behavior.

By using the present-tense verb, the verse shows that this departure from the boundary had become a repeated and continuing state. This point is very important. A fault may be a passing slip, but real law-breaking occurs when the human being leaves the boundary and insists upon it. For making up for fault, the path of lightening and forgiveness had been opened; but when that very path is changed and deformed, the path of forgiveness becomes blocked. They played with the very way that had been given to them so their sins could be lightened; for this reason, their slips turned into a deep deviation.

Verse 59 warns that distorting religion is not only tampering with the text; it is changing the direction and purpose of the command. A person may preserve the words but destroy their meaning: turn prostration into a soulless movement, asking forgiveness into an ineffective phrase, land into personal property, provision into a tool of domination, and ni'mah into one's exclusive right. This too is precisely an instance of changing the word of truth.

Against this, the right path was the path of the previous verse: entry with humility, seeking lightening, seeking forgiveness, and arriving at the level of doing good. Had they followed this path, their faults would have been forgiven and they would have been increased among the doers of good. But when the word was changed, the path of forgiveness and growth was changed into a path of ruin and fall.

Here the connection of this section with verse 61 also becomes visible. When the human horizon falls from seeking the lightening of the spirit and forgiveness into demanding food, entitlement, and descent into the humiliation of Egypt, it shows that the change of word did not happen only in language; the whole worldview of the human being had changed. The one who should have set down the burden of fault again goes after earthly burdens and humiliating habits.

For this reason, verse 59 is a mirror for all societies. Every society that stands at the threshold of ni'mah and ease has two paths before it: either to enter with humility and a request for lightening, and to reach doing good and growth; or to change the command and fall into wrongdoing and ruin. The name of the people or nation does not change the matter; God's way is always one. Wherever ni'mah becomes a tool of wrongdoing, wherever the rights of inhabitants are trampled, and wherever the language of humility becomes the language of force, the same Qur'anic warning is repeated.

So the great lesson of the verse is this: the main danger is not failing to hear the command, but hearing it and distorting it. The human being sometimes does not openly reject the law; rather, he changes it in his own favor, removes its real spirit, adapts it to the desires of his own self, and in the end imagines that he is still obedient. This deviation is more hidden and more dangerous than open disobedience.

Verse 59 says: when the desire to be lightened from sin is changed and the hope for forgiveness becomes the language of arrogance and distortion, that land will no longer be a place of mercy. When humility becomes outward performance or power-display, entry into ni'mah becomes the beginning of entry into corruption. And when the human being, instead of lightening his own shoulders, carries the same heavy burdens of wrongdoing, ruin and insecurity arrive; not because the divine presence needs revenge, but because a law-breaking society has placed itself on the path of the unavoidable consequences of the order of truth.
