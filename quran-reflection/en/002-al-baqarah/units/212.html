Al-Baqarah 2:212

ۘ ۗ

Worldly life has been made attractive to those who have disbelieved, and they ridicule those who have believed; those who have practiced mindfulness will be above them on the Day of Resurrection; and The God provides without measure for whomever He wills.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:211, God warned about those who change a divine blessing after receiving it. Now He points to an outlook that can prevent people from recognizing the true value of life and of other human beings:

“زُيِّنَ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا”

“Worldly life has been made attractive to those who have disbelieved.”

“Zuyyina,” from the root Z Y N, means adorning and making something appear beautiful. The verb is passive, and the verse does not identify the agent responsible for making worldly life attractive in these people’s eyes.

Elsewhere, the Quran sometimes attributes the beautifying of deeds to Satan (Al-Anam 6:43), sometimes to God (An-Naml 27:4), and sometimes speaks of it without identifying an agent. We should therefore not assign a particular agent in this verse without evidence.

The central point, however, is that worldly life appearing attractive is not the same as actually enjoying a good life.

A person may possess great wealth and power yet lack peace, justice, and healthy relationships. Another may have few possessions while all their thoughts and aspirations revolve around acquiring wealth and status.

In either case, the world can loom so large in a person’s eyes that they set aside truth, justice, and the Hereafter to obtain its benefits.

The Quran does not, however, condemn enjoying worldly blessings. In Al-Araf 7:32, it challenges the arbitrary prohibition of divine adornment and wholesome provisions, and in An-Nahl 16:97, it speaks of a good life for those who believe and act righteously.

The problem, then, is not the beauty and blessings of this world; it is making them the ultimate measure of value and success, and abandoning truth and justice to attain them.

This distinction is examined in detail in the study “Trials and the Promise of Good in the Quran: Is the World a Prison for the Believer and a Paradise for the Disbeliever?”.

God then says:

“وَيَسْخَرُونَ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا”

“And they ridicule those who have believed.”

“Yaskharun,” from the root S KH R, here means mocking and belittling others.

Those who measure human worth by possessions, power, and social standing may regard believers who do not place these standards above truth and justice as naive, backward, or worthless.

Yet wealth and power do not prove that a person is in the right, just as poverty and deprivation do not prove their faith or mindfulness.

In Al-Hujurat 49:11, the Quran also forbids believers from mocking one another. Ridiculing and humiliating others is therefore not confined to one religious group; every person must avoid such behavior.

God then introduces a different criterion for assessing a person’s standing:

“وَالَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا فَوْقَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ”

“And those who have practiced mindfulness will be above them on the Day of Resurrection.”

At the beginning of the verse, those being mocked are called “alladhina amanu”—those who have believed. But when describing the higher standing on the Day of Resurrection, the expression is “alladhina ittaqaw”—those who have practiced mindfulness.

This difference matters. God directs attention to mindfulness, rather than merely bearing the title of believer or belonging to a religious community.

Taqwa, from the root W Q Y, means care, protection, and conscious self-restraint. A mindful person strives to guard against oppression, arrogance, deception, self-superiority, and violating others’ rights.

Thus, someone who calls themselves a believer yet humiliates others and disregards their rights should not imagine that the designation of faith alone frees them from the responsibility of mindfulness.

The place of mindfulness and its relationship with justice and human conduct are examined in the article “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

Another point is that God relates the higher standing of the mindful in this expression to “يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ”—the Day of Resurrection. We should therefore not conclude from this verse that all believers will be poor and deprived in this world, or that all those who disbelieve are wealthy and successful.

At the end, He says:

“وَاللَّهُ يَرْزُقُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ”

“And The God provides without measure for whomever He wills.”

“Yarzuqu,” from the root R Z Q, means providing sustenance, gifts, and benefits. Yet provision in the Quran is not limited to food, money, and possessions.

Health, security, knowledge, understanding, opportunity, ability, tranquility, and guidance can also be forms of divine provision. A person may possess great wealth yet lack blessings that matter more for a good life and the recognition of truth.

The scope of this concept is examined in the separate study “The Quranic Root R Z Q”.

But what does “bi-ghayri hisab” mean?

“Hisab,” from the root Ḥ S B, relates to counting, assessing, and measuring. Divine giving without measure should not be understood as giving without order, wisdom, or justice.

In An-Nur 24:38, God speaks of rewarding good deeds and adding from His bounty, and in Az-Zumar 39:10, He describes the reward of the patient as being without measure.

These usages show that divine bounty cannot be reduced to a limited transaction between human effort and a reward exactly equivalent to it. God can give from His bounty more than a person expects.

Al-Isra 17:20 also reminds us that the Lord’s giving is not confined to one group, and different people benefit from it in this world.

Thus, receiving abundant provision does not, by itself, indicate spiritual superiority or God’s approval of everything a person does; nor does material deprivation prove that someone is worthless before God.

In this verse, the Quran sets two different standards against one another: those who regard worldly life so highly that they humiliate others according to outward measures, and those who, through mindfulness, seek life’s value in following truth and fulfilling their responsibility.

The world and its blessings are for enjoyment and a good life, not for self-superiority and humiliating others. Human worth cannot be measured by wealth, power, or a religious label; the criterion of higher standing before God is mindfulness.

The God is the source of provision, and His giving is not dependent on limited human calculations. Those who belittle others today because of their possessions and status should not forget that on the Day of Resurrection, the outward standards of this world will not determine their standing.
