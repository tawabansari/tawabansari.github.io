Al-Baqarah 2:75

Do you then expect (tatma'una) that they will believe you, while a group from among them used to hear the word of The God (Allah), then distort it after they had used reason upon it (aqaluhu), while they knew?

Our Reflection

In this verse, the direction of address changes. Up to this point, the Qur'an recalled part of the story of the Children of Israel: covenant, disobedience, the cow, the hidden killing, the disclosure of truth, giving life to the dead, and the hardening of hearts. Now the Qur'an turns toward the audience of the Qur'an and asks: “Do you then expect that they will believe you?” (a-fa-tatma'una an yu'minu lakum).

This question breaks a simple hope. Sometimes pure-hearted believers imagine that if truth is spoken clearly, everyone who belongs to scripture and knowledge will necessarily accept it. The Qur'an does not confirm this naivety. Someone who does not know the truth may awaken when truth becomes clear. But someone who has heard the word of God, used reason upon it, and then consciously distorted it, does not suffer from a lack of evidence. His problem is that he has seen the evidence, but placed it in the service of his own desire and interest.

So the verse is not speaking only about a religious disagreement. It uncovers a deeper illness: the human being can face the word of God, understand its meaning, and still turn that same word in another direction. Here the danger is not simple ignorance. The danger is an understanding that does not submit to truth.

The verse itself keeps the boundary clear: “a group from among them” (fariq minhum). The Qur'an does not treat everyone alike. It is not speaking about all Jews. It is speaking about a group who dealt with the word of God, heard it, used reason upon it, and then distorted it knowingly. This qualifier shows both the justice of the Qur'an and the severity of the warning.

Yet this history is also a mirror for every audience of the Qur'an. The Qur'an does not tell the history of the Jews only to condemn earlier people. It turns history into admonition so the followers of the Qur'an do not repeat the same error. The bitter reality is that Muslims too, in their own history, have many times come close to this same danger; sometimes even precisely where the Qur'an had warned against it.

“They used to hear the word of The God” (yasma'una kalama Allah) is not a simple expression. They were not hearing ordinary speech. They were facing the word of The God. The word of God is not an ethnic text, a cultural text, or a family inheritance that anyone may turn in favor of his own group. The word of God is a trust. Whoever hears it becomes responsible. And the greater his understanding, the heavier his responsibility becomes.

Then the verse says: “then they used to distort it” (thumma yuharrifunahu). “Then” shows sequence and distance. First there was hearing. Then came distortion. That means distortion did not begin from the word not reaching them. The word had reached. It had been heard. The problem was that after hearing, instead of submitting before truth, they pulled it toward another direction.

The root of “distortion” is “edge / side” (harf): an edge, a margin, a side. Distortion means moving speech away from its proper place and original direction. It means carrying meaning from the center of truth toward interest, power, fanaticism, sect, or one's own desire. Distortion does not always begin with open denial. Sometimes the wording of the verse remains, but the direction of the verse changes.

This is the most dangerous form of distortion: distortion with the language of religion. The outward form of the verse remains. The outward reverence for the text also remains. But the destination of the verse changes. The word of God is recited, but instead of handing the human being over to truth, it is placed in the service of power, group, government, the market of religion, or religious pride.

The heavy key of the verse is in “after they had used reason upon it” (min ba'di ma aqaluhu). If we translate this only as “after they understood it,” the basic meaning is not lost, but its depth becomes weaker. Reason in the Qur'an is not only hearing and surface knowledge. In the semantic field of reason, there is grasping, restraining, holding, and binding meaning to responsibility.

In Arabic, an “iqal” is a cord used to restrain a camel. This image helps us understand that reason is not loose and ineffective knowing. Reason means that you grasp the meaning, do not leave it scattered, understand its direction, and bind yourself to its responsibility. When the verse says “they reasoned it” (aqaluhu), it means they had not left the word of God vague and mute. They had grasped it. They had understood its meaning. The knot of the speech had been opened for them.

So “after they had used reason upon it” means after they knew what the word of God was saying and toward which direction it was moving. After they had perceived its subtlety and destination. After they could no longer say: we did not understand. Precisely at this point, they distorted.

This is very weighty. Someone who does not understand and transmits speech wrongly has one kind of error. But someone who understands and consciously turns speech enters into betrayal of meaning. The verse emphasizes exactly this: “while they knew” (wa-hum ya'lamun).

This sentence closes the path of excuse. The issue is not a simple mistake. It is conscious distortion. They knew what they were doing. They knew they were moving the word of God away from its destination. They knew they were taking the verse toward a direction for which God had not sent it.

Here the truth must be said plainly: distortion of religion, in its most dangerous form, is not the work of open enemies of religion or ordinary people. Common people may imitate. They may be unaware. They may be deceived. But the one who understands the text, knows the language of religion, has opened the knot of the verse, and still turns it toward his own interests carries the main burden of distortion.

Distortion begins where knowledge separates from reverent awe. If the scholar does not come down before the word of God, his knowledge becomes dangerous. If the jurist understands the verse but places it in the service of power, his understanding is not light; it is a tool. If the interpreter cannot tolerate the deliberate gaps of the Qur'an and fills them with story and conjecture, he has crossed beyond the wise silence of the Qur'an. If the owner of religion attaches people to himself instead of God, he has taken the word of God out of its path.

The Qur'an shows elsewhere that deviation after knowledge does not always come from a lack of understanding. Sometimes its root is transgressive rivalry (baghy): superiority-seeking, envy, competition, monopolizing, and the desire to keep power within one's own circle. When knowledge is joined with baghy, it becomes more dangerous than ignorance. The ignorant person does not know. But the knowledgeable person trapped in baghy knows, and turns that very knowledge in his own favor.

For this reason, distortion is not only an intellectual error. Sometimes it is a project for preserving power. Verse 75 says that a group used to distort the word of God after using reason upon it. A few verses later, the Qur'an removes another veil: people write the Book with their own hands, then say, “This is from God,” in order to sell it for a small price. Here distortion becomes connected to the economy of religion.

The market of selling religion needs a text whose direction has been turned. There must be verses that can be made to mean what the buyer wants. Sometimes the buyer is political power. Sometimes the buyers are fanatic common people. Sometimes it is a religious class that gains its position from ambiguity, fear, and people's dependence upon it. In such a market, the word of God is no longer a path of freedom, growth, and justice; it becomes capital for preserving influence.

From here, the verse is not only Jewish history. It is a warning to every audience of the Qur'an. Followers of the Qur'an too, in history, have repeatedly committed the same error. They have kept the name of the Qur'an, but changed the direction of the verse. They have preserved the wording of guidance, but buried the spirit of guidance beneath structures of power, sect, compulsion, and interest.

When zakat, which in the Qur'an is connected with purification, growth, giving, social responsibility, and delivering good, is transformed into government tax, religious pressure, or a tool of control over people, this is not merely a simple juristic disagreement. It is the turning of meaning's direction. Zakat had come to give life to the human being and society, not to make religion heavy and build an apparatus of power. For fuller discussion of this issue, see the article Zakat and Its Historical Distortion.

When the clear principle of freedom of faith and “there is no compulsion in religion” (la ikraha fi d-din) is transformed into religious compulsion, social pressure, threat, punishment, or control of human conscience, this too is not merely a simple misunderstanding. The Qur'an shows the path of invitation, clarification, reminder, and choice. If someone knows the verses and still turns religion back toward coercion and compulsion, he has turned the word of God away from its direction. For more explanation, see the article No Compulsion and Delivering the Message.

When the religion of God, which is built upon ease, mercy, removal of hardship, mindfulness, justice, and reason, is transformed into a network of sectarian details, scruple, fear, social control, and religious gatekeeping, one must ask: is this religion still moving in the path of the word of God, or has the word of God been turned toward another direction? Verse 75 shows exactly this danger: a human being may hear the word of God, understand it, and then use that very understanding as a tool for constructing another religion.

When a political opponent, an intellectual opponent, or someone outside our school is declared a coverer of truth through religious language, the same danger occurs again. Qur'anic words are removed from their place and turned into weapons of the group. “Coverer of truth” (kafir) is a weighty Qur'anic concept and must not be turned into a tool of exclusion, humiliation, or war against intellectual and political opponents. For fuller discussion, see the article Kafir in the Qur'an.

Another example is the way people deal with the silences of the Qur'an. The Qur'an has not stated every detail, and this not-stating is not always a deficiency. Sometimes the silence of the Qur'an is itself part of wisdom. Sometimes God has placed a limit so that the human being does not cross beyond it, does not put conjecture in the place of certainty, and does not make religion heavy with baseless stories.

But in the history of Islamic interpretation, some later scholars and interpreters slipped precisely here. Wherever they faced an informational gap in the Qur'an, instead of standing before the wisdom of the Qur'an's silence, they filled that gap with Isra'iliyyat, external reports, detailed stories, and historical conjectures. This phenomenon is not something the Islamic scholarly world itself can easily deny. Its name is known in the Muslim scholarly tradition: Isra'iliyyat.

The problem was not only that a report from outside was transmitted. The problem began when the silence of the Qur'an was imagined to be incomplete. Then that silence was filled with reports that gradually took the place of the Qur'an itself in people's minds. Where the Qur'an did not speak, they spoke. Where the Qur'an left open, they closed. Where the Qur'an passed over details, they made details. And people imagined that these details were religion itself.

This too is not unrelated to verse 75. God warns that a group from the People of the Book used to distort His word after using reason upon it. But the audiences of the Qur'an, instead of learning from this warning, reopened the same gate in some historical paths. When understanding the Qur'an, instead of submitting before the text, chases the filling of every gap with outside material, the danger of distortion comes alive again; even if this time it happens in the name of Islam.

Of course, the verse itself is about “a group” (fariq) from among them. This qualifier must remain. But the admonition of the verse does not remain limited to them. The Qur'an tells us their fate so that we do not imagine ourselves pure and immune. If they distorted the word of God, we too must ask: what have we done with the Qur'an? Have we carried the verse toward truth, or toward sect? Have we read it for freedom of conscience, or for compulsion? Have we read it for purity and growth, or for tax and power? Have we read it to understand kufr and covering truth, or to declare rivals kafir?

These are today's examples of the same danger. The verse speaks about a group from the Children of Israel, but the disease of distortion is not limited to one people and one time. Wherever a group deals with sacred text, has the power of interpretation, is trusted by people, and consciously turns the word of God toward interest, sect, government, market, or desire, the same warning becomes alive.

From this angle, verse 75 is the natural continuation of verse 74. In verse 74, the subject was hardness of heart. Here, the effect of that hardness appears in the language of religion. If the heart becomes hard, even religious understanding becomes dangerous. Such a heart understands the verse, but does not come down before it. It grasps the meaning, but does not submit. It opens the knot, but uses that very opening to close the path for people.

Distortion after using reason is heavier than unaware distortion. Reason makes the human being responsible. When a person speaks without understanding, he has one kind of error. But when he understands and consciously turns speech, error comes close to betrayal. In this verse, two phrases close the path of escape: “after they had used reason upon it” (ba'da ma aqaluhu) and “while they knew” (wa-hum ya'lamun). They had used reason, and they themselves knew.

So the opening question of the verse is very serious: do you expect such a group to believe you? If someone has done this with the word of God, his believing through simple invitation and emotional expectation is not easy. His problem is not a lack of evidence. His problem is that he has seen the evidence, understood the meaning, and placed it in the service of his own desire and interest.

This verse has the same warning for the audience of the Qur'an. Faith is not proven merely by having a Book. Having a Book, if it is not joined with reverent awe, sincerity, justice, and submission before truth, can even make the human being's responsibility heavier. The one who has no Book has one kind of test. But the one who hears and understands the word of God, then distorts it, has fallen from the position of awareness.

The lesson of the verse is this: the great danger is not only hostility to religion; it is using religion against the purpose of religion. The great danger is not only failing to hear the word of God; it is hearing, using reason, and then turning it. Whoever deals with the signs of God must ask himself: am I carrying meaning toward God, or toward myself? Does the verse correct me, or do I redirect the verse in my own favor?

The word of The God is a trust. Whoever understands it, before becoming a teacher of people, must submit before that same word. And whoever distorts it after using reason upon it cannot hide behind knowledge, title, clothing, school, history, or position. The God knows who carries His word toward truth and who pulls it toward himself.
