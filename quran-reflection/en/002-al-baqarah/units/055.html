Al-Baqarah 2:55

And when you said: O Musa, we will never believe in you and trust you (nu'mina laka) until we see God openly and unveiled (jahratan); so the thunderbolt (al-ṣāʿiqah) seized you while you were observing (tanẓurūn).

Our Reflection

This verse continues the same educational chain of the Children of Israel. In the previous verses, they were rescued from Pharaoh, saw the sea split, observed the drowning of the people of Pharaoh, took the calf, were pardoned, the Book and the criterion were given to Musa, and Musa called them to repentance and to killing the polluted self. But now in verse 55, we see that the root of the disease has still not been fully treated.

This time, the problem appears in another form: lan nu'mina laka ḥattā narā Allāha jahratan. They do not say that they have no belief at all in the One who is worshiped. Their statement is: lan nu'mina laka, we will not believe in you. That is, the main crisis is the crisis of trust in Musa: the messenger through whom all those signs, rescue, the Book, and the criterion had come.

This point is very important. The Children of Israel set a condition before Musa. It is as if they are saying: we will not accept your word unless we see God openly. Their faith in the messenger again becomes dependent on a new miracle and a greater display. This is the same spirit that does not become calm with one sign. Every time it sees a sign, it wants another sign.

Here the test of patience and khushu' is repeated again. Earlier it was said that patience and salat are weighty, except upon the khashi'in: those who take their return to their Lord seriously. The khashi' becomes humble before the divine unseen. But a people who say, “we will not believe in you until we see God openly,” have not yet reached the station of khushu'. They want to turn the unseen into a stage display.

This statement is not only a scientific or intellectual question. A kind of moral audacity can also be seen in it. They treat Musa like someone who must constantly prove his truthfulness through new displays. It is as if rescue from Pharaoh, the splitting of the sea, the drowning of the people of Pharaoh, pardon after the calf, and the giving of the Book and the criterion were not enough. The sensory-seeking self still says: show again; make it open again; convince us again through something extraordinary.

Here we must also pay attention to the psychological root of this demand. The Children of Israel had physically come out of Egypt and Pharaoh, but their religious imagination and mind had not easily become free from the Pharaonic apparatus. For generations they had lived in a space where power, lordship, gods, temples, and statues all had visible and touchable forms. Pharaoh displayed his claim of lordship before the people, and the gods of Egypt stood before the eye through figures, temples, and ritual displays.

Here the relation between God's justice and mercy also becomes clear. God is the Creator of the human being and knows the human psyche better than the human being knows himself. A people who lived for generations under Pharaoh's domination, and grew up in an atmosphere where power, lordship, temple, statue, and visible gods cast a shadow over their religious imagination, do not immediately become free from every mental chain by one geographical crossing. Coming out of Egypt was one stage; Egypt coming out of their mind and self was another. This is why the Qur'an shows their path through pardon, the Book, the criterion, repentance, the thunderbolt, being raised again, and gratitude. This view is more consistent with God's justice and lordly nurturing than the assumption of those who, because of outside reports, imagined that after calf-worship God commanded their collective slaughter. Divine lordship means step-by-step training of the wounded human being, not destroying a people still coming out of the darknesses of bondage and image-worship.

So when they said, “until we see God openly,” this was not a simple request. This statement showed that they still wanted to understand the divine matter through the form of the old world: a world in which God must have form, a threshold, visibility, and must come onto the stage like Pharaonic powers. Their bodies had been freed from Pharaoh, but their imagination had not yet been fully freed from Pharaoh's grip.

The Qur'an reveals this disease elsewhere as well. After crossing the sea, when the Children of Israel passed by a people devoted to their idols, they said to Musa:

“اجْعَل لَّنَا إِلَـٰهًا كَمَا لَهُمْ آلِهَةٌ”

“Make for us a god, just as they have gods” (Al-A'raf 7:138).

Musa answered: innakum qawmun tajhalūn, you are a people acting in ignorance. So the issue was not only the calf. The deeper root was the desire for a tangible, staged, and possessable god. They still wanted to have a threshold, a form, a visible center, and an object of worship that could be pointed to.

From this angle, the word jahratan is very precise. The Qur'an did not simply say, “we see God.” It said that they wanted to see God jahratan: openly, publicly, unveiled, in the field of appearance, before the eye. Jahr is not merely ordinary seeing. Jahr means that something does not remain hidden, that it comes onto the stage, becomes loud and open, and imposes its presence upon the senses.

This word shows that the problem is not only eyesight; the problem is their theology. They still had not understood that the One who is worshiped does not fall within the level of object, body, form, direction, and sensory phenomenon. They still want to receive God as something made public, made open, brought onto the stage, and placed before the eye. This means that divine transcendence had not yet become complete in their souls.

God is known through His signs, His guidance, His lordly nurturing, His rescue, His Book, His criterion, His mercy, and the signs He has placed in horizons and selves. But God is not an object that can be placed before the human eye. He is the One worshiped, not a statue. He is Rabb, not a phenomenon. He is Creator, not something among created things that can be brought onto the stage by a people's insistence.

Here again the connection with the calf becomes clear. The calf was something visible, sounding, manufactured, and pointable. In verse 55, the same desire for the sensory returns at a higher level. This time they do not merely want the calf; they want to see God Himself openly. So the verse shows that the problem of the calf was not only a statue. It was a deeper disease: inability to pass from sensory things toward humble faith.

The phrase lan nu'mina laka is also weighty. Lan is a strong future negation: we will never believe in you until this demand is fulfilled. This is no longer a calm search for truth. This is setting a condition before the messenger. They turn faith into a transaction: if we see God openly, then we will believe in you.

But Qur'anic faith does not stand on such a foundation. Faith is not blind belief, but tying it to seeing the essence of God is not faith either. The Qur'an calls the human being to see the signs, not to turn God into a visible object. The signs of God are spread through the world, history, the self, the Book, rescue, pardon, and guidance. Someone who sets all these signs aside and says, “only show God Himself openly,” has not passed through the sign; he has fled from the sign.

So the thunderbolt seized them: fa-akhadhatkum al-ṣāʿiqah. They demanded an unveiled openness for their eyes, but the answer came in the form of a thunderbolt: a striking, sudden, seizing phenomenon beyond the endurance of the senses. A people who wanted to bring God unveiled into the field of their gaze could not even bear one striking sign from the signs of power.

This reversal is very meaningful. They wanted to place God inside the frame of their gaze, but they themselves fell into the grip of the thunderbolt. They wanted to pull divine reality into the field of sense, but that very sense collapsed under the blow of the thunderbolt. This was a practical answer to their illusion: if you cannot endure a thunderbolt, how do you desire to see the essence of the One who is worshiped unveiled before the eye?

Akhadhatkum means it seized you. This expression shows that they were not in control of the scene. When the human being wants to make God the object of his gaze and control, he soon learns that he himself is powerless before divine power. They wanted to see; but they themselves were seized. They wanted to set a condition; but the thunderbolt seized them without asking their permission.

The end of the verse says: wa-antum tanẓurūn, while you were observing. This is the same root that appeared in verse 50. There, the Children of Israel were observing the drowning of the people of Pharaoh. Here, they themselves, while observing, were seized by the thunderbolt. In verse 50, observing should have carried them to gratitude and trust. But because that observing did not become khushu' and criterion, now observing reached another scene: seeing the consequence of their own audacity.

This repetition of tanẓurūn is very important. It is as if the Qur'an says: you were people of looking, but your looking did not become understanding. You saw the sea, but did not learn trust. You saw Pharaoh drown, but were not freed from sensory-worship. Now, while you were observing, the thunderbolt seized you. The problem was not only not seeing; the problem was that seeing did not become understanding, gratitude, and khushu' inside you.

Educationally, this verse shows that repeated miracle-demanding is not always a sign of faith. Sometimes the human being, instead of learning from earlier signs, demands a new miracle in order to flee from the responsibility of faith. Each time he says: if I see this, I will believe. But when he sees it, he creates another demand. Such faith is not faith; it is a sick dependency on sensory stimulation.

This verse also teaches a major lesson about the position of the messenger. Faith in the messenger does not mean worshiping the messenger; but constantly discrediting the messenger, after all those signs, is a sign of the illness of the people. Musa had brought nothing from himself. He was the messenger of the same God who had rescued them. When they said lan nu'mina laka, outwardly they addressed Musa, but inwardly they discredited the path of divine guidance.

So the issue of the verse is not only “seeing God.” The issue is the wrong relationship with the unseen, the messenger, and the sign. They still have not known God rightly, have not accepted the messenger rightly, and have not digested the signs rightly. That is why on one side they take the calf, and on the other side they demand God jahratan. Both have one root: the desire to pull the divine matter down to the level of form, sound, body, and display.

Here the Qur'an's theology of transcendence appears powerfully. In the Qur'an, God is not defined by body, direction, form, or figure. He is known through lordly nurturing, guidance, mercy, judgment, signs, and His effects. Whoever accepts God only when he sees Him like an open phenomenon has still not distinguished between Creator and creation.

As a result, verse 55 continues the same difficult treatment of calf-worship. In verse 54, the calf-making self had to be killed. In verse 55, we see that this self has not fully died. This time it does not make a calf; it demands God Himself in the form of open seeing. The thunderbolt is a blow against this sensory-struck and audacious self: the self that still does not understand that faith in God is not the same as making God into a display.

So verse 55 is not only a historical event. It is a warning for every human being who constantly ties his faith to new displays, extraordinary wonders, sensory visions, and external experiences. Such a human being may see many signs, but not become khashi'. The Qur'an takes us below and beyond this level: see the sign, but do not stop inside the sign; accept the messenger, but do not make him an object of worship; seek God, but do not turn Him into a visible object.

Cross-References
  • Al-Baqarah 2:50— The Children of Israel observed the splitting of the sea and the drowning of the people of Pharaoh; verse 55 again uses tanẓurūn, but this time in the scene of the thunderbolt.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:51— Taking the calf after seeing signs; verse 55 continues the same disease of sensory-seeking and desire for form.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:53— Giving the Book and the criterion for guidance; verse 55 shows that without the criterion, even after the Book and signs, the human being still creates a wrong demand.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:54— The command to kill the polluted self; verse 55 shows that the sensory-seeking self still needs further treatment.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:56— After the thunderbolt and death, they were raised again so that they might give thanks; the direct continuation of verse 55.
  • Al-A'raf 7:138— After crossing the sea, the Children of Israel asked Musa to appoint for them a god like the gods of another people; a clear witness for their continuing desire for a tangible and visible object of worship.
  • Al-A'raf 7:143— Musa says, My Lord, show Yourself to me so I may look at You; the answer comes, you will never see Me. An important witness for divine transcendence and the mountain's inability to bear manifestation.
  • An-Nisa 4:153— The People of the Book asked the Prophet to bring down a book from heaven, and before that they asked Musa for something greater, saying: show us God openly. A direct witness for this demand.
  • Al-A'raf 7:155— Musa chose seventy men from his people for the appointed meeting, and the trembling seized them; useful for understanding the people's test in connection with the appointed meeting and heavy demands.
  • Al-Isra 17:90-93— Examples of repeated miracle-demands and condition-setting for faith; showing that this illness was not limited to the Children of Israel.
  • Al-An'am 6:109-111— Those who swear that if a sign came they would believe, yet if everything were gathered before their eyes, they would not believe except by God's will; useful for critiquing conditional miracle-demanding.
  • Ash-Shura 42:51— It is not for any human being that God should speak to him except by revelation, from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger; an important witness for transcendence and the limits of human relation with God.
  • Al-Ikhlas 112:1-4— God is One, free of need, neither begetting nor begotten, and nothing is comparable to Him; a foundation of transcendence against bodily and visible conceptions of God.
