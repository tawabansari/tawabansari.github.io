Al-Baqarah 2:209

Then if you slip after clear evidence has come to you, know that The God is Invincible and Wise.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:208, The God called all believers to enter into peace and avoid following Satan’s footsteps. He now warns of a danger that threatens people even after clear evidence has reached them:

“فَإِنْ زَلَلْتُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْكُمُ الْبَيِّنَاتُ”

“Then if you slip after clear evidence has come to you.”

“زَلَلْتُمْ,” from the root ز ل ل, means slipping and losing one’s firm footing. A person may know the right path yet deviate from it through deception, weakness, or an error of judgment.

Here we should distinguish three Qur’anic concepts:

Disbelief in clear evidence means setting aside, rejecting, or concealing clear truth, particularly when someone recognizes it and knowingly stands against it (Al-Baqarah 2:99).

Concealing clear evidence means hiding a truth one knows, keeping it from others instead of making it known (Al-Baqarah 2:146 and Al-Baqarah 2:159).

Slipping means deviating from the right path, which can occur even with good intentions through deception or error. The word itself does not necessarily convey deliberate denial or concealment (Al-Baqarah 2:36 and Al-Baqarah 2:209).

These concepts can become connected in someone’s conduct, but they are not identical. Disbelief, its distinction from ignorance, and concealing truth are discussed more fully in the independent study “Who Is a Kafir, and What Is Kufr?”, while concealment is examined in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:159.

Receiving clear evidence increases human responsibility, but does not by itself establish that every person has correctly understood the whole truth and knowingly rejected it. In Al-Ahzab 33:5, the Qur’an also distinguishes error from what the heart deliberately intends. Intention, awareness, and conduct therefore matter when assessing human responsibility.

The story of Adam and his wife provides a clear example for understanding a slip.

In Al-Araf 7:20, Satan refers to the Lord’s command to deceive them, inventing a motive for it. He makes it appear that God prohibited approaching the tree to deprive them of a higher station or everlasting life. Then, in Al-Araf 7:21, he swears that he is their sincere adviser.

Al-Baqarah 2:36 expresses the result of this deception as “فَأَزَلَّهُمَا الشَّيْطَانُ”—Satan caused them both to slip.

Satan did not deceive them through an open invitation to evil. He gained their trust by attaching a deceptive claim to God’s command, swearing an oath, and professing goodwill.

This method has an important connection with the characters God described in the preceding verses. Al-Baqarah 2:204 speaks of a person whose speech is appealing and who even calls God to witness his intentions, yet is in reality the most stubborn of adversaries. In Al-Baqarah 2:205, the corruption of his conduct becomes apparent, and in Al-Baqarah 2:206, pride and prejudice prevent him from accepting correction.

Sometimes, then, the most dangerous deception is a false statement presented in God’s name under the guise of devotion and goodwill. A person may trust the speaker’s name, clothing, religious office, or oath and slip from the path of truth with good intentions.

In Al-Baqarah 2:169, the Qur’an identifies speaking about God without knowledge as one of Satan’s commands. In Al-Araf 7:28, people also justify their wrongdoing by claiming that God commanded it. God’s name and a claim to follow Him are therefore not, in themselves, proof that anyone’s words or deeds are right.

Yet there is a difference between someone who knowingly deceives others and someone deceived while acting with good intentions. What happens after recognizing the slip is also fundamentally important.

After recognizing their error, Adam and his wife acknowledged in Al-Araf 7:23 that they had wronged themselves and chose to return. Al-Baqarah 2:37, Ta-Ha 20:121, and Ta-Ha 20:122 speak of Adam’s repentance being accepted and his renewed guidance. Aal Imran 3:155 also mentions people whom Satan caused to slip because of some of their deeds, but whom God pardoned.

A slip, even when a person bears responsibility for it, does not mean that the path to correction and forgiveness has closed.

For example, someone may spend years, with the intention of serving religion, learning a ruling from their leaders and teaching it to others. But when they encounter the Qur’an’s clear evidence and recognize that the ruling is wrong, they face a new choice.

Will they acknowledge and correct their mistake? Or will fear of family, society, and losing position, followers, and reputation lead them to keep defending the same ruling?

The initial slip may result from being deceived, but knowingly persisting in error after recognizing the truth is another choice. If someone knows the truth and conceals it to preserve their interests or standing, the issue is no longer only the initial slip; disbelief or concealment of truth may also be involved.

In Al-Baqarah 2:159, The God condemns those who conceal clear evidence, but in Al-Baqarah 2:160 He leaves open the path of repentance, reform, and making the truth known. These three actions show that returning from error is more than inward regret: people must also work to correct their conduct and repair its harmful effects.

Here the contrast between the two characters in Al-Baqarah 2:206 and Al-Baqarah 2:207 becomes clearer: one persists in sin to preserve pride and personal interests; the other is willing to relinquish attachments and privileges for God’s pleasure.

Let us now return to the end of the verse:

“فَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ”

“Know that The God is Invincible and Wise.”

Why does God use these two descriptions in connection with slipping after clear evidence has arrived?

“Aziz,” from the root ع ز ز, concerns strength, dignity, firmness, and invincibility. No one can overcome God in His power and command, and His truth is not subject to human wishes or authority.

This description has particular significance in the context of the preceding verses. In Al-Baqarah 2:206, the corrupt person becomes caught in “الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ”—pride and dignity mingled with sin that prevent acceptance of truth. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:209, The God describes Himself as Aziz.

People may conceal truth to preserve their outward dignity, but no leader, scholar, government, or community can turn its own false statement into God’s command by using His name. True dignity belongs to The God.

But God is not only Invincible; He is also Wise.

“Hakim,” from the root ح ك م, concerns ruling, judgment, and soundness in governance. God’s power is inseparable from His wisdom. He knows the reality of human actions, and His judgment is not deceived by outward speech, oaths, status, or professions of goodwill.

In Al-Baqarah 2:220, The God distinguishes the corrupter from the reformer and says that, had He wished, He could have placed people in hardship. That verse likewise ends with “عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ”. Divine power thus accompanies knowledge of reality and wisdom in command and governance, rather than indiscriminate severity.

An even clearer witness to the connection of these descriptions with judgment and forgiveness is Jesus’ statement in Al-Maidah 5:118:

“إِنْ تُعَذِّبْهُمْ فَإِنَّهُمْ عِبَادُكَ وَإِنْ تَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ فَإِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ”

“If You punish them, they are Your servants; and if You forgive them, indeed You are the Invincible, the Wise.”

This verse shows that God’s might and wisdom are not confined to the power to punish; forgiveness is also inseparable from His might and wisdom.

Taken together, these verses show that God does not judge without distinction between a deliberate deceiver, a deceived person, someone who corrects their error, and someone who persists in it after recognizing the truth. Intention and awareness matter, but good intentions do not remove responsibility for correcting an error and repairing its harm.

The God is Invincible: His truth and command cannot be changed by oaths, status, prejudice, or misuse of His name. And He is Wise: His power and judgment accompany knowledge of the reality of human actions, responsibility, and the possibility of return.

People may slip, but should not turn their mistake into an unchangeable truth out of fear of family, society, position, or losing their reputation. The right path is to acknowledge and correct one’s error as Adam did, rather than knowingly persist in it to preserve pride and personal interests.
