Al-Baqarah 2:93

ۖ ۚ

And when We took your covenant and raised the Mount above you: Take what We have given you with strength, and listen. They said: We heard and we disobeyed; and because of their rejection of truth, the calf was made to be drunk into their hearts. Say: How evil is what your faith commands you to do, if you are believers.

Our Reflection

The verse continues to test the same claim: “We believe in what was sent down to us.” They said that they believed in what had been revealed to their own community; once again, the Qur’an sets precisely that inheritance before them: the covenant, the Mount, the Book, and the command that had required them from the outset to take firmly what they had received.

The covenant scene also appeared in Al-Baqarah 2:63, with the difference that there, after “Take what We have given you with strength,” came the command “Keep in remembrance what is in it”: keep its contents alive. Here the command is shorter and more decisive:

“And listen”; hear what is said.

Hearing in this context is not merely sound reaching the ear. The God’s word is heard so that it may be understood, accepted, and followed in practice. If sound reaches the ear but the will remains closed against it, hearing has not yet reached its purpose.

Their response reveals the entire distance between hearing and faith in two words:

“We heard and we disobeyed.” They heard, yet disobeyed.

The problem was not that the message had failed to reach them. They had heard it; but hearing had not led to obedience.

At the end of this same surah, the believers’ response stands directly opposite:

“We heard and we obeyed.” They heard and obeyed. Al-Baqarah 2:285

An-Nisa’ 4:46 also keeps this contrast alive, saying that it would have been better for them to say “We heard and we obeyed” instead of “We heard and we disobeyed.” Al-Anfal 8:21 extends the warning beyond the history of one people: “Do not be like those who say, ‘We heard,’ while they do not hear”; do not resemble people who profess to have heard but do not truly listen.

We may know a verse, recite it, and even understand its meaning, yet when the moment for decision and action comes, behave as though we had never heard it. The distance between “we heard” and “we obeyed” is where the profession of faith is tested.

The movement from taking the calf in the preceding verse to having the calf made to permeate the heart as a drink in this verse creates a profoundly weighty image:

“And they were made to drink the calf into their hearts.”

The calf is no longer merely something outside them. It has penetrated the heart so thoroughly that the Qur’an expresses it through the image of drinking: as though something has been absorbed into their being drop by drop, turning from an outward act into an inward attachment.

Commentators have spoken of “being made to drink the love of the calf,” but the Qur’an’s own image is more vivid than that explanation. What was once taken by hand has now taken its place in the heart. The idol is not simply standing before the eyes; it has made its home within its possessor.

The Qur’an does not conceal the root of this penetration either:

“Because of their rejection of truth.”

The calf has not settled in the heart without cause. Rejecting truth, resisting what is right, and repeated acts of disobedience opened the way for the internalization of what had initially stood outside the person. Someone repeatedly resists truth, then puts their own creation in its place, and finally that creation becomes so dear that separating it from faith grows difficult.

This is the danger that turns the calf from a historical incident into a mirror for every religious community. An idol is not always a golden statue. Sometimes it is an opinion we have fashioned ourselves, a report repeated over generations, a personality whose criticism we cannot tolerate, an institution whose survival we have equated with the survival of religion, or a tradition so firmly lodged in the heart that, if a verse is read against it, we find a way to restrict the verse before re-examining the tradition.

At this stage, the problem is no longer only an external error; the calf has been drunk into the heart.

The verse’s ending tests the claim to faith directly against its outcome:

“Say: How evil is that which your faith commands you to do, if you are believers.”

Say: How evil is what your faith commands you to do, if you are believers.

The verse’s irony is severe. They themselves had said: “We believe in what was sent down to us.” The Qur’an therefore asks: if this is your “faith,” what has this faith made of you? How can a faith whose outcome is disobedience, calf worship, and defiance of the very covenant in which you take pride serve as evidence that you are right?

The Qur’an is not saying that true faith commands disobedience. On the contrary, it tests the name “faith” by its fruit. Whatever we call faith does not become sacred merely through the name. We must see what it commands and what kind of human being it produces.

If what we call “religion” brings us closer to justice, truthfulness, fidelity to covenants, mercy, and obedience to truth, its fruit accords with its claim. But if that same name becomes a cover for prejudice, wrongdoing, violence, declaring others unbelievers, the pursuit of superiority, and the defense of our own creations, we can no longer escape the Qur’an’s question merely by repeating the word “faith.”

Like the preceding verses, this verse does not allow us to read the history of the Children of Israel while placing ourselves outside its test. We too may possess scripture, speak of faith, and take pride in our religious inheritance; but if in practice we live “we heard and we disobeyed,” possessing scripture does not solve the problem.

Faith is not a label for boasting or a shield for justification; it is a force that governs conduct. If what we call faith leads us toward prejudice, wrongdoing, and the defense of our self-made calves instead of submission to truth, we must test that professed faith itself before justifying our behavior. The danger begins when something made by our own hands takes such a firm place in the heart that we mistake its command for the command of The God.
