Al-Baqarah 2:90

ۖ ۚ

How evil is that for which they sold themselves: that they should reject what The God has sent down, out of transgressive rivalry that The God should send down of His bounty upon whomever He wills among His servants. So they brought upon themselves wrath upon wrath; and for those who reject truth there is a humiliating punishment.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse said that a truth they could recognize arrived, yet they rejected it. Now the Qur’an draws the curtain back one stage further and shows why recognition does not always lead to acceptance. The problem was not simply that they failed to understand what had been sent down; the verse speaks of transgressive rivalry (baghyan): truth had come through a channel that did not suit their wishes and their claim to exclusive possession.

The verse opens with the image of a transaction: “How evil is that for which they sold themselves.” The root SH R Y encompasses buying, selling, and exchange in Arabic; even in older usage, both buying and selling were expressed through this word family. Here, too, what matters is the transaction itself: no external commodity has been lost; their own selves (anfusahum) have become the capital of this exchange.

A few verses earlier, we were told of a group who took the near life of this world at the price of the Hereafter. Here the image becomes more severe. To preserve the prejudice and privilege they were unwilling to relinquish, they placed themselves in a transaction whose outcome was “to reject what The God has sent down”: covering and refusing the revelation of The God (kufr).

The Qur’an then identifies the motive with a single word: transgressive rivalry (baghyan). At its foundation, the root B GH Y is connected with seeking and pursuing; but when that pursuit exceeds its bounds, it becomes aggression, rebellion, and the pursuit of superiority. Thus, this transgression must not be reduced to envy alone. Envy can be one of its manifestations, but the concept is broader: a desire that no longer recognizes the limits of its own entitlement and oversteps them to obtain what it wants.

The verse itself also shows what this transgressive rivalry was directed against:

“That The God should send down of His bounty upon whomever He wills among His servants.”

That The God should send down from His own bounty upon whichever of His servants He Himself wills.

In one short sentence, three foundations of exclusive entitlement collapse. The bounty is “of His bounty”; it belongs to The God. The choice is “whomever He wills”; the authority is His. And those upon whom the bounty may descend are “among His servants”; they are His servants. No people, lineage, clerical class, language, or religious community is made a partner of The God in distributing bounty in this sentence.

The problem begins when we forget our servanthood and start issuing instructions to the owner of bounty. We want guidance to come, but from our lineage; truth to be spoken, but through people we consider authoritative; bounty to descend, but through the very channel our tradition has designated for it. If The God chooses someone beyond these boundaries, instead of breaking our own boundary, we may oppose His choice.

Bounty (faḍl) itself also breaks this claim to exclusivity. The root F Ḍ L concerns increase and surplus: something given to someone beyond what they can claim as a right. What belongs to The God as bounty is no community’s hereditary entitlement. No one can say: because my ancestors had prophets, because scripture was held by my people, because my language is the language of religion, or because my denomination considers itself closer, The God must therefore continue His bounty through this same channel.

The Qur’an repeatedly shows that disagreement does not always begin with ignorance. In Al ‘Imran 3:19, disagreement after knowledge has arrived is explained as “transgressive rivalry among them.” In Al-Baqarah 2:213, too, the disagreement of those given scripture after clear signs had arrived arose from “transgressive rivalry among them.” Ash-Shura 42:14 repeats the same pattern: knowledge has come, but transgression among them obstructs submission.

Sometimes ignorance is not the problem; knowledge has been contaminated by arrogance. Truth has been recognized, but accepting it carries a cost: it lowers one’s standing, breaks a monopoly, limits power, or calls into question a privilege that generations have regarded as natural. At that point it becomes clear whether we seek truth or our own superiority.

The verse describes the outcome of this transaction as “they brought upon themselves wrath upon wrath.” The verb for returning bearing something (bāʾū), from B W ʾ, conveys coming back with something and finding oneself in its consequences. What they chose did not remain separate from them; they returned with the consequence of their own choice: wrath upon wrath.

There is no need to attach each instance of “wrath” to a separate historical event. The expression itself conveys enough. One opposition has not ended before another is added to it: rejection after recognition, transgression after knowledge, and persistence after truth has become clear. A path repeatedly chosen also accumulates its consequences.

The verse ends: “And for those who reject truth there is a humiliating punishment.” Once again, the judgment is not attached to the name of a people. The Qur’an does not say “for the Children of Israel”; it says for those who reject truth. The same criterion applied to earlier people remains in force for anyone who repeats the same conduct.

The contrast between transgression (baghy) and humiliation (muhīn) also deserves attention. Transgression drives a person to seek superiority, exceed limits, and place themselves above others; the word for humiliating, from H W N, conveys abasement and being brought low. Someone willing to stand against truth to preserve an imagined superiority encounters a punishment that breaks that very superiority. Rising without right leads to humiliation.

This history has not been given so that we may throw earlier people’s errors in their faces and feel satisfied with ourselves. If the only fruit of reading these verses is that we insult the Children of Israel and imagine ourselves the pure heirs of guidance, then we have gained deviation, rather than guidance, from history.

A commentator who devotes all their energy to condemning the Children of Israel while failing to see the same diseases in their own history has not yet turned the Qur’an’s mirror toward themselves. The point is not to minimize the errors of the Children of Israel; it is to have the courage to apply the same criterion to ourselves without concessions.

If we regard the denial of freedom of expression and thought, coercion and compulsion in religion, declaring others unbelievers, forbidding what The God has not forbidden, treating one another’s blood as lawful, and turning religious disagreement into elimination and killing as wrong, Muslim history is not exempt from this test.

The Ridda wars, the Battle of the Camel, and Siffin are not merely names of old events. Muslims stood against Muslims, blood was shed, thousands were killed in civil wars, and among the dead were people from the first generation of Muslims and the Companions. Later, too, struggles over power, belief, and sect repeatedly led to imprisonment, declarations of unbelief, bloodshed, and the beheading of opponents.

Afterward, how many opinions, legal rulings, reports, and interpretations were put to work to justify or sanctify political and religious conflicts. Each faction fashioned a religious language for itself; each sect used part of the inheritance as a shield for its own claim to be right; and the body of a religion meant to gather people in servitude to The God was torn apart under the blades of politics, prejudice, power, and sect-making.

With such a history, our own record is not so spotless and pure that we can sit before the errors of Jews and merely shake our heads. If we condemn another’s injustice by the Qur’an, but when the same logic appears in our own inheritance we give it a respectable name, an exculpatory report, or a legal exception, we have fallen into the very blind spot these verses have come to break open.

When Satan declared his enmity toward Adam, he did not say that he would target one particular people. His field is the human being. Desire, arrogance, prejudice, transgression, the pursuit of power, and the wish to monopolize truth are not ethnic diseases. Muslims, Jews, Christians, Arabs, and non-Arabs are all vulnerable to them. No one’s religious name grants immunity from the test that the Qur’an sets before humanity.

Reflecting on history means not repeating past errors in new clothing. If the Qur’an recounts the transgression of the possessors of scripture, we must first ask where transgression hides within our own religious establishment. Where have we prescribed a channel for The God’s bounty? Where do we accept only a scholar from our own school? Where do we refuse to hear a true statement because it comes from another denomination? Where do we accept the Qur’an only as long as it endorses our inheritance, and when that same Qur’an challenges our assumptions, turn to interpretation to silence it?

The God’s bounty is not the property of any people, lineage, denomination, or class. Servanthood means accepting that He has the right to send down from His bounty upon whomever He wills among His servants. If we dictate to The God where truth must come from and who has the right to carry it, we are no longer merely dissatisfied with a person or a message; we have set limits upon the authority of the owner of bounty. And if we see the errors of earlier people but fail to see the same transgression, bloodshed, declarations of unbelief, and tearing apart of religion in our own history, we have read the verse without allowing it to judge us.
