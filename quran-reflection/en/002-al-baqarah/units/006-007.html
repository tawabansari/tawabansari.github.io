Al-Baqarah 2:6

إِنَّ الَّذينَ كَفَروا سَواءٌ عَلَيهِم ءَأَنذَرتَهُم أَم لَم تُنذِرهُم لا يُؤمِنونَ

English Translation

Indeed, those who have committed kufr — covering or rejecting truth — it is the same for them whether you warn them or do not warn them; they will not believe.

Our Reflection

This verse comes after the description of the muttaqīn in verses 2 to 5. At the beginning of the surah, the Qur’an introduced itself as a Book in which there is no doubt, and as guidance for the muttaqīn — those who carry readiness, vigilance, sincerity, and orientation toward truth within themselves. Now the verse speaks of a group standing in the opposite condition: alladhīna kafarū — those who have committed kufr.

Here, it is essential to understand that kufr in the Qur’an is not simple ignorance. Every person who does not know, has not yet reached the truth, or is sincerely searching is not included in this judgment. Kufr means covering, rejecting, or denying truth after truth has become clear, after signs have come, after the proof has been established, and after the path of understanding was open. For this reason, this verse is not speaking about the ignorant person, the questioner, the one in honest doubt, or the sincere seeker. It is speaking about those who have taken a posture of covering and resistance before clarified truth.

The phrase sawā’un ʿalayhim — “it is the same for them” — is very heavy. It means that warning them or not warning them has become equal. This equality does not mean that the warning is weak. Rather, it shows that the receiver of the warning is no longer in a state of receptivity. Just as rain, when it falls on living soil, brings forth growth, but when it falls on hard stone, does not penetrate it, the divine message affects a heart that still has an opening toward truth. But when a human being repeatedly covers truth, even warning becomes an ineffective sound to them.

Here, the role of the Prophet also becomes clear. The Prophet is entrusted with delivering the warning, not owning the result. He can warn, but he cannot force anyone into faith. The Qur’an repeats this principle many times: “upon the Messenger is only clear delivery,” as seen in Al-Mā’idah 5:99, An-Naḥl 16:82, An-Nūr 24:54, and At-Taghābun 64:12. Likewise, in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:21–22, the Prophet is described as a reminder, not as one who dominates over people.

Therefore, faith is the inner response of the human being to truth, not the product of external compulsion. In Al-Baqarah 2:256, the principle “there is no compulsion in religion” is stated, and in Yūnus 10:99, even the Prophet is asked whether he would compel people until they become believers. This shows that the Qur’anic path is not forced belief, but delivery, reminder, warning, and leaving the responsibility of choice open before the human being. We will later examine this principle in the independent article “There Is No Compulsion in Religion; the Prophet’s Duty Is Delivery”.

If the human being decides to live by their own assumptions, and to accept truth only where it agrees with desire, power, tradition, or self-interest, then divine warning will not affect them.

This verse also shows that the Qur’an is recited to all, but not all receive guidance from it. One and the same text can be guidance for the muttaqīn, while exposing the rejection of those who cover truth. The problem is not in the Book; the problem is in the orientation of the human being before the Book. The Qur’an is light, but one who closes their eyes does not benefit from light.

So verse 2:6 is an invitation to watch over the inner self. It should not be read only about “others.” Whenever a human being covers a truth that has become clear to them because of tribal loyalty, fear, arrogance, self-interest, or habit, they are stepping onto the path of kufr. The real danger is that a person may gradually reach a point where warning and no warning become the same for them.

Linguistic Notes

Kafarū comes from the root k-f-r, meaning to cover. In this context, it refers to those who cover or reject truth after it has become clear.

Sawā’un ʿalayhim means “it is equal for them” or “it is the same for them.” This phrase indicates a condition in which inner receptivity has become closed.

Andhartahum comes from the root n-dh-r, meaning to warn of a serious consequence. Warning here is not mere frightening; it is alerting the human being to the outcome of the path they have chosen.

Lā yu’minūn means “they do not believe.” But in this context, their not believing is not the result of simple ignorance; it is the result of the posture of kufr — covering and resisting truth.

Al-Baqarah 2:7

خَتَمَ اللَّهُ عَلىٰ قُلوبِهِم وَعَلىٰ سَمعِهِم ۖ وَعَلىٰ أَبصٰرِهِم غِشٰوَةٌ ۖ وَلَهُم عَذابٌ عَظيمٌ

English Translation

The God has sealed over their hearts and over their hearing; and over their sight is a covering; and for them is a great punishment.

Our Reflection

This verse continues and explains the condition of those mentioned in the previous verse. When warning and not warning become the same for someone, the matter is not merely that they do not hear a sound. It is a disorder in the human being’s tools of perception: heart, hearing, and sight.

In the Qur’an, the “heart” is not merely the center of emotions. The heart is the place of understanding, orientation, discernment, and moral decision. When the verse says that The God has sealed over their hearts, it should not be understood as a baseless and initial deprivation, as though The God closed the path of guidance against someone without cause. The Qur’an is not in conflict with divine justice. The sealing of the heart is the result of the human being’s path of covering truth. When a person rejects truth again and again, their ability to receive truth gradually becomes closed.

This principle becomes clearer in other verses. In Aṣ-Ṣaff 61:5, it is said that when they deviated, The God caused their hearts to deviate. This means that divine deviation is mentioned after the human being’s own choice of deviation. In Al-Jāthiyah 45:23, the Qur’an speaks of one who has taken his own desire as his god; then The God lets him go astray upon knowledge, seals his hearing and heart, and places a covering over his sight. This verse shows that sealing and misguidance come after a corrupt and chosen inner orientation.

Therefore, when the Qur’an says that The God guides whom He wills and lets go astray whom He wills, it should not be understood as randomness, injustice, or blind determinism. The will of The God in the Qur’an is not without wisdom or without law. The God acts through His sunnan — His consistent ways. Whoever is truth-seeking, humble, and ready for correction is placed on the path of guidance; and whoever repeatedly covers truth, acts arrogantly, and pushes signs aside in favor of desire or bias is left on the path of misguidance.

This can also be seen together with Al-Baqarah 2:26: The God guides many by the same example and lets many go astray by it, but the verse clarifies that He does not let any go astray by it except the fāsiqīn — those who break away from the right order. So divine misguidance is not baseless or accidental; it is connected to corruption, deviation, and the human being’s own orientation.

For this reason, the sealing of the heart in Al-Baqarah 2:7 is not an initial compulsion. It is the result of the divine way in response to persistent kufr. The human being covers truth; then covering truth becomes a habit of perception; and eventually, the heart, hearing, and sight no longer receive truth correctly. We will examine this subject later in the independent article “The Sealing of the Heart and the Divine Way of Guidance and Misguidance”.

Samʿ — hearing — appears in the singular in this verse, while qulūb — hearts — and abṣār — sights — are plural. This may indicate that the path of hearing the message is, in principle, one shared path: hearing truth. But hearts and sights — the centers of understanding and the angles of human perception — are multiple and scattered. Human beings see, analyze, and orient themselves in different ways; but when hearing truth becomes closed, the message no longer enters the system of understanding.

Ghishāwah over the sight means a covering that prevents clear seeing. This does not mean physical blindness. They may have healthy eyes, see the signs, read the verses, and observe the world, yet their seeing does not become insight. The eye receives the image, but if the heart is sealed, the image does not become understanding or guidance.

In this verse, three levels of perception are presented: heart, hearing, and sight. For guidance, the human being does not need only the eye. One must hear, understand, and become inwardly oriented. If the heart is sealed, hearing truth becomes ineffective, and seeing signs can turn into denial or mockery.

The phrase walahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm — “and for them is a great punishment” — is the natural result of this condition. The great punishment is not merely an external penalty; it is the continuation of that great separation from truth. A human being who closes the path of receiving truth against themselves becomes deprived of light, guidance, tranquility, and correction. In the end, this closing of perception leads the human being to a punishment whose greatness comes from the greatness of separation from truth.

This verse should not cause despair for sincere seekers. A person who still asks, still fears, still searches, still examines their doubt honestly, and is still ready for correction has not reached a state of complete closure. The danger of this verse concerns the one who no longer wants to see, no longer wants to hear, and no longer wants to reform themselves before truth.

So Al-Baqarah 2:7 is an image of the human being’s perceptive collapse: when kufr continues, the heart is sealed, hearing truth is closed, sight is covered, and the human being inwardly loses the path of guidance. This is not a sudden ending; it is the result of a path that began with covering truth.

Linguistic Notes

Khatama means to seal, close, or bring something to a state in which it is no longer easily opened or receptive.

Qulūb is the plural of qalb, heart. In the Qur’an, the heart is a place of understanding, discernment, intention, and moral orientation.

Samʿ means hearing. Its singular form here is worth reflection, because the path of hearing truth is, in principle, one shared path.

Abṣār is the plural of baṣar, sight or instruments of seeing. In the Qur’an, seeing is not only physical seeing; it can also relate to insight and understanding the signs.

Ghishāwah means a covering or veil that prevents clear seeing.

ʿAdhābun ʿaẓīm means a great punishment — a punishment proportionate to the greatness of the distance the human being has created from truth.
