Al-Baqarah 2:196

ۚ ۖ ۚ ۚ ۚ ۗ ۗ ۚ

And complete the Hajj and Umrah for The God. So if you are prevented, whatever hady is feasible; and do not shave your heads until the hady reaches its place. So whoever among you is ill or has an affliction of the head: a fidyah of siyam, charity, or nusuk. Then when you are secure, whoever benefits from Umrah until Hajj: whatever hady is feasible; and whoever does not find it: siyam for three days during Hajj and seven when you return; these are ten complete days. This is for someone whose family are not present at the Sacred Mosque. And practice taqwa toward The God, and know that The God is severe in punishment.

Our Reflection

After explaining the sanctity of the sacred month, the limits of confronting aggressors, and the command to spend in The God’s way, the Quran now turns to the rulings of Hajj and Umrah. This continuity matters, because Hajj is not merely a collection of movements and ceremonies. The House that The God established for people must be a place of security, servanthood, and taqwa. Worship at this House cannot be separated from Ibrahim’s way and the rights of people who travel toward it.

The God says:

“وَأَتِمُّوا الْحَجَّ وَالْعُمْرَةَ لِلَّهِ”

“And complete the Hajj and Umrah for The God.”

“أَتِمُّوا”, from the root ت م م, means bringing to completion. The verse’s command emphasizes completing Hajj and Umrah. They are two distinct designations in the Quran; Al-Baqarah 2:158 also names them separately. This distinction, however, does not mean that all later jurisprudential classifications, conditions, and details were prescribed in precisely the same form in the Quran.

A fundamental question arises here: what distinguishes Hajj from Umrah? Could their principal difference be the time at which they are performed?

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, the Quran says: “الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ”—Hajj is in known months. In Al-Baqarah 2:189, it also identifies the crescents as markers of time for people and Hajj. Hajj in the Quran is therefore connected with a specified time. No comparable time restriction is explicitly stated for Umrah.

This difference allows us to examine a possibility: perhaps Hajj means turning toward the House in its prescribed months, while Umrah is visiting and performing worship associated with the House at other times. This possibility accords with Hajj’s connection to a time period and the absence of an explicit specified time for Umrah.

Nevertheless, the fact that the Quran does not specify a time for Umrah does not yet establish that timing is the only difference between Hajj and Umrah. Moreover, “أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ” speaks of several months, not just one month called Muharram. The Quran itself does not list their names in Al-Baqarah 2:197. Their relationship to the four sacred months of At-Tawbah 9:36 requires separate examination.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 also speaks of “benefiting from Umrah until Hajj”. This phrase shows that Umrah and Hajj can form part of a continuous ritual sequence; it does not, by itself, prove that their acts are entirely identical and differ only in timing.

A difference in timing may therefore be considered one possible aspect distinguishing Hajj and Umrah. Until their other Quranic uses and the sequence of their acts are clarified, however, this possibility must not be turned into a definitive ruling. A broader examination appears in “Hajj in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights”, and we will pursue the question of the known months in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:197.

We must also distinguish between a command to complete worship and establishing an independent obligation to begin it. In Aal Imran 3:97, pilgrimage to the House is prescribed for those able to find a way to it. But regarding Umrah, an independent obligation upon every capable person cannot be inferred solely from “أَتِمُّوا”.

The qualification “لِلَّهِ” is also foundational: Hajj and Umrah are performed for The God, not to display religiosity, gain social standing, or submit to the authority of people who have added rulings to the rites on their own initiative. Ibrahim, whom The God made an imam for people, is known through monotheism and the upright way. Following him is not merely repeating outward actions; it also means fidelity to that same path of servanthood.

These foundations are examined in “Hajj in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights” and “Imamah in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Position and the Question of Leadership”.

The God then speaks of a situation in which a person cannot complete Hajj or Umrah in the usual manner:

“فَإِنْ أُحْصِرْتُمْ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ”

“So if you are prevented, whatever hady is feasible.”

“أُحْصِرْتُمْ”, from the root ح ص ر, is connected with being confined, restricted, and prevented from continuing an undertaking. This prevention may result from an obstacle beyond a person’s control, such as illness or a road being blocked through hostility and insecurity. The central issue in this verse’s context is being unable to complete worship that one has already embarked upon.

The Quran gives a clear example of people being prevented from reaching the Sacred Mosque in Al-Fath 48:25, which speaks of enemies’ obstruction and hady being prevented from reaching its place. A blocked route to Hajj is therefore a real issue in the Quran.

But must a person who is prevented endanger themselves to prove their servanthood, or remain trapped without any compensatory avenue? The God specifies hady and, through “مَا اسْتَيْسَرَ”, connects it with what is feasible.

“الْهَدْيِ” in this context refers to the ritual offering associated with Hajj, especially an animal designated for sacrifice. The concept of delivering something to a specified destination is important in this word. Hady is not merely something selected for sacrifice; it must also reach its prescribed place.

This connection is clear in other Quranic uses. Al-Maidah 5:95 contains “هَدْيًا بَالِغَ الْكَعْبَةِ”—hady reaching the Kaaba. Al-Fath 48:25 also speaks of hady held back and unable to reach its place. In this very verse, The God says: “حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْهَدْيُ مَحِلَّهُ”—until the hady reaches its place.

In Quranic uses of hady, the destination and delivery to its prescribed place have an important role. The word must therefore not be reduced merely to slaughter, nor should the hady’s arrival at its place be separated from the ruling.

The Quran also speaks elsewhere of sacrifice, its destination, mentioning The God’s name, and people benefiting from its meat, including Al-Hajj 22:28, Al-Hajj 22:36, Al-Hajj 22:37, and Al-Maidah 5:95. Al-Hajj 22:37 emphasizes that neither the meat nor the blood reaches The God; what matters is human taqwa.

Despite its real ritual place, hady must therefore not be magnified until the purpose of Hajj is reduced to slaughtering an animal. Sacrifice is one act within this worship, but taqwa, servanthood, and observing The God’s command cannot be reduced to an outward act.

What matters here is observing the verse’s condition. The ruling on hady when one is prevented must not, without grounds, be turned into an identical obligation for every pilgrim.

The verse continues:

“وَلَا تَحْلِقُوا رُءُوسَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْهَدْيُ مَحِلَّهُ”

“And do not shave your heads until the hady reaches its place.”

This command prohibits shaving the head until the hady reaches its prescribed place. Shaving the head in this context is therefore connected with a stage in completing the ruling concerning hady.

But we must not derive from this phrase an extensive list of prohibitions covering all body hair, beards, and nails. The God specifically speaks here of “رُءُوسَكُمْ”—your heads—not every part of the body.

He then provides a compensatory avenue for illness or an affliction of the head:

“فَمَنْ كَانَ مِنْكُمْ مَرِيضًا أَوْ بِهِ أَذًى مِنْ رَأْسِهِ فَفِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ”

“So whoever among you is ill or has an affliction of the head: a fidyah of siyam, charity, or nusuk.”

If someone needs to shave their head because of illness or an affliction, the ruling on fidyah opens a compensatory way. The God names three forms: siyam, charity, or nusuk.

It is important that fidyah in this phrase is connected with illness or an affliction of the head and the restriction concerning shaving it; it does not mean that every instance of cutting hair or nails under all Hajj circumstances requires fidyah.

Here the meaning of fidyah becomes clear again. It is a compensatory avenue in a specific situation, not necessarily a punishment assigned to every imagined violation of a collection of inherited customs.

This usage is also related to the discussion of fidyah in Al-Baqarah 2:184, although the subjects and conditions of the two rulings differ. Here The God names three forms of fidyah, but does not specify the number of days of siyam or the amount of charity in this phrase. Details absent from the verse itself must not be turned, without grounds, into a definitive command of The God.

“Nusuk”, from the root ن س ك, is connected with worship and ritual acts. The Quran uses this root in different contexts of worship, and in some instances it is clearly connected with sacrifice, including Al-Hajj 22:34 and Al-Anam 6:162.

Nusuk may involve self-sacrifice, generosity, and offering something in The God’s way, but its meaning must not be confined to animal slaughter in every Quranic use. Here, given the context of Hajj and fidyah, nusuk is a form of ritual compensation that may be connected with sacrifice.

The God next speaks of security returning:

“فَإِذَا أَمِنْتُمْ فَمَنْ تَمَتَّعَ بِالْعُمْرَةِ إِلَى الْحَجِّ فَمَا اسْتَيْسَرَ مِنَ الْهَدْيِ”

“So when you are secure, whoever benefits from Umrah until Hajj: whatever hady is feasible.”

This phrase shows the importance of security on the way to Hajj and Umrah. After describing being prevented, The God speaks of a situation in which the obstacle has been removed and security is established.

“تَمَتَّعَ”, from the root م ت ع, is connected with benefiting and enjoying provision. Here the verse speaks of benefiting from Umrah until Hajj and specifies hady for this situation as well.

This expression has particular importance for understanding the relationship between Hajj and Umrah. If timing is one of the principal aspects distinguishing them, we may consider the possibility that a person first performs Umrah and then benefits from the interval until the time of Hajj arrives. This reading is compatible with “بِالْعُمْرَةِ إِلَى الْحَجِّ”.

But the verse does not explain exactly what this benefit included. Does it concern only the time between the two acts of worship, or also enjoying things restricted in a particular ritual state? To determine this, other rulings related to Hajj, Umrah, and the state of hurum must be examined.

This phrase therefore neither rejects the hypothesis of a difference in timing nor establishes it fully on its own. What is clear is a connection between Umrah and Hajj and the prescription of hady for the stated situation.

Inherited jurisprudence recognizes Hajj tamattu, ifrad, and qiran with their sets of conditions and details. But one cannot immediately establish all these classifications as explicit Quranic rulings from “فَمَنْ تَمَتَّعَ بِالْعُمْرَةِ إِلَى الْحَجِّ”.

The types of Hajj and the place of hady are examined in detail in “Hajj in the Quran”.

The God then says:

“فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ فِي الْحَجِّ وَسَبْعَةٍ إِذَا رَجَعْتُمْ تِلْكَ عَشَرَةٌ كَامِلَةٌ”

“So whoever does not find it: siyam for three days during Hajj and seven when you return; these are ten complete days.”

Here the compensatory avenue is stated with an exact count: three days during Hajj and seven after returning, totaling ten complete days.

This numerical specification also matters. Whenever The God wishes to prescribe a particular number, He can state it clearly. We must not generalize the number specified in this ruling to the fidyah for illness or an affliction of the head, or to another ruling where no such number appears.

We must also attend to the differing conditions. These ten days of siyam concern someone who cannot find hady in the stated situation, not everyone who performs Hajj or Umrah.

In this ruling, siyam appears as an alternative to hady. This connection again shows that acts of worship and compensatory avenues can replace one another under specified conditions in the Quran. But such substitution depends on The God’s own command, not a person’s free choice to change any ruling.

The end of this section says:

“ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ أَهْلُهُ حَاضِرِي الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ”

“This is for someone whose family are not present at the Sacred Mosque.”

The God distinguishes between those whose families are present in the area of the Sacred Mosque and those who come from outside. This qualification must be examined in connection with the same ruling on benefiting from Umrah until Hajj, hady, and the alternative siyam, rather than extending it without grounds to all Hajj rulings.

The verse itself also sets no specified numerical distance for “حاضري المسجد الحرام”. A particular geographical boundary therefore cannot be presented as The God’s exact command without Quranic evidence.

The differing circumstances of residents and those coming from afar do not imply differences in the value of their servanthood or ownership of The God’s House by a particular group. In Al-Hajj 22:25, the Quran identifies the Sacred Mosque as being for people and speaks of equality between the resident and the one arriving from outside.

At the end of the verse, He says:

“وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ”

“And practice taqwa toward The God, and know that The God is severe in punishment.”

“Iqab”, from the root ع ق ب, is connected with following, outcome, and consequence. Human actions are not without consequences, and wrongful conduct can leave harmful effects and outcomes in individual and social life. Divine punishment, however, must not be confined to the natural, automatic consequences of actions; the Quran also speaks of The God’s punishment and reckoning.

This warning follows a set of rulings, conditions, compensatory avenues, and distinctions between people’s circumstances.

Taqwa here is not merely observing outward rites; it is fidelity to the limits The God Himself has stated. No one should disregard His clear command through indifference, just as no one has the right to add new obligations and prohibitions to religion in the name of Hajj and impose them on others.

“What Is Taqwa in the Quran?” examines this concept in connection with self-restraint, responsibility, and conscious obedience.

Hajj and Umrah are for The God. The Quran distinguishes them and identifies known months for Hajj, but does not explicitly state a comparable time restriction for Umrah. This difference provides grounds for examining the hypothesis of a distinction in timing, without by itself establishing all their differences.

The God has stated both the command to complete Hajj and Umrah and the conditions of being prevented, fidyah, and compensatory avenues. Hady must reach its prescribed destination, and ritual acts must serve servanthood and taqwa rather than reducing worship’s purpose to the outward form of sacrifice or constructed customs. Someone approaching Ibrahim’s House must know that their worship’s value lies not only in performing outward rites, but in understanding and observing the rulings The God has prescribed. Neither neglecting His clear command nor adding groundless rulings in religion’s name is taqwa.
