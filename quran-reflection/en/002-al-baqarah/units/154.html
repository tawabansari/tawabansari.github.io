Al-Baqarah 2:154

ۚ

Do not say of those killed in the way of The God that they are in a state of death; rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive it.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse called believers to seek help through patience and prayer and promised that God is with the patient. Now the discussion reaches one of life’s gravest realities: being killed in God’s way. God’s companionship does not mean that the patient will be immune to suffering, injury, or being killed in worldly life. People may remain steadfast in truth and even lose their lives. But does the end of their bodily life also mean the end of their entire existence?

The Qur’an says, “Do not say of those killed in the way of The God that they are dead.” Its wording matters. God does not deny that they are killed; the verse itself says “is killed.” The end of their bodily life is therefore real. Yet “rather, they are alive” immediately corrects our judgment. What appears to us as the end of a person’s life is not necessarily the end of their entire existence.

Al Imran 3:169 makes this clearer. It likewise forbids considering those killed in God’s way dead and describes them as alive with their Lord, receiving provision. Verse 3:170 speaks of their joy in God’s bounty and their gladness concerning those who have not yet joined them. This life therefore means more than their names, memories, or social influence surviving among people. A person’s memory exists in survivors’ minds, whereas the Qur’an speaks of life with the Lord and the person themselves receiving gifts, joy, and glad tidings.

“Provision” also matters here. Everyday usage often confines it to food, drink, wealth, and livelihood, but the Qur’anic field of the root R Z Q is broader, including gifts, benefits, and what comes from God to a creature. Al Imran 3:169 does not speak of eating, drinking, or nourishing a biological body. It reports divine provision without specifying its form or nature. The following verse connects this reception with bounty, joy, and glad tidings. We should therefore not restrict their provision to imagined bodily food and drink. See The Qur’anic Root R Z Q for further study.

To appreciate these verses, we must first distinguish the body’s biological life from the broader reality of life and existence. In the material world, what we ordinarily call life is understood biologically. Known terrestrial organisms generally have cellular organization, receive and use energy, metabolize, maintain internal balance, grow, and respond to environmental changes. Reproduction, transmission of hereditary information, and evolution across generations also characterize this system of life. These criteria are valuable for understanding life in the material world, but do not define every possible form of life throughout existence.

The human body belongs to this biological system. The heart, brain, nervous system, circulation, breathing, and other organs operate within material structures and chemical and biological processes. When this system’s vital activity irreversibly ends, the body’s biological life in this world also ends. This is what bodily observation and examination establish. But the end of this body’s activity does not immediately establish that the entire existence of the person who lived through it has been annihilated.

The death of the material, biological body is not identical to the absolute annihilation of human existence. The first statement concerns the end of bodily vital activity in the material world; the second judges the whole reality of a person’s existence. Recognizing the end of bodily life is not, by itself, sufficient to establish absolute annihilation. The body may no longer be alive, but that fact alone does not answer what has become of the person’s entire existence.

This distinction raises a more fundamental question: must being alive at every level of existence depend on cells, a nervous system, circulation, breathing, and carbon-based metabolism? Or are these features of the kind of life we know in the material world? If life is not confined to biological organization, on what basis might we think about other forms of being alive?

Philosophically, life can be considered not only through material composition and bodily structure, but through a being’s capacities. Receiving information from its environment or sphere of existence, analyzing and processing that information, and responding or acting accordingly are capacities we might consider when imagining different forms of life. Reception may occur through familiar biological senses or through another means whose nature we do not yet know. Nor need information processing be assumed to require a brain and nervous system like those of a human body.

Within this model, action and response may also take different forms. A being might decide through independent choice and will; another might exercise constrained, implicit agency within its abilities, possibilities, and conditions; another might act without independent choice or will, according to an order and command governing its existence. Absolute free will is therefore not a necessary condition of this conception of life. Awareness, degrees of choice, and modes of response may differ among beings without all being forced into the mold of human experience.

Human beings themselves illustrate limited agency. We choose, but our will is not absolute. We did not determine the time and place of our birth, many bodily characteristics, sensory limitations, mental capacities, or much of our life circumstances. Even conscious decisions occur within the knowledge and possibilities available to us. Yet the Qur’an recognizes humans as living beings. Absolute choice cannot therefore be made a condition of being alive.

The Qur’an also speaks of angels receiving, knowing, conversing, receiving commands, and acting. In Al-Baqarah 2:32, angels acknowledge their limited knowledge and what God has taught them. Al-Anbiya 21:27 describes them as servants who do not precede God in speech and act by His command. At-Tahrim 66:6 likewise speaks of their obedience. These verses show that the actions of divine creatures need not be explained through the same kind of human will and choice. Nevertheless, we should not definitively specify the degree or quality of angels’ agency beyond what the Qur’an states.

Regarding jinn, the Qur’an speaks of hearing, receiving a message, differences in understanding, and choosing different paths. In Al-Jinn 72:1, a group hears the Qur’an and responds. Verse 72:11 describes their differing paths and conditions; verse 72:14 identifies groups with different orientations. Ar-Rahman 55:14 and Ar-Rahman 55:15 describe human and jinn creation in different terms. The Qur’an thus presents beings whose reception, action, and relationship to guidance matter, but whose constitution cannot without justification be equated with the human biological body.

This philosophical approach does not mean that we have discovered a definitive, universal test for the life of every being. Reception, processing, and action are proposed here as ways of thinking about possible forms of life, not a law for issuing final judgments about every entity. Even the full reality before God of a stone, a piece of wood, or a human-made device cannot be judged in all its dimensions solely through our limited knowledge.

An-Nahl 16:21 describes objects invoked instead of God as dead and not alive, but this judgment should not be turned, without contextual examination, into a comprehensive philosophical definition of everything in existence. Conversely, Al-Isra 17:44 speaks of everything glorifying God and reminds us that humans do not understand that glorification. This does not establish that everything is alive in precisely the same sense as humans or jinn. What becomes clear is the limitation of our understanding of all forms of existence, reception, and action in creation.

This philosophical framework is therefore designed neither to prove that everything is alive nor to deny life to beings without familiar biological structures. Its purpose is to avoid restricting life from the outset to the material, biological body. It is one way of considering other possible forms of life, not a claim to certain knowledge of their nature.

The Qur’an itself does not use life only for bodily activity. Al-Anfal 8:24 calls biologically living people to something that gives them life. In Al-An‘am 6:122, becoming alive is connected with receiving light and walking among people. These uses show that Qur’anic life can also concern guidance, reception, and awakening beyond ordinary bodily activity. However, life through guidance, biological life, and life with the Lord must not be assumed to have an identical nature. A shared word does not permit transferring every feature of one use to another.

Al-‘Ankabut 29:64 identifies the life of the hereafter as true life. This matters because worldly life is not treated as life’s only complete and final form. We know this life, but familiarity with one level does not mean knowing every level.

This also connects with our earlier examination of Al-Baqarah 2:28. That verse begins the human path with a condition called “dead,” then speaks of receiving life, entering death, receiving life again, and returning to God. In our reflection, we considered this sequence as a model of different states of human existence. On that reading, before biological and individual emergence in the material world, the human being occupied a condition the Qur’an calls dead; then enters worldly life, experiences and chooses within it, and passes beyond that stage when this life ends.

Ghafir 40:11 also speaks of two deaths and two lives. Az-Zumar 39:42 distinguishes taking the self at death from taking it during sleep: in one case it is retained, while in the other it is sent back until an appointed time. As-Sajdah 32:11 likewise connects death with being taken and returning to the Lord. These verses allow death to be studied more broadly than merely the cessation of bodily activity, although the nature of the self, its precise relationship to the body, and life after death still require caution.

In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:28, we also considered this path in relation to the covenant of Adam’s children in Al-A‘raf 7:172 and return to the source. Such a reading is a philosophical and interpretive model of a group of verses; it does not mean that one verse explains the constitution and character of every stage of human existence. For closer examination of the terms, see The Qur’anic Root M W T and The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Y Y.

The distinction between the witnessed realm and what is unseen to us now becomes more significant. Al-An‘am 6:73 describes God as knowing the unseen and the witnessed. Above all, this affirms His comprehensive knowledge: He knows what is apparent to us and what remains hidden from our understanding. In worldly life, we primarily recognize life through bodily signs, but this world’s instruments of knowledge are not necessarily sufficient to investigate every form of existence in the unseen.

Al-A‘raf 7:27 speaks of beings who see humans from where humans do not see them. In describing death, Al-Waqi‘ah 56:85 also speaks of a reality beyond the observation of those present. These illustrate the distinction between a reality’s existence and human ability to observe it. Our inability to see something neither proves its existence nor its absence. Yet when revelation itself reports a reality, the limits of worldly senses alone should not become grounds for denying it.

Al-Baqarah 2:154 marks this boundary with “but you do not perceive.” It does not say that the addressees have no information about their life: the verse itself has informed them. It says they do not perceive it. Learning through divine report differs from directly observing or sensing a reality. The Qur’an teaches us that they are alive, but that knowledge is not experiential knowledge of how they live. This matters for the final verb: the limits of our perception do not limit the reality God reports.

Here we must also avoid constructing material images of their life. When the Qur’an says that they receive provision with their Lord, we need not transfer digestion, food, drink, or other worldly bodily needs to that level of life. Conversely, we cannot confidently specify its constitution, means of perception, or mode of existence. The Qur’an speaks of life, provision, bounty, joy, and glad tidings. These are the basis of our knowledge; their nature has not been specified in details unavailable to us.

Nor should the circumstances of everyone who dies be made identical without justification. Al-Baqarah 2:154 concerns those killed in God’s way. This particular report is not itself a complete account of everyone’s condition after death. Yet Al-Hajj 22:58 promises good provision to those who emigrated in God’s way and were subsequently killed or died otherwise. Divine gifts should therefore not be confined to the manner of being killed. The path and direction of a person’s life also matter.

This returns us to “in the way of The God.” The Qur’an does not issue this judgment about everyone killed in a war or entering battle in God’s name. Being killed, belonging to a people or community, or claiming to defend religion does not by itself establish that someone followed God’s way. That way must be recognized through the Qur’an’s own criteria, connected with truth, equity, doing good, and avoiding oppression. People can judge outward conduct, but do not possess complete knowledge of intention, the reality of actions, or each person’s final standing before God.

Another distinction must remain clear. The verse identifies people killed in God’s way, but does not name them “witnesses” (shahīd). Witness and testimony in the Qur’an concern presence, observation, awareness, and bearing witness to truth. God Himself is a witness; prophets bear witness concerning their communities; and in Al-Baqarah 2:143, the community of believers is also placed in a position of witnessing concerning people. Witnesshood should therefore not be reduced to a person’s manner of death. Someone may bear witness throughout life through understanding, honesty, justice, and steadfastness in truth, sometimes at the cost of their life. See Who Is a Shahīd in the Qur’an? for further discussion.

The connection with the command to remain patient now becomes clearer. In Al-Baqarah 2:153, God promised companionship to the patient, but not a life free of danger, calamity, or bodily death. Al Imran 3:168 reports people saying of those killed that they would not have been killed had they followed them. The Qur’an challenges them to ward off death from themselves if they speak truthfully. Al Imran 3:169 then reports life with the Lord for those killed in God’s way, rather than measuring human worth solely by bodily survival.

Al-Baqarah 2:155 speaks of trials involving fear, hunger, and losses of wealth, lives, and fruits. In 2:156, the patient recall their belonging to God and return to Him when calamity strikes. Verse 2:157 then speaks of their Lord’s blessings and mercy upon them. Verse 154 stands between the command to patience and these verses of trial, broadening our perspective beyond visible worldly outcomes. Steadfastness in truth may involve losing bodily life, but in the Qur’an’s account, that life’s ending is not necessarily the end of the entire path of human existence.

Ultimately, this verse confronts us with the boundary of our knowledge. We primarily experience life through a material, biological body and recognize its end as bodily death. Yet the Qur’an reports a life beyond our ordinary perception for those killed in God’s way. We may philosophically consider other forms and levels of life, but must not substitute our imaginings for a reality whose nature we do not yet know.

What we see in the witnessed realm is not all existence, and what we do not know of the unseen is not erased by our ignorance. The material, biological human body can die, but its death does not establish the absolute annihilation of human existence. God reports a life we do not perceive; its reality and manner are known to the one God.

God knows best.
