Al-Baqarah 2:256

ۖ ۚ ۗ

There is no compulsion in religion; the right way has become distinct from error. So whoever rejects taghut and believes in The God has grasped the firm handhold, which has no break; and The God is Hearing, Knowing.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse, Ayat al-Kursi, introduced The God through His power, knowledge, self-subsisting sustenance, and absolute sovereignty. Everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him; no power is independent of His sovereignty, and preserving all existence does not burden Him. Now, immediately after presenting such immense sovereignty, the Quran declares one of religion’s most fundamental principles:

«لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ»

“There is no compulsion in religion.”

The placement of these two verses beside each other carries profound meaning. The same deity who rules over all existence has given human beings choice in matters of faith and servitude. God’s absolute power does not conflict with the will and choice of His creatures. He could have created a world in which people had no possibility of disobedience. In the world the Quran describes, however, people can choose and are responsible for what they choose through their own will.

Here the difference between forced obedience and conscious servitude becomes clear.

If a person is forced by threats, torture, imprisonment, or deprivation to profess a belief, fear may lead them to repeat the words demanded by the coercive power. But is such a profession evidence of faith? If someone outwardly submits to a religion out of fear of family, society, a ruler, or religious authorities, can this outward submission be considered a conscious acceptance of truth?

Coercion can change a person’s outward behavior, but it does not, by itself, create understanding and genuine faith within them.

In Al-Hujurat 49:14, the Quran also distinguishes between professed submission and faith that has entered the heart. Faith therefore cannot be measured solely by verbal declarations, outward acts, or membership in a religious community.

This principle is also directly connected with the purpose of divine testing. In Al-Mulk 67:2, God says He created death and life to test which people act best.

A moral test has meaning when people have a real possibility of choice. If no alternative exists and they can perform only one action, that action alone does not demonstrate a conscious preference for truth over falsehood.

In life, people encounter the possibilities of good and evil, justice and oppression, honesty and deception, guidance and rebellion. The possibility of disobedience does not mean disobedience is good; rather, it is among the conditions that give human choice and moral responsibility meaning.

Freedom of choice is not merely a social privilege; it is connected with the reality of faith, human will, and the wisdom of divine testing.

This truth is expressed through a very clear question in Yunus 10:99:

“وَلَوْ شَاءَ رَبُّكَ لَآمَنَ مَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ كُلُّهُمْ جَمِيعًا أَفَأَنْتَ تُكْرِهُ النَّاسَ حَتَّىٰ يَكُونُوا مُؤْمِنِينَ” — Yunus 10:99

“And had your Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed, all of them together. Will you then compel people to become believers?”

This question addresses God’s Messenger. If the Messenger himself was not commissioned to make people believers through coercion, who has given rulers, scholars, religious leaders, or human communities the right to claim ownership of other people’s beliefs and consciences?

The Quran states in Al-Ghashiyah:

“فَذَكِّرْ إِنَّمَا أَنْتَ مُذَكِّرٌ” — Al-Ghashiyah 88:21

“So remind; you are only one who reminds.”

“لَسْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِمُصَيْطِرٍ” — Al-Ghashiyah 88:22

“You are not a controller over them.”

The same group of verses also clarifies responsibility for final judgment:

“إِنَّ إِلَيْنَا إِيَابَهُمْ” — Al-Ghashiyah 88:25

“Indeed, to Us is their return.”

“ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا حِسَابَهُمْ” — Al-Ghashiyah 88:26

“Then indeed, upon Us rests their reckoning.”

These verses clarify the boundaries of responsibility. The Messenger’s duty is to convey the message, make the truth clear, remind, and warn. He is not commissioned to impose faith on people’s hearts. Their return and final reckoning also rest with God.

No person can therefore seize control of others’ consciences in the name of religion or force them through threats and punishment to accept that person’s religious interpretation.

The Quran continues:

«قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ»

“The right way has become distinct from error.”

This expression shows that rejecting coercion does not mean truth and falsehood are equivalent. The Quran speaks of sound guidance becoming clear and distinct from error.

Rushd is connected with recognizing the right way and proceeding thoughtfully along a path that leads to what is sound and good. Ghayy stands opposite it here.

The central issue is therefore to make the path clear, not to compel a person to follow it.

The verb “تَبَيَّنَ”, from the root B Y N, denotes becoming clear and distinct. When the right way becomes clear, a person can recognize and consciously choose it. That clarity does not authorize depriving them of choice.

Someone inviting others to truth should therefore offer reasons and evidence, provide opportunities for questioning and examination, and keep the way to understanding open. If they threaten instead of giving reasons, prohibit questions instead of clarifying truth, and use fear to force acceptance instead of inviting, their method is incompatible with the principle “لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ”.

In An-Nahl 16:125, the Quran also connects inviting people to the Lord’s way with wisdom, good counsel, and discussion in the best manner.

Truth should be made clear, but the clarity of truth gives no person the right to impose it upon another’s conscience.

The Quran then moves from freedom of choice to the choice that connects a person with a firm path:

«فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ بِاللَّهِ»

“So whoever rejects taghut and believes in The God…”

These two concepts are connected. Faith in God is not merely a verbal declaration; it accompanies freedom from authorities that seek to put their own desires or power in the place of truth.

To understand taghut, we must consider the meaning of rebellion or transgression. Taghut is connected with the root T GH Y and exceeding a limit. In Quranic usage, taghut can be an authority or power that exceeds its rightful place and assumes a position of worship, guardianship, or judgment without having the right to do so.

But where does such rebellion originate?

The account of Adam and Iblis presents a fundamental question here:

If Iblis tempted Adam, who drew Iblis himself into disobedience? What force led him to defy God’s command?

The Quran does not identify another tempter behind Iblis’s first disobedience. Rather, Al-Baqarah 2:34 says of him:

“أَبَىٰ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ” — Al-Baqarah 2:34

“He refused and sought greatness.”

In Al-Araf 7:12, when God asks him about his disobedience, Iblis replies:

“أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِنْهُ خَلَقْتَنِي مِنْ نَارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُ مِنْ طِينٍ” — Al-Araf 7:12

“I am better than him; You created me from fire and created him from clay.”

This answer reveals the inner source of his defiance:

“أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِنْهُ”: I am better than him.

Iblis made himself the measure of superiority. Instead of measuring his judgment against truth, he measured God’s command against his own self-centered standard.

Here we can recognize one of the most fundamental grounds for rebellion: self-centeredness, a sense of superiority, and putting one’s own will before truth.

The source of rebellion is therefore not always external. A being endowed with will can develop an inward tendency toward self-worship and exceeding limits. When this tendency puts its own desires or authority before truth and justice, it can become an authority of taghut.

The human self and its natural desires are not, in themselves, taghut. The problem begins when people put their own desires in a position to which they have no right.

In An-Naziat 79:37, the Quran speaks of someone who has transgressed; by contrast, An-Naziat 79:40 describes someone who restrains the self from desire. This contrast shows that a person can choose between following selfishness and restraining it before truth.

The possibility of rebellion is among the preconditions of real choice in the arena of human testing; rebellion itself, however, is a wrong choice, and following it is not inevitable.

The account of Adam and Iblis also makes clear that the possibility of error is not the same as knowingly persisting in error. Adam slipped, but accepted responsibility for his error and returned to God. Iblis persisted in his sense of superiority and defiance.

This difference matters in human life. Someone may be deceived or make a mistake with good intentions. Once the truth becomes clear, however, a new choice arises: are they willing to correct their error, or will they persist in it to preserve their pride, interests, and standing?

The same reasoning can be seen in social life.

For example, someone may have defended a religious interpretation for years. Yet when faced with clear evidence that it is wrong, they may refuse to examine and correct it solely out of fear of losing their reputation or followers. In such a situation, personal desire has been placed before truth.

Or a ruler may treat political power as their absolute property rather than a limited responsibility, seeking to impose their religious interpretation or desires upon other people’s consciences. This is where inner rebellion can become outward domination.

Rejecting taghut, however, is not merely rejecting an oppressive ruler. People must free themselves from every false authority seeking to take the place of truth, even if that authority consists of their own prejudice, interests, or desires.

Independent thought does not mean making every personal desire the measure of truth. It means being able to recognize truth through evidence and place it above fear, prejudice, selfishness, and false authorities.

In An-Nahl 16:36, serving God and avoiding taghut are placed together. Az-Zumar 39:17 also gives good news to those who avoid serving taghut and return to God.

Then Az-Zumar 39:18 speaks of those who:

“يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقَوْلَ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ أَحْسَنَهُ” — Az-Zumar 39:18

“Listen to what is said and follow the best of it.”

This connection is very important. Freedom from taghut does not come from closing one’s ears to others; it is connected with listening, thinking, evaluating, and following what is more sound.

People can learn from those with knowledge, benefit from the experience of earlier generations, and consult others. But they should not place any human authority so far beyond questioning and evaluation that, even when an error becomes clear, following that authority takes precedence over truth.

Thus, rejecting taghut and believing in The God are two connected directions of one choice: liberation from false authority and a conscious turning toward truth.

For a detailed study of taghut, the inner source of rebellion, the distinction between the self and Satan, social and political examples, and the connection with human will and freedom, see Taghut in the Quran: from Self-Worship to Domination over People.

Freedom of choice, however, does not mean an absence of responsibility.

In Al-Insan 76:3, the Quran states:

“إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا” — Al-Insan 76:3

“We have shown him the way, whether he is grateful or rejects it.”

Al-Kahf 18:29 also states:

“فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِنْ وَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ” — Al-Kahf 18:29

“So whoever wills, let them believe, and whoever wills, let them disbelieve.”

Yet the same verse also warns of the consequences of wrongdoing.

The right to choose therefore does not mean truth and falsehood are equivalent or that human conduct has no consequences. Once the right way and error have become clear, a person can consciously choose either, but also bears responsibility for that choice.

People are free to choose their path, but that very free choice shapes their life, conduct, character, and ultimate outcome in the Hereafter.

The Quran then describes the result of rejecting taghut and believing in The God through a beautiful image:

«فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا»

“They have grasped the firm handhold, which has no break.”

Istamsaka belongs to the root family M S K and denotes grasping and holding firmly. Urwah is a handle or handhold that can be grasped, while wuthqa, from W TH Q, indicates the utmost firmness and trustworthiness.

The Quran likens conscious faith in God and rejection of taghut to a person grasping a firm, secure handhold.

This image is deeply connected with the beginning of the verse: coercion was rejected at the outset, and now the verse speaks of grasping and holding fast.

The person has not been forcibly tied to this handhold; they have grasped it through understanding, choice, and will.

The same expression appears in Luqman 31:22:

“وَمَنْ يُسْلِمْ وَجْهَهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ” — Luqman 31:22

“And whoever submits their face to The God while doing good has grasped the firm handhold.”

The verse in Al-Baqarah speaks of rejecting taghut and believing in God, while Luqman speaks of submitting one’s face to God together with doing good. These two statements show that grasping the firm handhold is not merely a verbal claim or religious label; it is connected with a person’s actual orientation and conduct.

“لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا” emphasizes the strength and unbreakability of the handhold itself. The firmness of the handhold does not mean that everyone who once grasped it is forever immune to slipping, regardless of later choices and behavior.

The handhold is firm, but people still possess will and remain responsible for holding fast to it.

The verse finally ends with two divine attributes:

«وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ»

“And The God is Hearing, Knowing.”

This ending is in harmony with the verse’s beginning.

God hears people’s words and knows the reality of their intentions, understanding, and choices. Someone may profess faith before others while not believing inwardly, or may be unable to reveal their true faith under pressure and threats.

People see one another’s outward speech and conduct, but God knows the reality of what takes place within them. No power can claim dominion over the reality of human faith by extracting a verbal confession or imposing religious behavior.

The end of the verse thus also reminds us that the truth of people’s faith and intentions, on which final judgment rests, is not hidden from the knowledge of The God.

The verse began by rejecting compulsion, spoke of the right way becoming distinct from error, connected rejecting taghut and believing in God with a firm handhold, and ended by recalling God’s hearing and knowledge.

Freedom and responsibility are not set against each other in this verse; they are connected. To choose truth consciously, people must have the possibility of choice. But this very freedom makes them responsible for what they understand and choose.

This truth is directly connected with Ayat al-Kursi. The absolute sovereignty of The God does not mean depriving people of choice. Within that same sovereignty, God has endowed people with will and the possibility of choice and has made the right way clear to them.

A religion founded on understanding, choice, and conscious faith cannot be established by seizing people’s consciences and imposing beliefs in God’s name.

People attain genuine freedom neither through blind servitude to external powers nor by placing their own desires in the position of truth. Rather, by recognizing truth, freeing themselves from the authority of taghut, and consciously choosing the right way, they grasp the firm handhold.

For a broader examination of this principle, see the principle of no compulsion in religion and No Compulsion in Religion: the Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey.

The next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:257, continues this discussion by presenting God’s guardianship and the guardianship of taghut, showing how people’s choices are connected with the guardianship they accept and the path they follow.
