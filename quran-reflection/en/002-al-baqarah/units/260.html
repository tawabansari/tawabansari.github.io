Al-Baqarah 2:260

ۖ ۖ ۖ ۚ

And when Abraham said, “My Lord, show me how You bring the dead to life.” He said, “Have you not believed?” He said, “Yes, but so that my heart may be reassured.” He said, “Then take four birds and incline them toward yourself; then place a portion of them on each mountain; then call them: they will come to you swiftly. And know that The God is invincible and wise.”

Our Reflection

This verse begins with a question from Abraham:

“رَبِّ أَرِنِي كَيْفَ تُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ”

“My Lord, show me how You bring the dead to life.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:258, we saw Abraham use logic and reasoning to refute the claim of a ruler who considered himself the master of life and death. In Al-Baqarah 2:259, the Quran related the experience of a man who was raised after a hundred years of death and witnessed signs of divine power to recreate.

Now Abraham himself, despite his faith in his Lord, wishes to observe this reality with his own eyes. He does not settle for knowing alone; his curious, searching, and adventurous spirit wishes to go beyond reasoned knowledge and witness how the dead are brought to life.

God asks:

“أَوَلَمْ تُؤْمِنْ”

“Have you not believed?”

Abraham immediately replies:

“بَلَىٰ وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَطْمَئِنَّ قَلْبِي”

“Yes, but so that my heart may be reassured.”

“لِيَطْمَئِنَّ,” from the root Ṭ M N, denotes becoming calm and reaching stability and reassurance. Abraham had faith, but wished his heart to attain greater calm and reassurance through direct observation.

The answer “بَلَىٰ” is explicit: Abraham affirms his faith. His request to observe is therefore not a sign of disbelief. He distinguishes between accepting a truth through reason and faith and seeing how it comes about.

This distinction is familiar in human life as well. We may be certain through reasoning that a phenomenon is real, while still being eager to see with our own eyes how it occurs. Observation does not replace Abraham’s prior faith; it expands another dimension of his knowledge.

God answers Abraham’s request with practical guidance:

“فَخُذْ أَرْبَعَةً مِنَ الطَّيْرِ فَصُرْهُنَّ إِلَيْكَ”

“Then take four birds and incline them toward yourself.”

In “فَصُرْهُنَّ إِلَيْكَ,” the meaning of inclining the birds toward oneself and bringing them close is involved. From this expression, one may understand that Abraham first brings them to himself and familiarizes them with him.

There is also another reading of the verb “فَصُرْهُنَّ” that connects it with cutting and separating. Yet whether we initially take this verb to mean bringing the birds close or understand cutting within it, we must also consider the expression “مِنْهُنَّ جُزْءًا” and the central purpose of Abraham’s request.

The command then follows:

“ثُمَّ اجْعَلْ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ جَبَلٍ مِنْهُنَّ جُزْءًا”

“Then place a portion of them on each mountain.”

Here the word “جُزْءًا” matters: it means a part of a collection or whole.

If the event consisted merely of placing four intact, trained birds on different mountains and then having them return on hearing their owner’s voice, such a demonstration would not by itself show how the dead are brought to life. The birds’ return becomes a meaningful answer to Abraham’s request when it is connected with the separation of their parts and the recreation of those same beings.

Thus, the placement of parts of the birds in separate locations and the subsequent return of those same birds form the core of this sign. The Quran does not explain the details of how the parts were separated, the kinds of birds, or the number of mountains, because the principal purpose is to demonstrate God’s power to recreate.

It then says:

“ثُمَّ ادْعُهُنَّ يَأْتِينَكَ سَعْيًا”

“Then call them; they will come to you swiftly.”

What matters in this command is the return of the same four birds. The pronoun “هُنَّ” in the command to call them refers to the same birds Abraham had taken at the outset, not four new birds or creatures that merely resemble them in appearance.

In this reading, Abraham first brings the birds close and familiarizes them with him; then, at the stage of recreation, those same birds respond to his call. This continuity takes the matter beyond rebuilding a bodily structure and raises a deeper question about the identity of a living being.

Does bringing something back to life mean merely producing a similar body, or restoring the same being with its prior identity?

Today, advances in technology and genetics have enabled human beings to modify the hereditary structure of organisms and clone certain animals. Human capabilities may become much more extensive in the future. Yet if a person can use genetic material (deoxyribonucleic acid) to produce a being resembling one that has died, will that being be the same original individual, with its previous memories, experiences, knowledge, and identity?

We also raised this question in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:258, concerning the difference between human technological abilities and divine power. Making a similar copy of a being is not the same as restoring that very being.

In the present verse, the Quran does not explain the details of how the birds’ memory and awareness are preserved, but it speaks of calling and of the return of the same birds. The identity of a living being and its continuity in recreation therefore provide valuable ground for reflection on this verse.

This subject is also connected with Ya-Sin 36:79, where the answer to the question about reviving decayed bones points to the power of the same Creator who first brought them into being. Qaf 50:4 likewise speaks of God’s knowledge of what the earth diminishes from human bodies.

The limits of human knowledge and tools should therefore not be made the measure of all the abilities of the Creator of existence. The same God who first created a being does not depend on the limited abilities of created beings to recreate it.

The verse finally says:

“وَاعْلَمْ أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ”

“And know that The God is invincible and wise.”

“ʿAzīz” denotes power and might that cannot be overcome, while “ḥakīm” denotes wisdom and firmness in directing and accomplishing affairs.

These two attributes are significant together. God’s power is not merely the ability to perform something astonishing; it is accompanied by wisdom. The first creation, causing death, bringing to life, and recreating beings all fall within His power and wisdom.

At the beginning of the verse, Abraham asked from a position of faith and wished to see. The divine response opened a path to observation and greater knowledge. This conduct demonstrates the value of sincere inquiry: a person can believe in a truth while continuing to seek a deeper understanding of it.

Abraham’s faith did not prevent his question; it drew him toward greater knowledge.

Thus, three successive verses present a connected picture of life and death: in Al-Baqarah 2:258, a claim to human power is tested by Abraham’s argument; in Al-Baqarah 2:259, a person undergoes death and being raised; and in Al-Baqarah 2:260, Abraham wishes to observe how the dead are brought to life.

Throughout these accounts, the reality introduced in Ayat al-Kursi through the description “الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ” becomes apparent from different angles: giving life and recreating lie within the power of The God—an invincible power accompanied by wisdom.
