Al-Baqarah 2:210

ۚ

Are they waiting for anything but that The God should come to them in canopies of cloud, and the angels should come, and the matter be decided? And all affairs are returned to The God.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:208, God called the believers to enter peace together and to avoid following Satan’s footsteps. In Al-Baqarah 2:209, He warned against slipping after clear evidence had arrived. Now He asks a reproachful question:

“هَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَأْتِيَهُمُ اللَّهُ فِي ظُلَلٍ مِنَ الْغَمَامِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ وَقُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ”

“Are they waiting for anything but that The God should come to them in canopies of cloud, and the angels should come, and the matter be decided?”

“Yanzurun,” from the root N Ẓ R, here means waiting. The verse challenges those who seem to await the coming of God and the angels and a decisive event before accepting the truth.

Is clear evidence and divine guidance not given so that people may reflect upon it, recognize the right path, and act responsibly? Must a person await an extraordinary event that leaves no choice but to accept the truth?

In Al-Isra 17:92, the Prophet’s opponents demand that God and the angels be brought before them. In Al-Furqan 25:21, some demand the descent of angels or the sight of their Lord, and the Quran connects this conduct with their arrogance and rebellion.

But a more significant parallel is Al-Anam 6:8:

“وَقَالُوا لَوْلَا أُنْزِلَ عَلَيْهِ مَلَكٌ وَلَوْ أَنْزَلْنَا مَلَكًا لَقُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ ثُمَّ لَا يُنْظَرُونَ”

“They said: Why has an angel not been sent down to him? Had We sent down an angel, the matter would have been decided; then they would have been granted no respite.”

The expression “la-qudiya al-amr” is directly connected with “wa-qudiya al-amr” in the verse under discussion. Likewise, “la yunzarun” speaks of not being granted respite, whereas “yanzurun” in Al-Baqarah 2:210 refers to waiting.

Thus, the coming of angels does not necessarily offer a new opportunity for faith, inquiry, and reform; it may be the very moment when no time remains.

In Al-Anam 6:158, the Quran also warns that when certain decisive signs arrive, newly professed faith will no longer benefit someone who did not previously believe or earn any good through their faith.

“Zulal,” the plural of “zullah,” means coverings or canopies, and “ghamam” refers to enveloping cloud. Al-Furqan 25:25 also depicts the sky splitting open with cloud and angels descending. These words themselves, however, do not explain how God comes or the physical nature of those clouds.

What matters in the context is the warning about the matter reaching its decisive conclusion and the opportunity for choice and reform coming to an end.

This warning also holds an important lesson for our religious life today.

Beliefs about the appearance or return of saviors have become widespread among followers of different religious laws and denominations. Many Sunnis speak of the appearance of the Mahdi and the return of Jesus; in Twelver Shiism, awaiting the appearance of the twelfth Imam has an important place; many Christians await Jesus’ return, and Jewish tradition also includes an expectation of the Messiah.

In these beliefs, the establishment of justice, the defeat of oppressors, and the end of corruption in the world are sometimes understood to depend upon the appearance or return of these figures.

Yet nowhere does the Quran command people to cease their efforts against oppression, corruption, and destruction and wait for the appearance of the Mahdi, the return of Jesus, or the coming of another savior.

In Ar-Rad 13:11, a people’s change of circumstances is connected with changing what is within themselves. An-Najm 53:39 emphasizes a person’s own striving, and in Al-Baqarah 2:148, God commands people to compete in good deeds.

Even concerning Muhammad, in Aal Imran 3:144, God asks the believers whether they would turn back if he died or were killed. Remaining steadfast in truth and fulfilling divine responsibility must therefore not depend upon a prophet’s enduring presence.

The Quran speaks of Jesus and his standing, but it has not issued a clear command postponing the reform of the world and the establishment of justice until his return. Nor does the Quran contain such a command concerning the appearance of the Mahdi with the characteristics known in religious denominations.

Someone may respond: We are not merely waiting for the appearance; we are preparing the way for it.

But preparing the way for what?

If this means striving for justice, educating people, fighting corruption, defending the oppressed, and reforming society, these are responsibilities that the Quran places upon people today. Why should we regard them solely as preparations for someone else’s appearance?

In which verse has God said that these deeds are conditions for a savior’s appearance, or that establishing justice must remain unfinished until he comes?

If that appearance does not occur during our lifetime, does our responsibility to reform society end? If the answer is no, this responsibility is independent of that appearance and must be fulfilled now.

If someone can remove an injustice today, they have no right to leave it in place in the hope of future reform. If they can build a school, help an oppressed person, defend a human right, or reduce corruption, that effort is valuable in itself, whether or not a savior appears in the future.

Nor is every act called “preparing the way for the appearance” necessarily good and just. In Al-Baqarah 2:204, Al-Baqarah 2:205, and Al-Baqarah 2:206, we learned that appealing speech and claims of goodwill must be measured against conduct and its actual results.

If someone spreads war and corruption, violates people’s rights, or presents oppression as a necessary prelude to salvation in the name of preparing for that appearance, the sacred name of the goal cannot turn wrongful conduct into righteous deeds.

It is true that belief in a savior may encourage some people to strive for justice. But the value of that effort comes from the right and good it accomplishes, not from an unseen relationship with a future appearance for which no clear Quranic evidence has been provided.

God has not called people to watch the world’s corruption and await a rescuer who will assume their responsibility. He has called them to understanding, mindfulness, justice, effort, and reform.

A fuller discussion of awaiting the Mahdi, Jesus’ return, and the consequences of end-times beliefs appears in the separate study “Human Haste in the Light of the Quran”.

At the end of the verse, He says:

“وَإِلَى اللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ الْأُمُورُ”

“And all affairs are returned to The God.”

“Turja‘u,” from the root R J ʿ, means being returned. This expression reminds us that all affairs ultimately return to God. Final judgment and decision belong to Him, not to any leader, government, or savior to whom people might wish to hand over ultimate authority and their own responsibility.

Do those waiting for God and the angels to come and settle the matter realize that this may no longer be the time to begin acting, but the end of their allotted time and the moment of reckoning?

The Quran has not called people to wait for others to fulfill their responsibilities. Those who desire justice must be just today; those who desire peace must work for peace today; and those who hope for a reformer to appear must themselves bring reform to the extent of their ability.

No hope for the future replaces a person’s responsibility today. Ultimately, all affairs are returned to The God.
