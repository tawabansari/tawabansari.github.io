Al-Baqarah 2:264

ۖ ۖ ۗ

O you who have believed, do not invalidate your charities through reproach and injury, like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in The God and the Last Day. His example is like a smooth rock with soil upon it: a heavy rain strikes it and leaves it hard and bare. They have no power over anything of what they have earned; and The God does not guide the disbelieving people.

Our Reflection

In the two preceding verses, Al-Baqarah 2:262 and Al-Baqarah 2:263, the Quran spoke of giving without reproach or injury and even judged decent speech and forgiveness better than charity followed by injury.

Now it addresses believers directly and warns of the consequences of reproach and injury:

“لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُمْ بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ”

“Do not invalidate your charities through reproach and injury.”

“Charity,” ṣadaqah, belongs to the root family Ṣ D Q, which is connected with truth and sincerity. Giving in God’s way can be a sign of sincere faith, but the name charity alone does not guarantee the giver’s purity of intention or right conduct.

“تُبْطِلُوا,” from the root B Ṭ L, refers to invalidating something and removing its validity and value. The Quran does not say merely that charity loses some of its value; it warns that a person can invalidate their charity through their conduct.

In this context, “مَنّ” means flaunting a gift and making the recipient feel indebted. “أَذَىٰ” refers to suffering and harm that can arise through speech, conduct, humiliation, or pressure on another person.

These concepts are close, but not identical. A person may hurt the recipient through humiliating behavior without directly recalling their help, or continually flaunt past kindness and expect obedience and repayment.

For example, someone helps a person of limited means and makes education, growth, or a better life possible for them. Years later, the benefactor makes a request that conflicts with that person’s wishes and decision. When met with refusal, the benefactor reproaches them: “Have you forgotten who helped you and brought you this far?”

Here, past kindness has become a means of imposing personal demands.

We may also give someone charity or a gift expecting repayment, then flaunt our past kindness when they later fail to act as we expected.

The giver has given away their wealth, not purchased another human being’s freedom and personality.

The purpose of kindness should be to free people from deprivation and need, not trap them in a new dependence and obedience.

This principle is also highly significant in social relationships and in the treatment of migrants and refugees.

A country may use public funds, raised through taxation and the fruits of people’s labor, to provide migrants with living resources, education, and security. But if the government, institutions, or media continually flaunt this assistance, portray migrants as burdens and inferior people, or expect unconditional obedience and gratitude in return, they have accompanied collective kindness with reproach and injury.

This conduct is not limited to government and public institutions. Even an individual citizen may ruin the ethical value of their share in that collective good by taunting and humiliating a migrant.

For example, someone who has contributed to public funds through work and taxes deprives themselves of the reward of their share in that social service if they tell a migrant, “You live off our money and hard work,” and use those words to humiliate them.

Public funds arise from people’s contributions, but contributing gives no one the right to use them to reproach another person. Helping a migrant must not turn into a claim of ownership over their personality, reputation, and future.

This social application is also connected with the principle of human dignity and the principle of providing refuge and safe passage.

Even if the assistance genuinely met a need, reproach and injury can erase the value of that very act before God. We must therefore distinguish between the benefit received by the recipient and the reward expected by the giver.

The Quran then presents another example to clarify this truth:

“كَالَّذِي يُنْفِقُ مَالَهُ رِئَاءَ النَّاسِ وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ”

“Like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in The God and the Last Day.”

“رِئَاءَ” belongs to the root family R A Y and relates to seeing and being seen. In this usage, a person performs an act to appear generous, charitable, or worthy of praise in people’s eyes.

Someone may give considerable wealth, establish a charity, or pay for patients’ treatment, while their main purpose is to gain fame, influence, and people’s praise.

Of course, giving openly is not in itself ostentation. In Al-Baqarah 2:271, the Quran also regards open charity as good, and in Al-Baqarah 2:274 it mentions giving openly and secretly together. The issue is making people’s attention and praise the principal purpose of one’s action.

In this verse, the ostentatious person is described with “وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ.” Someone who does not believe in God and the Day of Account, and whose aim is solely to attract people’s attention, seeks the reward for their action in that same worldly fame and praise.

Nevertheless, comparing a person who reproaches others over a gift with such a person does not mean that every believer who commits reproach and injury necessarily lacks faith. Rather, the Quran warns that such giving becomes fruitless.

God then explains this truth through a clear parable:

“فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌ فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا”

“His example is like a smooth rock with soil upon it: a heavy rain strikes it and leaves it hard and bare.”

“صَفْوَان” refers to smooth rock, “وَابِل” is heavy, intense rain, and “صَلْد” in this image describes hard, bare rock from whose surface the soil has been washed away.

Imagine a smooth rock with a layer of soil resting upon it. That soil may appear to be a bed for growth, but beneath it lies hard rock in which a plant’s roots cannot take hold.

When heavy rain falls, it washes the soil off the rock and carries it away. What seemed ready for growth is now a smooth, bare rock.

This parable reveals the difference between an action’s good appearance and its reality. Ostentatious giving may seem great and valuable to people, but when its reality and result become apparent, it is like a thin layer of soil resting on rock and washed away by rain.

A subtle point is that rain is not inherently a cause of destruction. The same rain that can make fertile land fruitful washes away the surface soil on smooth rock. The difference lies in the ground on which it falls.

This contrast becomes clearer in Al-Baqarah 2:265, where a garden on high ground yields twice its fruit when that same heavy rain reaches it.

The Quran then says:

“لَا يَقْدِرُونَ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا”

“They have no power over anything of what they have earned.”

Here, the wording shifts from the example of one person back to the plural and extends the outcome to those whose actions have this character.

“كَسَبُوا,” from the root K S B, relates to acquiring and obtaining. In this context, it concerns the yield of actions people have performed, yet in the end they possess none of their true fruit.

Charity may have a positive material effect on the recipient’s life, while the giver destroys their own reward and the value of their action through ostentation, reproach, and injury. Meeting another person’s need does not by itself guarantee that the giver has also received its reward.

The verse ends with this warning:

“وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْكَافِرِينَ”

“And The God does not guide the disbelieving people.”

This ending connects giving with faith and disbelief. In this same verse, the Quran speaks of someone who does not believe in God and the Last Day and spends wealth to be seen by people.

Someone who sets aside truth and God’s pleasure and makes people’s praise and personal interests the aim of their action moves away from the path of guidance.

This warning does not, however, mean that the way to repentance and reform is closed; elsewhere the Quran describes the possibility of returning to God.

The message of this verse is clear: giving is not measured by the amount of wealth alone; a person’s intention and conduct after giving are also decisive. Giving that becomes a means of ostentation, reproach, and injury is like soil on a smooth rock, washed away by heavy rain until nothing remains.
