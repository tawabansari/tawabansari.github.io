Al-Baqarah 2:114

ۚ ۚ

And who is more unjust than someone who prevented the name of The God from being remembered in His places of worship and strove for their destruction? It is not for them to enter them except in fear. For them is disgrace in this world, and in the hereafter a great punishment.

Our Reflection

The preceding verses criticized exclusive claims to salvation and the reciprocal dismissal of religious communities. Now the discussion turns to a practical consequence of that exclusivism: preventing remembrance of The God in places of worship and striving to destroy them.

The verse opens with a weighty question about this conduct:

“Who is more unjust?”

Notice that before mentioning destruction, the verse speaks of preventing remembrance of His name:

“Prevented the name of The God from being remembered in His places of worship.”

Injustice therefore does not begin only when walls collapse. When someone places themselves between another person and remembrance of The God, part of this injustice has already occurred. A further stage follows:

“And strove for their destruction.”

“Destruction” should not be detached from the actual meaning of physical ruin. Yet the verse’s structure shows that demolishing a building is not the only way to violate a place of worship. Preventing its primary function—the remembrance of the name of The God—has already been condemned separately.

Whose Are “The Places of Worship of The God”?

The expression itself is telling. The Qur’an does not attribute these places to a people, government, or sect. It attributes them directly to The God.

Al-Jinn 72:18 states the same point more explicitly:

“The places of worship belong to The God, so do not invoke anyone alongside Him.”

Someone may be responsible for managing or maintaining a place of worship, but that responsibility does not make them the owner of people’s relationship with God. By saying that these places are “His,” the Qur’an makes their ultimate belonging clear.

Nor should “the places of worship of The God” in this verse be restricted without justification to buildings that later Muslim usage came to call mosques. The Arabic word for a mosque or place of prostration (masjid) comes from S J D and fundamentally denotes a place of prostration.

Al-Hajj 22:40 is particularly illuminating here:

“Monasteries, churches, synagogues, and places of prostration in which the name of The God is frequently remembered would have been demolished.” The passage explains that this would occur were people not checked by means of one another.

The Qur’an places houses of worship bearing different names alongside one another and identifies remembrance of the name of God as their common feature.

This does not mean that the Qur’an calls all these places by the word “mosque,” or endorses every belief expressed within them. The clearer point is that the sanctity of a place where God is remembered is not confined to one sect’s place of worship.

The same criterion stands at the center of Al-Baqarah 2:114:

“That His name be remembered in them.”

Even the Sacred Mosque Is “For the People”

Surah Al-Hajj says of the Sacred Mosque itself:

“The Sacred Mosque, which We made for the people, with the resident and the visitor from outside alike.” Al-Hajj 22:25.

Even concerning the Sacred Mosque, the Qur’an’s language is “for the people,” not the exclusive property of a people, family, or religious class. Someone may be responsible for managing a place, but that responsibility does not entitle them to regard themselves as the spiritual owner of the house of The God or the gatekeeper of people’s relationship with Him.

“They Should Enter Them Only in Fear”

The position of those who obstruct and destroy is then reversed:

“It is not for them to enter them except in fear.”

The verse does not explain precisely what kind of fear this is, so we should not impose more upon it than the wording provides. But the contrast is clear: those who entered the sphere of others’ worship through power and obstruction are not, by the verse’s conclusion, entitled to enter at ease and in control. Their entry is described as fearful.

The ending is also severe:

“For them is disgrace in this world, and for them in the hereafter is a great punishment.”

This severity shows that preventing remembrance of The God in places of worship is no minor matter in the Qur’an’s view.

From Monopolizing a Place of Worship to Monopolizing Direction

The connection with the next verse should not be overlooked. Immediately after speaking of those who block access to a place of worship, we read:

“To The God belong the east and the west.” Al-Baqarah 2:115.

We are not yet entering into the interpretation of verse 115, but the proximity of the two verses matters: a person can shut the door of a place of worship, but cannot confine The God within that building. Just as a place of worship is not a sect’s absolute property, neither east nor west belongs exclusively to any group.

A Mirror for Muslims

If this verse is read only as an account of other people’s wrongdoing, its warning to us is lost. A Muslim, too, can regard a mosque as their sect’s property, bar another Muslim from worship because of a different school or interpretation, or treat a church, synagogue, or another place of worship as unworthy of respect merely because it is “not ours.” The same progression as in the preceding verses is then repeated: first truth is confined to one religious identity; then the other is said to “stand on nothing”; finally, even that person’s right to remember The God is restricted.

For a broader examination of this malady, see Paradise Is Not the Monopoly of One Sect or One Religious Law. For the distinction between the truth of religion and historical forms of religious law, see Religion, Surrender, and Religious Law in the Qur’an.

If a place of worship truly belongs to The God, no sect can regard itself as the owner of that God or as possessing His servants’ exclusive route to Him.
