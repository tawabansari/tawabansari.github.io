Al-Baqarah 2:89

ۚ

And when a Book came to them from The God, confirming what was with them—while previously they had sought an opening and victory against those who had rejected truth—then, when what they recognized came to them, they rejected it. So The God’s exclusion from mercy is upon those who reject truth.

Our Reflection

This verse speaks of people for whom what arrived was not wholly foreign. A Book came from the one true God, “confirming what was with them.” The verse then goes further: when “what they recognized” came to them, they rejected it. The issue was therefore not simply a lack of information or encountering an unfamiliar statement. Something in the new message was recognizable to them.

Confirming (muṣaddiq), from the root Ṣ D Q, concerns acknowledging truth and affirming it. But confirming what was with them does not mean that every belief, interpretation, custom, and addition accumulated around their inheritance over time was also endorsed. The new Book confirmed the truth from The God that remained among them. There is a great difference between confirming earlier truth and endorsing everything human beings later built around that truth.

This distinction can be one of the most difficult tests of religious commitment. A person may expect a new message not only to confirm the original truth they possess, but also to leave untouched all the inherited interpretations, boundaries, and privileges that generations have built around it. When that does not happen, something familiar and recognizable in one respect becomes unwelcome in another.

The verse then says: “Previously, they had sought an opening and victory against those who rejected truth.” Seeking an opening (yastaftiḥūna) comes from the root F T Ḥ, the field of opening, conquest, adjudication, and victory. Before this Book arrived, they sought an opening against a group whom the Qur’an describes as “those who rejected truth.”

Many commentators have read this passage as meaning that a group among the People of the Book knew of a coming messenger and, before his appearance, regarded his arrival as a means of attaining victory over their opponents. The core of this reading cannot be dismissed simply as an exegetical story, because an important part of it can be supported from the Qur’an itself.

In Al-A‘raf 7:157, the Qur’an speaks of “the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet” whom the people of the Torah and Gospel find “written with them in the Torah and the Gospel.” Thus, the presence of an indication of this messenger in their scriptural inheritance is itself a Qur’anic claim.

In As-Saff 61:6, we also read in the words of Jesus, son of Mary:

“And bringing good news of a messenger who will come after me, whose name is Ahmad.”

That is, Jesus announces a messenger after himself named Ahmad. The basic expectation of a messenger after Jesus can therefore also be drawn from the Qur’an itself.

Al ‘Imran 3:81 reveals another connection. It speaks of a covenant that, if a messenger comes after the giving of scripture and wisdom, “confirming what is with you,” they must believe in him and support him. This wording is very close to Al-Baqarah 2:89: the arrival of a new message or messenger confirming what the addressees possess.

Almost the same construction appears again in Al-Baqarah 2:101:

“And when a messenger came to them from The God, confirming what was with them…”

A messenger comes from the one true God, confirming what is with them, yet a group among the People of the Book casts The God’s Book behind their backs. The connection between a later messenger, confirmation of what they possessed, and a negative response from some of the People of the Book is therefore internal to the Qur’an; it does not rest solely on external historical reports.

Al-Baqarah 2:146 and Al-An‘am 6:20 also contain the expression “they recognize him as they recognize their own sons.” These verses can be considered within this network of prior recognition, although the referent of the pronoun in “they recognize him” must be assessed separately in each verse’s context. It is better to place the main weight of the argument on the clearer verses.

The core of the common reading therefore has Qur’anic support, but this does not endorse every historical detail later added to it. Al-Baqarah 2:89 itself does not say that Jews had come to Arabia to await Muhammad. It does not say that seeking an opening necessarily means asking The God for victory by Muhammad’s name. Nor does it call the opposing group “Arab polytheists.” The Qur’an uses its own precise expression: “those who rejected truth.”

This is no small distinction, because a few words later the same word returns to the addressees themselves in a striking reversal.

First:

“They sought an opening against those who rejected truth.” They sought an opening and victory against people who had rejected truth.

Then:

“When what they recognized came to them, they rejected it.” When what they knew came to them, they themselves rejected it.

And finally:

“So The God’s exclusion from mercy is upon those who reject truth.” Thus the one true God’s exclusion from mercy falls upon those who reject truth.

At first, “those who reject truth” are others: people on the other side of the opposition. But the Qur’an does not allow this description to become the permanent name of the opposing group. When recognized truth comes and these same people cover it and refuse it, the verb “they rejected” is applied to them. At the end, too, exclusion from mercy is not attached to the name of the Children of Israel, Jews, Arabs, or any other people; it is upon “those who reject truth.”

This is one of the verse’s most important lessons about the concept of a rejecter of truth (kāfir). A person can turn this word so thoroughly into another’s identity that they never consider the possibility of acting in rejection of truth themselves. Yet this verse breaks precisely that mental assurance. Those who yesterday saw another on the side of rejection have today themselves “rejected it.”

A rejecter of truth must therefore be understood through a person’s relationship with truth before being treated as an ethnic or religious label. This does not mean calling every intellectual disagreement rejection of truth or judging people’s inward reality from their outward appearance. Rather, this verse itself shows that rejection can be an act committed when confronting recognized truth. The concept is examined more broadly in Who Is a Kafir?.

The expression “what they recognized” is also decisive here. The root ʿ R F concerns knowing and recognizing. The verse does not say that something unknown arrived and they refused it out of ignorance; it says that something they recognized came to them. Recognition was present, but it did not lead to submission.

The verses immediately following this one make the cause clearer than any historical account. In Al-Baqarah 2:90, we read that they rejected what The God had sent down:

“Out of resentment that The God should send down of His bounty upon whomever He wills of His servants.”

The issue is connected to the one true God’s sending His bounty upon whichever of His servants He wills. The problem was therefore not merely that the signs were unrecognizable to them; accepting The God’s choice had also become difficult. It is as though a person had determined for themselves through which channel, upon which group, and according to which expectation divine bounty must descend.

Verse 91 makes this sickness clearer. When they are told to believe in what The God has sent down, they say:

“We believe in what was sent down to us.” We believe in what was revealed to our own community.

The Qur’an then says that they reject what comes after it, even though it is the truth, confirming what is with them.

The issue here is no longer simply recognizing a future messenger. The larger problem is turning truth into the property of one’s collective identity. A person wants The God to speak, but prefers that He speak from the place they expect; they want guidance to come, but would like it to confirm their ethnic, religious, and intellectual boundaries as well.

Someone may truly be waiting for truth. But what they call “waiting for truth” may actually be waiting for confirmation of themselves. As long as the new message says what they already wanted to hear, there is no conflict. The test begins when truth is familiar and confirms some of what they rightly possess, yet at the same time does not endorse the things they have added to it or the privileges they have claimed for themselves.

Here, the history recounted by the Qur’an becomes a mirror for our own time. The account of the People of the Book is not told so that we may observe their error and pass by the verse with a sense of superiority. Anyone whose religious identity is bound to an inheritance is always exposed to the danger of unconsciously expecting The God to endorse that entire inheritance without qualification.

Today we possess the Qur’an. Alongside it, over the centuries, a vast body of hadith, jurisprudence, theology, exegesis, religious customs, and inherited beliefs has emerged. The historical relationship of this situation is not identical to that of the People of the Book: most of this inheritance developed after the Qur’an. For that reason, the direction of assessment is even clearer for us: the Qur’an must not be measured against later inheritance; later inheritance must be measured against the Qur’an.

Where a statement in our inheritance agrees with the Qur’an, that agreement can be recognized. Where the Qur’an is silent on a matter, a human statement must not be made into The God’s definitive word in place of that silence. And if something conflicts with the Qur’an’s clear standard, faithfulness does not mean restricting or reinterpreting the verse until our inherited belief remains untouched.

The real test lies precisely here. When the Qur’an confirms something we already believe, acceptance is not difficult. But if that same Qur’an calls a cherished doctrine, a famous report, an inherited ruling, or a sectarian boundary into question, does the Qur’an still remain the standard?

The tragedy is to speak for years of truth’s victory while actually waiting only for our own confirmation. We may memorize many verses, quote the Qur’an extensively, and still refuse to let the Qur’an itself change our fundamental assumptions. Then the question is no longer whether we possess the Book; it is which judges which: the Book our beliefs, or our beliefs the Book?

“Confirming what was with them” also gains a deeper meaning from this perspective. The new truth had not come to destroy everything that preceded it; it confirmed the truth already present. But confirming earlier truth does not guarantee the survival of every human addition. What is from The God can be affirmed, while what people have built around it can be assessed anew against His standard.

The ending of the verse returns us once again from names to conduct: “So The God’s exclusion from mercy is upon those who reject truth.” As we saw in the preceding verse, divine exclusion is not a spoken curse; it is being cast out and distanced from mercy and guidance, connected within the divine order and its ways to the path a person chooses. For a detailed examination, see Laʿn in the Qur’an: What Exclusion from Divine Mercy Means.

This verse does not allow “rejecter of truth” always to be someone else’s name and “seeker of truth” always to be our own. A person may recognize truth and still refuse it if truth will not appear according to their expectations. A true seeker of truth is willing to correct themselves when truth puts their own beliefs to the test as well.
