Al-Baqarah 2:176

ۗ

That is because The God has sent down the Book in truth; and indeed, those who have disagreed concerning the Book are in deep division and discord.

Our Reflection

How did a Book that God revealed to guide people and resolve their disagreements itself become a subject of disagreement and division? In the preceding verses, the Qur’an spoke of those who conceal revealed truth, sell it for a small price, and choose misguidance instead of guidance and punishment instead of forgiveness. Now it explains the gravity of this conduct: “ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّ اللَّهَ نَزَّلَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ”. What they concealed or traded away was not a human being’s speech, but truth that God revealed in His Book.

In Al-Baqarah 2:213, God speaks of sending prophets and revealing the Book in truth to judge between people concerning their disagreements. Yet the same verse also describes another situation: those who received the Book disagreed about it after clear proofs had come to them, because of transgression among themselves. This contrast invites reflection. The Book that should serve as the authority for judgment between people becomes a subject of disagreement and conflict among its recipients. It is as though, instead of weighing their beliefs and desires against revealed truth, people try to make that truth conform to their own desires and beliefs.

In Ali Imran 3:19 and Ash-Shura 42:14, the Qur’an likewise connects disagreement after knowledge has come with transgression among people. Fussilat 41:45 also speaks of disagreement concerning the Book given to Moses. Access to the Book and knowledge of truth therefore do not, by themselves, protect a person against conflict and misguidance. Someone may know the Book yet refuse to accept its clear truth in order to preserve an advantage, a position, or inherited beliefs.

“اخْتَلَفُوا فِي الْكِتَابِ” means disagreeing concerning the Book. Nevertheless, not every difference in understanding the Book can be equated with concealment of truth or transgression. Two people may sincerely study a verse and reach different interpretations. This differs from the conduct of those who, after truth has become clear, knowingly conceal it or persist in opposition to preserve their superiority and influence. What makes disagreement dangerous is not merely having different opinions, but turning disagreement into prejudice, hostility, and defiance of truth that has come to guide humanity.

Muslim history also contains many examples of Qur’anic verses becoming a field of religious dispute. God speaks about ablution in Al-Maidah 5:6, yet one longstanding disagreement between schools concerns washing or wiping the feet. One group says the feet must be washed; another understands wiping them to be the verse’s command. Linguistic arguments, Qur’anic readings, and transmitted reports have been offered for both positions. Examining this evidence can itself be legitimate research. But when each group declares the other’s practice invalid and makes this difference a sign of religious superiority, we must ask how a command concerning cleanliness and preparation for worship became a cause of division among Muslims.

Another example is the Qur’an’s inheritance rulings. In An-Nisa 4:11, An-Nisa 4:12, and An-Nisa 4:176, God states shares for heirs, and at the end of An-Nisa 4:176 He says that He explains matters to people so that they do not go astray. Nevertheless, major disagreements arose over these very rulings in the history of jurisprudence. If someone dies leaving a daughter and a sister, does the sister also inherit? In juristic accounts, Ibn Abbas and some others allowed no share for the sister when a daughter was present, whereas another well-known position gives the sister the remainder. Schools also reached different conclusions about a grandfather inheriting alongside brothers and sisters: some hold that the grandfather excludes them, while others assign shares to both. These disagreements are not merely theoretical; their outcomes can determine who receives a family’s property after its owner’s death.

A similar dispute concerns bequests. Al-Baqarah 2:180 speaks of making a bequest for parents and relatives, but some jurists regarded its ruling as abrogated by the inheritance verses or on the basis of reports, while others proposed ways to reconcile bequests and inheritance. Disagreement about understanding the Book thus sometimes reached the point where one group regarded a verse’s ruling as still operative and another regarded it as having ended. Such disagreement affects people’s actual rights, especially those of women, children, and other heirs.

The same situation appears in daily worship. Must someone praying behind an imam recite Al-Fatihah? In Shafi‘i jurisprudence, its recitation is considered obligatory for the follower, while Hanafi jurisprudence prohibits recitation behind the imam. Both groups offer evidence from the Qur’an and transmitted reports for their views. Disagreement over an act of worship becomes harmful division when adherents of one school declare others’ worship invalid or worthless without clear evidence and place loyalty to their leader above the search for truth.

Religious disputes in Islamic history sometimes went far beyond disagreement over a ruling. Even Abu Hanifah, later recognized as one of the greatest leaders of Sunni jurisprudence, faced grave accusations in his own time. In the entry on Abu Hanifah in Al-Du‘afa al-Saghir, al-Bukhari transmits a report from Sufyan al-Thawri according to which Abu Hanifah had twice been called to repent over an accusation of unbelief. Al-Bukhari also criticizes Abu Hanifah’s views and narration in Al-Tarikh al-Kabir. Al-Bukhari’s transmission of that accusation is, of course, not the same as his directly issuing a declaration of unbelief himself. Yet the presence of such reports in the works of prominent scholars shows how fierce and severe religious disputes could become. A man whom millions of Muslims today regard as an imam and juristic leader once faced accusations as serious as unbelief from some other scholars.

Another historical example is the Abbasid ordeal over the “createdness of the Qur’an.” Disagreement over whether the Qur’an was created or uncreated led to tests of scholars’ beliefs and intervention by political power. Ahmad ibn Hanbal, another major Sunni juristic leader, was imprisoned and punished for opposing the doctrine the government sought to impose on scholars. Disagreement concerning a Book that should guide people toward truth and justice became, in that period, a means of testing and punishing those who held different beliefs.

These examples raise a fundamental question: where do such disagreements originate? Some arise from real differences in understanding language, readings, the context of verses, and methods of reasoning. These differences should be examined through research and dialogue and are not, in themselves, evidence of corruption or misguidance. But when conflicting reports, leaders’ legal opinions, juristic analogies, and inherited traditions are placed in the position of God’s definitive command, scholarly disagreement can become competition over religious authority. Each group then not only defends its own view, but sometimes declares another view false and its proponent misguided in order to preserve its authority.

The greater danger comes when religious partisanship prevails over scholarly integrity. If an imam’s opinion is made the standard of truth in advance, verses and reports may be gathered and interpreted solely to defend that opinion. Instead of the Book judging human statements, human statements then become the final judge of the Book’s meaning. Such a method departs from the purpose the Qur’an identifies for the Book’s revelation in Al-Baqarah 2:213. God revealed the Book in truth to judge between people concerning their disagreements, not so that its name would become a pretext for entrenching inherited divisions.

This warning does not mean that every jurist or scholar who understands the Qur’an differently has knowingly concealed truth. People with knowledge, diligence, and good intentions may reach different conclusions. Nor is it our responsibility to accuse others of concealment or transgression without knowing their intentions and reasons. The proper standard is for every claim to be presented with the Qur’an’s verses, language, and context, together with clear reasoning that can be examined. Respect for past scholars does not replace asking for evidence, and disagreeing with a scholar does not mean disagreeing with God.

“Shiqaq,” from the root SH Q Q, is connected with splitting and separation, and “ba‘id” conveys the severity of this division. Fussilat 41:52 uses the same expression concerning those who oppose revealed truth. Al-Baqarah 2:176 therefore does not merely report the existence of different opinions; it warns of a deep division arising in people’s relationship with the divine Book. In the context of the preceding verses, concealing truth, seeking personal gain, and exchanging guidance for misguidance are among the behaviors that can lead to this outcome.

For Muslims today, the question is not simply which school they were born into or which jurist offered the more accurate view. The more fundamental question is whether they are willing to reexamine inherited statements and their own beliefs in the light of revealed truth. Can they distinguish God’s ruling from human inference? And in disagreement, can they listen to one another’s evidence and base judgment on the Book and truth rather than declarations of unbelief, humiliation, and division?

The Book was revealed so that people might find their way in the light of truth, not so that they might fashion new banners of hostility toward one another from its verses. No religious label, scholarly reputation, or juristic inheritance should be placed above the truth God has revealed. When people defend the standing of their group and leader instead of truth and close the path of reform to themselves, disagreement moves away from the search for truth and approaches the deep discord against which the Qur’an warns.
