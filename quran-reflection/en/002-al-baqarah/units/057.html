Al-Baqarah 2:57

And We spread the cloud as shade over you (ẓallalnā) and sent down upon you the mann and the salwa; eat from the wholesome things (ṭayyibāt) of what We provided for you. And they did not wrong Us, but they were wronging their own selves.

Our Reflection

This verse comes after the thunderbolt, death, and being raised again. In the previous verse, God raised the Children of Israel after their death so that they might respond with responsible gratitude (shukr). Now in verse 57, the field of gratitude opens before them: the shade of the cloud, provision, mann, salwa, and eating from the wholesome things.

This order is very beautiful. After all those heavy events: the calf, pardon, the Book and the criterion, repentance, the thunderbolt, death, and being raised; the Qur'an brings them back again to earth, shade, food, wholesomeness, and daily life. It is as if after those shaking blows, the time had come for them to learn gratitude not only through miracle, but through ordinary life.

In verse 56, they were raised again so they might give thanks. In verse 57, God places the means of gratitude before them. This shows that gratitude is not only in response to great rescues and extraordinary events. Gratitude also becomes visible in the right use of daily provision: in the bite of food, in shade, in safety, in calm, and in the human being placing ni'mah in its proper use.

Wa-ẓallalnā ʿalaykumu al-ghamām means: We spread the cloud as shade over you. A people who had come out from under the heavy shadow of Pharaoh are now placed under the shade of God's mercy and care. Earlier, the shadow over their life was the shadow of slavery, humiliation, and fear. Now the cloud becomes a sign of covering, the coolness of mercy, and divine protection on their desert path.

The word ghamām is also subtle. Its root is connected with covering, and from the same root comes ghamm, grief, because grief covers the heart and casts a shadow over the soul. But in this verse, this same covering appears as mercy. What can darken the heart becomes, in God's care, a shade of calm. So the covering of the cloud is not the darkness of grief; it is a covering that softens the harshness of the desert sun.

This point is also harmonious with the previous verses. In verse 55, they wanted to see God jahratan, openly, and the thunderbolt seized them. There, a kind of hard covering and seizing took place. In this verse, a covering from God comes again, but this time for mercy, not for crushing. It is as if the same Lord who can break the pride of the senses through the thunderbolt can also calm the tired human psyche through the cloud.

This shade should not necessarily be understood as a legendary image detached from the order of nature. The Qur'an does not need to explain the natural details of this event. What matters is that what happened along their path of life is remembered in the language of the Qur'an as ni'mah and divine care. The cloud, whether in the ordinary course of nature or in a special condition for them, is in either case a sign of God's provision and mercy.

Then He says: wa-anzalnā ʿalaykumu al-manna wa-al-salwā. Mann and salwa were sent down upon them. The Qur'an does not enter here into a precise material explanation of these two forms of provision, because the purpose of the verse is not food science; its purpose is remembering ni'mah. In the desert, after leaving the Pharaonic system, they encountered provision that answered the need of their bodies and the calm of their souls.

Here we must also consider the psychological condition of such a people. The Children of Israel had not only come out of a land; they had come out of generations of fear, slavery, anxiety, and insecurity. The enslaved human being, even when freed, still fights inside himself against fear of hunger, homelessness, and the future. Such a human being must slowly learn that provision does not come only from the hand of the master, Pharaoh, the palace, and the system of slavery.

From this angle, mann and salwa were not only food; they were part of their training. God provided for them in the desert, where there were neither the palaces of Egypt nor the tables of Pharaoh, so they would understand that the true Provider is living. They had to learn that the security of provision is not in returning to slavery; it is in trusting God's lordly care.

Mann in Qur'anic language is also connected with ni'mah, gift, and generosity: a ni'mah that brings the human being out of helplessness. Salwā is also close to calm, consolation, and the settling of anxiety. So these two provisions should not be limited only to food. They were a sign of nourishment for the body and calming of the soul: a gift for a people who had passed through captivity, fear, the calf, the thunderbolt, and collapse, and still had to learn gratitude.

Then the command comes: kulū min ṭayyibāti mā razaqnākum. Eat from the wholesome things of what We provided for you. This short sentence is very important. The Qur'an does not simply say eat; it says eat from the wholesome things. This means the issue is not only consuming provision, but the human being's relationship with provision.

Ṭayyibāt means wholesome things: pure, good, healthy, pleasant, that which is harmonious with the health of body, self, and path. Many forms of religiosity later focused more on outward permitted and forbidden, or on formal signs of food. But the Qur'an repeatedly speaks of ṭayyib. Something may be considered formally permitted, yet not be wholesome: it may be polluted, unhealthy, produced through wrongdoing, heavy upon the human self, or destructive to society through corruption and disease.

So ṭayyib is not merely a legal label. Ṭayyib is a quality that carries purity, health, goodness, and harmony with human growth. Provision reaches gratitude when the human being consumes it in a pure, right, balanced, and proper way. Eating from the wholesome things means receiving God's provision without pollution, excess, ingratitude, and wrongdoing.

Here the background of verse 61 can already be seen from a distance, where they later complain about a single kind of food and ask for lentils, onions, garlic, and more earthbound foods. This shows that a mind accustomed to slavery can grow tired even of wholesome provision if gratitude does not form within it. Sometimes the human being is free, but the taste of slavery still remains in his mouth. Sometimes he grows tired of God's wholesome things and again longs for foods tied to humiliation.

In this verse, the contrast between gratitude and wrongdoing becomes visible again. In verse 52, after pardon, the purpose was laʿallakum tashkurūn. In verse 56, after being raised again, the purpose was again laʿallakum tashkurūn. Now in verse 57, concrete and daily ni'mahs are given, but the end of the verse says: wa-mā ẓalamūnā wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn. They did not wrong Us; rather, they were wronging their own selves.

This means that when ni'mah does not arrive at gratitude, it turns into wrongdoing against the self. God is not harmed by human ingratitude. God's providing does not decrease, God's lordly care does not collapse, and God's greatness is not diminished by human covering of ni'mah. But the human being, by misusing ni'mah, ruins himself: his body, his self, his society, and his path.

Wa-mā ẓalamūnā, they did not wrong Us, expresses a great Qur'anic principle: whenever the human being misuses God's ni'mah, he does not harm God; he wrongs himself. Human sin does not scratch God, but it darkens the mirror of the human soul. God is free of need. If the human being takes provision and does not give thanks, if he receives shade and still becomes ungrateful, if he eats wholesome things and still follows the path of corruption, in reality he has used ni'mah against his own self.

The shift in tone at the end of the verse is also worth noticing. In the previous verses, the addresses were mostly direct: you did this, this happened upon you, We raised you. But at the end of this verse, the speech moves to “they”: wa-mā ẓalamūnā wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn. This distancing of expression is itself a kind of warning. When the human being abandons gratitude after ni'mah and continues wronging the self, he moves away from the warmth of direct address and becomes a subject of historical lesson.

Wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn takes us back again to verse 54: innakum ẓalamtum anfusakum bi-ittikhādhikum al-ʿijl. There, by taking the calf, they wronged their own selves. Here too, by ingratitude before provision and ni'mah, they again wrong their own selves. So wrongdoing had not happened only once; it was a repeated pattern in their behavior. Each time ni'mah came, the test of gratitude also came; and each time they did not give thanks, wronging the self returned.

In this verse, God again gives the Children of Israel favor and opportunity. After all those failures, they are not removed. After the thunderbolt and death, they are raised again. After being raised, they are placed under the shade of the cloud. After shade, they receive provision. After provision, they are invited to eat from the wholesome things. This is a path of training; God gives opportunity so that gratitude may take shape in their souls.

But this same opportunity also carries responsibility. Ni'mah, if not understood rightly, does not reform the human being. Even abundant ni'mah can turn into entitlement, excess, laziness, ingratitude, and wrongdoing. The Children of Israel had to understand that God's provision is for building a grateful self, not for returning to ingratitude.

From this angle, verse 57 brings us back from miracle-demanding to daily life. The human being sometimes imagines that faith is tested only in the thunderbolt, the splitting of the sea, or seeing great signs. But the Qur'an shows that faith is also tested in eating: what you eat, how you eat, where you eat from, and whether you turn provision into gratitude or into wrongdoing.

This verse is also very important for today. People speak often about permitted and forbidden, but ask less about ṭayyib. Is the food we eat wholesome? Does it harm the body? Was it obtained through wrongdoing and exploitation? Does it make the self heavy and sick? Does it push society toward corruption, excess, and cruelty? With the word ṭayyibāt, the Qur'an takes us to this deeper level.

As a result, verse 57 continues the same lesson of gratitude. After raising them again, God brought them under shade and provision, and said: eat from the wholesome things of My provision. But if the human being does not carry this ni'mah to gratitude, he has not wronged God; he has wronged himself. God's ni'mah was a path of growth, but human ingratitude turns it into a path of collapse.

Cross-References
  • Al-Baqarah 2:52— Pardon after calf-worship with the purpose of gratitude; verse 57 shows that ni'mah and provision must also arrive at gratitude.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:54— Wronging the self by taking the calf; verse 57 again says they did not wrong God, but were wronging their own selves.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:56— Being raised again after death with the purpose of gratitude; verse 57 shows the practical field of gratitude in shade, provision, and wholesome things.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:61— Requesting other foods after mann and salwa; showing that an ungrateful mind can complain even about wholesome provision and sometimes prefer familiar humiliation to grateful freedom.
  • Al-A'raf 7:160— The cloud being spread as shade and the descent of mann and salwa in another Qur'anic account of the Children of Israel; a direct witness for these ni'mahs.
  • Taha 20:80— Rescuing the Children of Israel from their enemy, the promise at the side of Tur, and the descent of mann and salwa; connecting the ni'mah of provision with rescue and guidance.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:168— The command to eat from what is lawful and wholesome on the earth; an important witness that wholesomeness matters alongside permissibility.
  • Al-Ma'idah 5:88— Eat from what God has provided for you, lawful and wholesome; connecting provision, wholesomeness, and taqwa.
  • An-Nahl 16:114— Eat from what God has provided you, lawful and wholesome, and give thanks for God's ni'mah; the closest link between wholesome provision and gratitude.
  • Ibrahim 14:7— If you give thanks, I will surely increase you; and if you are ungrateful, My punishment is severe. An important witness for the relation between ni'mah, gratitude, and ingratitude.
  • Luqman 31:12— Whoever gives thanks gives thanks for himself; and whoever is ungrateful, God is free of need and praiseworthy. A clear witness for “they did not wrong Us, but were wronging their own selves.”
  • Az-Zumar 39:7— If you commit kufr, God is free of need from you; and if you give thanks, He approves it for you. Useful for understanding that ingratitude does not harm God.
  • Fatir 35:15— You are the ones in need of God, and God is free of need, praiseworthy; a witness for God's freedom from human gratitude or ingratitude.
