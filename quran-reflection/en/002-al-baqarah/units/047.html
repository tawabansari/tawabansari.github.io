Al-Baqarah 2:47

O Children of Israel, remember My favor and blessing (ni'mah) that I bestowed upon you, and that I gave you added grace and distinction (fadl) over the worlds.

Our Reflection

This verse comes after the definition of the khashi'in. In verse 46, the khashi'in were described as those who keep the meeting with the Lord and return to Him present in the calculation of their lives. Now the Qur'an addresses the Children of Israel again and calls them to remember ni'mah and fadl. This connection is not accidental. Whoever has received more ni'mah also carries a heavier return. The clearer the divine gift, the greater the human responsibility before it.

The address ya bani Isra'il repeats the address of verse 40, but here it comes after the discussion of khushu', sabr, salat, meeting, and return. This means the Children of Israel must read their ni'mah and fadl within the horizon of return to the Lord, not within the horizon of ethnic pride. If the khashi' is one who knows that everything returns to God, the bearer of Book and ni'mah must also know that Book, knowledge, history, rescue, prophets, and special position will also be returned to the same Lord.

The word udhkurū is very important. Dhikr is not merely mental recollection of the past. It is making a truth present in awareness and action. The Qur'an does not say: preserve the ni'mah as historical pride. It says: remember the ni'mah. That is, reread the divine inputs in your history: what was given to you? From where did it come? For what was it given? And what have you done with it now?

Ni'mati, in this context, does not refer only to ordinary and material favors, even though those too are from God. Here the discussion is about a historical and guiding ni'mah: rescue from Pharaoh, Book, prophets, clear signs, teaching, judgment, and the opportunity to stand in the path of tawhid. If such a ni'mah is remembered rightly, it draws the human being toward shukr and fulfilling covenant. If it is forgotten, it becomes closed identity, pride, concealment of truth, and bargaining with the signs.

The phrase an'amtu 'alaykum shows that the doer of the ni'mah is God. The Children of Israel were not the inherent producers of this ni'mah. The favor did not spring from their blood, race, or ethnic essence; it was placed upon them. This 'alaykum, upon you, is also subtle: the ni'mah came upon them, but it also became a burden upon them. Divine giving is not only privilege; it is also accountability.

The key sentence of the verse is wa-anni faddaltukum 'ala al-'alamin. The root F Ḍ L in Arabic is tied to increase, surplus, remainder beyond the normal measure, and extra giving. So faddaltukum means that God gave the Children of Israel a special added measure. Their vessel was filled more fully with signs, Book, prophets, rescue, teaching, and responsibility. This increase was not ornamental privilege or biological superiority. It was greater capacity for carrying guidance.

From here it becomes clear that fadl in the Qur'an is not the same as race. If we turn fadl into blood and lineage, we damage the meaning of the root and the logic of the verses. Fadl means an added gift, something beyond the ordinary level that a person or community receives. But every increase brings increased accounting. A vessel that has been filled more fully becomes heavier if it is not used rightly. A community that has seen more signs carries heavier responsibility if it covers truth.

For a more precise understanding of this meaning, Yusuf's speech in prison is a key. Before interpreting the dream, Yusuf clarifies the source of his knowledge: dhalikuma mimma 'allamani rabbi, this is from what my Rabb taught me. Then he explains the path of this gift: he has left the millah of a people who do not have faith in God and are kafir toward the Hereafter, and he has followed the millah of his fathers Ibrahim, Ishaq, and Ya'qub. Then he says: ma kana lana an nushrika billahi min shay'; dhalika min fadlillahi 'alayna wa 'ala an-nas, walakinna akthara an-nasi la yashkurun.

In this statement, Yusuf connects fadl with several clear elements: teaching from the Rabb, leaving the path of kufr, following the tawhidic millah, avoiding every kind of shirk, and shukr. This definition separates fadl from blood and family and connects it to tawhid, guidance, knowledge, responsibility, and shukr. Even when he says 'alayna, upon us, he immediately says wa 'ala an-nas, and upon people. God's fadl is not originally imprisoned inside one people; but sometimes it appears in one family or society in a clearer and more responsibility-bearing way.

From this angle, the fadl of the Children of Israel means that, in one historical stage, they received a greater capacity of guidance. Prophets came among them, Book was given to them, they were rescued from Pharaoh, they saw clear signs, and a greater trust was placed upon them. This fadl did not mean they became owners of truth; it meant truth was entrusted to them with greater intensity.

The connection of verse 47 with verse 46 becomes clear here. Verse 46 said that the khashi'in are those who know that they will meet their Lord and return to Him. Verse 47 says that the Children of Israel must remember their ni'mah and fadl within that same horizon of return. What was given to them does not stop with them. Book, ni'mah, knowledge, rescue, prophets, and the opportunity for guidance all finally return to the Lord, and in that return their treatment of this gift is weighed.

If someone has received less ni'mah, his accounting is according to what he received. But one who has recited the Book, whose history contains prophets, who has seen divine rescue, and who has benefited from clear signs, cannot treat himself like one to whom no sign has come. More fadl means a clearer field; and a clearer field means more precise responsibility.

So faddaltukum 'ala al-'alamin must not be understood as absolute racial superiority, guaranteed salvation, or special ethnic right. Al-'alamin comes from the root ʿ L M, tied to sign, knowing, and sign-marked worlds. In this verse, it may be understood as the human groupings and known worlds within that historical field. The Children of Israel, in that field, received a special place in the volume of signs, Book, prophets, and experience of guidance. This was fadl, but responsibility-making fadl, not pride-making fadl.

The same logic appears in 45:16, where the Qur'an says that the Children of Israel were given Book, judgment, prophethood, pure provision, and fadl over the worlds. This verse explains the content of the fadl: Book, judgment, prophethood, and pure provision. So the fadl of the Children of Israel was in the tools of guidance and responsibility, not in blood or genes.

Musa, too, in Al-Ma'idah 5:20 reminds his people that God placed prophets among them, made them possessors of authority, and gave them what He had not given to any of the worlds. This is the same logic: prophets, social capacity, rescue, and special giving. But immediately after this reminder comes the question of test and responsibility. Fadl opens the path for responsibility, not escape from it.

So verse 47 is a two-edged reminder. On one side, the Qur'an does not deny the historical place of the Children of Israel. They truly carried great favors. On the other side, it reminds them of this very place so they know that ni'mah is not private property. If fadl is not joined to shukr, tawhid, justice, fulfilling covenant, and receiving truth, it becomes proof against the one who possesses fadl.

This meaning is completed by Yusuf's speech. Yusuf shows that fadl means knowledge that comes from the Rabb, a path that separates from shirk, a millah connected to Ibrahim, Ishaq, and Ya'qub, and a shukr that pulls the human being out of imagining ownership. If the Children of Israel understand their fadl in this way, the reminder of the verse returns them to khushu', shukr, and responsibility. But if they turn it into racial superiority, they have moved precisely away from the truth of fadl.

As a result, verse 47 is the natural continuation of the previous verses. After speaking of khushu' and return, the Qur'an says to the Children of Israel: remember what was added upon you. This increase was not for boasting; it was for carrying truth. The more signs, the more accounting. The clearer the Book, the clearer the responsibility. And the greater the fadl, the greater the need for khushu'.
