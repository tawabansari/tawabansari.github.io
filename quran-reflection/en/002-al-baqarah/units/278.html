Al-Baqarah 2:278

O you who have believed, be mindful of The God and relinquish what remains of riba, if you are believers.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:275, the Quran declared riba forbidden, and in Al-Baqarah 2:276, it contrasted the destruction of riba with the growth of charities. In Al-Baqarah 2:277, it also recalled faith and righteous action. Now it addresses believers directly and commands them to relinquish what remains of riba.

“يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ”

“O you who have believed, be mindful of The God.”

An important point is that the Quran does not here address people in general, “النَّاس,” but directs its address to “الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا”: those who have believed.

Faith in the Quran, however, is not the exclusive name of an ethnic group or religious community. In Al-Baqarah 2:62, the Quran speaks of those who believed, Jews, Christians, and Sabians, setting the criterion as faith in The God and the Last Day, and righteous action. The same principle is repeated in Al-Maidah 5:69.

Thus, within the broad Quranic meaning of faith, this address can encompass Jewish and Christian believers as well as followers of the revelation sent down to Muhammad. The criterion is not merely a person’s religious label but their faith and practical fidelity to truth. The command to avoid riba should therefore not be confined to a sectarian identity.

For a detailed examination, see “What Is Faith in the Quran, and How Was Its Meaning Contaminated?” and “The Principle of Faith in The God, the Hereafter, and Righteous Action”.

“اتَّقُوا,” from W Q Y, concerns guarding, care, and self-restraint. Taqwa in the Quran is not merely fear; it is conscious care over one’s conduct and avoidance of what leads to oppression, corruption, and disobedience.

This command is especially important regarding riba, because greed, economic habit, or fear of reduced income may cause a person to continue improper financial relations even after recognizing that they are wrong.

Taqwa becomes apparent when people can stand against even their own personal advantage and relinquish a claim they make contrary to God’s command.

This concept has been present since the beginning of Al-Baqarah. In Al-Baqarah 2:2, the Quran was described as guidance for the God-conscious, and Al-Baqarah 2:3 described faith, establishing salah, and giving as their characteristics.

For a broader understanding, see “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

The Quran then says:

“وَذَرُوا مَا بَقِيَ مِنَ الرِّبَا”

“And relinquish what remains of riba.”

“ذَرُوا,” from W DH R, refers to leaving and relinquishing. “بَقِيَ,” from B Q Y, concerns what remains.

This expression shows that, when the command was given, transactions and claims involving riba already existed, and some remained outstanding. The Quran does not merely speak of refraining from new transactions; it also commands that the remaining riba be relinquished.

This matters for understanding the verse’s historical setting. Riba was not a new phenomenon in Muhammad’s time: before the Quran’s revelation, forms of lending, demanding an increase, and making debtors dependent on creditors existed in various societies.

Since the Quran does not confine faith to a religious label and also speaks of believers among Jews and Christians, consulting the books of the Old Testament has particular relevance here. These books formed part of the recognized religious heritage of Jews and Christians and help us understand how riba and interest-bearing loans were understood and evaluated in those traditions before the Quran’s revelation.

This does not mean imposing Quranic rulings on Jews and Christians, since riba was also prohibited within their own religious laws and texts, with clear examples in the books of the Old Testament.

In Hebrew, the words tarbit (תַּרְבִּית) and marbit (מַרְבִּית) derive from a root meaning increase and growth and are used in the Old Testament for an increase associated with lending. They appear alongside neshekh (נֶשֶׁךְ), meaning interest and also etymologically connected with biting. In Aramaic, the root R B Y likewise conveyed growth and increase, and ribita (רביתא), written in Syriac as “ܪܒܝܬܐ,” was used for interest on a loan. These words are linguistically and semantically related to Arabic R B W and show the long history of the concept of financial increase in Semitic languages. Notably, in Leviticus, the prohibition against taking this increase appears in the same context as supporting a person in need—a connection also semantically consistent with the Quran’s attention to debtors’ rights and preventing economic injustice. Nevertheless, these linguistic and ethical similarities do not imply that all these societies’ historical regulations were identical, or that Quranic riba can be defined solely through Hebrew and Aramaic vocabulary.

This evidence shows that, centuries before the Quran’s revelation, loans, interest, demands for an increase on money and goods, and economic pressure on debtors were known. It also shows that societies’ regulations and understandings of these transactions differed.

Exodus 22:25 discusses lending money to people in need and prohibits taking interest from them.

Leviticus 25:35, Leviticus 25:36, and Leviticus 25:37 command support for a relative in poverty and prohibit demanding an increase on money or food given to them. The Hebrew passage uses neshekh and tarbit, expressions concerning interest and increase on a loan.

Deuteronomy 23:19 and Deuteronomy 23:20 also discuss taking interest on loans of money, food, and other property, while distinguishing between fellow community members and foreigners.

Nehemiah 5:7, Nehemiah 5:8, Nehemiah 5:9, Nehemiah 5:10, and Nehemiah 5:11 present a social example of these relations: people are burdened by debt and economic need, and those with power are called upon to stop taking interest and return the property and assets they have demanded.

Thus, for believers familiar with earlier religious traditions, the Quran’s command to relinquish riba was not entirely unprecedented. They could also recognize examples of prohibitions against demanding an increase from people in need within their own religious heritage.

The presence of these rulings in the Old Testament does not mean that all those practices and regulations existed unchanged in the society of the Quran’s revelation. These texts provide evidence for part of riba’s historical and cultural background; its Quranic definition and ruling, however, must be understood from the Quran itself and the connections among its verses.

Within the Quran itself, An-Nisa 4:161 speaks of taking riba by people who had previously been forbidden to do so. This verse confirms that prohibitions of riba also had a history in religious traditions before the Quran.

Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:278, the command is clear: those who have believed must relinquish their outstanding claims to riba.

For example, if someone entered a transaction involving riba before learning of this command and still claims an increase constituting riba from the other party, the verse commands them to relinquish that outstanding claim.

This command also connects with Al-Baqarah 2:275, which distinguished past conduct from desisting after receiving admonition.

Recognizing that conduct is wrong is not enough; real reform occurs when people stop continuing it and demanding illegitimate gain.

This point should not be understood as prohibiting lending itself, every kind of financing, or every economic profit. The verse concerns relinquishing what the Quran calls riba. For the differences between past and present economic relations and identifying instances of riba, see “The Book of Riba: Beyond the Concept of Interest”.

The verse ends with this expression:

“إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ”

“If you are believers.”

This expression connects the command to relinquish riba directly with faith.

The Quran has addressed those who have believed, but now asks them to make their faith apparent in their conduct as well. Just as faith and righteous action appeared together in the preceding verse, here too faith must not remain merely a verbal claim.

People cannot acknowledge the truth of God’s command on the one hand while knowingly insisting on a claim that God has commanded them to relinquish on the other.

True faith must also become apparent in people’s economic conduct, especially when respecting truth and justice means forgoing personal advantage.

For a fuller examination, see “The Principle of Faith in The God, the Hereafter, and Righteous Action”.

The message of this verse is that avoiding riba does not merely mean refraining from new transactions; it also includes relinquishing outstanding claims to riba. Riba existed before the Quran’s revelation, and examples of lending and demanding increases can also be found in ancient books. This background was recognizable to believers familiar with Jewish and Christian traditions. For believers, however, the criterion for identifying and correcting riba is the Quran’s command. Taqwa and faith affect a person’s life when they can choose what is right even against their own financial advantage and desist from wrongful conduct.
