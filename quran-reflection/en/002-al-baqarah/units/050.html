Al-Baqarah 2:50

And when We split and distinguished a path (faraqnā) through the sea (baḥr) because of you, then We rescued you (anjaynākum) and drowned (aghraqnā) the people of Pharaoh, while you were observing (tanẓurūn).

Our Reflection

This verse is the direct continuation of the previous verse. In verse 49, it was remembered that God rescued the Children of Israel from the people of Pharaoh: from a system that drove them into the worst punishment, slaughtered their sons, and kept their women alive. Now verse 50 recalls the final scene of that rescue: the splitting of the sea, the rescue of the Children of Israel, the drowning of the people of Pharaoh, and the direct observation of this event by the Children of Israel.

The verse begins with wa-idh, and when. This means that this event too is part of the same unforgettable memory of ni'mah. The Qur'an does not only say that you were freed from Pharaoh. It also recalls the moment of crossing: the moment when the right path opened from a place where, according to ordinary logic, there was no path to walk. The sea, which by normal expectation should have become the end of the road and the wall of escape, suddenly became a road of rescue by God's command.

The phrase faraqnā bikum al-baḥr is very precise. The root F R Q is tied to separating, splitting, dividing into two, and setting a boundary. God split the sea and made separation in the single flow of water so that this very separation became the exact boundary between death and life. This expanse of water opened a path for the Children of Israel, but became the place of drowning for the people of Pharaoh. One single event had two completely different faces for two different fronts: the oppressed who were walking in the path of rescue were released, while the people of Pharaoh who rushed to return them to bondage went into death.

The word bikum is also very important here, and in translation it finds its meaning through “because of you.” The verse does not say only that We split the sea; it says bikum. This means that the splitting of the sea had a direct connection with the steps of the Children of Israel, their movement, their rescue, and their desperation. This splitting was not a purposeless display of power. The opening of the path was precisely at the point reached by this suffering community through God's command and Musa's companionship.

Here, “cause” takes on a precise meaning. Cause here is not only a mental reason or a pretext on paper, but a real chain that connects events to one another. The Children of Israel entered the path of departure, the people of Pharaoh entered the path of pursuit, and the sea appeared at the point of dead end; and God split that very point of dead end because of their movement. So the verse shows that divine rescue in history does not happen without direction and in a vacuum. It happens precisely at the point of movement, the decision to leave, facing danger, and standing at the threshold of needing an opening.

Al-baḥr, the sea, is not merely a large mass of water. Baḥr means a vast, deep, and, from the human view, untamable expanse. For the Children of Israel, the sea was the boundary of despair and the end of the horizon of escape. Behind them stood the lash of the Pharaonic apparatus, and before them was a deep expanse that they could not cross by ordinary ability. Exactly in this meeting point between the pressure of oppressive power and the dead end of nature, the unseen hand of God entered the event.

This farq, separation, resonates with the broader meaning of furqan. Furqan is not always a mental and verbal discussion. Sometimes it happens within history itself and on the stage of nature. The very sea that appeared to be an obstacle became a field of separation: the separation of the path of rescue from the path of destruction, the separation of helpless oppressed people from the pursuing apparatus, the separation of faith (iman) from arrogant domination, and the separation of a free future from the past of bondage.

Then He says: fa-anjaynākum, so We rescued you. The rescue, again, is from God. The Children of Israel did not build the road through the sea themselves. They were in a condition where the people of Pharaoh were behind them and the waves of the sea were in front of them. Elsewhere, the Qur'an shows this breath-stopping dead end with greater intensity: when Musa's companions said in despair, We have been overtaken, Musa said, No; my Lord is with me and He will guide me. So rescue here was not only a simple geographical relocation. It was emergence from an absolute human and historical dead end.

Fa-anjaynākum comes with the letter fa, showing that immediately after the splitting of the sea, rescue was realized. The path opened and they were carried across safely. But this rescue was not only the end of a chase. It was also the beginning of a new stage of responsibility. A people who have crossed through the sea can no longer treat themselves like a people who have seen no sign. They had seen the bitterness of Pharaoh's punishment, they had seen the sea split, they had seen their own rescue, and they had watched the end of the people of Pharaoh with their own eyes.

Then the verse says: wa-aghraqnā āla fir'awn. Here again the expression is āl fir'awn, the people of Pharaoh, not only the person of Pharaoh. The people of Pharaoh were not merely his private family or personal relatives. They were those attached to the Pharaonic center of power, those who received direction from him, obeyed his command, and pursued the Children of Israel to prevent their freedom. They were the network of obedience, violence, pursuit, and the arms preserving the Pharaonic system.

So the drowning of the people of Pharaoh was not only the death of a few people in a natural incident. It was the complete collapse of the pursuing apparatus. Those who wanted to return the Children of Israel to bondage were themselves destroyed in the very passage God had opened for the rescue of the oppressed. In this way, the Pharaonic logic was reversed: a system that imagined water, land, labor, bodies, and the future of people to be under its control became completely powerless before water acting by God's command.

Aghraqnā comes from the root GH R Q: to drown, to submerge in water, and to be buried beneath something that leaves no path out. The people of Pharaoh wanted to keep the Children of Israel drowned inside their own system: drowned in punishment, powerlessness, forced labor, fear, and dependency. But the end was that they themselves drowned in water. What became a bridge of crossing for the Children of Israel became a covering of death for the people of Pharaoh.

The end of the verse says: wa-antum tanẓurūn, while you were observing. This sentence does not merely say that you were present there or that you simply looked. Tanẓurūn comes from the root N Ẓ R, which is tied to focused looking, attention, waiting, consideration, and deep receiving. The Children of Israel did not merely hear about this event from others; they were living witnesses to it. The splitting of the sea, their own crossing, and the drowning of the people of Pharaoh were registered forever in their minds and eyes.

This observation creates responsibility. The one who has only heard the story of rescue is very different from the one who has watched the scene of rescue himself. The Children of Israel were not merely listeners to a memory; they were eyewitnesses. With their own eyes, they saw the collapse of a power that yesterday had been killing their sons and keeping their women alive. This observation should have become a deep ethical belief in their memory, so they would know that no Pharaoh is absolute, no oppressive system is eternal, and the path of rescue opens from where the human being does not expect.

But this same wa-antum tanẓurūn also carries another weight. Whoever has observed such a sign, if later he covers truth, forgets ni'mah, or attaches his heart to forms and structures other than the One worshiped, his responsibility becomes far heavier. The verse does not yet enter the story of the calf here, but what follows will make clear that seeing immense signs does not protect the human being from falling if seeing does not become gratitude, khushu', and faithfulness.

In this verse, three movements come one after another: faraqnā, fa-anjaynākum, and wa-aghraqnā. First separation happens, then rescue, and finally the end of the pursuing apparatus. This order shows that divine rescue is not only pulling out the oppressed; it also removes the structure of pursuit and domination from the field. The Children of Israel were rescued, but the people of Pharaoh were not merely pushed back. They were drowned. That is, the system built on pursuing freedom reached its end in that very pursuit.

So verse 50 is not only a report of a natural miracle. It is a certificate of freedom. God reminds the Children of Israel that their rescue came with the separation of the road, crossing through the dead end, destruction of the pursuing apparatus, and direct observation of the sign. If such a great collective memory remains alive, it makes the human being humble, grateful, and responsible. But if it turns into an old and lifeless story, the human being may, despite having seen rescue, fall again into forgetfulness, concealment, and ingratitude.

As a result, verse 50 is the completing link of verse 49. In verse 49, the Children of Israel were rescued from the system of punishment, and in verse 50, they saw the final frame of that liberation with their own eyes. The sea split, the road opened, the oppressed crossed, the people of Pharaoh drowned, and the Children of Israel observed all these scenes. Their ni'mah, then, was not only heard; it was seen. And a ni'mah seen in this way carries a far heavier proof upon the human nafs.
