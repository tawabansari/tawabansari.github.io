Al-Baqarah 2:100

ۚ

Is it that every time they made a covenant, a group among them cast it aside? Rather, most of them do not believe.

Our Reflection

Making a covenant in peaceful times is easy, but the true measure of faith becomes clear when that covenant conflicts with personal interests. Verse 100 of Surah Al-Baqarah exposes a recurring historical pattern: casting aside divine commitments as soon as honoring them becomes costly.

“Is it that every time they made a covenant…”

The force of this conduct lies in the Arabic verb “cast aside” (nabadha). It does not mean merely abandoning a covenant, forgetting it, or falling short in fulfilling it; it means throwing away something no longer considered worth keeping. When a covenant they had once accepted conflicted with their desires and interests, a group among them cast it aside as though discarding something worthless.

This address comes within the history of the Children of Israel, and that context makes its significance greater. The Qur’an speaks of a people whom it had described as favored above the worlds during a period of their history. Many prophets and messengers were raised among them; they received scripture and judgment, witnessed signs and clear proofs, were rescued from Pharaoh, received provision in the wilderness, and repeatedly entered into covenants and solemn pledges with The God. These were not people whom guidance had never reached or who had been deprived of religious experience. Yet that same history showed that having prophets, scripture, signs, and a covenant does not make any people immune to error and deviation.

If a people with such a history of guidance can still cast aside a covenant, others have even less reason to imagine themselves inherently immune. Being Muslim, possessing the Qur’an, claiming affiliation with the Prophet, or belonging to a religious tradition is no guarantee of faithfulness. Past distinction guarantees nothing unless it leads to faith and fidelity today.

The verse first speaks of a particular group:

“A group among them cast it aside.”

But it then looks beyond that group:

“Rather, most of them do not believe.”

The problem was not merely the visible conduct of one group of covenant-breakers. It lay deeper, in a faith that had not become firm enough in the majority to sustain their commitment. When faith has not taken root within a person, a covenant remains respected only for as long as it serves their interests.

This is the same human pattern that recurs again and again. We defend justice until it bears witness against us. We want freedom until our opponent exercises that same freedom. We speak of covenants, law, and principles for as long as upholding them costs us nothing. The moment faithfulness becomes costly and betrayal profitable, the reality of what we call “faith” is revealed.

Faith becomes an inward reality, rather than merely a verbal claim, when a person stands by their covenant at the very moment when betrayal would be profitable.
