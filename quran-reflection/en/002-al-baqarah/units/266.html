Al-Baqarah 2:266

ۗ

Would any of you like to have a garden of date palms and grapevines, with rivers flowing beneath it and every kind of fruit for him in it, while old age has reached him and he has weak offspring, then a whirlwind containing fire strikes the garden and it burns? Thus The God makes the signs clear to you, so that you may reflect.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:264, the Quran spoke of invalidating charities through reproach and injury, and in Al-Baqarah 2:265 it compared giving with a sound intention to a fruitful garden. Now, through another parable, it depicts the extent of the loss caused by ruining the fruits of one’s good deeds.

“أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ تَكُونَ لَهُ جَنَّةٌ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ لَهُ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ”

“Would any of you like to have a garden of date palms and grapevines, with rivers flowing beneath it and every kind of fruit for him in it?”

The Quran sets before us an image of a flourishing, fruitful garden: a garden with date palms, grapevines, various fruits, and flowing rivers. Such a garden is not merely its owner’s property; it may also be his source of livelihood and the fruit of years of effort.

In the context of these verses about giving, this garden can be understood as an image of the fruits of the good deeds and charities a person has accumulated throughout life. Just as a garden grows and yields produce over time, a person’s good deeds can also bear fruit for them.

The Quran then describes the circumstances of the garden’s owner:

“وَأَصَابَهُ الْكِبَرُ وَلَهُ ذُرِّيَّةٌ ضُعَفَاءُ”

“And old age has reached him, and he has weak offspring.”

These two characteristics are essential.

The garden’s owner has reached old age, when his physical strength and opportunity to start again are no longer what they once were. He also has weak offspring who need support and resources for living and cannot easily take his place in providing a livelihood.

For this family, then, the garden is not merely extra wealth; it is the support of their lives and their hope for the future.

This image also carries an important meaning when reflecting on giving. Throughout life, a person has the opportunity to do good, give, and prepare provision for their future, but this opportunity does not last forever. In Al-Baqarah 2:254, the Quran pointed to this limit and called believers to give before a day arrives when making amends is no longer possible as it is in worldly life.

Now imagine the owner of this garden facing a devastating event in precisely these circumstances:

“فَأَصَابَهَا إِعْصَارٌ فِيهِ نَارٌ فَاحْتَرَقَتْ”

“Then a whirlwind containing fire struck the garden, and it burned.”

“Iʿṣār,” from the root AYN Ṣ R, refers to a whirlwind that blows forcefully and sets air and dust in rotation. But the Quran does not speak merely of a whirlwind; it explicitly states that there is fire within it and that the garden burns.

In this image, the severity of the calamity lies in the combination of two conditions: losing what one has prepared for the future and being unable to replace it at the time of need.

In the context of the preceding verses, this fiery whirlwind can vividly portray the consequences of ostentation, reproach, and injury: conduct through which a person ruins the value of their charities.

A person may have spent their wealth for years, met many people’s needs, and imagined that they had prepared valuable provision for their future. But if their gifts were for show, or if they later accompanied them with reproach and injury, they have emptied of true value the very deeds on which they had placed their hopes.

In Al-Baqarah 2:264, the Quran explicitly warned:

“لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُمْ بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ” — Al-Baqarah 2:264

“Do not invalidate your charities through reproach and injury.”

Now, through the image of a burning garden, Al-Baqarah 2:266 invites us to reflect on the magnitude of this loss.

The verse finally says:

“كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ”

“Thus The God makes the signs clear to you, so that you may reflect.”

“يُبَيِّنُ,” from the root B Y N, denotes making clear and manifest, while “تَتَفَكَّرُونَ,” from the root F K R, refers to thinking and conscious examination.

Through the question “أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ,” the Quran asks us to imagine ourselves in the garden owner’s place. Would anyone wish to lose the entire fruit of their efforts precisely when they need it most and no longer have the strength to rebuild it?

This question is not merely intended to stir sorrow; it is an invitation to reflect on the fate of our own deeds.

The verse’s message is that, just as people strive to protect their possessions and the future of their lives, they should also safeguard the value of the good deeds and charities they have performed. Ostentation, reproach, and injury can ruin the fruits of years of kindness. What greater loss could there be than discovering, when one needs the fruits of one’s deeds, that one has personally destroyed what one had prepared for the future?
