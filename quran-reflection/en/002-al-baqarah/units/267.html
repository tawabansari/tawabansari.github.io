Al-Baqarah 2:267

ۖ ۚ

O you who have believed, give from the good things you have earned and from what We have brought forth for you from the earth; and do not seek out its impure and inferior portion to give, while you yourselves would not accept it except by overlooking its defects; and know that The God is free of need and praiseworthy.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verses, the Quran spoke of the growth of the fruits of giving, sincerity of intention, and avoiding reproach and injury. It now addresses another important matter: what we give must itself be wholesome and worthy.

“يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْفِقُوا مِنْ طَيِّبَاتِ مَا كَسَبْتُمْ وَمِمَّا أَخْرَجْنَا لَكُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ”

“O you who have believed, give from the good things you have earned and from what We have brought forth for you from the earth.”

“كَسَبْتُمْ,” from the root K S B, denotes what people acquire through their efforts and activities. In this verse, human income, wealth, and earnings are mentioned alongside what God has brought forth from the earth.

Yet the concept of earning and acquisition in the Quran is not limited to money. In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:254 and the study The Principle of Giving and Using Blessings, we examined how knowledge, art, talent, experience, skill, time, and human abilities are also blessings that can be put to good use.

Although the direct subject of this verse is giving from acquired possessions and produce, the criterion of wholesomeness can also be applied to the use of other blessings. Someone who teaches should convey sound and beneficial knowledge, not incorrect and misleading information, just as someone who gives wealth should give from wholesome and worthy possessions.

“طَيِّبَات,” from the root Ṭ Y B, refers to purity, goodness, pleasantness, and worthiness. In this context, the wholesomeness of wealth concerns its quality; in light of other Quranic principles, the rightness of the means of acquiring it can also be examined.

Wealth acquired through oppression, theft, deception, or violation of others’ rights does not become pure and legitimate by giving some of it to those in need.

For example, one cannot steal from the rich, as in the story of Robin Hood, give what was stolen to the poor, and then call it giving in God’s way.

Or suppose someone kidnaps a merchant, extracts money through threats, and then distributes it among the needy. Can helping the poor justify the oppression committed to obtain that money?

In Al-Baqarah 2:188, the Quran forbids consuming one another’s wealth wrongfully, and in An-Nisa 4:29 it emphasizes respecting rights and consent in trade.

A good aim does not purify an impure means. One cannot violate one person’s rights to help another and then call it beneficence.

The Quran then presents another criterion for recognizing the quality of giving:

“وَلَا تَيَمَّمُوا الْخَبِيثَ مِنْهُ تُنْفِقُونَ”

“And do not seek out its impure and inferior portion to give.”

“تَيَمَّمُوا,” from the root Y M M, refers to intending and deliberately turning toward something. Here the command is not to choose inferior things intentionally for giving.

“خَبِيث,” from the root KH B TH, stands opposite ṭayyib and can denote impurity, inferior quality, corruption, and unworthiness.

Thus, the verse is not concerned only with unlawfully acquired wealth; the quality of what we give to another person matters too.

For example, someone who keeps healthy, good-quality food for themselves but sets aside spoiled or unsuitable food for those in need cannot regard their action as good merely because they have given something.

The Quran offers a very simple criterion for assessing such conduct:

“وَلَسْتُمْ بِآخِذِيهِ إِلَّا أَنْ تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ”

“While you yourselves would not accept it except by overlooking its defects.”

“تُغْمِضُوا,” from the root GH M Ḍ, refers to closing the eyes and, in this construction, overlooking faults and deficiencies.

If something is so inferior that we ourselves would accept it only by making allowances, why should we consider that same thing suitable for a person in need?

A person’s need does not mean that they are worthless or that every inferior thing is fit for them.

This is the same principle of respect and regard for others’ rights that we examined in the preceding verses. Giving should not become a means of disposing of unsuitable possessions or passing harm on to someone in need.

In Aal Imran 3:92, the Quran also connects giving from what one loves with attaining goodness.

God’s freedom from need and human responsibility

The verse finally ends with two divine attributes:

“وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ”

“And know that The God is free of need and praiseworthy.”

“Ghanī,” from the root GH N Y, denotes freedom from need, while “ḥamīd,” from the root H M D, means praised and worthy of praise.

This ending is fundamentally connected with the subject of giving.

God does not need our wealth, knowledge, efforts, or gifts. If He wills to provide for someone, His ability does not depend on our possessions or help.

But this truth does not mean abandoning our responsibility toward others’ needs. In Ya-Sin 36:47, the Quran quotes those who, when called to give, said:

“أَنُطْعِمُ مَنْ لَوْ يَشَاءُ اللَّهُ أَطْعَمَهُ” — Ya-Sin 36:47

“Should we feed someone whom The God would have fed if He had willed?”

The Quran presents these words as those of deniers who used them as an excuse to withhold giving.

In fact, we are not the source of other people’s provision, but we are responsible for how we use what God has placed at our disposal.

In Al-Anam 6:165, God connects differences in people’s resources and positions with testing them through what He has given them.

Thus, one person’s possession of wealth, knowledge, or ability and another person’s need can provide a setting in which their responsibility and conduct toward each other are tested. Someone with greater resources should reflect on how to use them for good, rather than regard possession as proof of superiority over a needy person.

When we help someone in need, we have not only benefited them; we too have benefited from an opportunity to do good. Beyond divine reward, helping with education, treatment, production, and overcoming deprivation can contribute to the welfare, progress, and ease of the society in which we and our children also live.

In Al-Baqarah 2:272, the Quran states more explicitly that the good of giving returns to the giver.

Why, then, should we reproach the recipient over our gift?

If God has given us wealth, knowledge, ability, and opportunities to serve, and granted us conscience and will so that we can use them for good, we ourselves have in fact benefited from God’s blessing and kindness.

The Quran also reminds us in Al-Hujurat 49:17:

“بَلِ اللَّهُ يَمُنُّ عَلَيْكُمْ أَنْ هَدَاكُمْ لِلْإِيمَانِ” — Al-Hujurat 49:17

“Rather, The God has bestowed a favor upon you by guiding you to faith.”

If we are speaking of kindness and blessings whose true source must be recognized, it is we who are indebted to God. Giving a portion of His blessings does not entitle us to make others personally beholden to us and our wishes.

The blessings God has placed at humanity’s disposal are beyond counting:

“وَإِنْ تَعُدُّوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ لَا تُحْصُوهَا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَغَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ” — An-Nahl 16:18

“And if you count the blessing of The God, you cannot enumerate it; indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.”

The ending “غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ” thus recalls that God is free of need for human giving and Himself worthy of praise. By giving well, a person adds nothing to God’s freedom from need, but uses the blessing they have received in the right way.

The message of this verse is that both the source of wealth and what we choose to give should be wholesome and worthy. One cannot acquire wealth through oppression and corruption and justify it under the name of kindness, just as one cannot choose inferior things for the needy and expect the mere act of giving them to count as goodness.
