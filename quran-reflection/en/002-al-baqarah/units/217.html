Al-Baqarah 2:217

ۖ ۖ ۚ ۗ ۚ ۖ ۖ

They ask you about the sacred month and fighting in it. Say: Fighting in it is a grave matter; and barring people from the path of The God, disbelieving in Him, barring them from the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people from it are graver before The God; and persecution is graver than killing. They will continue fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they can. And whoever among you turns back from their religion and dies in disbelief—their deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter; they are the people of the Fire, and they remain in it.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:213, the Quran spoke of disagreement over the Book after clear evidence had arrived, out of transgression and a desire for superiority. In Al-Baqarah 2:214, it warned believers that they were not exempt from the trials of earlier people. Now it brings before us a grave form of oppression: barring people from God’s path, denying them access to the Sacred Mosque, expelling its people, and seeking to turn people back from their religion.

“يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ قِتَالٍ فِيهِ”

“They ask you about the sacred month and fighting in it.”

The question concerns fighting in a month held sacred. God does not diminish its sanctity, and says:

“قُلْ قِتَالٌ فِيهِ كَبِيرٌ”

“Say: Fighting in it is a grave matter.”

“Qital” here speaks of fighting and armed confrontation, not simply killing a person. This distinction matters for understanding the verse.

Yet the sanctity of the sacred month must not become a means of justifying aggression and continued oppression. In Al-Baqarah 2:190, God limited fighting to confronting those who fight and forbade transgression. In Al-Baqarah 2:193, He also speaks of ending persecution and ceasing hostilities once aggressors desist. This verse therefore neither authorizes warmongering nor permits violating others’ rights in the name of defending truth.

God then identifies actions even graver than fighting in the sacred month:

“وَصَدٌّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَكُفْرٌ بِهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَإِخْرَاجُ أَهْلِهِ مِنْهُ أَكْبَرُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ”

“And barring people from the path of The God, disbelieving in Him, barring them from the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people from it are graver before The God.”

“Sadd” refers to barring and preventing. In this verse, barring people from God’s path and the Sacred Mosque stands alongside disbelief in God and expelling the people of this sanctuary.

The expression “ahlihi”—its people—is also noteworthy. The Quran does not say “the people of Mecca,” but speaks of the people of the Sacred Mosque. This expression should not, without evidence, be confined to all the inhabitants of a city.

The Sacred Mosque is connected with Abraham’s House, which God describes in Al-Baqarah 2:125 as a place of return and security for people. In Al-Hajj 22:25, the Sacred Mosque is likewise established for people, with equality between those residing there and those arriving from elsewhere.

The House God has established for people is not the exclusive property of any nation, government, or religious community. Being its custodian gives no one the right to regard themselves as the owner of the servants’ path to God.

But are those who call themselves followers of the Quran today, while abandoning it beneath heaps of hadith, transmitted accounts, and inherited rulings, truly following the way of Abraham, the upright?

Abraham called people to divine oneness and wholehearted service to God. In Al-Hajj 22:26, the command to purify the House for worshippers is accompanied by the prohibition of associating anything with God. How, then, can someone claim to follow Abraham’s way while barring other worshippers of God from that same House on the basis of rulings not established by the Quran?

Do those who exclude Jews, Christians, and other seekers of God from the Sacred Mosque solely because of differences in religious law or denomination not repeat the very actions the Quran condemns in this verse?

At-Tawbah 9:28 is cited to justify such exclusion. Yet that verse speaks of “al-mushrikun”—those who associate partners with God—not all the People of the Book.

In Al-Baqarah 2:105, the Quran names the People of the Book and those who associate partners with God as distinct categories. At-Tawbah 9:28 speaks of those who associate partners, while At-Tawbah 9:29 identifies the People of the Book separately. Al-Hajj 22:17 likewise names Jews, Christians, and those who associate partners alongside other groups under separate designations.

These distinctions cannot be ignored by placing all followers of other religious laws into one group without evidence.

The Quran does, of course, describe some beliefs and conduct among the People of the Book as disbelief, and some as associating partners with God. For example, Al-Maidah 5:72 condemns attributing divinity to the Messiah and warns against associating partners. At-Tawbah 9:31 also connects taking scholars and monks as lords apart from God with such association.

Being among the People of the Book therefore neither proves that someone associates partners with God nor guarantees freedom from it. The criterion is a person’s belief and conduct, not their religious name.

But what follows if we apply the same criterion to ourselves?

Associating partners with God in the Quran is not limited to worshipping stone and wooden idols. In Ash-Shura 42:21, God speaks of partners who have legislated religious matters for people without His permission. At-Tawbah 9:31 condemns taking scholars and monks as lords apart from God. Al-Anam 6:121 also connects obedience to others, in the context of opposing the divine command, with associating partners.

In An-Nahl 16:116, people are forbidden to fabricate declarations of what is lawful and unlawful and falsely attribute them to God.

The matter is therefore clear and serious: if a scholar, leader, or government devises religious rulings for people without God’s permission and imposes them in the name of divine command, they have assigned a share in legislation to someone other than God. This is the danger the Quran warns of concerning associating partners in religion and command.

The same criterion also applies to those who place accounts attributed to the Prophet, his companions, or religious leaders in the position of independent, binding divine rulings without examining them against the Quran.

Respecting scholars and benefiting from their knowledge is one thing; taking them as lords apart from God is another. No scholar, account, denomination, or government has the right to substitute its own words for God’s command.

If associating partners in legislation is condemned, why should those who call themselves Muslims be exempt from this criterion? Could those who call other worshippers of God polytheists and bar them from the Sacred Mosque themselves be caught in the very association they attribute to others?

This becomes especially grave when human-made rulings are imposed upon millions of people, restricting their rights, freedom, and path of worship.

There is, of course, a distinction between someone who unknowingly follows another’s words and someone who, after recognizing the truth, consciously places human command above God’s command. We cannot judge people’s ultimate fate without knowing their intentions and awareness. Yet this distinction does not remove the reality of associating partners in legislation or the responsibility of those who issue rulings in God’s name.

For a closer examination, see the studies “The Quranic Root SH R K” and “Lawful and Unlawful in the Quran: Divine Legislative Authority and the Danger of Associating Partners in Religion”.

The place of Abraham’s House, the concept of associating partners, the scope of At-Tawbah 9:28, and people’s rights at the Sacred Mosque are also examined in detail in “Hajj in the Quran: Abraham’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights” and “Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Quran”.

“وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَكْبَرُ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ”

“And persecution is graver than killing.”

Fitnah, from the root F T N, relates to testing and placing under pressure. In this verse, it must be understood in connection with the actions God has mentioned before this expression: barring people from His path, disbelief in Him, denying people access to the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people. The verse then speaks of fighting to turn people back from their religion.

Fitnah in this context therefore relates to harassment, repression, exclusion, and pressure intended to turn people away from serving God. Preventing worshippers of God from entering places of divine worship in order to impose belief and deprive them of worship can also be an instance of this oppression.

In Al-Baqarah 2:114, God also condemns those who prevent His name from being remembered in places of worship and strive to destroy them.

Yet persecution being graver than killing is not a command to kill religious opponents. In Al-Baqarah 2:190, the Quran forbids transgression, and in Al-Baqarah 2:192 and Al-Baqarah 2:193, it makes the end of aggression the basis for ceasing to fight. One cannot resort to repression, aggression, and bloodshed in the name of ending persecution.

“وَلَا يَزَالُونَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَرُدُّوكُمْ عَنْ دِينِكُمْ إِنِ اسْتَطَاعُوا”

“And they will continue fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they can.”

This statement concerns those same aggressors who fight the people addressed by the verse and seek to turn them back from their religion, not everyone who holds a different belief or follows a different religious law.

“Dinikum” refers to the religion of those addressed, but din in Quranic language is not confined to the historical name of Muhammad’s religious law or a denomination that arose after him. In the context of service to God, din concerns the source of command, the direction of obedience, submission, and human accountability before God. In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Quran speaks of different laws and paths prescribed for communities.

Using force, threats, and repression to turn people away from serving God is not oppression only when Muhammad’s enemies do it. If Muhammad’s followers pressure others to accept their denomination and rulings, their conduct must be assessed by the same criterion.

The distinction between din, Islam, and shariah is examined in “Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Quran”, and the boundary between invitation and coercion in “There Is No Compulsion in Religion: The Prophet’s Duty Is to Convey”.

God then says of those who turn back from their religion:

“وَمَنْ يَرْتَدِدْ مِنْكُمْ عَنْ دِينِهِ فَيَمُتْ وَهُوَ كَافِرٌ فَأُولَٰئِكَ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ”

“And whoever among you turns back from their religion and dies in disbelief—their deeds have come to nothing in this world and the Hereafter.”

The Quran states a decisive condition here: “فَيَمُتْ وَهُوَ كَافِرٌ”—and dies in disbelief.

The judgment concerning deeds coming to nothing in this verse applies to someone who turns back from their religion and dies in that state of disbelief, not everyone who at some point experiences doubt, error, or a slip.

In Al-Baqarah 2:209, the Quran speaks of slipping after clear evidence has arrived. In Al-Baqarah 2:160, it also opens the way of repentance, reform, and making truth clear for those who had concealed it. A slip, turning back from error, and persisting in disbelief until death are therefore not identical conditions.

“Habitat a‘maluhum” speaks of deeds being ruined and rendered fruitless. God connects this outcome with the conditions stated in the verse itself.

There is no command in this verse to kill someone who has turned back from their religion. The Quran speaks of deeds coming to nothing and an outcome in the Hereafter; people cannot turn this judgment into authorization to execute someone who has changed their belief.

At the end, He says:

“وَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ”

“And they are the people of the Fire; they remain in it.”

This outcome concerns the same people described in the preceding expression. The condition of dying in disbelief still governs this judgment and must not be removed.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 begins with the sanctity of the sacred month, but carries the discussion to the sanctity of God’s path, the Sacred Mosque, freedom to worship, and human responsibility toward truth.

In Al-Baqarah 2:213, we read of those who differed out of transgression after receiving the Book and clear evidence. In Al-Baqarah 2:214, we were warned that we too are not exempt from testing. Now this question confronts us: are we repeating today, in the name of Islam and defending religion, the conduct the Quran condemned among earlier people?

Those who have set the Quran behind them, placed transmitted accounts and human-made rulings above the command of The God, and used those rulings to bar other worshippers of God from Abraham’s House must assess their own conduct by divine oneness and God’s command before accusing others of associating partners.

Abraham’s House must not become an instrument of religious exclusivism, just as fighting to remove oppression must not become a pretext for new oppression. Serving The God is incompatible with making humans partners in His command, depriving seekers of God of worship, and imposing belief in religion’s name.
