Al-Baqarah 2:198

ۚ ۖ

There is no sin upon you in seeking bounty from your Lord. So when you depart from Arafat, remember The God at the Sacred Monument; and remember Him as He guided you, though before that you were indeed among those astray.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, The God spoke of the months of Hajj, refraining from rafath, fusuq, and jidal, doing good, and taqwa. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:198, He declares that seeking divine bounty during Hajj is no sin, speaks of movement between two specific places, and then calls people to remember an important truth: you too were among those astray before receiving guidance.

The God first says:

“لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَنْ تَبْتَغُوا فَضْلًا مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ”

“There is no sin upon you in seeking bounty from your Lord.”

“تَبْتَغُوا”, from the root ب غ ي, means seeking and requesting, while “fadl” is connected with increase, giving, and a benefit beyond one’s basic entitlement.

This phrase shows that seeking lawful benefit during Hajj is not, in itself, sinful. Alongside worship, travelers may engage in buying and selling, work, providing services, or other beneficial activities.

In Al-Jumuah 62:10, the Quran also speaks of dispersing through the land and seeking The God’s bounty after salah ends. Earning a lawful livelihood is therefore not incompatible with serving The God.

But “لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ” means the absence of sin, not a command making trade obligatory. Likewise, “فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِ” in Al-Baqarah 2:158 must not be turned into a command of obligation without examining the other verses.

Lawful trade is also not the same as exploiting people’s religious needs. Someone providing pilgrims with food, accommodation, transport, or essential services may earn income from their work; but this permission does not confer a right to monopoly, impose unjust costs, or turn people’s right to serve The God into a commodity.

This issue is examined in detail in the discussion of trade and Hajj’s economic benefits in “Hajj in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights”.

The God then says:

“فَإِذَا أَفَضْتُمْ مِنْ عَرَفَاتٍ فَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ عِنْدَ الْمَشْعَرِ الْحَرَامِ”

“So when you depart from Arafat, remember The God at the Sacred Monument.”

“أَفَضْتُمْ”, from the root ف ي ض, is connected with flowing and streaming forth. Here it refers to people moving and departing from Arafat.

“Arafat” is the name of a specific place along the Hajj route. “الْمَشْعَرِ الْحَرَامِ” is also a place endowed with sanctity, at which The God has prescribed remembrance of Him.

Presence at Arafat, departure from it, and remembrance of The God at the Sacred Monument therefore have explicit Quranic support.

This phrase, however, specifies neither exact starting and ending times for presence at Arafat, nor the duration of staying at the Sacred Monument, nor a range of practical details found in customary rites. The distinction between the Quranic command itself and historical details is examined in the article on Hajj.

The important point is that after mentioning departure from Arafat, The God directs people’s attention to remembering Him. Hajj must not be merely the movement of bodies from one place to another; people must also remember The God and His guidance along this route.

He then says:

“وَاذْكُرُوهُ كَمَا هَدَاكُمْ”

“And remember Him as He guided you.”

Remembrance of The God is connected here with His guidance. People must recognize that knowing the right way is not solely an achievement of their abilities, ancestry, culture, or personal superiority.

The rest of the verse makes this clearer:

“وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ لَمِنَ الضَّالِّينَ”

“Though before that you were indeed among those astray.”

The God reminds the very people now performing Hajj and remembering Him of their past: you too were among those astray before guidance.

This reminder is not merely an account of the past; it contains a profound lesson for how people treat others today.

How can someone who was astray yesterday and recognizes the path of guidance today boast over those who have not yet recognized it, humiliate them, exclude them from society, or consider their blood lawful merely because their beliefs differ?

Did we bring the truth into the world with us from the beginning of life? Did we choose our birthplace, family, language, culture, and earliest beliefs ourselves?

Perhaps if we had been born into a Hindu family, we would have learned those same beliefs and rites from childhood and considered them the right path. Perhaps if we had grown up elsewhere, we would have remained faithful to our ancestors’ customs and traditions without ever questioning their correctness.

Even now, how much of what we believe have we accepted through investigation and understanding, and how much have we merely inherited from family, surroundings, and religious leaders?

In Al-Baqarah 2:170, the Quran questions following what one’s ancestors practiced in the face of an invitation to what The God has revealed. This criticism is not directed only at other people’s ancestors; everyone must also assess their own inherited beliefs against guidance.

Without the grace of divine guidance, what guarantee would we have that we would not still be in the very condition for which we now belittle others?

This question must not lead to indifference toward truth and falsehood. The Quran distinguishes guidance from misguidance and invites people to recognize truth. But recognizing truth does not authorize arrogance, oppression, or humiliation of others.

In Aal Imran 3:164, The God presents sending a messenger from among the believers as His favor: a messenger who recites the verses to them and teaches the Book and wisdom. He then says of those same believers:

“وَإِنْ كَانُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ لَفِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ”

“Though before that they were indeed in clear error.”

The Quran therefore does not exempt those now receiving guidance from remembering their own past. They must not imagine themselves inherently superior to other human beings because they were born into a people, are associated with a prophet, or follow a denomination.

But the verse that connects this lesson especially clearly with how people treat others is An-Nisa 4:94.

There, The God commands believers, when they go forth in His way, to investigate and verify, and not to say to someone who offers them peace: “لَسْتَ مُؤْمِنًا”—you are not a believer.

He then says:

“كَذَٰلِكَ كُنْتُمْ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَمَنَّ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ فَتَبَيَّنُوا”

“You too were like that before; then The God bestowed His favor upon you, so investigate and verify.”

This verse directly relates to the lesson of Al-Baqarah 2:198. In both cases, The God reminds people of their former condition. In An-Nisa 4:94, however, the reminder occurs in a situation where a mistaken judgment about someone’s faith could lead to taking their life.

The God reminds those who consider themselves believers that they too were once in a similar situation and received His grace. They therefore have no right now to judge others hastily and endanger human lives.

What a clear lesson for us today!

Someone who considers themselves guided should be especially careful in judging people. They should not readily label people disbelievers, polytheists, or misguided and then use those labels to deprive them of freedom, security, or the right to life.

A person may be mistaken in recognizing truth; but an error in belief does not, by itself, authorize killing, expulsion, humiliation, or deprivation of rights. In Al-Baqarah 2:256, the Quran rejects compulsion in religion, and in An-Nahl 16:125 it commands inviting with wisdom and debating in the best manner.

These foundations are examined in “There Is No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey”.

Let us now return this verse to the setting of Hajj.

Someone going from Arafat to the Sacred Monument is commanded to remember The God and not forget their guidance. This remembrance must also affect their conduct.

If, after returning from Hajj, they still consider themselves superior because of their denomination, ethnicity, language, or faith; if they humiliate those who disagree with their beliefs; or if they remain silent before their persecution and killing, they should ask what they have learned from “وَاذْكُرُوهُ كَمَا هَدَاكُمْ”.

Guidance should not lead people to arrogance; it should make them humbler, fairer, and more responsible.

Someone who once sought the way themselves should also provide other seekers with opportunities to listen, ask, reflect, and choose.

Someone whom The God has brought out of error must not block others’ path to guidance through humiliation, threats, and coercion.

And someone who does not know what they would have believed had they been born into another family and environment—how can they regard themselves as entitled to rights and others as without rights solely on the basis of different inherited beliefs?

This does not mean considering all beliefs identical or disregarding truth. It means that inviting people to truth must be accompanied by humility, justice, and respect for human rights.

In this verse, Hajj leads people from movement between Arafat and the Sacred Monument to remembrance of The God and His guidance. Remembering guidance becomes fuller, however, when people also remember their own past. We too once did not know the way; today we must therefore not belittle those whom we believe have not found it, drive them out of society, or deem their lives worthless in the name of religion.

If The God’s grace has brought us to recognize truth, gratitude for that grace lies not in arrogance and boasting, but in humility, fairness, gracious invitation, and preserving the rights of those very people who may tomorrow recognize a path that is not yet clear to them today.
