Al-Baqarah 2:67

And when Musa said to his people: Indeed, The God (Allah) commands you to slaughter a cow (baqarah). They said: Are you taking us in mockery (huzuwan)? He said: I seek refuge in The God (Allah) from being among the ignorant (jahilin).

Our Reflection

This verse begins the section from which the longest surah of the Qur'an takes its name: Surat Al-Baqarah. This point itself shows that these few verses are not merely a secondary report about slaughtering a cow. In this story, the Qur'an reveals something from inside the human being: when the command of The God comes clear and simple, how does the human being face it? With trust, responsibility, and mindful guarding (taqwa)? Or with suspicion, excuse-making, questions of escape, and making difficult what was not difficult at the beginning?

This verse is not separate from the previous verses. Earlier, in verse 63, the command had come that what was given should be taken with strength and what is in it should be remembered, perhaps the path of mindful guarding (taqwa) would open. Then came the speech about turning away after the covenant. After that, the story of the Sabbath was recalled: the place where a group broke the divine limit and their account became a deterrent punishment (nakal) and an admonition (maw'izah) for the people of mindful guarding. Now the story of the cow shows another face of the same disease: the problem is not always only the open breaking of a limit; sometimes the problem begins at the very moment when the human being does not take a clear command seriously.

The first command is very simple: “Indeed, The God commands you to slaughter a cow” (inna Allaha ya'murukum an tadhbahu baqarah). At this stage, there is no speech about the color of the cow, nor its age, nor its kind, nor whether it has worked or not worked. The command is open and short: slaughter a cow.

This simplicity is the key to the rest of the story. The later difficulties do not begin from the command itself; they begin from the reaction of the people. The God did not make the command complicated at the beginning. But when the heart is not ready for obedience, even a simple command turns into a field of delay, repeated questions, and manufacturing details.

Musa does not attribute the speech to himself. He does not say: “I want” or “I suggest.” He says: “Indeed, The God commands you” (inna Allaha ya'murukum). The word “indeed” (inna) strengthens the speech, and “He commands you” (ya'murukum) shows the command as living and direct. The subject is not a joke, a preference, or a personal opinion. The command is from The God, and the people must face it.

But their first response is not obedience. It is not even a question for better understanding. They say: “Are you taking us in mockery?” (a-tattakhidhuna huzuwan). This speech shows that from the beginning they did not place the command of The God in its serious position. The divine command seemed so unfamiliar, unrelated, or unpleasant to them that they accused the prophet of ridicule.

Mockery (huzuwan) is not merely laughing. This word means belittling in a humiliating way: bringing something that should be heard with gravity and seriousness down to the level of play and ridicule. In truth, they did not simply say: we did not understand. They said, in effect: it is as if you are playing with us. This is one of the ways of escaping responsibility: instead of facing the command of The God, the human being accuses the bearer of the message.

Musa's answer is short, but very deep: “I seek refuge in The God from being among the ignorant” (a'udhu billahi an akuna mina al-jahilin). Musa does not merely say: I am not mocking you. He shows the root of the matter: playing with the command of The God, humiliating people, or turning the divine message into a joke belongs to ignorance.

In the Qur'an, ignorance (jahl) is not always only lacking information. Sometimes this root points to moral rawness, lack of gravity before truth, haste without wisdom, lack of forbearance, and light conduct in a place where the human being should stand with dignity. Musa seeks refuge in The God from being, in the station of conveying the divine command, part of such lightness and foolishness.

This story is alive for us today too. Sometimes the religious human being runs away from the very principle of obligation, like that same people who had not yet carried out the simple command and were delaying action with questions and accusations. Sometimes another form of the same disease appears: the basic action is performed, but we become so drowned in the details of the form of performance that the spirit of the command loses its color. The God has asked from us living prayer, purifying fasting, purity, justice, giving, purification, covenant-keeping, and avoiding wrongdoing and usury; yet we sometimes sink religion into endless disputes: should the feet in ablution be washed or wiped, should the opening recitation be read aloud or quietly, should the hands in prayer be folded or open, what should prostration be upon, should the finger move in tashahhud or not, and dozens of similar details.

This does not mean that understanding the correct form of action has no value. Details that help understanding, order, and better performance of the command have their place. The problem is where details take the place of the principle: when prayer becomes separated from the remembrance of The God and restraining from indecency and wrong, and turns into a field of formal disputes; when fasting becomes separated from mindful guarding and purification of the self, and is reduced only to secondary discussions; when the purifying social due (zakat) and giving, which should give life to society, become lost under dry rules and exhausting debates.

In some narration-centered currents, this same danger can also be seen. The Qur'an brings the command clearly, but piles of reports, conditions, cautions, and disagreements sometimes sit upon it in such a way that people see the original guidance less. Then religion, instead of making the human being lighter, more just, purer, and closer to The God, turns into a heavy burden of disputes and fears. This is exactly where the story of the cow becomes a mirror: not for condemning one past people, but for seeing a disease that can repeat itself in every religious community.

From this view, the story of the cow is not only about slaughtering an animal; it is also about slaughtering excuses. The human being must recognize what stands between him and obedience to The God: sometimes that thing is an obvious attachment, sometimes religious pride, sometimes fear of action, sometimes love of endless debate, and sometimes the habit of making difficult what The God left simple.

This command can also be seen within the wider context of the Children of Israel. This same people had already been tested with worshiping the calf, and the Qur'an says elsewhere that the love of the calf was made to drink into their hearts. From this angle, slaughtering the cow may also recall cutting oneself off from sanctified attachments and inner idols. But even if we do not make this interpretation certain, the clear message of the verse remains: the command of The God must not be turned, through suspicion, mockery, and excuse-making, into a field of escape from responsibility.

So the beginning of the story is this: the command came simply, but the heart was not ready. Musa brought the command of The God, but the people, instead of the courtesy of obedience, accused the prophet of mockery. Musa answered that mockery in the station of the divine command belongs to ignorance. From here, the Qur'an prepares us to see in what follows how the human being can make a simple command difficult with his own hands.

The lesson of the verse is this: when the command of The God is clear, the first duty of the human being is not suspicion and playing with details; it is courtesy, trust, understanding for the sake of action, and responsibility. A question for understanding is light; a question for escape is a veil.
