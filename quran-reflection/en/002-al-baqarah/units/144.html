Al-Baqarah 2:144

ۖ ۚ ۚ ۗ ۗ

We certainly see the turning of your face toward the sky. We will surely turn you toward a direction of prayer that pleases you. So turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque; wherever you may be, turn your faces toward it. Those who were given the Book certainly know that this is the truth from their Lord; and The God is not unaware of what they do.

Our Reflection

The preceding verse presented the change in the direction of prayer as a test that would reveal following and turning back. Now the new direction itself is specified:

“We certainly see the turning of your face toward the sky.”

This waiting does not occur in isolation. For several verses, the Qur’an has been drawing Muhammad away from rival religious identities, exclusive claims to guidance, discrimination among prophets, and seeking the approval of groups, returning him to Abraham’s way of devotion to God alone. This is the same Abraham who, with Ishmael, raised the foundations of the House, asked for surrender for himself and his descendants, and whose way was described as devoted to God alone.

The prophet’s turning his face toward the sky shows his dissatisfaction and anticipation. He does not want his orientation to remain bound to the divisions and prejudices repeatedly criticized in the preceding verses. He turns his face toward the sky, and God sees this waiting.

The answer comes:

“We will surely turn you toward a direction of prayer that pleases you.”

“That pleases you” takes on a particular meaning in this context. Earlier, the approval of religious groups was said to depend on following their way. Here, however, Muhammad does not wait for their approval. God establishes a direction for him that accords with that Abrahamic path and with which he is pleased.

The command is explicit:

“So turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque.”

Earlier in this surah, the Sacred Mosque was connected with Abraham and Ishmael: the House established for people, whose foundations they raised. Now the prophet’s direction also returns to that Abrahamic center.

Here, the face and one’s orientation, the direction of prayer, the word for “toward,” and the Sacred Mosque’s connection with Abraham’s way all come together. A broader discussion of these connections, with Qur’anic evidence and an examination of how the direction of prayer relates to orientation and devotion to God alone, appears in Qiblah, Wajh, and Ḥanīf in the Qur’an. For the relationship of the Sacred Mosque and the House to Abraham, Ishmael, and pilgrimage, see Hajj in the Qur’an.

The command does not stop with the prophet:

“Wherever you may be, turn your faces toward it.”

What began with “your face,” addressed to one person, becomes “your faces,” addressed to the community. The prophet’s anticipation and orientation now become the community’s shared direction. The same community placed in the middle in the preceding verse to bear witness concerning people now also receives a common direction.

This accords with the earlier context. A community of the middle was not meant to create a new religious faction pursuing superiority, but neither was it meant to have no direction. A community charged with bearing witness takes its direction from the same path the Qur’an connects with Abraham, surrender, and devotion to God alone.

The verse then returns to the People of the Book:

“Those who were given the Book certainly know that this is the truth from their Lord.”

The wording is emphatic: they certainly know. Yet the Qur’an does not explain here exactly which passage in their scripture, or which historical process, gave them this knowledge. What the verse itself emphasizes is the existence of knowledge and the responsibility arising from it.

“From their Lord” also matters. A few verses earlier, we read:

“He is our Lord and your Lord.”

Now this truth is likewise identified as coming from their own Lord. This is therefore not a rivalry between two gods or two sources of guidance: the source is one.

The phrase also connects with testimony and concealment in the preceding verses. Verse 140 spoke of someone who possesses testimony from God but conceals it. Here, it speaks of people who know that this is the truth from their Lord. Knowledge brings responsibility; it has value when it is also visible in how a person responds to truth.

For this reason, the verse does not end with knowledge. It moves to action:

“The God is not unaware of what they do.”

The distance between knowing and acting is one of this verse’s central concerns. People may recognize a truth, yet attachments, habits, and boundaries of identity prevent their conduct from matching what they know. The Qur’an does more than ask what they know: it reminds them that God is also not unaware of what they do.

This warning is not directed only at the People of the Book. Any religious community can fall into the same gap. We may recognize a truth in the Qur’an but take a different course in practice because it conflicts with inherited structures, religious habits, or our community’s interests. Knowing what is true is not sufficient by itself if it does not lead to orientation and action.

The lesson for us is the same: religious orientation involves more than knowing a truth; it requires readiness to move with it. The prophet waits, accepts the command, and turns his face; the community is commanded to take its direction with him. On the other side are people who “know,” and the Qur’an immediately recalls that the one God is not unaware of what they do.

Knowing draws closer to faith when people are willing to align their own direction with what they have recognized as true.
