Al-Baqarah 2:199

ۚ

Then depart from where the people departed, and ask The God for forgiveness; indeed, The God is Forgiving and Merciful.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, The God spoke of people departing from Arafat and remembering Him at the Sacred Monument. Now He again refers to movement during Hajj, but in terms important for understanding this worship’s place:

“ثُمَّ أَفِيضُوا مِنْ حَيْثُ أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ”

“Then depart from where the people departed.”

“أَفِيضُوا”, from the root ف ي ض, means flowing and streaming forth. Here a command is given to move along the Hajj route, and the place of this movement is specified by “مِنْ حَيْثُ أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ”—from where the people departed.

The important point, however, is the choice of the word “النَّاسُ”: people.

In Al-Baqarah 2:125, The God describes the House as “مَثَابَةً لِلنَّاسِ”—a place of return for people. In Aal Imran 3:96, He also calls the first House established for people “مُبَارَكًا وَهُدًى لِلْعَالَمِينَ”—blessed and a guidance for all peoples. In Al-Hajj 22:27, He commands: “وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ”—announce the Hajj among people. In Al-Hajj 22:25, the Sacred Mosque is also identified as being for people.

Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:199, He again says: “أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ”—the people departed.

If Hajj were meant to be exclusive to followers of one religious law or religious community, why did The God not say “أَفَاضَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ”—the believers departed—but instead say “أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ”—the people departed?

In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Quran speaks of different laws and paths prescribed for communities, and in Al-Hajj 22:67 it says:

“لِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ”

“For every community We have appointed rites that they perform.”

The Quran itself therefore recognizes differences in communities’ rites and religious laws. But this difference is not, by itself, a reason for placing The God’s House under the exclusive control of one religious law or denomination.

“Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Quran” examines the distinction between din and shariah. “Hajj in the Quran: Ibrahim’s Way, Rites, and People’s Rights” also examines in detail the House’s place, the universal invitation to Hajj, and exclusive claims concerning it.

The proponents of juristic opinion and hadith have, through religious exclusivism, diverted the meaning of Hajj and the House that The God established for people, and have even resorted to declaring Quranic verses abrogating and abrogated to justify their inherited rulings. These unsupported claims are of the same kind as the “amani” against which the Quran commands in Al-Baqarah 2:111: “هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ”—bring your proof. A critique of this approach appears in “Naskh in the Quran”.

The command “أَفِيضُوا مِنْ حَيْثُ أَفَاضَ النَّاسُ” also teaches another lesson: Hajj is no place for ethnic or religious superiority. Everyone attending this worship must observe its prescribed limits; no one should invent privileges over others on the basis of ancestry, social standing, or religious affiliation.

The God continues:

“وَاسْتَغْفِرُوا اللَّهَ”

“And ask The God for forgiveness.”

After commanding movement along with people, The God commands seeking forgiveness.

“Istighfar”, from the root غ ف ر, is connected with covering and forgiveness. In seeking forgiveness, people attend to their shortcomings and errors and ask The God to pardon them.

This command has particular significance amid the Hajj rites. Someone who has performed an act of worship must not imagine that completing a few outward actions removes any need to reform their thinking and conduct.

In the preceding verse, The God reminded those addressed that they too had been among those astray before guidance. Now He commands the same people to seek forgiveness. This continuity calls people to humility.

How can someone who once needed guidance and still needs forgiveness boast over others and consider themselves immune to error and misguidance?

In An-Nisa 4:94, The God also reminds believers: “كَذَٰلِكَ كُنْتُمْ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَمَنَّ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ”—you too were like that before, then The God bestowed His favor upon you. In that same verse, this reminder is connected with the command to investigate and avoid hasty judgments about others’ faith.

Someone who has received the grace of guidance should therefore be humbler and fairer toward others, not more arrogant.

Seeking forgiveness is not merely uttering a phrase. Someone who has violated another’s rights must strive to make amends. Asking forgiveness must not take the place of correcting conduct and restoring people’s rights.

Finally, The God says:

“إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ”

“Indeed, The God is Forgiving and Merciful.”

After commanding people to seek forgiveness, The God describes Himself as Forgiving and Merciful. He leaves open the way to return and reform.

A person may previously have been caught in prejudice, self-superiority, or mistaken judgments about others; but they can turn away from their error, reassess their inherited beliefs, and correct their conduct.

We should not desire this divine mercy only for ourselves. Someone hoping for The God’s forgiveness should also avoid hastily condemning, humiliating, and excluding other human beings.

In this verse, The God speaks of moving along with people and then commands seeking forgiveness. Hajj must not become a means of asserting superiority and separating people from one another. Someone moving with people toward The God must be humble before Him, recognize their errors, and not despair of His mercy.

Ibrahim’s House is a place of servanthood to The God; true servanthood is incompatible with arrogance, exclusivism, and violations of others’ rights.
