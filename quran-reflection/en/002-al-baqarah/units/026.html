Al-Baqarah 2:26

Indeed, the One God does not hold back from setting forth a parable, even of a mosquito or what is above it. As for those who have faith, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. But as for those who cover truth, they say, “What did the One God intend by this as a parable?” Through it He leads many astray and through it He guides many; and He does not lead astray through it except the defiantly departing.

Our Reflection

After portraying reward and consequence in the previous verses, this verse enters a deeper layer: the layer of knowledge and the way meaning is received. The Qur’an is not only speaking about an example here; it is speaking about the very mechanism of giving examples and the reaction of human beings before the language of guidance. For this reason, the verse may appear sudden on the surface, but within the structure of the surah it is exactly in place. After showing truth, consequence, reward, and the linguistic challenge, the Qur’an now explains why one statement becomes guidance for one group and a cause of misguidance for another.

The phrase “Indeed, the One God does not hold back” shows that, in expressing truth, there is no contraction, hesitation, or cautious avoidance of small examples. The root Ḥ Y Y is originally connected with life, livingness, freshness, and flow. At the human level, haya can mark a living inner structure and a kind of moral contraction that preserves existential boundaries. But the verse says that the One God does not retreat from conveying truth by giving an example as small as a mosquito. The criterion here is not the outward greatness of the example, but its power to carry meaning.

The choice of “mosquito” is very precise. A superficial mind may ask how divine speech could give the example of a mosquito. But that very question reveals a human error in perception. Human beings sometimes see greatness in size, volume, and outward splendor, rather than in order, structure, and precision. Despite its smallness, the mosquito is a living, complex, and precise system. So the smallness of an example does not show that its meaning is low. It may be that a small example unveils a greater order.

The verse then shows two reactions to one parable. Those who have faith do not stop at the example; they pass through the example and recognize truth in it: “that it is the truth from their Lord.” But those who cover truth do not search for the meaning. They remain at the level of objection and say: “What did the One God intend by this as a parable?” Their problem is not only lack of understanding; it is a kind of resistance to the way truth is expressed.

Here a major principle of knowledge is stated: “Through it He leads many astray and through it He guides many.” One text, one parable, and one sign can produce different outcomes in different human beings. This does not indicate a flaw in the text. It shows that the reception of meaning also depends on the inner condition of the receiver. The same sign that lights the path for one person can become an excuse for denial in another.

To bring this meaning closer to the mind today, we can use a contemporary analogy. Revelation and Qur’anic parables are, in one respect, like a single input given to different processing systems. If we give one prompt to several AI systems, the outputs will not be identical. A system built on precision, logic, clean data, and sound calculation will produce a different answer from a system tuned for attention, profit, bias, or a particular agenda. The input is one, but the processing structure is different; therefore the output becomes different as well.

The human being works in a similar way. If a person approaches the verses with truthfulness, humility, readiness for reform, reason, recognition, an awakened conscience, and an elevated view of the Lord, the same parable bears the fruit of guidance. But if a person approaches the text with inherited bias, tribal pride, fear, selfishness, hidden interests, pre-judgment, and a closed, unreformable faith, the same parable can produce corruption and rupture in them. So the verse teaches us that the problem is not always in the sign. Sometimes the problem is in the receiver’s structure of reception.

The ending, “and He does not lead astray through it except the fasiqin,” is the key to this section. “Fasiqin” here does not only mean sinners in a narrow legal sense. The root F S Q is originally connected with coming out of a shell, covering, or preserving frame, like something bursting out of its casing. In this verse, the fasiq is one who has exited the preserving frame of truth, reason, humility, and meaningful coherence. Such a person no longer receives meaning within its connected field. They receive it in fragments, disconnected and polluted by their own assumptions.

Therefore verse 26 shows that the Qur’an does not only deliver a message; it also exposes the structure by which the human being receives. The divine parable is like a mirror that reveals the inner state of the listener. The faithful recognize in it the truth from their Lord, while the coverer of truth turns it into an excuse for objection. Guidance and misguidance in this verse arise from the encounter between the text and the human inner structure; and final misguidance occurs only for those who have departed from the frame of truth.
