Al-Baqarah 2:54

And when Musa said to his people: O my people, you have wronged your own selves (anfusakum) by taking the calf. So return in repentance (tūbū) to your Originator (bāri'ikum) and put to death the calf-making selves within you (faqtulū anfusakum). That is better for you before your Originator. So He turned back to you; indeed, He is the Ever-Accepting of return and the Merciful.

Our Reflection

This verse is one of the most sensitive verses in this section, because both in the history of the Children of Israel and in the history of Muslim interpretation, it has been greatly misused. The outward phrase faqtulū anfusakum, if separated from the Qur'anic context, can be understood as a command for suicide, collective killing, or slaughtering the calf-worshipers. But when we read the verse alongside the verses before and after it, its meaning moves in another direction.

In verse 51, the Children of Israel took the calf after Musa's absence and stood in the position of wrongdoing. In verse 52, God pardoned them after that, so that they might give thanks. In verse 53, Musa was given the Book and the criterion, so that the people might be guided. Now in verse 54, Musa, with that same criterion, shows his people where the root of the disease lies and what the way back is. So this verse stands in the atmosphere of pardon, gratitude, guidance, criterion, and repentance, not in the atmosphere of bloody revenge.

Musa begins the speech with yā qawmi: O my people. This address is not the address of an enemy to an enemy. Musa is speaking to a people who have been rescued from Pharaoh, have crossed the sea, have taken the calf, and must now return to the way. He does not separate them from himself. He calls them with a close and corrective tone. This beginning of the verse already shows that Musa's speech is about training and bringing the people back, not declaring war against them.

Then he says: innakum ẓalamtum anfusakum bi-ittikhādhikum al-ʿijl. You wronged your own selves by taking the calf. This sentence is the key to understanding the verse. The Qur'an does not first say that they wronged others; it says they wronged their own selves. This means that calf-worship, before being only an external error, was an inner pollution. They lowered their human reality, did not place the ni'mah of rescue in its proper place, and bent their hearts away from the One who is worshiped toward a made form.

So when a few words later it says faqtulū anfusakum, we must ask: to what does this same anfusakum return? The verse itself answers: to the same selves that were wronged by taking the calf. This means that the subject of the verse from the beginning to this point is the polluted self: the self that, after seeing all those signs, still clung to a tangible, sounding, handmade form.

This is exactly where the Torah-based and report-based reading changed the direction of the verse. In Exodus, the story of the calf is narrated with the command to fasten swords, kill brother, friend, and neighbor, and the death of thousands. Then this Torah atmosphere, through Israelite reports and interpretation built on opinion and narration, cast its shadow over the Qur'anic verse. Many said that the meaning of faqtulū anfusakum is that the Children of Israel were commanded to kill one another or to destroy the calf-worshipers from among themselves.

But the Qur'an does not narrate such a scene in this verse. It does not speak of swords, Levites, three thousand dead, or a command to kill brother and neighbor. The Qur'an does not say faqtulū ikhwanakum, kill your brothers. It says anfusakum, your own selves. A few words earlier it had also said ẓalamtum anfusakum, you wronged your own selves. So if our measure is the Qur'an itself, we must take this inner connection seriously, not impose the details of the Torah upon the Qur'anic text.

The very concept of repentance also does not fit the reading of collective slaughter. Repentance in the Qur'an means returning: returning from the path of error to God, changing direction, leaving sin, repairing, and continuing life in a more rightly guided way. Repentance has meaning through staying alive, waking up, repairing, and rebuilding the self. If the human being is killed or kills himself, he no longer has room in this world for gratitude, guidance, repair, and cleaning the path of life.

Verse 52 said: ʿafawnā ʿankum... laʿallakum tashkurūn, We pardoned you so that you may give thanks. Verse 53 said: We gave the Book and the criterion so that you may be guided. If in verse 54 there were a real command for physical slaughter of the same calf-worshipers, the connection of the verses would become difficult and inconsistent. Pardon for gratitude, the Book and criterion for guidance, and then the slaughter of the very people who are supposed to experience gratitude and guidance? This does not harmonize with the movement of the verses.

Even the following verses strengthen this meaning. In verse 55, the same current falls into another error and says: We will not believe you until we see God openly. If the calf-worshipers had been massacred in verse 54 and only the pure had remained, why does the same desire for the tangible and the same demand to see continue again? This shows that the Qur'an is showing the training of a people: a people who must repeatedly pass beyond the sensory-seeking, idol-making, appearance-worshiping self.

Verse 56 is also worth contemplating in the same path: thumma baʿathnākum min baʿdi mawtikum laʿallakum tashkurūn. If we turn the killing of the self in verse 54 into bodily slaughter, we are forced to take “We raised you after your death” toward bodily resurrection after a collective death. But in the context of pardon, repentance, gratitude, and guidance, one can see that the Qur'an also speaks about the death and raising of an inner and collective condition: the death of that self polluted by the calf, and rising again for gratitude. This reading is more harmonious with the educational movement of the verses.

So faqtulū anfusakum in this context is closer to the language of purifying the self than killing the body. Killing in its root is not limited only to shedding blood; it also carries the meaning of ending, disabling, destroying the effect of a force, and making a movement ineffective. When the subject of the verse is the self polluted by the calf, the clearer meaning becomes: kill within yourselves that calf-seeking self; disable that desire for a tangible form, that haste to make an object of worship, that need for something visible and touchable, that collective imitation, and that hidden idol-making.

This is the same criterion that had been given in the previous verse. The criterion means the ability to separate and discern. With the criterion, Musa makes the people distinguish between their true self and the self that has been polluted. He does not say that you are essentially destroyed. He says that by taking the calf, you wronged your own selves. So return from that self and kill it. This killing is not the killing of human beings; it is the killing of pollution. It is not killing the body; it is ending the life of the idol inside the self.

The word bāri'ikum, your Originator, is also very important in this verse. Musa does not say only return to God or to your Rabb. He says: return to your Originator. Bāri' comes from the root B R A, a root tied to creating, bringing into being, separating from impurity, and bringing a being out from a mixed condition. It is as if Musa says: you polluted your self with the calf; now return to the One who brought you into being in purity and can separate you again from this pollution.

This expression is completely harmonious with faqtulū anfusakum. The issue is not merely verbal regret; the issue is rebuilding the self. The self that took the calf must return to its Originator so that it can again be separated from pollution. The human being must separate from himself that part which clung to the calf. Repentance here is not a sentence; it is cutting away from a polluted self.

So dhālikum khayrun lakum ʿinda bāri'ikum means: that is better for you before your Originator. What is better? Returning to the Originator and killing the polluted self. Your good is not that the human appearance remains alive while the calf remains alive inside. Your good is that the part of the self that carried you from tawhid to image-worship is killed. If the inner idol is not killed, even after the outer idol is broken, the human being will build another idol again.

Here the meaning of ʿinda bāri'ikum, before your Originator, is also weighty. The measure of good is not the view of people, the report of a community, or a religious justification; the measure is before the Originator. Perhaps the human self runs away from killing its own desires. Perhaps society wants to make calf-worship seem small. Perhaps religious reports want to place the sin only on a few individuals. But before the Originator, the real good is that the polluted self be repaired.

Then the verse says: fa-tāba ʿalaykum, so He turned back to you. This part strengthens the repentance-centered reading of the verse. The verse began with fa-tūbū, so return in repentance, and received its answer in fa-tāba ʿalaykum, so He turned back to you. You return to your Originator; then He turns back to you. If the center of the verse were physical slaughter, the end of the verse should have emphasized execution of punishment and destruction. But the end of the verse stands upon acceptance of repentance and the opening of mercy.

And at the end He says: innahu huwa al-tawwāb al-raḥīm. Indeed, He is the Ever-Accepting of return, the Merciful. This ending clarifies the atmosphere of the verse. God introduces Himself at the end of this verse with two names: the Ever-Accepting of return and the Merciful. Not Avenger, not severe in punishment, not Compeller, not Overwhelming Subduer. This does not mean that calf-worship was small; rather, it shows that the way out of it is understood through repentance and mercy, not by copying the blood-filled Torah narration onto the Qur'an.

Of course, killing the polluted self is not easy. The human being is sometimes willing to put his body through hardship, but not to kill his idol-making self. Killing this self means ending the centers that the human being has built in the place of God: the golden calf, the sacred leader, the sacred-looking report, the superior tribe, the immune sect, or anything that takes the place of truth in the heart and judgment of the human being.

For this reason, verse 54 is not only about an old event. Whenever the Book and the criterion are given, the first work of the criterion is to compel the human being to know his own self. The criterion is not only for criticizing others. With the criterion, Musa did not tell his people only to condemn the outer calf. He said: you wronged your own selves. This means that the root of idol-worship is inside you. If you do not kill it, the outer calf will go and another calf will come.

So this verse must not be turned into a command for religious killing. Such a reading is inconsistent with the context of pardon and guidance, inconsistent with the meaning of repentance in the Qur'an, inconsistent with the end of the verse where God is the Ever-Accepting and Merciful, and inconsistent with the following verses that show the continued training and repeated failures of the same people. Imposing the narration of Exodus and Israelite reports upon this verse pulls the Qur'an out of the path of mercy, repentance, and repair of the self, and turns it into a text of ritual violence.

As a result, faqtulū anfusakum means: return to your Originator and kill within yourselves that calf-making self. Kill the self that, even after seeing the sea, still wants a visible form; after pardon, still clings to sanctification; after the Book, still puts the criterion aside. This killing is the condition of true life. Because as long as the self polluted by the calf is alive, the human being, even if his body is alive, remains far from guidance.

Cross-References
  • Al-Baqarah 2:51— Taking the calf and the Children of Israel standing in wrongdoing; verse 54 explains that same wrongdoing against the self.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:52— Pardon after calf-worship; this context shows that verse 54 must be understood within pardon, gratitude, and return.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:53— Giving the Book and the criterion; verse 54 is an example of the criterion becoming active in diagnosing the self polluted by the calf.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:55— The people's later error in demanding to see God openly; showing that the issue is the continuing training and repeated testing of the people, not their physical end in verse 54.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:56— Being raised after death so that they may give thanks; this can be read in continuation of the death and revival of the people's inner and collective condition after the fall of the calf.
  • Al-A'raf 7:148-149— Taking the calf and then the people's regret; these verses clarify the atmosphere of remorse and return.
  • Al-A'raf 7:152-153— The consequence of taking the calf, and then mercy for those who repent from evil and believe; an important witness for the repentance-centered reading of verse 54.
  • Taha 20:88-90— The explanation of the calf, the speech of the Samiri, and Harun's warning that the people were tested by it and that their Lord is the Most Merciful.
  • An-Nisa 4:153— The Children of Israel took the calf after clear signs had come to them, then God pardoned that; a direct witness for pardon after calf-worship.
  • At-Tahrim 66:8— Sincere repentance; useful for understanding repentance as reforming return, not the physical destruction of the human being.
  • Al-Furqan 25:68-71— After major sins, the way of repentance, faith, and righteous action remains open; an important witness that repentance in the Qur'an has meaning through a repaired life.
  • Hud 11:3— Seeking forgiveness and turning to God is connected with good enjoyment until an appointed time; showing that repentance in this world is tied to continued life and repair.
  • Az-Zumar 39:53— Do not despair of God's mercy; God forgives all sins. A witness for the vastness of mercy before a great failure.
  • Exodus 32:27-28— The Torah narration of fastening swords, killing brother, friend, and neighbor, and around three thousand people being killed in the story of the calf.
  • Common interpretations and translations influenced by the Torah-based and report-based reading— Many have interpreted «فاقتلوا أنفسكم» as killing the calf-worshipers or slaughter among the Children of Israel. This reading must be re-weighed against the Qur'anic context of pardon, repentance, criterion, and mercy.
