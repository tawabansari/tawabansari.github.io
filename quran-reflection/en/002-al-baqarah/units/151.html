Al-Baqarah 2:151

Just as We sent among you a messenger from among yourselves, who recites Our signs to you, purifies you and helps you grow, teaches you the Book and wisdom, and teaches you what you did not previously know.

Our Reflection

At the end of the preceding verse, God spoke of completing His favor upon the addressees and guiding them. Now, with “just as,” He recalls sending a messenger from among those same people. This connection matters after the verses about the direction of prayer. The community turning toward Abraham’s House has among it a messenger whose raising is directly connected with Abraham and Ishmael’s prayer.

In Al-Baqarah 2:128, Abraham and Ishmael asked their Lord to bring forth a community surrendered to Him from their shared descendants. Then, in 2:129, they prayed that He raise among those people a messenger of their own who would recite God’s signs to them, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them and help them grow. The Qur’an’s precise wording matters: their joint prayer uses the singular “a messenger,” not the plural “messengers.” Now the same expressions and tasks return in verse 151. There, the messenger was “among them, from among them”; here, he is “among you, from among you.” What Abraham and Ishmael once asked of their Lord now finds an expression of its fulfillment in Muhammad’s mission.

This connection reveals another dimension of Abraham’s great standing. After divine trials, he was appointed a leader for people, raised the House’s foundations with Ishmael, and prayed for future generations. Now Muhammad’s presence and mission are also seen as a continuation of that prayer. Abraham’s legacy is more than a House toward which people turn; his path of guidance continues through later generations. Al Imran 3:164 likewise presents the raising of a messenger from among the believers as a great favor from God. For a broader examination of Abraham’s position, see Imamate in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Position and the Question of Leadership.

In Al-Jumu‘ah 62:2, the Qur’an connects this same mission with raising a messenger from among people previously without scripture, now being taught divine signs, the Book, and wisdom. The people of the Torah and Gospel, by contrast, had already received scripture and divine guidance. The Qur’an confirms the earlier revelation in their possession and describes itself as a guardian over it. In Al-Ma’idah 5:47, the People of the Gospel are called to judge by what God revealed in the Gospel. In 5:48, God identifies different prescribed ways and paths for communities and calls them to compete in good deeds. Muhammad’s mission brings scripture and divine teaching to people who previously had no scripture, while the prescribed way of the People of the Book retains its place; differences among these paths are not turned into coercion and enforced uniformity. For detailed discussion, see Faith in the Qur’an and Dīn, Islām, and Sharīʿah in the Qur’an.

The messenger’s task begins with reciting God’s signs, but does not end with reading words. He conveys the signs, purifies people and helps them grow, and teaches them the Book and wisdom. Purification is connected with both cleansing and growth: receiving guidance should lead people away from associating partners with God, arrogance, deception, and injustice, and toward growth in truthfulness, justice, and doing good. This has particular significance after the long discussion of the Children of Israel. They too had scripture and messengers, yet possessing scripture alone did not prevent broken covenants, concealed truth, or following personal desires. Divine teaching must bear fruit in human life. In Abraham and Ishmael’s prayer, teaching preceded purification; here, purification comes first. This difference also shows their interdependence: learning opens the way to purification, while freedom from prejudice and selfishness prepares a person to receive truth more fully.

Alongside teaching the Book, the verse mentions teaching wisdom. The Qur’an itself shows how to understand this wisdom. Luqman 31:2 describes the Book as wise, and Ya-Sin 36:2 describes the Qur’an as wise. Al-Baqarah 2:231 also speaks of the Book and wisdom that God has sent down. Most explicitly, after commands concerning devotion to God alone, ethics, and social life—including parents’ rights, those in need, justice, keeping commitments, and human responsibility—Al-Isra 17:39 identifies those teachings as wisdom revealed to Muhammad. The Qur’an thus presents clear examples of revealed wisdom within its own text. Divine wisdom is present in this Book: in truths, commands, and criteria to be understood and brought into human judgment and conduct. Understanding this wisdom does not require positing a hidden collection of rulings alongside the Qur’an. The Qur’an’s authority and reports attributed to the messenger are discussed more fully in Obeying the Messenger and the Authority of Hadith.

The ending again emphasizes teaching: “And teaches you what you did not previously know.” People previously unaware of many truths of revelation, the histories of prophets, divine commands, and criteria of guidance now gain, through the messenger’s mission, knowledge they did not possess before. Yet, as the history of the Children of Israel showed, receiving this favor does not itself guarantee lasting guidance. The Book must be understood, its wisdom applied in life, and purification made visible in conduct. The next verse therefore brings the command to remember God and give thanks.

Abraham and Ishmael’s prayer bore fruit in Muhammad’s mission. Gratitude for this favor is not fulfilled merely by taking pride in the messenger’s name or facing Abraham’s House. Faithfulness to Abraham’s way becomes visible in listening to the signs, learning the Book and wisdom, and making human life purer and more fruitful.
