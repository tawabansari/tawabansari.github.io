Al-Baqarah 2:169

He commands you only to evil and indecency, and to say about God what you do not know.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, God warned people against following Satan’s footsteps. This warning is not left unexplained: the Quran now shows the path to which Satan calls people—evil, indecency, and attributing statements to God without knowledge. “Suʾ” refers to badness and what brings harm and unwelcome consequences, while “fahsha” denotes grave ugliness in speech or conduct. But how is this grave ugliness recognized, and why does the Quran place it alongside speaking about God without knowledge?

To understand fahsha, we must examine the Quran’s own uses of the word. In Al-Isra 17:32, zina is called a fahishah; in An-Nisa 4:22, marrying women whom one’s fathers had married is condemned with the same description. Yet in Al-Baqarah 2:268, Satan threatens people with poverty and commands fahsha, in a context concerned with spending and giving good possessions. Its semantic field therefore extends beyond sexual conduct to grave moral and behavioral wrongs. The standard for recognizing these wrongs is not merely what an individual or society finds disagreeable; the nature of the act and its relationship to truth, justice, and God’s command must be understood.

For a deeper understanding of the warning in Al-Baqarah 2:169, the answer lies before us in the Quran itself, particularly in Al-Araf 7:28. God speaks of people who commit an indecency and offer two justifications: first, that they found their ancestors following that way, and second, that God commanded them to do it. The Quran’s response is unequivocal: God does not command indecency; are you attributing to Him what you do not know? Here, wrongdoing begins with following ancestral tradition and acquires a cloak of sanctity when that conduct is attributed to God.

The depth of the warning in Al-Baqarah 2:169 becomes apparent in this connection. The Quran places fahsha beside speaking about God without knowledge, and Al-Araf 7:28 shows how the two can come together in a single act. People may not only commit a wrong but also present that same wrong as God’s command. Their error then extends beyond their personal lives: in the Lord’s name, they govern others’ beliefs and conduct and close the way to criticism and reform. From this perspective, attributing unwarranted rulings and statements to God is itself one of the clearest and most dangerous manifestations of fahsha.

The importance of this responsibility is also stated explicitly in Al-Araf 7:169. God asks those who had inherited the Book whether its covenant had not been taken from them that they should attribute nothing but truth to God, even though they had read and studied its contents. Possessing the Book, bearing the title of religious scholar, or holding a position of leadership therefore gives no one authority to place their own opinion in the position of God’s word. In Al-Anam 6:144, after challenging fabricated prohibitions concerning livestock, the Quran also asks who is more unjust than someone who invents a lie against God to mislead people without knowledge. This is no longer merely a simple error in judging food or ritual; it is a grave injustice that misuses God’s name to lead others astray.

This danger also exists in our time. When religious authorities turn opinion, narration, and inherited interpretation into divine law without clear Quranic warrant, the same fundamental question arises: where has God given such a command? They may label music as fahsha, prohibit girls’ education under the same label, or describe peaceful expression of beliefs and invitations to other faiths as indecency and sin, although the Quran has not called these acts, in themselves, fahsha. No individual or institution, by virtue of religious office, has the right to declare forbidden in God’s name something He has not forbidden. If there is no clear evidence from revelation for such rulings, imposing them in God’s name is precisely the speaking without knowledge that this verse strongly condemns.

In Yunus 10:59, the Quran asks those who divide divine provision into lawful and forbidden whether God has authorized them or they are inventing falsehood against Him. An-Nahl 16:116 likewise warns against inventing declarations of lawful and forbidden and attributing them to God. Ash-Shura 42:21 even speaks of partners who prescribe religious matters for people without God’s permission. Legislation without authorization is therefore not merely a disagreement over a subsidiary ruling; it concerns God’s authority in religion and the danger of assigning partners to His position of sovereignty. People may research and express their views, but they have no right to impose their interpretations and conjectures on others as the Lord’s definitive command without warrant.

This deviation takes deeper root when later generations put the religious suppositions and wishes of their predecessors in place of God’s Book. Al-Baqarah 2:78 mentions people whose knowledge of the Book is confined to “amani” and conjecture, while Al-Baqarah 2:79 condemns those who present what their own hands have written as coming from God. Over time, such conduct can produce a system of rulings and rituals clothed in apparent sanctity, in which people trust only the speakers’ reputations and the antiquity of traditions instead of asking for divine evidence.

This is the danger revealed by the context of these verses in Al-Baqarah. After the warning against following Satan’s footsteps and the description of his commands, the next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:170, presents people who prefer their ancestors’ way when called to follow what God has sent down—even if those ancestors lacked understanding and guidance. Groundless following of predecessors can thus lead people to accept human speech as God’s word and even defend it against revelation itself.

The lesson of this verse is clear and decisive: one of the gravest wrongs is to present one’s conjecture, opinion, wish, or inherited tradition as God’s command and impose it on people in the name of religion. This does not safeguard religion; it encroaches upon The God’s position of sovereignty. Loyalty to God consists not only in carrying out His commands, but also in attributing no ruling or statement to Him without knowledge and warrant. A person may strictly avoid acts they themselves consider fahsha, yet, by inventing groundless rulings in the Lord’s name, commit the very grave wrong that the Quran identifies among Satan’s ways.
