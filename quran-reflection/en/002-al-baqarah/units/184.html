Al-Baqarah 2:184

ۚ ۚ ۖ ۚ ۖ

A limited number of days; whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. And upon those who endure it with difficulty there is a fidyah: food for a person in need. Whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them. And that you abstain is better for you, if you know.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, The God prescribed al-siyam for believers and identified attaining taqwa as its purpose. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:184, He speaks of a limited number of days, the circumstances of the sick and travelers, human capacity, and the provision of fidyah. Yet the expression “وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ” has received different interpretations throughout history—interpretations with very different consequences not only for understanding the verse but also for determining people’s actual obligations.

To understand this disagreement, we must first examine the meaning of yutiqunahu. It comes from the root ط و ق, associated with a collar, encirclement, limitation, and capacity to endure. Capacity does not mean only ordinary ability; it can also express the extent and limit of human strength. In his Mufradat, al-Raghib al-Isfahani describes al-taqah as the measure of work a person can perform with difficulty.

The distinction between ordinary ability and enduring a difficult task is clear in human life. One person may carry a load easily, while another can move the same load only by exerting all their strength and enduring considerable hardship. Both can perform the task, but their circumstances and capacities are not the same.

The verb ataqa, from which yutiqunahu is formed, belongs to this same semantic field of ability and endurance. Thus, when the Quran speaks of yutiqunahu and fidyah alongside siyam, we must distinguish between being able to do something comfortably and enduring it with severe difficulty.

Three different readings of this expression have been proposed concerning the relationship between siyam, ability, and fidyah.

First reading: siyam and feeding, two concurrent obligations

In this reading, the verse is understood and translated as follows:

“A limited number of days; whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. And upon those able to perform it, [in addition to siyam] there is a fidyah: food for a person in need. Whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them. And that you practice siyam is better for you, if you know.”

Here, yutiqunahu refers to those able to perform siyam. Feeding a person in need is also considered an obligation accompanying siyam: the able person does both. The bracketed addition is an interpretive conclusion of this reading, not a separate expression in the Arabic text. The sick and travelers are also expected to make up siyam on other days.

Second reading: a choice between siyam and feeding, followed by a claim of abrogation

In this reading, the verse is understood and translated as follows:

“A limited number of days; whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. And upon those able to perform it, [if they choose not to practice siyam] there is a fidyah: food for a person in need. Whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them. And that you practice siyam is better for you, if you know.”

This reading also understands yutiqunahu as the ability to perform siyam, but treats fidyah as a substitute: an able person may choose either to perform siyam or to feed a person in need. The qualification added to the translation expresses this interpretive choice.

According to some historical reports, this choice existed initially but was later abrogated by the revelation of Al-Baqarah 2:185. Some commentators have regarded “فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ” as abrogating that choice. In some reports, even “وَأَنْ تَصُومُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ” within Al-Baqarah 2:184 itself has been invoked to abrogate the earlier choice.

Third reading: fidyah for those for whom siyam lies at the limit of their endurance

In this reading, the verse is translated as follows:

“A limited number of days; whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. And upon those who endure it with difficulty there is a fidyah: food for a person in need. Whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them. And that you abstain is better for you, if you know.”

Here, in light of the semantic field of endurance, yutiqunahu is understood to concern those for whom performing siyam lies at the limit of their ability and involves severe hardship. Fidyah is understood as a means of compensation for this situation.

The linguistic possibility of this reading follows from the meaning of taqah and the form yutiqunahu. Yutiqunahu is an affirmative form of ataqa and denotes ability to perform an action; yet that ability may involve hardship and lie at the limit of a person’s endurance. Thus, “الَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ” can be understood as referring to those for whom siyam requires exerting their capacity and enduring considerable difficulty. There is no need to add a negation to the verb: the distinction between easy ability and difficult endurance is examined through the word’s own semantic field and the context of the fidyah ruling.

We must now consider which of these three readings is most consistent with the Quran’s language, the logic of its rulings, justice, and the actual circumstances of human life.

1. The problem of the meaning of fidyah and its relationship to siyam itself

In the first reading, feeding a person in need is an additional obligation alongside siyam. But the Quran calls this feeding fidyah. Fidyah is associated with compensation, substitution, and release from a particular obligation or situation.

In Al-Baqarah 2:196, The God also describes fidyah under specific conditions of illness or an ailment of the head:

“فَفِدْيَةٌ مِنْ صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ”

“Then a fidyah of siyam, charity, or a sacrificial rite.”

This verse shows that, within the Quran’s system of rulings, fidyah can take different forms, and siyam and charity can serve as means of compensation in specified circumstances. The connection of fidyah with worship and generosity is therefore not unprecedented in the Quran.

If Al-Baqarah 2:184 meant that all able people must feed a person in need in addition to performing siyam, why is this feeding called fidyah? And if feeding is a universal and binding obligation, how does it differ from the voluntary good immediately mentioned in “فَمَنْ تَطَوَّعَ خَيْرًا”?

In the second reading, too, fidyah first becomes an option for an able person, and abrogation is then invoked to end that option. But this reading must explain where The God states such a general choice and then its abrogation.

2. The problem of abrogation and its inconsistency with the Quran’s completeness and coherence

Abrogation goes beyond a simple disagreement over translation. In An-Nisa 4:82, the Quran calls people to reflect and says that, had this Book come from anyone other than The God, they would have found much inconsistency in it. Al-Anam 6:115 speaks of the completion of the Lord’s word in truth and justice, and Al-Kahf 18:27 declares that no one can change The God’s words.

These verses guide us toward a fundamental principle: when encountering two Quranic commands, our first responsibility is to understand their relationship and compatibility, not to declare one invalid because we cannot understand that relationship.

The God, of course, possesses authority to command and can change a ruling, lighten it, or set its endpoint. But how has The God’s authority to legislate and alter commands been transferred to a commentator or jurist? Who has authorized a human being to declare an explicit verse’s ruling abrogated on the basis of a report or personal interpretation?

In Al-Baqarah 2:184, the ruling on fidyah is clearly stated. The following verse also gives the command of siyam. If these rulings concern different human circumstances, no contradiction exists that requires abrogation to resolve it. Why, then, should we first assume a contradiction and then set aside one of the two rulings?

This issue is examined in detail in the article “Abrogation in the Quran: A Critique of Setting Aside Verses in the Name of Abrogation”. The fundamental distinction is that The God may change a command, but people have no right to attribute their inability to reconcile verses to The God under the name of abrogation.

It is striking that some proponents of opinion and transmitted reports, seeking to impose their interpretation, have turned to declaring a Quranic command abrogated rather than revisiting their interpretation. This is no longer merely a lexical disagreement: human opinion is being placed in judgment over the validity of The God’s ruling.

A verse that The God has included in His Book continues to be recited, yet people are told that its ruling is no longer valid. Where is the authority for this claim? If the Quran itself has not announced the ruling’s end, how can quoting a report or repeating an earlier opinion justify attributing such a major claim to The God?

3. The problem of chronic illness, continuous travel, and exhausting occupations

When we move from theoretical discussion to people’s actual lives, the difficulties of these readings become clearer.

Consider someone whose chronic illness prevents them, both now and in the foreseeable future, from performing siyam without serious harm. If their only option is to transfer the obligation to other days, when should they fulfill it? Changing the timing of an obligation solves the problem only if fulfilling it later is possible.

The same question arises for someone who travels continually because of their work and circumstances. If their journeys are continuous or very long and no suitable opportunity to make up the days arises, must the unfulfilled days remain an endless burden upon them?

Differences in living conditions must not be overlooked either. Imagine a poor boy who works ten hours a day in a coal mine to provide for his family, amid heat, dust, and intense physical strain. Contrast him with someone born into privilege, sitting at a desk in cool, air-conditioned surroundings with ample comfort.

Do these two people have the same ability to refrain from food and drink for long periods? Is it just to disregard their actual circumstances and impose the same manner of obligation on both, without considering physical capacity, working conditions, and risk of harm?

A poor person may not even have the freedom to leave an exhausting job. If they must continue working to support their family, and prolonged abstention from water and food endangers their health or life, why should they be trapped between providing for their family, protecting their health, and worshiping?

Human equality before The God does not mean identical circumstances and physical capacities. A conception of justice that disregards people’s actual circumstances can, in practice, impose hardship on the vulnerable and greater ease on those with resources.

4. The problem of very long days and fatwas beyond the Quranic text

The difficulty becomes clearer in the earth’s northern regions. In parts of Sweden and other lands near the pole, summer days are very long; in some places, there are periods when the sun does not set or ordinary nighttime darkness and the usual signs of dawn do not appear.

In Al-Baqarah 2:185, The God connects the command of siyam with witnessing the month through “فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ”. In Al-Baqarah 2:187, He describes its starting and ending signs as follows:

“وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ”

“And eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread.”

He then says:

“ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ”

“Then complete siyam until night.”

Yet some proponents of juristic opinion and hadith, when their inherited interpretations reach an impasse before the earth’s actual conditions, have produced new fatwas from their own conjectures and opinions instead of returning to the Quran’s own wise provision. Sometimes they have said that people in these regions should align their siyam hours with Saudi Arabia and Mecca; sometimes they have used a neighboring country or region as the standard. Others have required enduring the very long days unless another excuse is established.

But where has The God said that a person must use Mecca’s clock or a neighboring country’s timetable as the standard for worship instead of the signs stated in the Quran? Where is the authority for these commands?

Even if such fatwas were issued to alleviate difficulty, the fundamental problem remains: why first close the path of fidyah through a restrictive interpretation or a claim of abrogation, and then introduce a new ruling from outside the Quran to resolve an impasse created by that very approach?

This is the danger of constructing a parallel religious law: inherited understanding becomes the standard instead of submission to The God’s ruling, and commands absent from the Quranic text are created to preserve it.

Was The God, who created the earth, its movement, the changing seasons, and diverse geographical conditions, unaware that such lands existed? Are we to imagine that He prescribed siyam but forgot to determine the obligation of people in part of the earth, leaving jurists to complete it later with the clocks of Mecca and neighboring countries?

Or did the problem begin when people, by following opinion and transmitted reports, disregarded the path of ease and compensation stated in the Quran itself?

5. The problem of opposing the principle of ease and the limits of human capacity

Later in this same passage, in Al-Baqarah 2:185, The God explicitly says:

“يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ”

“The God desires ease for you and does not desire hardship for you.”

Al-Hajj 22:78 also says:

“وَمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ مِنْ حَرَجٍ”

“And He has placed no constraint upon you in religion.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, He says:

“لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا”

“The God does not burden any soul beyond its capacity.”

Later in the same verse, His servants pray:

“رَبَّنَا وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ”

“Our Lord, do not impose upon us what we have no strength to bear.”

These verses give us clear criteria for understanding divine obligation. The God asks for effort, devotion, and perseverance, but does not obligate people beyond their capacity and ability.

The ordinary difficulty of worship differs from overwhelming, harmful hardship. In Al-Baqarah 2:173, too, the Quran opens a path of relief in necessity and assigns no sin to someone who does not exceed its limits. Thus, actual human circumstances and the necessity of preserving life are not irrelevant to the logic of divine rulings.

If our interpretation of siyam leads to the conclusion that a chronically ill person, a worker exposed to extreme heat, or someone living with very long days must continue the practice contrary to their actual capacity, we should reassess our understanding before attributing such an obligation to The God.

6. The problem of exposing people to sin and disobedience

A dangerous consequence of these interpretations is that a person may needlessly be trapped between two situations: harming their body and life to perform worship, or living with the belief that they are disobeying The God.

If someone genuinely cannot perform siyam, why should they be regarded as sinful? If the Quran itself has opened a means of compensation for that situation, closing it through a report, fatwa, or claim of abrogation exposes people to disobedience of a requirement that The God has not imposed upon them in that form.

Worship prescribed for attaining taqwa should not become a means of trapping the vulnerable in fear, sin, and harm. The problem is not The God’s command, but an interpretation that disregards His provision of ease.

7. The problem of inconsistency with the Quran’s clarity and the place of its firmly established verses

In An-Nahl 16:89, the Quran describes itself as “تِبْيَانًا لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ”, and in Aal Imran 3:7 it speaks of firmly established verses that are the foundation of the Book. Al-Anam 6:114 likewise speaks of a Book sent down in detail.

This explanatory character does not mean that the Quran must separately name every illness, occupation, and geographical region. A wise command can encompass many different human circumstances by setting out clear principles.

But if the Quran has addressed siyam itself, illness, travel, fidyah, the limits of human capacity, and ease, why should we assume that a parallel religious law is needed to resolve the fundamental questions of this very ruling?

Does the problem lie in an inadequacy of the Quran’s explanation, or in an interpretation unable to bring its commands together?

In the article “Logic and Authority in the Quran”, we have examined how a claim, however famous or inherited, cannot replace evidence. Anyone attributing a ruling to The God must have clear authority and proof for it.

Thus, neither declaring a Quranic command abrogated nor imposing new time standards and obligations can be justified solely by a report’s fame, a jurist’s standing, or a religious school’s custom. Human rulings must be measured against The God’s command; The God’s command must not be made subordinate to human rulings.

If we now assess the three readings introduced at the outset by these criteria, we can see how the third reading may resolve the difficulties of the first two.

In this reading, siyam remains prescribed. Those able to perform it fulfill the command. Those unable to perform it at the appointed time because of illness or travel make it up on other days. But those for whom siyam lies at the limit of endurance, involves severe hardship or serious harm, and whose difficulty is not resolved by moving it to other days have the path of fidyah before them.

Here, the significance of yutiqunahu becomes clear. The word concerns endurance and the extent of human capacity: a situation in which a person must exert themselves to the limit of endurance to perform a task. When this expression occurs alongside “فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ”, the ruling can be understood to concern those for whom siyam involves overwhelming difficulty. Fidyah is then a means of compensation, not an additional obligation for all able people, nor a ruling that must be declared abrogated to uphold the following verse.

This understanding distinguishes ordinary difficulty in worship from actual incapacity or severe hardship. Not every instance of fatigue or minor discomfort puts someone in a situation of overwhelming hardship. But when siyam entails serious harm, enduring physical limitations, or circumstances beyond a person’s actual ability, their situation cannot be equated with that of a healthy person in favorable conditions.

Such circumstances are not confined to old age. Chronic illness, continuous or lengthy travel, work in mines and factories with extreme heat, and life in geographical regions with difficult daylight conditions may confront people with questions of ability and endurance. The criterion is not occupation, age, country, or social status: it is the person’s actual capacity in relation to the obligation.

Another wisdom also lies in this ruling: the connection between devotional abstention and feeding a person in need.

Those able to practice siyam cultivate their will and taqwa through conscious abstention. Those unable to do so because of incapacity or severe hardship contribute through fidyah to feeding a person in need. Thus, on the very days when some refrain from food and drink, a better meal may reach the table of someone in need.

What a beautiful balance between individual worship and social responsibility! One person approaches taqwa by restraining their desires, while another follows the path of goodness and ihsan by feeding someone in need. The person with limited capacity is neither excluded from responsibility nor forced to harm themselves to prove their devotion. Both can pursue good in accordance with their abilities and circumstances.

The Quran then says: “فَمَنْ تَطَوَّعَ خَيْرًا فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَهُ”—whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them.

Tatawwu, from the root ط و ع, is connected with acceptance and acting willingly and eagerly. In the context of fidyah, a person may give more food than the prescribed amount or help more people in need. But voluntary goodness must not be turned into another compulsory obligation. The Quran distinguishes between what is prescribed and what someone willingly does beyond it.

At the end, it says: “وَأَنْ تَصُومُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ”—that you practice siyam is better for you, if you know.

This statement preserves the value of siyam. The existence of fidyah does not make devotional abstention worthless. For someone able to perform it, siyam offers an opportunity to practice self-restraint and attain taqwa, the very purpose stated in Al-Baqarah 2:183. Yet the superiority of a devotional act for an able person does not justify imposing that same act on someone for whom it is overwhelming or harmful.

Even within the history of interpretation, this reading is not without precedent. Reports attributed to Ibn Abbas and others apply fidyah to elderly people with limited capacity and those for whom siyam is difficult. In Tafsir al-Manar, Muhammad Abduh also examines severe hardship and the circumstances of people whose livelihood depends on exhausting work, including coal miners. Mehdi Bazargan likewise understood “وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ” as concerning those who lose their capacity to endure.

These reports are not sources of religious authority for us. They do, however, show that connecting fidyah with incapacity and severe hardship is neither unprecedented nor a modern invention. The final validity of this understanding must be assessed through the Quran’s own language and its consistency with other divine commands.

Under this assessment, the third reading offers a coherent path. Siyam itself remains in force; sick people and travelers who can make it up do so on other days; and those unable to perform it because of enduring limitations or overwhelming hardship can benefit from fidyah.

There is then no need to declare fidyah abrogated, to substitute the hours of Mecca or other countries for the Quran’s time signs as a divine command, or to obligate someone to perform harmful worship. Nor is there a need to construct new rulings outside the Quranic text to solve difficulties created by a restrictive interpretation.

The God prescribed siyam to bring people to taqwa, not to trap them in an obligation beyond their capacity. By preserving worship itself and the validity of fidyah, the third reading provides a way for justice, mercy, responsibility, and ease to remain together. The able person worships through abstention, while the person unable to do so feeds someone in need and follows the same path of goodness and ihsan.

The problem arises when, instead of measuring their understanding against The God’s clear commands, people try to fit His command to their inherited interpretation—even if this requires declaring a verse abrogated or imposing a religious obligation on people without clear authority from the Quran.

At the beginning of the verse, The God speaks of “أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ”: days that are numbered, counted, and limited. Some researchers who study the Quran independently of outside sources, citing uses of ma‘dud and ma‘dudat in verses such as Yusuf 12:20, Al-Baqarah 2:80, and Aal Imran 3:24, have concluded that this expression refers only to a few days, usually between two and ten, and cannot encompass the whole month of Ramadan. This interpretation merits examination because ma‘dudat in Arabic can suggest a small number; however, suggesting a small number does not establish a definite limit of two to ten days. The Quran itself does not establish that the feminine plural ma‘dudat is confined to such a fixed numerical range in all its uses.

The Quran itself shows that the extent of the numbered days must be understood from each verse’s context. For example, Al-Baqarah 2:203 says, “وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ فِي أَيَّامٍ مَعْدُودَاتٍ”, and then speaks of someone who hastens within two days and someone who delays. Here, too, “أَيَّامٍ مَعْدُودَاتٍ” does not by itself determine the exact number of days; the continuation of the verse speaks of two days and the possibility of delaying within that period. Al-Baqarah 2:184 likewise mentions numbered days, but the following verse, by introducing “شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ” and commanding “فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ”, connects the period of siyam with Ramadan, and then emphasizes completing the prescribed count through “وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ”. We therefore cannot derive a few days of our own choosing from ma‘dudat alone and disregard the clear qualification in the next verse.

Another wisdom can also be seen in this choice of wording. In Al-Araf 7:142, concerning Musa’s appointed meeting, the Quran speaks of thirty nights completed to forty by ten additional nights. There, the subject is an appointment with a specified count; with siyam, however, the duration of worship is tied to a lunar month. Lunar months do not always contain a fixed number of days: they may be twenty-nine or thirty days long. Moreover, identifying the end of the month is not always easy. On cloudy or rainy nights, when the crescent cannot be seen, a person may be unable to determine by direct observation whether the day just passed was the month’s last or whether another day remains. The issue is therefore not merely counting the days that have passed; knowing the full number of days in that particular month also depends on identifying its end. Setting a fixed number for every Ramadan would disregard this actual variation, whereas “أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ”, together with the qualification “شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ”, ties the number of days to the duration of that particular month.

Here the precision of the Quran’s wording becomes apparent. The God has neither prescribed a fixed number of twenty-nine or thirty for every year nor left the duration of siyam to people’s choice of a few days. The days are numbered, but their full count is connected with the month the Quran has specified. Just as Al-Baqarah 2:189 presents lunar crescents as time markers for people, here too the lunar month forms the basis for determining the duration. Thus, variation in the month’s length and possible difficulty in identifying its end matter when understanding the relationship between ma‘dudat and Ramadan.

This issue is also connected with the principle of the Quran’s clarity and explanatory character. In An-Nahl 16:89, The God speaks of the Book’s explanatory role, and in Aal Imran 3:7 He identifies the firmly established verses as its foundation. Is it right to read “أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ” apart from the continuation of The God’s words, construct a numerical limit that the verse itself does not set, and then disregard “فَلْيَصُمْهُ” and “وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ”? The Quran must be understood through the continuity of its own verses. The numbered days in Al-Baqarah 2:184 are clarified by the identification of Ramadan in Al-Baqarah 2:185, not by imposing a number taken from the use of the same word in another context. This is the difference between following the Quran’s complete logic and constructing a ruling from only part of its evidence.
