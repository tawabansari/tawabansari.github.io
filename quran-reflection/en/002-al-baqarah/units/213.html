Al-Baqarah 2:213

ۚ ۖ ۗ

People were one community; then The God raised up the prophets as bearers of good news and warners, and sent down with them the Book in truth, to judge between people concerning their disagreements. And none differed over it except those who had been given it, after clear evidence had reached them, out of transgression among themselves. Then The God, by His permission, guided those who believed to the truth over which they had differed; and The God guides whomever He wills to a straight path.

Our Reflection

“كَانَ النَّاسُ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً”

“People were one community.”

“Ummah,” from the root A M M, relates to intention, orientation, and gathering around something shared. People can form a single community around a belief, goal, or set of values, but such unity does not mean that all thoughts are identical or that disagreement has ended.

Al-Muminun 23:52 speaks of one community and one Lord, yet in Al-Muminun 23:53 that same community splits into groups, each content with what it has. Al-Anbiya 21:92 and Al-Anbiya 21:93 show this relationship as well. Yunus 10:19 states it even more plainly: people were one community, then they differed.

Today, too, people gather for justice, freedom, or a shared goal, yet may later disagree over how to interpret those very principles, how to implement them, leadership, and interests.

Thus, one community is a body of people with a shared bond and orientation, not a community in which disagreement is impossible.

“فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ النَّبِيِّينَ مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنْذِرِينَ وَأَنْزَلَ مَعَهُمُ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ فِيمَا اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ”

“Then The God raised up the prophets as bearers of good news and warners, and sent down with them the Book in truth, to judge between people concerning their disagreements.”

Disagreement existed before the Book was sent down. The Book came so that people would have a standard beyond personal desires and group interests for recognizing truth and judging justly.

Yet God did not compel people to think alike in everything. In Al-Maidah 5:48, despite the differences in the communities’ laws and paths, He calls them to compete in good deeds.

Therefore, difference and disagreement themselves are not the problem; the problem arises when people sacrifice truth and justice to their own desires.

“وَمَا اخْتَلَفَ فِيهِ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ أُوتُوهُ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْهُمُ الْبَيِّنَاتُ بَغْيًا بَيْنَهُمْ”

“And none differed over it except those who had been given it, after clear evidence had reached them, out of transgression among themselves.”

A different disagreement is addressed here. The first was among people in need of guidance; the second concerns the very Book sent down in truth, whose recipients have received clear evidence.

The issue is no longer simply the absence of a standard for recognizing truth. The Book and clear evidence have arrived, yet some dispute the truth out of transgression.

“Baghy,” from the root B GH Y, means seeking, but in this context it is a desire that crosses the bounds of what is right and becomes aggression and a pursuit of superiority.

The Quran therefore does not condemn every difference of opinion. Two well-intentioned people may understand a matter differently. But when the truth has become clear to a person and they refuse to accept it to preserve power, advantage, reputation, or prejudice, the disagreement is no longer an honest inquiry.

Aal Imran 3:19, Ash-Shura 42:14, and Al-Jathiyah 45:17 also connect disagreement after knowledge has arrived with “baghyan baynahum”—transgression among themselves.

In Aal Imran 3:23, the Quran speaks of people called to the judgment of God’s Book, yet a group turns away. In Al-Baqarah 2:79, it also condemns those who attribute their own writings to God to gain a benefit.

The great danger is that, instead of correcting their beliefs by the Book, people may try to interpret the Book to fit their own beliefs and interests.

This warning is not only about past communities. Every follower of the Quran must also ask: if the Quran’s clear words conflict with my inherited belief, which will I accept?

Not every disagreement, of course, indicates a conscious rejection of truth. People may err or be deceived, as we examined in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:209. What this verse condemns is disagreement out of transgression after clear evidence has arrived.

“فَهَدَى اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لِمَا اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ مِنَ الْحَقِّ بِإِذْنِهِ”

“Then The God, by His permission, guided those who believed to the truth over which they had differed.”

Despite people’s disagreements, truth does not disappear. Those who believed were guided to the truth under dispute.

In the separate study “Divine Permission in the Quran”, we have examined permission in relation to the laws, conditions, and established ways God has made govern creation. In this reading, human guidance is likewise inseparable from these divine ways.

In Yunus 10:100, faith is made dependent on God’s permission, and the same verse speaks of those who do not use their reason. Al-Ankabut 29:69 connects guidance with striving, and Ash-Shura 42:13 connects it with turning back to God. By contrast, As-Saff 61:5 speaks of the deviation of those who themselves chose a crooked path.

Someone who listens fairly to an opposing view, examines the evidence, and is prepared to abandon a mistaken belief is not the same as someone who persists in error out of fear of family, society, or losing their position.

Seeking truth, using reason, and being willing to reform open the way to recognizing truth; prejudice and transgression close it.

“وَاللَّهُ يَهْدِي مَنْ يَشَاءُ إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ”

“And The God guides whomever He wills to a straight path.”

Guidance is by God’s will, but His will should not be imagined as a decision without wisdom or apart from the established ways of creation. In Muhammad 47:17, the Quran speaks of increasing the guidance of those who have found the way.

Human beings therefore do not lack choice in facing the truth. Their task is to understand, reflect, accept what is right, and reform their conduct, though God knows each person’s actual circumstances and intentions better.

Today we see examples of these disagreements among Muslims themselves. The Quran is one, yet they have many disagreements over hadith, transmitted accounts, the caliphate, the imamate, interpretation, denominations, and religious authority. Sometimes disagreement has gone beyond scholarly debate and led to declaring one another unbelievers and to bloodshed.

Even among movements with a Sunni background, groups have gone to war with one another. ISIS-K rejects the Taliban’s religious legitimacy, and the two have fought. In Afghanistan, Hekmatyar’s Hezb-e Islami forces also fought the Taliban and Jamiat-e Islami, and the civil wars took many lives.

Political rivalry, the pursuit of power, and interests have also played a role in these wars; they cannot all be attributed solely to disagreements over interpreting the Quran. Nor should these groups’ conduct be generalized to all followers of Sunni or Shia denominations.

Yet the verse’s question remains: if the Book came to judge in truth, why do those who claim to follow it sometimes declare one another unbelievers and even shed one another’s blood, instead of measuring their own beliefs against the truth?

Al-Baqarah 2:213 does not merely speak of people’s disagreements; it warns of a greater danger: that after receiving the Book and clear evidence, people may sacrifice truth to prejudice, power, and their own interests. The path to guidance lies in returning to truth, not in one sect’s victory over another.
