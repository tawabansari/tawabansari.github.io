Al-Baqarah 2:165

ۖ ۗ

And among people are those who take equals besides The God, loving them as they love The God; but those who believe are stronger in their love for The God. If only those who have done wrong could realize, when they see the punishment, that all power belongs to The God and that The God is severe in punishment.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:163, the Quran declared that humanity’s god is one God. In Al-Baqarah 2:164, it placed the signs of this truth before people in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the order of nature. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:165, it points to one of humanity’s most fundamental deviations: despite God’s oneness, some people take “andad” for Him.

“Andad” is the plural of “nidd,” from the root N D D, meaning an equal, counterpart, or rival placed in opposition to another. In the Quran, this word is not confined to stone idols or outward objects of worship. The central issue is whom or what a person places in a position that truly belongs only to The God.

The Quran does not say that God actually has equals; it says that some people take equals for Him: “يَتَّخِذُ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ أَندَادًا.” Other Quranic uses of “andad” likewise employ expressions of setting up equals. Thus, the making of equals is a human act, not a reality in which The God has a rival or counterpart. In Ash-Shuara 26:98, those acknowledging their error also say that they made their objects of worship equal to the Lord of all worlds. That equality was constructed by their own beliefs and conduct, not by the reality of those fabricated deities.

A “nidd,” therefore, is not merely a statue or a fabricated deity. It can be a religious figure, a leader, a legal system, an inherited tradition, political power, wealth, a people, an idea, or any other authority that someone places in God’s unique position. The issue is not the inherent nature of these things, but the position people assign to them.

Knowledge, law, leadership, and social ties can all benefit human life. But if a theory or method of knowing is made so absolute that it becomes the sole judge of all the truth of existence, or if human law and authority are placed above God’s clear command even when they conflict with it, that authority has been elevated beyond its proper position. In such a situation, someone may verbally accept God’s oneness while making an equal for Him in practice or thought.

This also applies to religious figures. In At-Tawbah 9:31, the Quran speaks of people who took their scholars and monks as lords besides God. In Ash-Shura 42:21, it mentions partners who prescribe religion for people without God’s permission. These verses show that making equals can also occur in obedience and religious legislation, when a person grants another authority a prerogative that belongs to God.

In Al-Furqan 25:43 and Al-Jathiyah 45:23, the Quran even speaks of a person who takes their own desire as their god. Desire is neither a stone idol nor an external figure; it is an inclination that can govern a person’s decisions. People can thus place their own desires in a position where, instead of truth and divine guidance, those desires become the final standard for their choices.

The verse also reveals an important sign of this making of equals: “يُحِبُّونَهُمْ كَحُبِّ اللَّهِ”—they love them as they love God.

Love here is not merely an inward feeling. It can involve loyalty, trust, following, and preferring the wishes of the beloved. People may greatly love their family, homeland, people, leader, or beliefs; these loves do not in themselves constitute making a “nidd.” But when love for something leads someone to set aside clear truth, sacrifice justice, or put that authority’s command before God’s command, there is a danger that this love will become an allegiance that occupies God’s position.

The Quran therefore says in contrast, “وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَشَدُّ حُبًّا لِلَّهِ”: those who believe are stronger in their love for God. This does not negate love for others; it clarifies the ultimate place of love and loyalty. A believer can love others, respect knowledge and law, and follow worthy leaders, without making any of them so absolute that God’s truth and command are set aside before them.

Yet not every error, sin, intense love, or misguided following should immediately be called “andad.” A person may disobey out of weakness, fear, or greed without attributing divine rank to anything. The question of making equals arises when a person, being, or authority is placed alongside God in worship, lordship, ultimate authority, or another position unique to Him. The distinction between a human failing and making an equal for God must be preserved.

At the end of the verse, the Quran speaks of those who, upon seeing the punishment, realize “أَنَّ الْقُوَّةَ لِلَّهِ جَمِيعًا”: all power belongs to God. This statement exposes the reality of fabricated equals. A person may rely on a ruler’s power, a religious figure’s influence, wealth, or a system’s authority and grant it an ultimate position; yet all these powers are limited and dependent. None possesses independent power equal to that of The God.

The root of the deviation in making “andad” is that people elevate something limited and dependent to an absolute and ultimate position. What should be a means of understanding, service, guidance, or organizing life becomes an authority against whose wishes even truth must be measured. Such making of equals can occur in worship, thought, law, love, and everyday conduct.

The criterion, then, is not merely what a person verbally calls their god, but to whom they ultimately remain loyal, whose command they regard as higher, and which they put first when truth conflicts with other wishes.

The God has no equal either in creation or in the ultimate position of receiving servitude and exercising sovereignty. No person, idea, power, or authority should occupy in human belief or conduct a position that belongs to Him alone.
