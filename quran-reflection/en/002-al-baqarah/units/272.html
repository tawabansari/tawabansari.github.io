Al-Baqarah 2:272

ۗ ۚ ۚ

Their guidance is not your responsibility, but The God guides whomever He wills; and whatever good you spend is for yourselves; and you spend only in seeking the countenance of The God; and whatever good you spend will be repaid to you in full, and you will not be wronged.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:271, the Quran regarded making charities public as good and recalled that The God knows the reality of human conduct. A giver therefore cannot be accused of ostentation merely because their giving is public, since the truth of human intentions rests with God.

Now, in this verse, God addresses Muhammad directly:

“لَيْسَ عَلَيْكَ هُدَاهُمْ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يَهْدِي مَنْ يَشَاءُ”

“Their guidance is not your responsibility, but The God guides whomever He wills.”

“هُدَاهُم,” from the root H D Y, refers to their guidance and finding the way. The Quran reminds Muhammad that guiding people is not his responsibility. He is commissioned to convey the message, explain the truth, give good news, and warn; but people’s acceptance of guidance and the reality of their faith have not been placed under his control.

In Al-Qasas 28:56, the Quran tells Muhammad that he cannot guide everyone he loves; rather, God guides whomever He wills. In Yunus 10:99, it also asks whether he would compel people to become believers. In Al-Ghashiyah 88:21 and Al-Ghashiyah 88:22, his task is described as reminding, and authority to dominate people is denied to him.

Thus, even Muhammad, though he was the Messenger of The God, was not responsible for imposing guidance on people or forcing them to believe.

This principle also matters in giving. Someone who invites others to give can explain to everyone, through Quranic verses, the goodness of generosity, the value of sincerity, and the consequences of ostentation, reproach, and injury. But they cannot pry into others’ intentions and affairs and cause them harm. In Al-Hujurat 49:12, the Quran forbids spying. Thus, not only is accusing someone of ostentation without knowledge wrong, but secretly searching their life to uncover their intentions is also inconsistent with this principle.

Examining observable conduct, respect for the rights of those in need, and trustworthiness in the use of public donations is a separate matter and should not be equated with prying into personal intentions.

For a detailed examination of the limits of Muhammad’s responsibility in guiding people, and how his task is to convey the message rather than impose faith, see the independent study “There Is No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Task Is to Convey”.

The Quran then states a fundamental truth about giving:

“وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَلِأَنْفُسِكُمْ”

“And whatever good you spend is for yourselves.”

“Good” in this context refers to what a person spends for a good purpose. “فَلِأَنْفُسِكُمْ” returns the benefit of that giving to the givers themselves.

In Al-Baqarah 2:261, spending wealth was compared to a grain capable of producing multiplied fruits. In Al-Baqarah 2:265, giving to seek God’s pleasure was connected with strengthening oneself from within. In Al-Baqarah 2:269, wisdom was described as abundant good that God gives to whomever He wills.

These verses show that goodness does not benefit only the recipient: the giver can also benefit through developing character, strengthening resolve, cultivating wisdom, and the fruits of their conduct.

For example, in a society where people have purchasing power, merchants’ businesses thrive and the government receives greater tax revenue. In turn, through creating jobs, giving, and empowering people, merchants and rulers help make that prosperity possible and also give others the opportunity to give.

The return of the good of giving is not limited to the Hereafter. Within the all-encompassing mercy of The God, the effects of goodness can also appear in this world as welfare, flourishing, security, and new opportunities. Beyond these effects, divine reward and bounty are not limited to material outcomes and economic calculations.

We have examined this subject in the independent study “The Principle of Giving and Using Blessings”.

The Quran then clarifies the direction and aim of giving:

“وَمَا تُنْفِقُونَ إِلَّا ابْتِغَاءَ وَجْهِ اللَّهِ”

“And you spend only in seeking the countenance of The God.”

“Ibtighāʾ,” from the root B GH Y, refers to seeking and pursuing. In this construction, wajh is connected with the direction and destination toward which a person turns through their conduct.

In giving for the countenance of The God, a person’s ultimate aim is not to gain praise, obedience, or repayment from the recipient; their action is directed toward God. For a fuller examination of this expression and the various uses of wajh, see the independent study “Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Quran”.

This orientation is also clear in Al-Insan 76:9, where the righteous say that they feed for the countenance of The God and seek neither reward nor thanks from the recipients.

In Al-Baqarah 2:267, God described Himself as “غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ,” free of need and praiseworthy. God does not need our wealth or charities to provide for His creatures. It is we who have benefited from His blessings through possessing wealth, knowledge, ability, will, and opportunities to do good.

Why, then, should we reproach the recipient over our giving? In Al-Hujurat 49:17, the Quran reminds those who claimed their acceptance of Islam as a favor to Muhammad that, if they are sincere in faith, it is God who has bestowed a favor upon them by guiding them to faith.

Rather than claiming a favor over God or His servants, a giver should be grateful to The God for placing abilities, resources, and opportunities to do good at their disposal. This gratitude should also become evident in using blessings rightly, as we examined in the study “Gratitude in the Quran”.

The verse finally says:

“وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يُوَفَّ إِلَيْكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تُظْلَمُونَ”

“And whatever good you spend will be repaid to you in full, and you will not be wronged.”

“يُوَفَّ,” from the root W F Y, refers to fulfilling completely and delivering something without deficiency. Here the Quran promises that the good resulting from a person’s giving will be returned in full.

“وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تُظْلَمُونَ” also emphasizes that no person’s right is violated in this return of good.

The return of good is not limited to financial gain, nor do all its fruits necessarily become apparent in this world. A person can benefit from the good effects of giving here, but the Hereafter must not be forgotten either. Someone may give for good without seeing all its worldly fruits during their lifetime. God’s promise to return good in full extends beyond what a person can see and measure in the limited opportunity of worldly life.

In An-Nisa 4:40, the Quran also states that God does not wrong anyone even by an atom’s weight and multiplies a good deed.

Thus, no true good is lost with The God, and no person is deprived of their due in His just reckoning and recompense.

The message of this verse is that guiding people is not Muhammad’s responsibility, nor can the truth of intentions be determined through uninformed human judgments. Giving should seek the countenance of The God, not impose personal demands on others or win their praise. Whatever good a person puts into circulation in this way ultimately benefits them: through the growth and firmness of their character, through society’s flourishing and capability, or through the reward God has promised. The God wrongs no one and returns the fruits of goodness without deficiency.
