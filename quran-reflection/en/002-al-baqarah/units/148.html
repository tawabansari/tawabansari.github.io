Al-Baqarah 2:148

ۖ ۚ ۚ

Each has a direction toward which they turn; so compete with one another in good deeds. Wherever you may be, God will bring you all together. Indeed, God has power over all things.

Our Reflection

For several verses, the discussion has concerned the direction of prayer and religious orientation. The direction of the Sacred Mosque has been established for Muhammad’s community, but now the Qur’an says:

“Each has a direction toward which they turn.”

Commanding Muhammad and his community to face the Sacred Mosque does not mean that every other community has also been required to adopt the same direction of prayer. Al-Baqarah 2:145 said that the People of the Book do not follow Muhammad’s direction, that Muhammad does not follow theirs, and that some of them do not follow the direction of others. Now this difference is expressed concisely: each has a direction.

The appointed direction is therefore real and binding for Muhammad’s community, but that obligation does not establish that the same direction must be imposed on every prescribed way and community. The Qur’an distinguishes each community’s responsibility toward the path made clear to it from a community’s claim to exclusive possession of God or salvation.

The verse immediately says:

“So compete with one another in good deeds.”

Precisely where different directions could become a competition over identity, ancestry, and communal superiority, the Qur’an changes the field of competition. What people should strive to excel in is good deeds.

Al-Baqarah 2:115 matters here: east and west belong to God, and wherever you turn, there is God’s face. This does not abolish the direction of prayer, since the same surah later establishes a specific direction for Muhammad’s community. It does show, however, that the one God is not confined to a geographical direction, and no community can turn its direction of prayer into a deed of exclusive ownership over God.

At the beginning of the discussion of the change in direction, Al-Baqarah 2:142 recalls the same principle: east and west belong to God, and He guides whom He wills to a straight path. Before the direction of the Sacred Mosque is established, it is already clear that changing the direction of prayer does not mean moving God from one direction to another.

Al-Baqarah 2:177 adds another perspective. Righteousness is not reduced to turning one’s face toward east or west; the verse goes on to discuss faith, giving, keeping commitments, and steadfastness. This helps explain why 2:148 moves from different directions directly to “compete in good deeds.” Direction has its place, but the value of religious life cannot be reduced to direction alone.

The same reasoning appeared earlier in the criticism of exclusive claims to Paradise. Al-Baqarah 2:111 challenges the claim that Paradise belongs to a particular religious community. Rather than making another community’s name a guarantee of salvation, 2:112 makes surrender to God and doing good the criteria. The Qur’an does not replace one religious monopoly with a new one in the name of Muhammad’s followers.

An-Nisa 4:123 expresses the same principle from another angle: God’s judgment is not based on your wishes or the wishes of the People of the Book. A name, ancestry, prescribed way, or group affiliation cannot by itself guarantee salvation.

The closest parallel to Al-Baqarah 2:148 is Al-Ma’idah 5:48. There, different prescribed ways and paths are mentioned for different groups. It says that God could have made everyone one community, but did not; then comes the same command: “Compete in good deeds.” Finally, everyone returns to God, who will judge their disagreements.

The structures of the two verses are closely related.

Al-Baqarah 2:148 moves from different directions to competition in good deeds, then to everyone being gathered before God.

Al-Ma’idah 5:48 moves from different prescribed ways and paths to competition in good deeds, then to everyone returning to God.

This correspondence shows that differences in religious paths and forms do not in themselves mean different gods or guarantee one community’s superiority.

Al-Hajj 22:34 also matters here: it speaks of appointing rites for communities while affirming that their god is one.

Al-Hajj 22:67 again mentions a rite for each community and discourages turning that difference into dispute. Differences in forms of worship and direction are thus recognized within the Qur’an without compromising the oneness of God.

The relationship among the direction of prayer, orientation, and good deeds is examined in detail in Qiblah, Wajh, and Ḥanīf in the Qur’an. Its connection with different rites, prescribed ways, and paths of worship is developed in Hajj in the Qur’an.

The verse then says:

“Wherever you may be, God will bring you all together.”

“All together” naturally continues “each has a direction.” People are dispersed across different directions, communities, and paths, yet none stands outside the domain of that one God. Ultimately, everyone is gathered before Him.

The verse thus begins with plurality and arrives at one final authority:

Directions differ; the field of competition is good deeds; the final authority is God.

Within this structure, the concluding statement—

“Indeed, God has power over all things”—

does not begin a new subject. It completes what precedes it. Different directions, distant locations, and scattered communities do not divide the domain of the one God. The same God to whom east and west belong gathers everyone before Him despite these differences.

A plurality of directions does not undo the unity of the final authority.

Muhammad’s followers were likewise not meant to repeat, under a new name, the religious exclusivity the Qur’an had criticized in others. The Sacred Mosque was established as their direction of prayer, but that direction was not to become an automatic guarantee of salvation or a certificate of inherent superiority. In this context, turning toward the Sacred House meant returning to a sign the Qur’an connected with Abraham and Ishmael, surrender to the one God, and devotion to Him alone—a path not intended to become a new boundary of religious self-superiority. The House’s connection with Abraham, devotion to God alone, and the meaning of the direction of prayer are explored in Qiblah, Wajh, and Ḥanīf in the Qur’an; the House, rites, and different communities are discussed in Hajj in the Qur’an. The criticism of treating the name of a prescribed way or community as a guarantee of Paradise is developed in Paradise Is Not the Exclusive Possession of One Sect or Prescribed Way.

Today, too, if the Sacred House becomes merely a symbol of their own community’s superiority in the minds of Muhammad’s followers, the outward direction remains while its meaning is damaged. Reviving the House’s meaning requires more than physically facing it. It must be reconnected with the same Abrahamic message: surrender to the one God, relinquishing exclusive claims to God and salvation, and accepting that people with different directions and paths ultimately gather before that same God.

In this reading, “compete in good deeds” is more than an ethical statement placed beside a discussion of prayer’s direction. It is the spirit that prevents that direction from becoming an instrument of religious boasting.

If followers of different prescribed ways are to compete in something, the Qur’an itself has named the field:

“Compete with one another in good deeds.”
