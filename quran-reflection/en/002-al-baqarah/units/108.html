Al-Baqarah 2:108

ۗ

Do you wish to make requests of your Messenger as requests were made of Moses before? And whoever takes rejection of truth in place of faith has certainly lost the right way.

Our Reflection

The preceding verses returned authority over guidance to The God: He sends down from His bounty upon whomever He wills, has power over all things, possesses dominion over the heavens and the earth, and is the only guardian and helper. The discussion now turns to human conduct toward the Messenger:

“Do you wish to make requests of your Messenger as requests were made of Moses before?”

The Qur’an itself shows that not every question is blameworthy. It repeatedly reports people’s questions with the expression “They ask you” and answers them. The problem begins when a question no longer seeks to understand truth but becomes a condition imposed on accepting it.

One of the clearest examples is what appeared earlier concerning Moses:

“We will not believe you until we see The God openly.” (Al-Baqarah 2:55)

An-Nisa 4:153 also recalls this conduct, saying that they demanded of Moses:

“Show us The God openly.”

This is no longer a question asked in order to learn. A new condition has been placed on faith: unless what we want happens, we will not accept.

Another form of the same failing appears in the account of the cow. They had been commanded to slaughter a cow, but questions about its nature, age, color, and characteristics continued. Each answer narrowed the available choice, until they finally carried out the command, though they had nearly failed to do so. This sequence can be followed in Al-Baqarah 2:67, 2:68, 2:69, 2:70, and 2:71.

An important distinction becomes clear here: the question is not for understanding, but for escaping a clear answer. The command is clear, yet people manufacture so many “ifs,” “buts,” and new scenarios that the command itself becomes buried beneath details. On the surface they are asking questions; the result, however, is delayed obedience and the search for a way out of something clear from the beginning.

This danger is familiar to us as well. The God did not reveal religion to make life difficult. Even in the verse describing purification before prayer, He explicitly says that He does not intend to impose hardship on people, but wishes to purify them (Al-Ma’idah 5:6). The command to purify ourselves and establish prayer has been given, and the basic means of becoming clean has been stated. Yet, as in the account of the cow, we have surrounded it with a mass of conditions and qualifications: whether the right hand or the left comes first; whether each body part is washed once, twice, or three times; whether the feet are washed or wiped; how much of the head is wiped; what rule applies to the ears; whether the order of the body parts is obligatory; how long an interval may pass between stages; how the intention is made; and then detailed lists of what invalidates this purification—sleep of which kind, touching a woman or a man, touching the genitals, bleeding, vomiting, laughing during prayer, eating certain foods, and many other matters for which different schools have constructed different rulings. Worse, each group takes satisfaction in its own system: the Shia in their school, Sunnis in their schools, Ismailis in their method, and others in their own inheritance. The danger is the same as in the account of the cow: a command intended to be clear and practicable becomes so complicated beneath questions, elaborations, and human rulings that following a school ultimately takes the place of following the command directly.

Another expression also recurs in the account of Moses:

“Call upon your Lord for us.”

This expression occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:61, 2:68, 2:69, and 2:70. The words “your Lord” alone do not establish that they did not regard The God as their own Lord, but the repetition is worth examining. Moses is repeatedly placed in the role of intermediary for their demands: call upon your Lord for us, ask Him to make this clear for us, ask Him to do this for us.

In Al-A‘raf 7:138, after crossing the sea, they also asked Moses to appoint a deity for them like the deities of other people. These examples reveal a common danger: instead of bringing themselves into alignment with guidance, people ask the Messenger to adjust guidance, a sign, or the form of religion to their wishes.

Guidance has not come to satisfy our wishes; we must bring ourselves into alignment with guidance.

The same failing can appear today with the same reasoning expressed in different language. A principle is clear, but so many hypothetical cases, exceptions, and fresh questions are constructed that a way is eventually found not to accept it. The question still looks scholarly, but it no longer seeks understanding; it seeks release from a conclusion that was clear from the beginning.

Another form is making faith conditional. The reasoning “We will not believe until we see The God openly” can appear today as: if The God exists, why did He not answer my prayer in the way I wanted? If He loves me, why have illness, poverty, failure, or death come my way? If this religion is true, why have I not received a personal sign, a dream, an extraordinary spiritual favor, or a miracle?

In this situation, people are no longer simply examining truth; they are prescribing how The God must prove Himself to them. Faith becomes a transaction whose acceptance depends on the fulfillment of a human wish.

The end of the verse shows how serious the issue is:

“Whoever takes rejection of truth in place of faith has certainly lost the right way.”

The Arabic verb “takes in exchange” (yatabaddil) comes from B D L: taking one thing in place of another. Here, rejection of truth is taken in place of faith. A person confronted by truth who makes acceptance dependent on their own conditions and wishes can move from seeking truth to setting aside what they should have trusted.

The verse’s warning is therefore not against asking questions. Honest inquiry for understanding is a path to knowledge. The danger begins when a question becomes a means of imposing conditions on truth, postponing obedience, or escaping a clear answer.

We ask in order to understand truth better and correct ourselves in its light, not to manufacture so many questions and conditions that we eventually change truth to suit our own wishes.
