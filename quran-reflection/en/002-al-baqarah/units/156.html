Al-Baqarah 2:156

Those who, whenever calamity strikes them, say: “Indeed, we are for The God, and indeed, to Him we shall return.”

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:155, God spoke of testing people through fear, hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and crops, ending with the command to give good news to the patient. He now identifies these same people: when calamity reaches them, they say, “We are for The God, and to Him we shall return.” Patience here is more than enduring pain; it is connected with understanding our place before our Lord and the outcome of our lives. We previously considered its wider meaning in Al-Baqarah 2:153, with a detailed study in Patience in the Qur’an: The Meaning of Steadfastness and the Criterion of Its Value.

The words “aṣābat-hum” and “muṣībah” both come from the root Ṣ W B, associated with reaching and striking. In this context, a calamity is an adverse event that reaches a person. Yet the word itself does not establish that every calamity punishes the sufferer or results directly from their own conduct. People may suffer from their own actions, others’ oppression, or events beyond their control. The relationship between testing, God’s ways of governing creation, human choice, and the responsibility of those who cause suffering is examined in detail in Trials and the Promise of Good in the Qur’an: Is the World a Prison for the Believer and a Paradise for the Disbeliever? (Persian).

The central expression here, however, is “innā lillāh.” It is usually translated as “We belong to God.” This is a valid reading, expressing belonging and ownership: human beings are created by the one God and do not independently own their existence. Another reading is also possible, opening a wider philosophical perspective: “We are for God.” This does not deny ownership but places it within a more encompassing meaning. We are for God because He is our Creator, Owner, and Lord; this relationship also raises questions about why we were created, the purpose of life, and our place as His servants. In this reading, “lillāh” speaks not only of whom we belong to but also of the direction and ultimate purpose of our existence.

The difference between these readings is subtle but fundamental. “Belonging to God” chiefly brings ownership and belonging to mind; “being for God” includes that belonging while also revealing the relationship between Master and servant, between the One worshipped and the worshipper. Human beings do not merely belong to God: they were created to serve Him and direct their lives, choices, and conduct toward His pleasure. Al-An‘am 6:162 presents prayer, rites of devotion, life, and death as being for the one God, while Adh-Dhariyat 51:56 identifies worshipping Him as the purpose of creating jinn and humans. These verses illuminate the broader horizon of the second reading. Thus, when the patient person says “innā lillāh” in calamity, they recall not only God’s ownership but the reality and purpose of their own life: We were created for the one God, and even amid trials we do not lose our direction of service and responsibility.

This does not mean human beings were created to meet a need in God. In Adh-Dhariyat 51:57, God declares that He needs neither provision nor food from His servants. Human servitude therefore does not meet a need of the Lord; it concerns our own position and responsibility before our Creator. For a fuller exploration, see The Qur’anic Root ʿ B D.

The meaning of patience in this expression now becomes clearer. A person who says “We are for God” recalls that life’s purpose is not merely to preserve wealth, comfort, and worldly blessings, valuable though these are and deserving of gratitude. Calamity must not cause them to lose their direction of service. This does not mean silently accepting oppression. Belonging to God does not mean belonging to an oppressor. Our ultimate belonging to God, together with all we possess, does not cancel our legitimate rights against other people. Someone who wrongfully takes property or harms another person cannot escape responsibility by claiming that everything belongs to God. An-Nisa 4:29 likewise prohibits consuming one another’s wealth wrongfully. Serving God should make people steadfast in truth, not powerless before oppression.

The second half of the patient person’s statement is equally significant: “And to Him we shall return.” In the first part, we recognize our belonging and the direction of our lives; in the second, we recall where our journey ends. This return is more than a phrase to soothe grief. Al-Baqarah 2:28 speaks of human states of death and life, and finally of returning to God. Worldly life, with all its blessings and hardships, is therefore not the whole course of human existence. We live for God and ultimately return to that same Lord.

The verse describes what the patient say, but the truth of their words must not be reduced to repeating a phrase at death or calamity. Those who say “We are for God” recall their very existence belonging to their Lord and declare the direction of their service. This understanding must also become visible in their conduct. Commendable patience means preserving our direction of service, pursuit of truth, and responsibility amid loss and hardship.

The response of the patient therefore neither denies grief nor declares defeat. They know that what was lost had value, but it was not the whole meaning of their lives. They were created for the one God and will ultimately return to Him. In the next verse, God describes the standing of these same patient people in relation to His mercy and guidance.
