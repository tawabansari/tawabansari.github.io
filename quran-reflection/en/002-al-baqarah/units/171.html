Al-Baqarah 2:171

ۚ

And the likeness of those who disbelieve is like that of someone calling out to a creature that hears nothing but the sound of a summons and a call; deaf, mute, and blind, they do not reason.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, the Quran spoke of people who prefer their fathers’ way when called to follow what God has sent down, even if those fathers had neither reasoned nor found guidance. It now portrays their condition through a clear comparison: they hear the call to truth, yet seem not to grasp its meaning. The words reach their ears, but inherited beliefs so dominate their thinking that they do not move from hearing to understanding, or from understanding to assessing truth.

The verb “يَنْعِقُ,” from the root N ʿ Q, conveys calling out loudly, as someone calls sheep or other livestock. An animal hears the caller’s voice and may respond, but does not understand human speech as a human being does. In the structure of this comparison, “الَّذِي يَنْعِقُ” is the caller, while “مَا لَا يَسْمَعُ إِلَّا دُعَاءً وَنِدَاءً” refers to the creature being addressed. The image depicts speech remaining fruitless when the listener receives only its sound without reaching the speaker’s intended meaning.

This comparison reveals an important distinction between hearing sound and understanding speech. “Samʿ” originally concerns hearing, but in the Quran’s uses concerning guidance, hearing is not merely a sensory event; what matters is using what one hears to recognize truth. In Al-Anfal 8:21, God speaks of people who claim to have heard while they do not hear. They have received the sound, but their hearing has not led to understanding and a conscious response. The next verse, Al-Anfal 8:22, describes this condition in terms very close to Al-Baqarah 2:171: the deaf and mute who do not reason. Effective hearing on the path of guidance therefore brings people closer to thinking and recognizing truth, rather than merely familiarizing their ears with the sounds of words.

People may likewise hear the Quran every day, memorize its verses, recite it melodiously, and even read it to others, yet, when understanding and carrying out God’s command are at issue, merely repeat predecessors’ words instead of reasoning about those verses. Such a person may be highly skilled at transmitting religious information without ever asking which parts of what they have accepted as religion were truly sent down by God. The Quran is then heard, but its position as the standard of guidance and judgment is not accepted in practice.

This danger is clearly visible in matters of lawful and forbidden, the very subject of the preceding verses. When someone says, “Eat locusts, which are lawful, but toads or frogs are forbidden; lizards are lawful, but do not eat snakes, which are forbidden,” they should be asked: where has God established these boundaries? Were these rulings derived from clear Quranic verses or from reports attributed to the Prophet generations later? Even when examining the reports themselves, one must distinguish eating an animal, prohibiting the killing of another, and declaring it permanently forbidden for everyone. A report that an animal was or was not eaten does not itself constitute a command making it lawful or forbidden for every generation. What must be established is God’s authorization to attribute such a ruling to religion, not the number of people who transmitted it from predecessors.

This issue is not confined to food. Prohibiting men’s use of gold and silk, declaring music forbidden, prohibiting keeping a dog to guard a home, making a beard obligatory and shaving it forbidden, and hundreds of other rulings and restrictions presented in religion’s name must all be assessed by this standard. Which Quranic verse establishes this specific obligation or prohibition? If such rulings rest on transmitted reports, earlier legal opinions, or religious customs, how can they be called God’s definitive command without establishing divine authorization? Liking one behavior, disliking another, or even relating a habit from the Prophet’s life does not by itself create a right to legislate for all humanity.

In Al-Anam 6:143 and Al-Anam 6:144, the Quran confronts claims of prohibitions concerning livestock with a demand for knowledge and evidence. Al-Anam 6:150 asks claimants to bring witnesses that God has forbidden such things. This same question must confront inherited rulings today: if a ruling truly comes from God, where is its evidence? Merely naming al-Bukhari, Muslim, a hadith scholar, or a jurist cannot place a historical report in the position of “مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ.” God’s Book is revealed scripture; a narration is a human report whose attribution, meaning, and claimed ruling’s scope must each be established separately.

The independent studies “Lawful and Forbidden in the Quran: The Divine Right to Legislate and the Danger of Ascribing Partners in Religion” and “Confusing Obedience to the Messenger with Obedience to Hadith and Disparate Narrations” examine this distinction through Quranic evidence and detailed arguments. The central issue is replacing revelation with inherited hearsay and then making binding rulings in God’s name.

In this situation, the verse’s comparison becomes very tangible today. Someone may hear the Quran daily, yet, when encountering a verse inconsistent with an inherited legal opinion, immediately seek to justify that opinion instead of reflecting on God’s words. Asked where God gave such a command, they may merely repeat leaders’ names and chains of transmission instead of presenting Quranic evidence. Such conduct can exemplify the hearing without reasoning depicted in the verse. The sound of truth has reached the ear, but instead of assessing it, the person has made inherited tradition the standard of truth.

The Quran concludes: “صُمٌّ بُكْمٌ عُمْىٌ فَهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ.” These descriptions do not concern physical disability; they describe a condition in which the means of knowing truth are not put to use. Ears are present, but true speech is not properly received; a tongue is present, but unsupported statements are repeated instead of truth being expressed; eyes are present, but clear signs of guidance are disregarded. In Al-Anfal 8:22, deafness and muteness are likewise placed alongside the failure to reason. The central problem is therefore not lacking the means of understanding, but failing to use them in response to truth.

This danger deepens when people assume that numerous followers or an ancient tradition suffice to establish correctness. In Al-Anam 6:116, God warns that a majority may also follow conjecture and lead people away from His path. By contrast, Az-Zumar 39:18 describes the guided and people of understanding as those who listen to speech and follow its best. Hearing’s value therefore lies not in the number of statements or the speakers’ fame, but in recognizing and following truth. Someone who has heard thousands of narrations but never assessed them against God’s Book is not counted as guided merely for possessing abundant information.

The lesson is clear and decisive: hearing the Quran without reasoning about it and accepting the authority of divine guidance does not protect people from error. Someone who makes declarations of lawful and forbidden in God’s name, yet relies only on predecessors’ statements instead of clear revelation, may be continuing the very path condemned in the preceding verses. No chain of transmission, religious fame, or scholarly rank can replace divine evidence. God calls people to conscious listening, thought, and following truth—not to hearing revelation’s sound while, in practice, giving inherited human speech priority over it.
