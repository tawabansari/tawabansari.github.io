Al-Baqarah 2:164

Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth, in the alternation of night and day, in the ships sailing through the sea with what benefits people, in the water The God sent down from the sky, bringing the earth to life with it after its death and dispersing every kind of moving creature in it, and in the turning and changing of the winds and the clouds subjected between the sky and the earth, there are signs for people who reason.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, the Quran declared that humanity’s god is one God. It now directs human attention to the creation of the heavens and the earth, the alternation of night and day, the movement of vessels, water, life, winds, and clouds. These are signs of the vast, interconnected order of creation—an order in which, the more people understand it, the more they discover new dimensions of the Creator’s greatness and wisdom. Hence the verse ends with “لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ”: signs for people who put their reason to use.

One expression in the verse that invites reflection is “وَالْفُلْكِ الَّتِي تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ بِمَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ.” In a first reading, it speaks of ships moving through the sea and carrying what benefits people. This meaning was fully understandable to the earliest hearers, and the Arabic language of the verse supports it. Commentators such as al-Tabari and al-Zamakhshari also read the Quran with the knowledge and means of understanding available in their times. Naturally, we should not expect them to have explained the earth’s motion within the solar system, and that system’s motion within the galaxy, through today’s scientific understanding.

But is the horizon of this verse confined to that initial understanding?

Today we know that the earth is a spherical planet that rotates on its axis, travels around the sun, and moves with the solar system through the Milky Way. With this broader understanding of existence, if we view the verse through the lens of the mutashabihat, another image opens before us: could the earth itself be like a vast ship moving through the expanse of the cosmos and carrying life with it?

In this image, “fulk” can evoke the floating earth as a carrier of life, while “bahr” can be an image of the vast expanse of the cosmos. Just as a ship moves on water and carries its passengers and their necessary provisions, the earth, in its motion, carries human beings, animals, plants, waters, air, and the conditions needed for life to continue.

The importance of this motion is not merely the earth’s change of position. Suppose the earth departed from its present pattern of motion: would the conditions on which our lives now depend remain in place? Its suitable distance from the sun, its orbital motion, its rotation on its axis, and the connection of these motions with receiving energy, night and day, and the seasons, together with the earth’s water and atmosphere, form part of the order within which life as we know it unfolds. In this perspective, “بِمَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ” also gains a broader horizon: the moving earth carries not only human beings but also the resources and conditions of their lives.

This reading has noteworthy connections with several other Quranic images and expressions.

In Al-Anbiya 21:33 and Ya-Sin 36:40, the Quran speaks of motion in a “falak” through the expression “يَسْبَحُونَ”—an image of celestial bodies floating and moving along their courses. In Ya-Sin 36:38, it also uses the verb “تَجْرِي” for the sun’s motion, the same verb used in Al-Baqarah 2:164 for the movement of fulk in bahr. This shared language for the motion of vessels and celestial bodies provides an important connection for reflection on the order of motion in creation.

In Ya-Sin 36:41, the Quran presents the carrying of human offspring in “فُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ” as another of God’s signs. The placement, within a sequence of verses, of the image of falak as the course of celestial motion and fulk as the carrier of human generations broadens the horizon for reflecting on a vessel that carries life.

Another connection appears in Ibrahim 14:32 and Ibrahim 14:33. The Quran first speaks of subjecting fulk to sail through bahr, and then of subjecting the sun, moon, night, and day. Luqman 31:29 also discusses the motion of the sun and moon until an appointed term, while Luqman 31:31 again counts fulk moving through bahr among God’s signs. These verses place motion at sea and the motion of celestial bodies alongside one another within the framework of divine order and subjection.

The other parts of Al-Baqarah 2:164 also acquire a broader meaning in this perspective. Water descending, the earth coming to life after its death, the dispersal of creatures, the changing winds, and the movement of clouds are all connected with the conditions for life on this planet. In Al-Anbiya 21:30, the Quran also connects water with the life of living beings. Thus, the signs in the verse can be viewed not only individually but also in relation to one another: an earth in motion, carrying life, endowed with water and an atmosphere, and with a system of cycles and changes that makes the continuation of life possible.

This cosmic reading, however, belongs to the realm of interpretive possibilities and the mutashabihat. The direct meaning of ships and a sea of water remains valid, while the image of the earth as a vessel carrying life may open another horizon for thinking about the verse. This interpretation does not establish that “fulk” is lexically a name for the earth or that “bahr” necessarily denotes cosmic space. What we see is a connection worth considering between Quranic images and our broader understanding of creation today. The final interpretation of such matters rests in the knowledge of The God, as recalled in Aal Imran 3:7 and explained in Forqan’s Method of Translation and Reflection.

In both readings, the verse’s central message is clear: creation is not a disordered collection of unrelated things. From the movement of vessels on water to the motion of bodies through the cosmos, and from rainfall to the revival of the earth and the continuation of life, there are signs through which to understand the order of creation.

The Quran does not invite people merely to watch these phenomena; it asks them to reason about them, recognize their connections, and reflect on the greatness of the order The God has created. Human knowledge can continually expand and disclose new horizons of these signs, but the ultimate reality and depth of creation rest in its Creator’s knowledge. And God knows best.
