Al-Baqarah 2:268

ۖ ۗ

Satan promises you poverty and commands you to indecency; and The God promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty; and The God is expansive in bounty and knowing.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:267, the Quran called believers to give from the good things they have earned. It now addresses a major obstacle to giving: fear of poverty and the concern that giving what we possess will leave us with less.

The verse sets two promises against each other: Satan’s promise and God’s promise.

“الشَّيْطَانُ يَعِدُكُمُ الْفَقْرَ”

“Satan promises you poverty.”

“يَعِدُكُم,” from the root W AYN D, refers to promising and creating an expectation about the future. Poverty concerns need and a lack of sufficient resources.

By frightening people about the future, Satan seeks to prevent them from doing good. This fear can appear in everyday life in various forms.

For example, we decide to help someone in need from our income, but suddenly think that if we give this amount, we might not have enough savings at the end of the month or might need it ourselves one day.

Or someone has an art or skill they could teach others, but thinks: “If I teach this craft to someone else, tomorrow they will open a shop too, and my business will lose customers.”

In both examples, concern about diminished possessions or lost future benefit can prevent people from putting the blessings at their disposal into circulation.

In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:254 and the study The Principle of Giving and Using Blessings, we examined how giving is not limited to wealth: knowledge, art, talent, experience, time, and human abilities can also be used for good.

Of course, not every financial concern is a temptation from Satan. People are also responsible for meeting their own needs and those of their families, and the Quran does not command indiscriminate giving that leaves them deprived. Al-Isra 17:29 forbids both tightfistedness and opening one’s hand without restraint.

The problem arises when fear of shortage becomes an excuse for selfishness and withholding good, even when a person is able to help.

The Quran then says:

“وَيَأْمُرُكُمْ بِالْفَحْشَاءِ”

“And commands you to indecency.”

“Faḥshāʾ,” from the root F H SH, denotes severe ugliness and gross wrongdoing that exceeds the bounds of decent conduct. In the Quran, this word is not limited to sexual conduct; it can also encompass grave moral, social, and behavioral wrongs.

In this verse, faḥshāʾ appears in the context of fearing poverty and withholding giving. Severe stinginess, selfishness, and wrongfully refusing to help others are therefore possible instances to examine in this context.

In classical Arabic, fāḥish was also used to describe an extremely stingy person. Thus, the connection between faḥshāʾ and stinginess in this context is not merely a modern interpretation. For a fuller examination, see the study of the Quranic root F H SH.

But why does Satan first frighten people with poverty and then command indecency?

Because fear can prepare the ground for justifying wrongdoing. Someone constantly worried about losing possessions or status may turn to stinginess, selfishness, and even violating others’ rights to protect their interests.

Thus, fear of poverty can turn from a concern about the future into a cause of wrongdoing in the present.

In Al-Baqarah 2:169, the Quran also describes Satan’s command to evil and indecency. This connection shows that Satan does not merely invite people to perform one particular act; through fear, rationalization, and temptation, he can draw them toward wrongful conduct.

The Quran now sets another promise against the fear of poverty:

“وَاللَّهُ يَعِدُكُمْ مَغْفِرَةً مِنْهُ وَفَضْلًا”

“And The God promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty.”

“Maghfirah,” from the root GH F R, refers to forgiving and covering a fault, together with protecting a person from its consequences.

“Faḍl,” from the root F Ḍ L, concerns increase, giving, and a benefit beyond a baseline amount.

In contrast to Satan, who frightens people with diminished possessions and poverty, God speaks of forgiveness and the increase of His bounty.

This contrast is deeply meaningful: one directs a person’s attention to what they might lose, while the other directs it to the good they can gain through right conduct.

Someone who is God-conscious and remembers God’s promise knows that giving for good is not merely a loss of wealth or resources.

In Al-Baqarah 2:261, the Quran compared spending wealth in God’s way to a grain growing seven ears, with a hundred grains in each ear, then stated that God multiplies for whomever He wills.

This parable shows that an act of giving can bear fruits far exceeding its initial amount. Wealth spent on education, treatment, or empowering a person may make further good possible for them and later generations.

Likewise, knowledge taught to others does not necessarily diminish the teacher’s knowledge; it can spread and lead to fresh innovations and abilities.

The fear that giving or teaching something necessarily makes us poorer and less capable therefore does not show the whole truth.

Of course, the promise of divine bounty does not mean that every amount given necessarily returns several times over as money to the same person. God’s bounty is not limited to an increase in personal wealth. Reward in the Hereafter, forgiveness, new opportunities, and the far-reaching effects of goodness can also belong within this horizon.

In Saba 34:39, the Quran also speaks of replacing what a person spends, and in Al-Hadid 57:18 it describes the multiplication of the reward for charity and a good loan.

People should therefore not assess the value of giving solely by calculating how much their possessions have diminished today; they should also consider its effects and fruits and the divine promise.

The verse ends with two divine attributes:

“وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ”

“And The God is expansive in bounty and knowing.”

“Wāsiʿ,” from the root W S AYN, denotes breadth, expansiveness, and capacity, while “ʿalīm,” from the root AYN L M, denotes all-encompassing knowledge.

These two attributes have special significance in contrast to the promise of poverty.

A person may view their resources and future only within the limits of their present possessions, but the breadth of God’s giving and bounty does not depend on limited human calculations.

God is also knowing. He knows people’s circumstances, abilities, needs, and intentions. Giving in God’s way therefore means neither neglecting life’s responsibilities nor surrendering to a fear that prevents all giving.

The message of this verse is that people should not let fear of shortage and lost future benefit deprive them of doing good. Through the promise of poverty, Satan draws people toward stinginess and selfishness; The God, however, promises forgiveness and bounty and calls people to trust, God-consciousness, and the right use of their blessings.
