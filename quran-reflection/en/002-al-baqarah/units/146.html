Al-Baqarah 2:146

ۖ

Those to whom We have given the Book recognize it as they recognize their own children; and a group of them certainly conceal the truth while they know.

Our Reflection

Two verses earlier, those given the Book were said to know that this matter is the truth from their Lord. The next verse continued to discuss the direction of prayer, knowledge, and following. Now the Qur’an says:

“They recognize it as they recognize their own children.”

The pronoun has been understood in two ways. In one reading, it refers to Muhammad; in another, to the truth and the validity of the matter concerning the direction of prayer discussed in this context.

Given the continuity of the verses, the second reading seems more natural. Verse 144 says, “They certainly know that it is the truth from their Lord.” Verse 145 discusses the direction of prayer and the knowledge received by the prophet. This verse continues with “they recognize it” and again speaks of “concealing the truth”; the following verse begins with “The truth is from your Lord.” The thread of the discussion remains the recognition of this direction and the matter of the direction of prayer as true.

The comparison with recognizing one’s children conveys exceptional clarity. This is not remote, uncertain, or conjectural knowledge. The truth is so familiar to them that the Qur’an compares it to recognizing their own children.

The other reading is also possible: “they recognize him” may refer to Muhammad. In that case, the comparison “as they recognize their own children” becomes especially significant. The People of the Book living alongside Muhammad were not encountering a distant historical figure. They saw him, heard his invitation, and received his message within the living setting of his mission. This concerns close and clear recognition.

That distinction prevents us from applying this verse indiscriminately to every Jew or Christian today.

How does someone today come to know Muhammad? Their knowledge may come mainly from the media. They may read a translation of the Qur’an in which the translator’s explanations and beliefs are mixed into the text. They may know Muhammad through hadith collections, conflicting historical accounts, sectarian disputes, the conduct of governments claiming to represent Islam, or Muslims’ own behavior.

These do not produce a single, unmediated picture.

Someone may even approach the Qur’an sincerely, yet never reach, amid conflicting translations, reports, religious rulings, and interpretations, the clear recognition that the Qur’an compares to recognizing one’s child. Hearing Muhammad’s name or encountering a portrait others have constructed of him is not the same as “They recognize him as they recognize their own children.”

The second half of the verse itself sets the boundary for judgment:

“A group of them certainly conceal the truth while they know.”

The Qur’an does not say that all of them do this. It says:

“A group of them.”

Nor is this group’s problem merely a failure to accept. The Qur’an places two terms side by side:

Concealment and knowledge.

They cover up the truth while they know.

This is the boundary separating a lack of knowledge from knowing denial. Someone who has not yet recognized the truth is not in the same position as someone who recognizes it and then conceals it out of prejudice, self-interest, or a pursuit of superiority.

In An-Naml 27:14, the Qur’an gives a clear example:

“They denied them out of injustice and arrogance, although inwardly they were certain of them. So consider how those who spread corruption ended.” — An-Naml 27:14

Clear recognition therefore does not necessarily compel acceptance. Truth may be recognized, yet attachments and interests dominate a person’s response. It is precisely here that concealment becomes meaningful.

This principle matters today as well. A Jew or Christian cannot be counted among those who conceal truth while knowing it merely because they have not embraced Muhammad’s mission. Before making that judgment, it would have to be clear that the truth had actually become known to them.

A person from the People of the Book may even, after research, come to believe in the truth of Muhammad’s mission and the Qur’an while continuing to live within their family, social, and religious legal ties. Recognizing Muhammad’s mission, not concealing truth, and completely abandoning one’s previous prescribed way are three distinct claims; they should not be treated as identical without justification.

The verse’s center is therefore not a change of religious label, but honesty toward a truth that has been recognized.

If “they recognize it” refers to the truth of the matter concerning the direction of prayer, then a group of the People of the Book concealed a truth clear to them.

If it refers to Muhammad, the same rule remains: recognition must be real and clear for knowing concealment to apply.

This rule does not concern only the People of the Book. A Muslim, too, may see a truth clearly in the Qur’an but cover it up or reinterpret it to preserve a predetermined conclusion because it conflicts with a transmitted account, a denomination, social standing, or a cherished belief.

Concealing truth is not the disease of one people. Wherever knowledge exists but a person is unwilling to remain honest with what they know, the same danger returns.

Above all, this verse teaches us caution in judgment. We cannot tell from someone’s outward religious identity how clearly truth has become known to them. For ourselves, however, the question is closer and harder:

When we recognize a truth, do we allow it to change us, or do we conceal it so that what we already cherish remains untouched?
