Al-Baqarah 2:194

ۚ ۚ

The sacred month is for the sacred month, and sacred bounds are subject to qisas. So whoever transgresses against you, transgress against him in the same measure as he has transgressed against you; and practice taqwa toward The God, and know that The God is with those who practice taqwa.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verses, The God set out the limits of fighting aggressors, ending fitnah, and the necessity of desisting from war and aggression. Now, in Al-Baqarah 2:194, He again emphasizes justice and proportionality in responding, even when the enemy has violated the sanctity of a sacred time or place.

“الشَّهْرُ الْحَرَامُ بِالشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ وَالْحُرُمَاتُ قِصَاصٌ”

“The sacred month is for the sacred month, and sacred bounds are subject to qisas.”

The sacred months are times endowed with sanctity. In this verse, The God makes clear that their sanctity must not become a means of shielding an aggressor. If a group violates a sacred month and commits aggression during it, it cannot use that same sanctity to prevent a just response.

But the more important point is the expression “وَالْحُرُمَاتُ قِصَاصٌ”.

In Al-Baqarah 2:178, The God spoke of qisas in relation to killing. Now He uses the same word concerning sacred bounds. This usage again shows that qisas does not mean “getting even” and seeking revenge to assuage personal anger, resentment, and grievances. Qisas must serve the pursuit of rights and the establishment of justice, not become a means of soothing anger and seeking revenge.

“Qisas” comes from the root ق ص ص, connected with following a trace, pursuing, and reaching what has occurred. In this verse, qisas concerns pursuing violations of sacred bounds and responding proportionately. The aggression must be identified, its perpetrator established, and the response must not exceed what is right.

Qisas in cases of killing must therefore not be reduced to revenge and hasty killing either. As we examined in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:178, addressing a killing requires investigation, follow-up, identifying responsibility, and administering justice. Here, the use of qisas concerning sacred bounds shows that pursuing rights and responding justly are principles not confined to killing.

The God immediately also states the measure and limit of the response:

“فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فَاعْتَدُوا عَلَيْهِ بِمِثْلِ مَا اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ”

“So whoever transgresses against you, transgress against him in the same measure as he has transgressed against you.”

The phrase “بِمِثْلِ مَا اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ” is the fundamental principle of this command: the response to aggression must not exceed the measure of the initial aggression.

If the enemy violates the sanctity of a sacred month, that does not authorize you to violate every sacred bound. If a group infringes your rights, you have no right to trample innocent people’s rights under the pretext of responding. If the enemy causes damage, you cannot, in the name of resistance, inflict greater and disproportionate damage upon them or others.

A linguistic point also matters here. The God uses the root ع د و on both sides of the expression: first “اعْتَدَىٰ” for the initial aggression, and then “فَاعْتَدُوا” for the response. But the two do not have the same moral status, because the response is restricted by “بِمِثْلِ”. The initial aggression violates rights; the permitted response is a confrontation that must remain within justice and proportionality.

This command does not permit repeating every crime. If the enemy has killed children or other innocent people, we have no right to kill their children and innocent people and call it a response in kind. Responsibility for a crime belongs to its perpetrator, not their family, people, or fellow believers. As Al-Anam 6:164 states, no one bears another’s burden of sin.

A response in kind must therefore involve pursuing responsibility and observing the limits of justice, not blind revenge.

The same principle appears in An-Nahl 16:126: The God limits the response to harm and oppression to its measure and then describes patient endurance as better. In Ash-Shura 42:40, He also speaks of a proportionate response to wrongdoing and honors pardon and reconciliation.

The right to respond therefore exists, but having a right does not authorize excess in exercising it.

At the end of the verse, He says:

“وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُتَّقِينَ”

“And practice taqwa toward The God, and know that The God is with those who practice taqwa.”

Why does The God command taqwa again immediately after permitting a response in kind?

Because in anger, hostility, and revenge, a person may be more likely than at any other time to cross the bounds of justice. Someone who has been oppressed may have a right to respond, but must take care not to become an oppressor in exercising that right.

Taqwa, from the root و ق ي, means care, protection, and self-restraint. In this verse, taqwa means guarding oneself against oppression, excess, and violations of others’ rights even when confronting an aggressor. The broader meaning of this concept is examined in “What Is Taqwa in the Quran?”.

At the end, The God does not say that He is with whoever wins, or that every group considering itself oppressed will enjoy His support. He says: “إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُتَّقِينَ”—The God is with those who practice taqwa.

Even victory over an enemy is therefore not the ultimate measure of being in the right. A person may initially be oppressed, yet exceed the limits in responding and commit oppression themselves. What keeps them in The God’s way is observing taqwa and justice, not merely claiming to have been oppressed or achieving victory over an enemy.

Qisas serves the pursuit of rights and the administration of justice, not “getting even” and assuaging personal resentment. Even when the enemy initiates aggression, our response must not exceed what is right. The God is with those who practice taqwa: those who observe the limits of justice even at the height of hostility and power, not those who commit fresh transgression in the name of responding in kind.
