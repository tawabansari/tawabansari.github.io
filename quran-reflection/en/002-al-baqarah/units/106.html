Al-Baqarah 2:106

ۗ

Whatever sign We supersede or cause to be forgotten, We bring one better than it or like it. Do you not know that The God has power over all things?

Our Reflection

To understand this verse, we first need to distinguish its Arabic verb, “We supersede” (nansakh), from the later technical meaning assigned to it in jurisprudence. The legal doctrine of abrogation treats one Qur’anic verse as canceling the ruling of another. That definition, however, is not the only meaning carried by the Arabic root N S KH.

The Qur’an itself uses this root in several different settings. In Al-A‘raf 7:154, the noun “inscription” (nuskhah) refers to the writing on Moses’ tablets. In Al-Jathiyah 45:29, the verb “We had recorded” (nastansikhu) concerns the recording and writing of deeds. In the field of writing, then, this root can concern a copy, transfer, or transcription. In Al-Hajj 22:52, The God also performs an act expressed by this root, but its object is not His own verses:

“The God removes what Satan casts, then makes His own verses firm.”

This use alone does not allow us to say that the root always means “rewriting,” any more than it allows us to say it always means “canceling a ruling.” Its range is broader. Something may be set aside, removed, transferred, or, in a written context, copied again. The precise meaning must be determined from what the act affects and from the sentence’s context.

The context of Al-Baqarah 2:106 is particularly decisive here. The preceding discussion has concerned the continuation of divine guidance. In 2:87, The God gave Moses the Book and sent messengers in succession after him; guidance did not stop with Moses. Then 2:90 identifies the reason for one group’s resistance to a new revelation:

“That The God should send down from His bounty upon whomever He wills among His servants.”

The problem was not revelation itself. It was that revelation had come through a channel outside their expectations. The next verse exposes the same outlook in their own words:

“We believe in what was sent down to us.”

But the Qur’an immediately says that they reject what lies beyond it, even though that very message is “the truth” and “confirming what is with them.” It is true and confirms what they themselves possess. The new message has not come to destroy all earlier truth. On the contrary, it confirms it; yet The God has not made Himself a prisoner of one people’s inheritance.

In 2:97, the same point is made again about the Qur’an itself: Gabriel brought it down upon the Prophet’s heart by the permission of The God, “confirming what came before it.”

In 2:101, we then encounter a more disturbing image. A messenger comes from The God confirming what the recipients of scripture possess, yet a group among those given the Book cast the Book of The God behind their backs. The issue is not merely a few lost letters. A book can remain in existence while losing its place as the standard of judgment.

Earlier in this same part of the surah, there had also been discussion of distorting words after understanding them, writing human words and saying “This is from The God,” and believing in part of the Book while rejecting another part. Elsewhere, the Qur’an describes the same failing as displacing words from their proper places. By the time we reach 2:106, we therefore have behind us the history of a scripture that was revealed and read, part of it preserved, part set aside, other material added to it, and its words sometimes diverted from their proper place.

Just one verse before “Whatever We supersede,” the question of new revelation returns:

“Those who have rejected truth among the People of the Book, and those who associate others with The God, do not wish any good to be sent down to you from your Lord.”

The verse’s answer is that mercy does not belong to them:

“The God singles out for His mercy whomever He wills.”

Immediately after this comes:

“Whatever sign We supersede or cause to be forgotten, We bring one better than it or like it.”

Read in this sequence, our understanding is that the primary subject is not the invalidation of verses within this Qur’an, but The God’s authority to restore, purify, and continue guidance. If a sign or revelation from earlier guidance has been displaced, covered by human fabrications and other intrusions, or lost to people in its proper form, the source of revelation can restore it to its rightful place. And if something has been forgotten, He can bring something better than it or like it.

The pairing of “We supersede” with “We cause to be forgotten” also fits this reading. On one side is something requiring replacement and proper restoration; on the other is something that has been forgotten. In both cases, what makes good the loss comes from the One who first sent guidance:

“We bring one better than it or like it.”

“Better” does not mean that the earlier words of The God were bad. At another stage, for another audience, or in the continuation of a mission, guidance may bring a more appropriate command. Jesus confirmed the Torah while also saying that he had come to make lawful some of what had been forbidden to the Children of Israel. Confirming the past does not prevent The God from issuing a new command.

The Arabic word “sign” (āyah) must not automatically be restricted to “one of the numbered verses in this written Qur’an.” The Qur’an uses it for divine signs, miracles, and revealed verses. Turning “any sign” into “any verse of this Qur’an whose ruling will later be canceled” is therefore itself an interpretation, not something the wording alone states.

The next verse makes this direction clearer:

“Do you not know that to The God belongs dominion over the heavens and the earth?”

The central issue is thus the authority of The God. The same God who gave Moses the Book, brought messengers after him, sent Jesus, revealed the Qur’an, and bestowed His bounty on whomever He willed has the right to continue guidance and correct what has departed from its proper form. No recipient of scripture can say that, after what He has given us, The God no longer has the right to bring anything new.

Al-Hajj 22:52 makes the direction of this removal still more significant for us. There, The God removes “what Satan casts” and then makes His own verses firm. What must go is Satan’s interference and suggestion; the divine verses are not to be silenced in order to preserve that interference. In our reading, this principle accords with the context of Al-Baqarah: new revelation can clear away what has been displaced, forgotten, or mixed with other statements in the course of earlier guidance, and establish the truth firmly again.

This reading does not mean that The God never changes a command. Command belongs to Him. He can end a ruling, lighten a duty, or bring a new command. The question is who has announced that change. In 2:106, The God Himself is the subject of the verb:

“We supersede.”

And He Himself is the One who brings the replacement:

“We bring.”

Even if someone insists that this verse can also encompass verses of this Qur’an, a question remains: which verse? Al-Baqarah 2:106 declares no particular Qur’anic verse abrogated, nor does it say that later generations may use a transmitted report or a legal theory to suspend the ruling of an existing verse.

A detailed examination of this issue—the uses of N S KH, Al-Hajj 22:52, An-Nahl 16:101, the change of prayer direction, and examples later classified as abrogating and abrogated—appears in Naskh in the Qur’an: Challenging the Claim That Its Verses Can Be Set Aside. For the present verse, this much is sufficient: its context connects the discussion to earlier guidance, fresh revelation, and divine authority, not to a hidden list of Qur’anic verses that should be recited while their rulings are ignored.

The example of stoning makes this reversal particularly tangible. Torah law prescribed stoning for certain forms of illicit sexual relations. When the Qur’an states the ruling on illicit sexual relations for the community it addresses, it does not repeat that punishment:

“The woman and the man who commit illicit sexual relations: lash each of them a hundred lashes.” (An-Nur 24:2)

In this reading, that is precisely an example of guidance continuing through a new command: the ruling found in the earlier law has not remained the final standard, and the Qur’an has brought its own ruling. A detailed study of this issue, from Surah An-Nur’s ruling to An-Nisa 4:25, the reciprocal oaths of spouses in an accusation of infidelity, and the report of a “stoning verse,” appears in Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Rule Derived from Reports?

The end of the verse returns everything to the true holder of authority:

“Do you not know that The God has power over all things?”

He does not seek permission from the recipients of scripture to send fresh guidance, nor does He need the approval of religious institutions to correct what has gone off course. The power to supersede, bring a replacement, and make His verses firm belongs to Him.

The bitter irony of history is that some of our esteemed commentators and religious jurists reintroduced, through hadith and transmitted reports, a ruling found in the earlier law for which the Qur’an had supplied a different command. Then, to preserve that restored ruling, they declared the Qur’an’s clear ruling abrogated. In the case of stoning, what should have been judged by the Qur’an was once again given authority over it: the earlier ruling was restored, and the Qur’anic verse itself was set aside to make room for it.
