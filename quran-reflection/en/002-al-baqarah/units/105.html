Al-Baqarah 2:105

ۗ ۚ

Those who have rejected truth among the People of the Book, and those who associate others with The God, do not wish any good to be sent down to you from your Lord; and The God singles out for His mercy whomever He wills, and The God is the possessor of immense bounty.

Our Reflection

In the name of The God, the All-Merciful God and the Ever-Merciful Lord and Nurturing Master

The verse begins with a precise distinction:

“Those who have rejected truth among the People of the Book, and those who associate others with The God.”

The Qur’an does not call all the People of the Book rejecters of truth, nor does it identify the People of the Book with those who associate others with The God. “Among the People of the Book” specifies those within that community who have rejected truth; those who associate others with Him are then named separately. We saw the same distinction earlier in Al-Baqarah 2:96, where the Children of Israel were not identified with “those who associated others with The God.”

Being among the People of the Book, associating others with The God, and rejecting truth are therefore not three names for the same thing. Rejection of truth is not the name of a fixed ethnic or religious group; it is a condition that can arise within a religious community as well. The Qur’an even speaks of people who believed and then rejected truth. Just as the designation “People of the Book” does not automatically make someone a rejecter of truth or one who associates others with The God, neither “believer” nor “Muslim” makes someone immune to the danger of rejecting truth.

The problem with this group becomes apparent in what follows:

“They do not wish … that any good should be sent down to you from your Lord.”

The word “good” is left open in the verse. In this context, guidance and revelation are among the clearest instances of divine good, but the word is not restricted to one instance. The issue is that someone may oppose a good thing not because they consider it false, but because they do not want that good to reach someone else.

A few verses later, the Qur’an also names this failing:

“Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you back … out of envy arising from within themselves.” (Al-Baqarah 2:109)

Envy is not merely wanting someone else’s wealth or position. Religious partisanship can take the same form: we accept truth as long as it comes through our own path, our people, our denomination, or those whom we consider worthy. When the same good comes from somewhere else, truth itself is no longer the issue; the question becomes, “Why that person?”

Within this verse, there is a striking contrast between “wishes” (yawaddu) and “wills” (yashāʾu). A person dislikes something and wishes that good would not reach someone else; yet a few words later the Qur’an says:

“The God singles out for His mercy whomever He wills.”

Human wishes and partisanship do not set boundaries for the will of The God. People want to keep mercy within the limits of their own community, but mercy belongs to Him. No people, family, religious community, or denomination can dictate the channels through which His good must pass or whom His mercy may reach.

The same failing has appeared in different forms in the preceding discussion of the Children of Israel: the assumption of a special standing, guaranteed salvation, being the children and beloved of God, or the afterlife belonging exclusively to one community. But if Muhammad’s followers repeat the same reasoning in their own name, the underlying failing remains unchanged. We, too, can turn “Muhammad’s community” into a certificate of inherent superiority, or detach the expression “the best community” from the responsibilities stated in the same verse and use it to guarantee our own superiority.

The will of The God is independent of these inherited claims. He has not confined His mercy to the names people have adopted for themselves.

At the end, the verse says:

“The God is the possessor of immense bounty.”

If His bounty were small and limited, someone might fear that another person’s receiving a share would reduce their own. But precisely against this narrow outlook, the Qur’an speaks of “immense bounty.” There is no shortage in His bounty; the narrowness lies in our outlook.

When His mercy reaches someone else, it does not diminish His mercy for us. Envy of another person’s good therefore arises not only from partisanship, but also from imagining the bounty of The God to be small.

Partisanship asks, “Why that person?” Faith asks, “Is this true?”
