Al-Baqarah 2:231

ۚ ۚ ۚ ۚ ۚ

And when you divorce women and they reach the end of their term, retain them honorably or release them honorably; and do not retain them to cause harm and transgress. And whoever does that has wronged himself. And do not make a mockery of the signs of The God; and remember the blessing of The God upon you and what He has sent down to you of the Book and wisdom; through it He admonishes you. And be mindful of The God, and know that The God knows all things.

Our Reflection

In setting out the rulings on divorce, the Quran repeatedly emphasizes a fundamental principle: married life must either continue properly or end properly.

In Al-Baqarah 2:229, it stated:

“فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ”

“Then retaining honorably or releasing with kindness.”

In the present verse, it states once again:

“فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ سَرِّحُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ”

“Then retain them honorably or release them honorably.”

And in At-Talaq 65:2, it repeats the same command:

“فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ”

“Then retain them properly or part from them properly.”

This repeated emphasis shows that honorable conduct is an undeniable principle of marital relationships. The Quran neither regards continuing married life at any cost as a virtue nor makes separation a license for hostility and violating one another’s rights.

The marriage covenant must be preserved with affection, respect, and responsibility. If it cannot continue, separation must likewise respect rights and human dignity. No one has the right to keep another person in a relationship that offers neither a decent shared life nor a way out.

“وَإِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَبَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ”

“And when you divorce women and they reach the end of their term.”

“أَجَل” refers to a specified period and endpoint. Here the discussion concerns the waiting period after divorce, described in Al-Baqarah 2:228.

During this period, returning to married life is possible; however, the purpose of this opportunity is to repair the relationship, not prolong the woman’s suffering and uncertainty.

God continues in this same verse:

“وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِتَعْتَدُوا”

“And do not retain them to cause harm and transgress.”

This expression clarifies the boundary between returning for reconciliation and returning to inflict harm.

A man may divorce his wife but take her back near the end of the waiting period—not out of affection or a decision to repair their life together, but to prevent the woman from becoming free and building another future. He may then continue his earlier harmful conduct and divorce her once again.

Is such a return reconciliation, or using the right of return to continue oppression?

To prevent this very abuse, the Quran has not left the opportunity to divorce and return unlimited.

In Al-Baqarah 2:229, it stated:

“الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ”

“Divorce is twice.”

This means that the possibility of divorce and return within this framework is limited to two occasions, so that a man cannot divorce his wife, take her back, and repeat this behavior endlessly.

After the third divorce, as explained in Al-Baqarah 2:230, there is no longer a path to returning directly to the same woman. After observing the relevant rulings, she can marry another husband and build a new life. Only if that marriage also later ends in divorce does the possibility of a new marriage contract with the first husband arise—and that is conditional upon both thinking they can observe the divine limits.

Thus, the Quran both forbids retaining a woman to harm her and, by limiting the occasions of divorce and return, closes the way to repeated abuse of this authority.

But harm is not confined to divorcing a woman and taking her back. Sometimes a man refuses separation altogether while turning married life into an environment filled with violence, humiliation, and deprivation.

“ضِرار,” from the root ض ر ر, is connected with causing loss and harm. In this verse, its combination with “لِتَعْتَدُوا” shows that the issue is retaining the relationship in order to transgress another person’s rights.

This harm can be physical, financial, psychological, or social. Beating a woman, threatening and humiliating her, depriving her of necessities, preventing her education and work, cutting off her contact with family and relatives, or using economic dependence to silence her can all be forms of abuse and violations of rights.

In some patriarchal societies, such behavior is justified in the name of obedience to the husband, preserving family honor, or following ancestral customs—as though marriage gave the man authority to command every aspect of the woman’s life and obliged her to endure any treatment.

Yet the Quran speaks of “بِمَعْرُوفٍ.”

Ma‘ruf, from the root ع ر ف, is connected with knowing and recognizing; in social and ethical usage, it refers to conduct recognized as good and proper. This concept cannot be confined to every inherited custom, because a practice may be widespread among people while remaining incompatible with justice and human dignity.

For example, some families in Afghanistan still hold the view that a daughter has no right to return to her father’s home after marriage. Sometimes she is told: “You leave your father’s home in a wedding dress and return only in a shroud.”

These words may weigh on the woman’s mind from the beginning of marriage. If she encounters violence and humiliation in her husband’s home, is deprived of her rights, or finds life transformed into constant suffering, she knows that her family of origin is also unwilling to receive or support her.

Sometimes parents ask their daughter to endure any oppression for the family’s reputation, yet if their son were treated in the same way, they would be the first to defend him.

Why should a son’s safety and dignity matter, while a daughter’s life and peace are sacrificed to family reputation?

Have parents fulfilled their moral duty when they leave their daughter defenseless against violence and abuse merely so that people will not say their daughter has divorced?

In such a culture, the family’s outward reputation is sometimes valued above the woman’s life, safety, and dignity.

This possessive outlook may not end even with the husband’s death. A widow who has lost her husband sometimes hears from her family of origin that there is no longer a place for her in that home and that she must remain in her husband’s home for the rest of her life.

In some families, a widow is also forced to marry her deceased husband’s brother—as though, after the man’s death, the woman had been inherited by his family like his land and property.

Sometimes preserving the children, inheritance, land, or family reputation is invoked to justify such behavior, while the central issue is the disregard of the woman’s agency and rights.

God explicitly states in An-Nisa 4:19:

“لَا يَحِلُّ لَكُمْ أَنْ تَرِثُوا النِّسَاءَ كَرْهًا”

“It is not lawful for you to inherit women against their will.”

A woman is not her husband’s inheritance for relatives to decide her fate after his death. Just as marriage does not give a man ownership of the woman, the husband’s death does not transfer control over her life to his family.

All these behaviors share one assumption: that after marriage, the woman is no longer an independent person with rights and must remain under the authority of her husband or his family for the rest of her life.

Yet in Al-Baqarah 2:228, the Quran recognizes the mutual rights of women and men and states:

“وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ”

“And women have rights corresponding honorably to their obligations.”

Married life is therefore more than a collection of the woman’s duties toward the man. The man, too, is responsible toward his wife, and no social custom can abolish the rights God has recognized for her.

In An-Nisa 4:19, God commands honorable companionship with women, and in Ar-Rum 30:21, He identifies tranquility, affection, and mercy as signs of the marital bond.

How can a relationship in which the woman continually lives with fear, humiliation, and deprivation be considered the decent life of which the Quran speaks?

Neither husband, parents, relatives, nor social customs have the right to keep a woman in a situation where her life becomes a field of abuse and oppression in the name of marriage, family honor, or religious law.

The Quran’s command is clear: retain honorably or release honorably—not retain at any cost and justify every form of conduct in the name of preserving the family.

“وَمَنْ يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ”

“And whoever does that has wronged himself.”

After forbidding the retention of women to cause harm, God describes such conduct as wrongdoing against oneself.

A man may imagine that he has preserved his power by keeping his wife in a situation marked by fear and deprivation. Lacking support, the woman may be unable to seek justice, and others may not know the reality of their life.

But the woman’s inability to defend her rights does not make the man’s conduct just.

Someone who oppresses another takes on responsibility before The God through their conduct. They may outwardly hold power over another person, but by choosing the path of oppression, they have also harmed themselves.

In Al-Baqarah 2:281, God reminds us that every soul will receive in full what it has earned and that no one will be wronged.

A person cannot free themselves from responsibility for their actions by depriving another of their rights. Oppressing others also confronts a person with the reality of their own conduct.

“وَلَا تَتَّخِذُوا آيَاتِ اللَّهِ هُزُوًا”

“And do not make a mockery of the signs of The God.”

This warning comes immediately after the prohibition against retaining women to harm them.

Someone may outwardly exercise the right of return and say that the Quran has granted him this authority. But if his purpose is to prevent the woman’s freedom and prolong her suffering, he is in fact using the outward form of the ruling to undermine the very honorable conduct and reconciliation the Quran prescribes.

This is more than an abuse of a family-related authority; it is turning a divine ruling into a means of pursuing an aim contrary to that very ruling.

People sometimes become so entangled in words, formalities, and religious customs that they forget the purpose and reality of God’s command. Someone may regard himself as deeply religious, discuss the rulings on divorce and return, and insist on implementing religious law, yet treat his wife at home as though she had no rights or agency.

Has such a person actually carried out the Quran’s command, or is he using the Quran’s name to justify his own conduct?

The divine signs cannot be made a mockery of—whether through repeating divorce formulas, returning with the intention of causing harm, or constructing rulings that violate people’s rights and dignity.

“وَاذْكُرُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ وَمَا أَنْزَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَالْحِكْمَةِ يَعِظُكُمْ بِهِ”

“And remember the blessing of The God upon you and what He has sent down to you of the Book and wisdom; through it He admonishes you.”

After stating these rulings and warnings, God recalls His blessing and what He has sent down of the Book and wisdom.

The Book and wisdom are not there merely for people to memorize the wording of rulings or feel superior to others because they know them. Through this guidance, God admonishes people to reform their conduct and recognize others’ rights.

What benefit is there in a man having extensive knowledge of divorce, the waiting period, and return, yet oppressing his wife in his own life? What value is there in a family speaking of religion and religious law, yet leaving its daughter defenseless against violence and abuse?

In Al-Isra 17:39, after stating a series of ethical and social commands, God identifies them as part of the wisdom revealed to the Prophet.

Wisdom should therefore not be separated from people’s conduct and practical responsibility. In this very verse, honorable treatment, avoiding harm, protecting rights, and refraining from oppression are expressions of the guidance through which God admonishes people.

How has someone who knows these commands but acts against them in their own life benefited from the wisdom of the Book?

“وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ”

“And be mindful of The God, and know that The God knows all things.”

The verse ends with the command to be mindful and a reminder of God’s all-encompassing knowledge.

Taqwa, from the root و ق ي, is connected with guarding and taking care. A mindful person must watch their conduct, especially when their authority and power can affect someone else’s life.

In family relationships, much behavior occurs in private. A man may be kind, courteous, and religious in the presence of others, yet humiliate his wife at home, disregard her rights, and use his power to hurt her.

People may not know the reality of that life, but The God knows people’s intentions and actions.

“أَنَّ اللَّهَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ”

“The God knows all things.”

A man who returns to his wife to repair the relationship and a man who exercises the right of return merely to continue hurting her may outwardly perform the same act, but their intentions and the reality of their conduct are not the same.

God knows this difference.

The verse’s message is clear: the Quran has repeatedly commanded that married life continue honorably or end with honorable conduct and kindness. The right of return is for reconciliation, not harm; limiting the occasions of divorce and return also closes the way to endless abuse of this authority.

No man has the right to keep his wife bound to a relationship in order to harm her, and no family custom or religious claim can turn oppression of women into honorable conduct. Anyone who traps a woman in suffering and deprivation in the name of marriage, the right of return, family honor, or religious law must know that he has also wronged himself, because The God knows the intentions and actions of all people.
