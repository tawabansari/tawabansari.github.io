Al-Baqarah 2:37

Then Adam received words from his Lord (Rabb), so He turned toward him in acceptance (tawbah). Indeed, He is The Oft-Returning, The Compassionate.

Our Reflection

After the slip and the exit from the previous state, the verse suddenly turns the path from fall to return. The Qur'an does not say that Adam was abandoned, cursed, or that his descendants were stained with inherited sin. Instead, it says: fa-talaqqa adamu min rabbihi kalimat; Adam received words from his Lord. At the very point where the human being has slipped, the gate of lordly nurturing opens. His Lord does not abandon him on the path of training, repair, and return.

The choice of the word talaqqa is very important. God could have used “wahy” or “awha,” but He does not do so here. Talaqqa, from the root L Q Y, is connected with meeting, encountering, facing, and receiving something in encounter. The word does not only show the delivery of a message from above to below; it also shows the state of the receiver. Adam did not merely “hear” information. He received words, accepted them, and placed them within himself. Here, after the slip, the human being enters a state of receptivity. This is the difference: after error, Iblis justified; after error, Adam received.

The fact that the Qur'an does not speak here of wahy may indicate that this stage is not necessarily the station of prophethood or public legislation. These words may be nurturing guidance, an inspiration of return, or the opening of the language of confession and repentance inside Adam. The Qur'an does not quote the words themselves in this verse. In Al-A'raf 7:23, Adam and his spouse say: rabbana zalamna anfusana; Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. That verse may be the clearest Qur'anic resonance for the spirit of those words, but it is better to say cautiously that Al-Baqarah itself does not state that the exact words were this phrase. What is certain is that these words brought Adam to repentance.

The word kalimat itself is not small. In the Qur'an, a word is not merely an empty utterance. A word can carry meaning, direction, command, promise, criterion, and transforming force. In the root K L M, a layer of impact and deep marking can also be seen, as though speech leaves a mark of meaning in the receiver. After the slip, Adam needed words that could move him from confusion to confession, from shame to return, and from fall to guidance.

Then the verse says: fa-taba alayhi. In common language, repentance is usually spoken of as the human being returning to God, but here the subject of tawbah is God: God turned toward Adam. This is very delicate. Tawbah in the Qur'an is a two-directional movement in the field of mercy: the human being turns back from error, and God turns toward them with mercy and acceptance. If God does not open the way of return, the human being does not even find the language of repentance.

In terms of the sequence of events, there are strong Qur'anic signs that Adam's repentance occurred before final settlement on earth. In Al-Baqarah, verse 36 brings the command of descent after the slip, but verse 37 immediately states the receiving of words and the acceptance of repentance, and then in verse 38 it says again: qulna ihbitu minha jami'an. This repetition shows that verse 37 is an important pause between two stages: first, the declaration of the consequence of the slip; then, the receiving of words and repentance; and after that, the broader command of descent joined with the promise of guidance.

This sequence appears more clearly in Surah Taha. There, after Adam's disobedience, it says: thumma ijtabahu rabbuhu fa-taba alayhi wa-hada; then his Lord chose him, turned toward him, and guided him. After that comes: qala ihbita minha jami'an. So the Qur'anic structure shows that Adam received repentance and guidance before full entry into the earthly stage. He was sent to earth, but not as a cursed and abandoned being. He was sent as a being who slipped, learned, had his repentance accepted, and then entered the field of earth with the promise of guidance.

Here the difference between Adam and Iblis becomes more complete. After his error, Iblis said: bima aghwaytani; because You misled me. Adam, however, received the words. Iblis threw responsibility outward; Adam accepted it. Iblis took the path of justification; Adam took the path of return. Therefore, the dignity of the human being was not in being errorless, but in the capacity to receive words, accept responsibility, and return to the Lord.

The verse closes with two divine names: al-tawwab al-rahim. God is not merely “able to accept repentance.” He is Tawwab, the One who turns repeatedly toward the servant and repeatedly opens the way of return. And He is Rahim, possessing continuous and nurturing mercy. These two names show that the story of Adam is not the story of an original curse. It is the story of lordly nurturing, education, slipping, words, repentance, and mercy.
