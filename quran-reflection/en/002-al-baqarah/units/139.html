Al-Baqarah 2:139

Say: “Do you debate with us about The God, while He is our Lord and your Lord? Our deeds are ours, and your deeds are yours; and we make our religion and devotion pure for Him.”

Our Reflection

The previous verse spoke of “the coloring of The God”: a coloring understood in this passage through God’s oneness, surrender, faith without discrimination, freedom from religious exclusivity, and service to God. Now Muhammad is commanded to continue this same reasoning in dialogue with the People of the Book:

“Do you dispute with us about God?”

The Arabic verb rendered “dispute with us” (tuḥājjūnanā) belongs to the root Ḥ J J and the Form III verb ḥājja: two sides bring arguments and evidence before one another. The word itself therefore does not necessarily mean hostility or antagonism. The Qur’an does not reject dialogue or reasoning. The issue is that this dispute concerns a God whom no group owns:

“He is our Lord and your Lord.”

This statement breaks the logic of religious exclusivity at its foundation. Differences in peoplehood, prescribed ways, history, and religious understandings do not divide God’s lordship. No community can speak as though God belongs to it first and others can reach Him only by accepting its identity.

The same God who is our Lord is your Lord too.

The Qur’an then makes the boundary of responsibility clear:

“Our deeds are ours, and your deeds are yours.”

This does not mean declaring all deeds and all beliefs right. The Qur’an itself judges between truth and falsehood. But responsibility for each person’s choices and actions rests with that person. Disagreement in belief does not give us the right to take control of another’s conscience or force them to make the choice we want.

Here, freedom of belief goes together with responsibility. A person is free to choose, but is also responsible for that choice. We answer for our deeds, and they answer for theirs. Invitation, criticism, and reasoning remain; coercion does not take their place.

Ash-Shura 42:15 provides a complementary witness, expressing the same reasoning in closely similar words:

“So call to this, and stand firm as you have been commanded, and do not follow their desires. Say: I believe in every Book that The God has sent down, and I have been commanded to act justly among you. The God is our Lord and your Lord. Our deeds are ours, and your deeds are yours. There is no further argument between us and you. The God will gather us together, and to Him is the return.”
Ash-Shura 42:15

The same words from Al-Baqarah are heard again: “The God is our Lord and your Lord. Our deeds are ours, and your deeds are yours.” The invitation continues, and disagreement is not concealed, but the endless quarrel over ownership of God comes to an end. Ultimately, God will gather everyone together, and the return is to Him.

At its close, however, Al-Baqarah 2:139 identifies the believers’ position through a particularly important word:

“And we devote ourselves sincerely to Him.”

The verse does not use “pure” (khāliṣūn) but “those who make something pure or dedicate it exclusively” (mukhliṣūn) from akhlaṣa. The point is not merely to regard oneself as pure, but to make one’s religion and devotion pure for Him.

Our Lord and your Lord are one. Each answers for their own deeds. Yet the position the Qur’an asks of the community of believers is to free their relationship with that shared Lord from admixture with other partners:

“For Him, making our devotion pure.”

Sincere devotion means that God remains at the center. Religion must not become so mixed with ethnic idolization, devotion to personalities, religious prejudice, political power, or unquestioning obedience to human beings that God’s name remains while the actual direction of service shifts elsewhere.

This forms a meaningful connection with the preceding verses. The Qur’an said:

“And to Him we surrender.”

Then it said:

“And we serve Him.”

And now it says:

“And we make our religion and devotion pure for Him.”

We do not need to treat these as separate technical stages, but their appearance alongside one another is meaningful. In all three, one expression remains unchanged:

“For Him.”

Surrender for Him, worship for Him, and sincere devotion for Him.

Here the claim to sincerity must also be turned upon ourselves. Merely saying “We devote ourselves sincerely to Him” is not enough. If, in practice, our religion becomes an instrument of ethnic prejudice, religious superiority, the pursuit of power, or devotion to personalities, we must ask how much of this service is still truly “for Him.”

Sincere devotion is not only a matter of an intention hidden in the heart; it also shows itself in the way our religious life is organized. Wherever a person, denomination, people, or religious institution becomes so central that its word effectively stands above truth, the danger of admixture has begun.

The lesson for us is clear. We can debate with another person, offer reasons, and acknowledge our disagreements, but we cannot make God the property of our own sect. He is our Lord and the Lord of others. Each person is responsible for their own actions. Our duty is not to take control of another’s faith. We must first free our own religion from prejudice, power, personalities, and our own interests so that we can honestly say:

“We devote ourselves sincerely to Him.”
