Al-Baqarah 2:271

ۖ ۚ ۗ

If you disclose your charities, how good that is; and if you conceal them and give them to the needy, that is better for you; and He removes some of your misdeeds from you; and The God is aware of what you do.

Our Reflection

In Al-Baqarah 2:264, the Quran forbade ostentatious giving and showing off before people. But is every charitable act performed in others’ sight an act of ostentation?

This verse gives a clear answer:

“إِنْ تُبْدُوا الصَّدَقَاتِ فَنِعِمَّا هِيَ”

“If you disclose your charities, how good that is.”

“تُبْدُوا,” from the root B D W, refers to revealing and making visible. The Quran regards disclosing charities as good and does not identify it in itself as a sign of ostentation.

Yet in some traditional interpretations, anyone who makes their assistance public is accused of showing off, whereas public giving can also have good motives and valuable social effects.

For example, someone creates content on YouTube encouraging people to help those in need. They collect donations, arrange for them to reach the needy, and publish video reports showing people how their donations have been used.

Such activity can encourage more people to do good, increase public trust through transparency in the use of donations, and make assistance possible for more people in need.

If this person also earns legitimate income from their content and viewership, earning income alone does not amount to ostentation. Likewise, in At-Tawbah 9:60, the Quran includes “الْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا”, the workers and administrators of charity, among its recipients. Compensating the expenses and actual work of those collecting and distributing charity can therefore form part of this activity, provided the rights of those in need are respected and donations are used honestly and faithfully.

There is, however, an important distinction between making good work public and doing it to show off.

Imagine, for example, a politician who helps the needy only during elections and takes their pictures so that people will consider him benevolent and vote for him. If his purpose is not to meet people’s needs but to gain fame and votes, that is ostentation.

Another example is the lavish welcome given to some pilgrims returning from Hajj: an animal is slaughtered and given in charity at their feet, their car is covered with flowers, and crowds in the streets inconvenience others. If the purpose of these ceremonies is to display the person’s status and religiosity before people, one should ask: was Hajj performed for the pleasure of The God or to gain people’s respect and praise?

Thus, the criterion of ostentation is not merely an act being seen, but the intention and purpose pursued through it.

The Quran then says:

“وَإِنْ تُخْفُوهَا وَتُؤْتُوهَا الْفُقَرَاءَ فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ”

“And if you conceal them and give them to the needy, that is better for you.”

“تُخْفُوهَا” refers to keeping charities hidden. Here the Quran presents concealing the gift and delivering it to those in need as better.

One important wisdom behind this preference is preserving the needy person’s reputation and dignity.

In Al-Baqarah 2:273, the Quran speaks of needy people whom others consider self-sufficient because of their restraint, and who do not ask people insistently. Such people may live in hardship but do not want their need exposed before others.

Thus, even if our purpose in filming and publicizing aid is to encourage others to do good, we have no right to reveal a recipient’s identity and need publicly without their consent.

We can report the amount of money collected, how it was spent, and the results of the assistance without sacrificing the reputation and privacy of those in need.

The recipient’s consent and freedom of choice also matter. Someone dependent on our assistance to meet a need should not be forced to accept filming and the publication of their face or private life in order to receive it. In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:264, we examined how reproach and injury can invalidate charity. Therefore, if publicizing charity causes distress to the needy recipient, it should be prevented; otherwise, it invalidates the charity.

Secret charity being better does not mean public charity is bad. Rather, the Quran draws attention to the good in concealing a gift and safeguarding the recipient’s dignity.

The verse then says:

“وَيُكَفِّرُ عَنْكُمْ مِنْ سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ”

“And He removes some of your misdeeds from you.”

“يُكَفِّرُ,” from the root K F R, here refers to covering and removing the effects of misdeeds. “سَيِّئَات” are a person’s bad deeds.

Here the Quran speaks of another good that returns to the giver: the removal of some of their misdeeds.

Hud 11:114 also states that good deeds remove bad deeds. In Al-Furqan 25:70, God speaks of transforming the misdeeds of those who repent, believe, and perform righteous deeds.

Good giving is therefore not merely for meeting others’ needs; the giver also benefits from its effects. By speaking of the expiation of misdeeds, the Quran recalls that good conduct can help reform a person’s ultimate outcome and cleanse them of their wrongs.

The verse finally says:

“وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ”

“And The God is aware of what you do.”

“Khabīr,” from the root KH B R, denotes precise awareness and knowledge of the reality of things.

This ending is highly significant for the subject of ostentation. God knows the outward and inward aspects of human conduct and a person’s true intention.

Thus, we cannot judge people’s intentions conclusively merely from whether their charity is public or secret. Someone may give before thousands of people with the sincere aim of helping those in need, while someone else may give even in secret to obtain a personal benefit.

Of course, observable conduct, such as humiliating a needy person or misusing charitable funds, remains open to examination and criticism. But knowledge of the truth of intentions rests with God.

The verse’s message is clear: public charity is good too, but concealing it when helping those in need can hold greater good. The central criteria are sincerity of intention, preserving human dignity, and acting rightly; The God knows the reality of what we do.
