Al-Imran 3:14

ۗ ۖ

The love of desirable things—women, sons, accumulated heaps of gold and silver, marked horses, livestock, and cultivated land—has been made attractive to people. That is the enjoyment of worldly life; and with The God is goodness of return.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verses, God spoke of the defeat of those who stood against truth despite their power and resources. He now draws our attention to things that attract people in worldly life and sometimes take such a hold on their hearts that they sacrifice truth and their hereafter to acquire or retain them.

“زُيِّنَ لِلنَّاسِ حُبُّ الشَّهَوَاتِ”

“The love of desirable things has been made attractive to people.”

“Al-shahawat,” from SH H W, refers to human desire and attraction toward things considered desirable and pleasing. In contemporary Persian, shahwat is often confined to sexual desire. Yet this verse itself shows that the Quran uses the word much more broadly: it can encompass love of family, wealth, possessions, material comforts, and other things people want in life.

The existence of these desires is not sinful in itself. Without wanting and caring about things, people would not strive to establish families, provide for life, cultivate the earth, or improve their own circumstances and those of others. What matters is how people respond to their desires and which paths they choose to fulfill them.

In At-Tawbah 9:24, the Quran clarifies this criterion: love for fathers, children, spouses, relatives, wealth, trade, and homes leads people astray when these become dearer to them than The God and His way. The problem is therefore not love itself; it begins when human desires displace faithfulness to truth.

“مِنَ النِّسَاءِ وَالْبَنِينَ وَالْقَنَاطِيرِ الْمُقَنْطَرَةِ مِنَ الذَّهَبِ وَالْفِضَّةِ وَالْخَيْلِ الْمُسَوَّمَةِ وَالْأَنْعَامِ وَالْحَرْثِ”

“Of women, sons, accumulated heaps of gold and silver, marked horses, livestock, and cultivated land.”

The Quran gives examples of familiar objects of human desire: affection and family bonds, children, accumulated wealth, valuable means of living, livestock husbandry, and agriculture.

Naming women and children alongside possessions does not mean equating human beings with property or commodities. The verse speaks of different things to which people are attached.

“Banin” means sons. Having a child and loving that child are also natural human desires. But love for a child, like other desires in life, must not lead a person to abandon truth and justice.

“Al-qanatir al-muqantarah” evokes abundant, accumulated wealth. “Al-khayl al-musawwamah” likewise recalls distinguished, marked horses, which in that era could provide transport, power, and social standing. Today, large bank accounts, luxurious homes, and expensive cars can be comparable examples of such attachment.

Alongside these are livestock and cultivated land, which provide the means of production, food, and the development of life.

None of these blessings, however, is inherently objectionable. Wealth can enable giving, building schools and hospitals, and helping those in need. Land and livestock can likewise provide livelihoods and serve people.

In Al-Araf 7:32, the Quran questions those who forbid God’s adornment and wholesome provisions to His servants. In Al-Qasas 28:77, people are also reminded to seek the home of the hereafter through what they have been given, without forgetting their share of this world.

The issue is therefore not whether people possess worldly blessings, but whether they use them for good or abandon truth and justice to acquire and retain them.

For example, a father may work to provide comfort for his children and earn a living honestly. Yet that same love for family can become an excuse for taking bribes, betraying a trust, or violating others’ rights. In the first case, his love supports good conduct; in the second, he has placed his desire above truth and justice.

We have examined this distinction in “The principle of governing one’s desires”: human desires must not replace truth and justice as the standard.

“ذَٰلِكَ مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا”

“That is the enjoyment of worldly life.”

“Mata‘” denotes something from which a person benefits. Worldly blessings are for use in life, but enjoyment of them is limited and eventually ends.

In Al-Kahf 18:46, the Quran describes wealth and sons as the adornment of worldly life, while regarding enduring righteous deeds as better in reward and hope.

In Al-Baqarah 2:86, it also speaks of those who purchased worldly life at the price of the hereafter.

This is the same losing exchange expressed by the Quranic phrase “thamanan qalilan,” a small price. A person may gain enormous wealth, power, or social standing in exchange for abandoning truth. Yet what they receive is a paltry price compared with the truth and hereafter they have lost. Al-Baqarah 2:41 likewise forbids selling divine signs for a small price.

The fundamental loss is therefore not in enjoying this world, but in forfeiting the lasting good of the hereafter for the world’s limited enjoyment.

“وَاللَّهُ عِنْدَهُ حُسْنُ الْمَآبِ”

“And with The God is goodness of return.”

“Al-ma’ab,” from A W B, refers to returning and a place of return. This expression recalls that worldly life is not humanity’s final destination, and the value of people’s choices cannot be measured solely by their present possessions and success.

The next verse, Aal Imran 3:15, makes this comparison clearer, speaking of what is better with the Lord than worldly enjoyment.

The verse’s message is that people may love and enjoy family, wealth, beauty, and life’s amenities, but must not fulfill these desires at the cost of truth, justice, and the hereafter. Worldly blessings are means for living, not life’s ultimate purpose.

Cross-references
  • Aal Imran 3:15: What is better with the Lord than worldly enjoyment.
  • At-Tawbah 9:24: A warning against loving family, wealth, and life’s amenities more than The God and His way.
  • Al-Araf 7:32: Questioning the prohibition of divine adornment and wholesome provisions.
  • Al-Qasas 28:77: Seeking the hereafter through worldly blessings without forgetting one’s share of worldly life.
  • Al-Kahf 18:7: The earth’s adornment as a setting for testing people in goodness of conduct.
  • Al-Kahf 18:46: Wealth and sons as worldly adornment, and the superiority of enduring righteous deeds.
  • Al-Munafiqun 63:9: A warning against being distracted from remembering The God by wealth and children.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:86: Purchasing worldly life at the price of the hereafter.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:41: Selling divine signs for a small price.
  • At-Tawbah 9:38: The smallness of worldly enjoyment compared with the hereafter.
  • An-Nahl 16:58: A negative reaction to news of a daughter’s birth.
  • An-Nahl 16:59: Criticism of contemptuous attitudes toward daughters.
  • Maryam 19:59: Following desires alongside neglecting prayer.
  • Az-Zukhruf 43:71: Desire and the soul’s longing in relation to the blessings of paradise.
  • Al-Hadid 57:20: Adornment, boasting, and rivalry for greater wealth and children in worldly life.
