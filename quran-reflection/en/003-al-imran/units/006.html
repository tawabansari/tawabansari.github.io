Al-Imran 3:6

ۚ

He is the One who shapes you in the wombs however He wills; there is no deity except Him; the Invincible, the Wise.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, God stated that nothing on earth or in heaven remains hidden from Him. He now directs our attention to one of creation’s most remarkable manifestations: the formation of a human being in the mother’s womb, where life begins out of our sight and gradually takes shape.

God attributes this shaping directly to Himself:

“هُوَ الَّذِى يُصَوِّرُكُمْ فِى الْأَرْحَامِ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ”

“He is the One who shapes you in the wombs however He wills.”

“كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ” indicates that how human beings take shape is not outside the will and sovereignty of The God.

Human formation in the womb does, of course, occur through natural and biological processes. Genetic characteristics, pregnancy conditions, nutrition, illnesses, chemicals, and even certain human behaviors or errors can affect fetal development. Yet the existence of these factors does not mean that God is removed from creation. These factors themselves operate in a world whose laws and possibilities of causal influence were established by The God. We have examined the relationship between natural causes and divine permission in detail in “Divine Permission in the Quran”.

This verse also has particular philosophical significance. Sometimes a child is born with visible physical differences, congenital disabilities, developmental disorders, or severe cognitive limitations. Such a person may never be able to walk or speak as others do, or independently understand and make decisions about certain matters. Should we imagine that their creation was pointless, or that their existence has no value within creation?

In Aal Imran 3:191, the Quran speaks of people of understanding who reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth and tell their Lord that He has not created it in vain. In Al-Muminun 23:115, God likewise asks people whether they supposed they were created without purpose and would not be returned to Him.

A human being’s worth therefore cannot be determined by physical beauty, bodily health, mental ability, or decision-making capacity. Someone born with severe limitations is also a creation of The God. We may not know all the wisdom in their existence, but our lack of knowledge does not establish that their creation is pointless. In At-Taghabun 64:3, God also speaks of making people’s forms good—a goodness that should not be measured solely by our limited standards of physical beauty, bodily health, or mental abilities.

The existence of such people can present a field of trial and testing for families, society, and all of us. Do we uphold justice toward someone unable to defend their rights? Do we provide a child needing continual care with opportunities for life, education, treatment, and support? Or do we belittle and marginalize them because of their differences and disabilities? Al-Anbiya 21:35 recalls that people are tested through good and evil. Enjoying health and ability, like encountering limitations, can test human responsibility.

Yet these individuals are not merely instruments for testing others; they are themselves human beings with dignity, rights, and an independent place in creation. In Al-Isra 17:70, God speaks of honoring the children of Adam, not only those with full health or customary abilities. Society therefore has a responsibility to support vulnerable people, rather than make their disability a pretext for depriving them. This connects directly with the principle of human dignity and the principle of society’s responsibility toward individuals.

Moral responsibility, in turn, relates to a person’s actual ability, understanding, and freedom of choice. Someone whose severe cognitive limitations prevent understanding certain matters or making conscious decisions cannot be assessed in that respect in the same way as someone who has those abilities. In Al-Baqarah 2:286, God explicitly states that He charges no soul beyond its capacity. This difference in responsibility does not mean a difference in human worth; it expresses justice in judgment.

“لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ”

“There is no deity except Him; the Invincible, the Wise.”

The verse ends with the two attributes “الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ.” “Aziz” expresses the power and invincibility of The God, while “Hakim” recalls that His creation and governance are inseparable from wisdom. Differences among people should therefore not be regarded as evidence that their existence is worthless or pointless. Nor does attributing their formation to divine will remove human responsibility to prevent avoidable harm, provide treatment and care, and support others.

All human beings, whatever their form, physical condition, or level of ability, are creations of The God. One may enjoy abundant health and ability, while another needs help with life’s most basic necessities; neither, because of these differences, lies outside the scope of the Lord’s knowledge, power, and wisdom. What is ours to do is not judge the worth of others’ existence, but recognize our responsibility toward them.
