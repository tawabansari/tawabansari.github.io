Al-Imran 3:3

He sent down the Book upon you in truth, confirming what is between its hands; and He sent down the Torah and the Gospel.

Our Reflection

God sent down the Book to Muhammad “بِالْحَقِّ”—in truth—and describes it as “مُصَدِّقًا,” confirming the truth of earlier revelations. The revelation of the Quran therefore does not mean that the Books revealed before it are false; their source is the same God.

The expression “لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ” points to what is between the hands and accessible. In Persian, the expression “having in hand” and the word for “access” convey the same idea. Thus, what is confirmed is the Books present among the People of the Book and available for consultation when the Quran was revealed, not merely their original, lost copies.

This reading also accords with other Quranic verses. Al-Baqarah 2:41 speaks of “مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا مَعَكُمْ”—confirming what is with you. In Aal Imran 3:93, the People of the Book are asked to bring the Torah and read it, and Al-Maidah 5:43 explicitly states that the Torah is with them and contains God’s judgment.

Despite these clear verses, some rely on reports and sources outside the Quran to confine the confirmation of earlier Books to their original, vanished copies. Through claims of abrogation and setting aside the Torah and Gospel, they seek to make divine guidance the monopoly of the followers of one Book and one community. Yet the Quran not only confirms earlier Books but also calls their people to consult and act upon the divine truth present in them. We have examined this subject in detail in “Mu’min or Muslim?”.

However, confirming existing Books does not mean endorsing every statement and addition within them. In Al-Baqarah 2:79, the Quran condemns writing human words and attributing them to God, and in Al-Maidah 5:13 it speaks of distorting words. Here the Quran serves as Furqan: it confirms the truth remaining in earlier Books and provides a criterion for distinguishing it from distortion and fabricated statements. In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Quran is likewise described as confirming and “مُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ”—a guardian over it. The next verse of this surah further illuminates this subject by naming the Furqan.

The name “Injil” is another indication of the Quran’s connection with Books known at the time of revelation. The word ultimately comes from Greek and means “good news” or “glad tidings.” Jesus spoke primarily Aramaic, but the oldest surviving manuscripts of the known Gospels are in Greek. The Quran likewise uses the name familiar in Christian tradition, not an unknown name for an original, vanished copy. Alongside the verses mentioned above, this point strengthens our reading that the Quran confirms Books present and accessible to the People of the Book.

The Quran’s connection with the Torah and Gospel also recalls the common source of the Messengers’ missions. This connects directly with the principle of believing in all Messengers without discrimination and the principle of the unity of religion and diversity of prescribed ways.
