Al-Imran 3:55

ۖ

When The God said: O Jesus, indeed, I will take you in full, raise you to Myself, purify you from those who disbelieved, and place those who followed you above those who disbelieved until the Day of Resurrection. Then your return is to Me, and I will judge between you concerning that over which you differed.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, we read that Jesus’ opponents devised plans, and God devised plans, and God is the best of planners. The present verse directly continues that statement and describes part of God’s planning in response to Jesus’ opponents.

One characteristic of makr is that aspects of the planning remain hidden from the other party’s awareness. God has made the failure of Jesus’ opponents’ plans known to us, but has not placed every aspect of His own planning within our knowledge.

The meaning of the verse

In this verse, God makes four promises to Jesus:

«إِنّى مُتَوَفّيكَ»

Indeed, I will take you in full.

Tawaffi, from the root “و ف ي,” is connected with taking or rendering something in its entirety. The Qur’an uses this word both of people at death and during sleep. We have examined the distinction between tawaffi and death in detail in Life and Death in the Qur’an.

«وَرافِعُكَ إِلَىَّ»

And raise you to Myself.

Raf‘ means raising, and in the Qur’an it is used both for physical elevation and for elevating status or rank. Al-Baqarah 2:63 speaks of raising Mount Tur; Yusuf 12:100 of raising Joseph’s parents onto the throne; and Al-Mujadilah 58:11 of raising the ranks of believers and those given knowledge.

In the present verse, Jesus himself is the object of the raising, and its direction is also specified: toward God.

«وَمُطَهِّرُكَ مِنَ الَّذينَ كَفَروا»

And purify you from those who disbelieved.

This promise connects with the opponents’ plans in the preceding verse. God delivered Jesus from their harm; Al-Maidah 5:110 likewise speaks of restraining the Children of Israel from harming him.

Jesus’ purification can also be considered from another perspective. His opponents were not only seeking to harm his body. They had also slandered his mother, as God recalls their grave slander against Mary in An-Nisa 4:156.

By describing Mary’s chastity, Jesus’ extraordinary creation, affirming his mission, and rejecting the opponents’ claim to have killed and crucified him, the Qur’an exonerates Jesus and his mother from these accusations. Removing the opponents’ accusations against Jesus may therefore also be a manifestation of his purification.

«وَجاعِلُ الَّذينَ اتَّبَعوكَ فَوقَ الَّذينَ كَفَروا إِلىٰ يَومِ القِيٰمَةِ»

And place those who followed you above those who disbelieved until the Day of Resurrection.

The opponents’ plans not only failed to eliminate Jesus; they also failed to end his call. God has made a promise concerning Jesus’ followers that extends from his time until the Day of Resurrection.

In the rest of this reflection, we will first consider disagreements about Jesus’ fate, and then the meaning of following him and the superiority promised to his followers.

Disagreement about Jesus’ fate and his return

Qur’an researchers disagree about Jesus’ fate and his return.

One group believes that God raised Jesus to Himself while he was alive, that he remains alive, and that he will return to earth before the Resurrection.

Another group believes that Jesus has died and will not return to earth. Even within this group, there is disagreement about the nature of Jesus’ tawaffi and raising. Some understand his tawaffi as the end of his earthly life and his raising as the elevation of his rank with God. Others believe that, after being rescued from his opponents’ plans, Jesus continued living on earth and eventually died a natural death. The possibility that Jesus was raised alive and died after being raised has also been proposed.

To understand the grounds of this disagreement, we will examine the evidence advanced by those who affirm Jesus’ return alongside that of those who reject it.

1. The meaning of tawaffi and the sequence of taking and raising

One of the most important disputed questions is the meaning of Jesus’ tawaffi.

God says:

اللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى الْأَنْفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا وَالَّتِي لَمْ تَمُتْ فِي مَنَامِهَا ۖ فَيُمْسِكُ الَّتِي قَضَىٰ عَلَيْهَا الْمَوْتَ وَيُرْسِلُ الْأُخْرَىٰ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

Az-Zumar 39:42

The God takes the selves at their death, and those that have not died during their sleep; then He retains the one for which He has decreed death and sends the other back until an appointed time. Indeed, in this are signs for people who reflect.

Those who affirm the return cite this verse to argue that Jesus’ tawaffi does not necessarily mean his death, since God explicitly speaks of taking those who have not died. Al-Anam 6:60 also speaks of taking people at night.

Those who reject the return do not deny this distinction between tawaffi and death, but attend to the context in which the word is used. In many verses, such as An-Nahl 16:70 and As-Sajdah 32:11, a person’s tawaffi is connected with the end of earthly life. Where taking during sleep is intended, the verse includes a reference to night or sleep, whereas the present verse contains no such indication.

Another issue is the order in which taking and raising are mentioned. Those who reject the return argue that God first speaks of taking Jesus and then of raising him, and therefore understand the taking to precede the raising.

In response, those who affirm the return note that the Arabic conjunction “و” does not necessarily indicate chronological order. Mentioning the taking before the raising does not, by itself, establish that Jesus’ death occurred first.

Of course, the fact that “و” does not necessarily indicate chronological order also does not prove that the raising preceded the taking. Thus, disagreement remains about the nature of the taking and the order in which these two promises were fulfilled.

2. The nature of Jesus’ raising to God

Those who affirm the return attend to the context of the verses in Surah An-Nisa. After rejecting the opponents’ claim to have killed and crucified Jesus, God says:

بَلْ رَفَعَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَيْهِ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًا

An-Nisa 4:158

Rather, The God raised him to Himself; and The God is Invincible, Wise.

The particle “بَلْ” establishes a contrast between what Jesus’ opponents claimed and what God did. Those who affirm the return argue that, in contrast to the claim that Jesus was killed, God reports a different event, and that Jesus’ raising should not be reduced merely to making his name and memory renowned.

God also did not say that He raised Jesus’ name or ranks; He made Jesus himself the object of the raising.

Those who reject the return respond by citing other Qur’anic uses of raising. For example, God says of Idris:

وَرَفَعْنَاهُ مَكَانًا عَلِيًّا

Maryam 19:57

And We raised him to a high station.

The Qur’an also speaks of raising Idris, yet says nothing about his return. Al-Mujadilah 58:11 speaks of raising the ranks of believers and those given knowledge, and Fatir 35:10 of good speech ascending to God.

Furthermore, movement toward God cannot necessarily be understood as bodily movement through physical space. At the end of the present verse, God also speaks of all those addressed returning to Him.

Thus, while those who affirm the return understand the context of Jesus’ raising as evidence of his rescue and raising while alive, those who reject it hold that the expression “raising to God” does not specify the event’s bodily nature or Jesus’ continued life.

3. Jesus’ words on the Day of Judgment

One of the principal pieces of evidence cited by those who reject the return is Jesus’ own statement on the Day of Judgment:

مَا قُلْتُ لَهُمْ إِلَّا مَا أَمَرْتَنِي بِهِ أَنِ اعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ رَبِّي وَرَبَّكُمْ ۚ وَكُنْتُ عَلَيْهِمْ شَهِيدًا مَا دُمْتُ فِيهِمْ ۖ فَلَمَّا تَوَفَّيْتَنِي كُنْتَ أَنْتَ الرَّقِيبَ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَأَنْتَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدٌ

Al-Maidah 5:117

I told them nothing except what You commanded me: worship The God, my Lord and your Lord. And I was a witness over them as long as I remained among them; then, when You took me, You Yourself were the Watcher over them, and You are a witness over all things.

In this verse, Jesus refers to his tawaffi as the end of his presence among the people and his direct oversight of them.

Those who reject the return ask: if Jesus returns to earth and encounters the false beliefs that have developed about him, why does this conversation, whose subject is precisely those beliefs, make no mention of his renewed presence among people and his confrontation with those beliefs?

Those who affirm the return answer that this conversation takes place on the Day of Judgment, and that the taking Jesus mentions could be his final taking after his return to earth. The verse itself does not specify when Jesus’ taking occurs.

4. The Messengers who passed before Muhammad

وَمَا مُحَمَّدٌ إِلَّا رَسُولٌ قَدْ خَلَتْ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ الرُّسُلُ ۚ أَفَإِنْ مَاتَ أَوْ قُتِلَ انْقَلَبْتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ ۚ وَمَنْ يَنْقَلِبْ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ فَلَنْ يَضُرَّ اللَّهَ شَيْئًا ۗ وَسَيَجْزِي اللَّهُ الشَّاكِرِينَ

Aal Imran 3:144

Muhammad is only a Messenger; Messengers have passed before him. If he dies or is killed, will you turn back on your heels? Whoever turns back on his heels will not harm The God at all; and The God will reward the grateful.

Those who reject the return argue that Jesus was one of the Messengers before Muhammad, and that there is no basis for excluding him from this verse’s general statement. Al-Maidah 5:75 also uses a similar expression about the Messengers before Jesus.

Those who affirm the return hold that the passing of earlier Messengers may refer to the end of their presence among people without specifying how each one’s life ended. They consider the verses concerning Jesus’ taking and raising to be evidence of an exceptional divine arrangement for him.

5. General laws of life and death and exceptional Qur’anic events

God says of John:

وَسَلَامٌ عَلَيْهِ يَوْمَ وُلِدَ وَيَوْمَ يَمُوتُ وَيَوْمَ يُبْعَثُ حَيًّا

Maryam 19:15

And peace be upon him the day he was born, the day he dies, and the day he is raised alive.

In the same surah, Jesus says of himself:

وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَيَّ يَوْمَ وُلِدْتُ وَيَوْمَ أَمُوتُ وَيَوْمَ أُبْعَثُ حَيًّا

Maryam 19:33

And peace be upon me the day I was born, the day I die, and the day I am raised alive.

Those who reject the return note the similarity in the Qur’an’s descriptions of these two Prophets. Both are born, die, and are raised. They ask why we should assume a different course of life for Jesus.

Aal Imran 3:185 also describes every self as tasting death. Al-Anbiya 21:8 speaks of the Messengers’ need for food and their lack of immortality, while Al-Anbiya 21:34 denies human immortality.

In Al-Maidah 5:75, Jesus and his mother’s eating food is evidence of their humanity. Jesus himself also states in Maryam 19:31 that he has been enjoined to perform salat and give zakat as long as he lives.

If Jesus is still alive in his human body, how should this prolonged life be understood in light of human bodily needs and the general laws of life and death?

Al-Muminun 23:99 and Al-Muminun 23:100, which speak of a person’s request to return when confronting death and of a barrier until the day of resurrection, are also cited by those who reject the return.

Those who affirm the return distinguish, in response, between immortality and continued life until a specified time. Jesus, too, will eventually die and be raised. The verses concerning Jesus and John do not specify the time of their deaths. Furthermore, if Jesus has not yet experienced death, the verses about the barrier after death do not rule out his return.

They also cite other exceptional events in the Qur’an. Al-Baqarah 2:56 speaks of raising people after death, Al-Baqarah 2:243 of causing a group of people to die and then bringing them to life, and Al-Baqarah 2:259 of causing a person to die for a hundred years and then raising him.

Al-Kahf 18:25 also reports the Companions of the Cave remaining for a long period. According to Aal Imran 3:49 and Al-Maidah 5:110, Jesus himself brought the dead to life by divine permission.

The God who brought about these events is also capable of preserving Jesus’ life until a time He has appointed.

However, the possibility of an event differs from proof that it occurred. Just as ordinary human experience is insufficient to deny God’s power, appealing to God’s power alone does not prove that a particular event took place.

6. The People of the Book believing before death

وَإِنْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ إِلَّا لَيُؤْمِنَنَّ بِهِ قَبْلَ مَوْتِهِ ۖ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يَكُونُ عَلَيْهِمْ شَهِيدًا

An-Nisa 4:159

There is none of the People of the Book but will believe in him before his death; and on the Day of Resurrection he will be a witness over them.

Disagreement about this verse largely concerns the referent of the pronoun in “مَوْتِهِ.”

Those who affirm the return believe that the pronoun refers to Jesus. Thus, before Jesus’ death, the People of the Book will believe in him. Since universal belief in Jesus among the People of the Book has not yet occurred, they consider this verse evidence for his continued life and future return.

Those who reject the return understand the pronoun concerning death as referring to each individual among the People of the Book. On this reading, the truth about Jesus will become clear to each of them before their death. Such belief is not necessarily beneficial to the person, as Ghafir 40:85 speaks of faith that did not benefit its holders after they had seen divine punishment.

Questions arise for both readings. If Jesus’ death is meant, does the verse include all the People of the Book throughout history, or only those alive at his return? If each individual’s death is meant, how will such belief come about?

7. Jesus’ connection with the Hour

وَإِنَّهُ لَعِلْمٌ لِلسَّاعَةِ فَلَا تَمْتَرُنَّ بِهَا وَاتَّبِعُونِ ۚ هَٰذَا صِرَاطٌ مُسْتَقِيمٌ

Az-Zukhruf 43:61

And indeed, he is knowledge of the Hour; so do not doubt it, and follow me. This is a straight path.

Those who affirm the return, considering the preceding verses’ discussion of Jesus, understand the pronoun in “إِنَّهُ” to refer to him. They consider Jesus’ reappearance a sign of the Hour’s approach and an instance of his connection with the Resurrection.

Reports of variant readings of this verse also transmit “عِلْم” as “عَلَم,” meaning a sign, which those who affirm the return regard as support for their interpretation.

Those who reject the return argue that, even if the pronoun refers to Jesus, the nature of his connection with the Hour remains open to discussion. Jesus’ extraordinary creation and his ability to bring the dead to life by God’s permission are themselves evidence of God’s power to raise people. Jesus may therefore be a means of understanding the reality of the Hour without the verse referring to his reappearance.

Another reading has also been proposed, according to which the pronoun refers to the Qur’an, since it informs people of the coming of the Hour and the events associated with it.

8. Jesus speaking in the cradle and in maturity

وَيُكَلِّمُ النَّاسَ فِي الْمَهْدِ وَكَهْلًا وَمِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

Aal Imran 3:46

And he will speak to people in the cradle and in maturity, and will be among the righteous.

In Al-Maidah 5:110, God also recalls Jesus’ speaking in the cradle and in maturity among the special blessings bestowed upon him.

Those who affirm the return ask: a child speaking in the cradle is exceptional, but an adult speaking is ordinary. Why, then, does God mention these two together?

According to widely known historical estimates, Jesus was about thirty-three at the time of the crucifixion episode. Those who place the beginning of maturity after this age believe that Jesus had not yet reached that stage of life and that the promise of his speaking at that stage will be fulfilled after his return to earth.

However, the Arabic meaning of “كَهْل” is not identical to what modern Persian conveys by kuhulat and old age. The word refers to the stage of maturity and middle age between youth and old age.

Lisan al-Arab records different age ranges for this word: over thirty to forty, thirty-three to fifty, and thirty-four to fifty-one.

Thus, age thirty-three may also fall within the semantic range of “كَهْل.” Moreover, the Qur’an does not specify Jesus’ age at the time of the crucifixion episode.

Considering this lexical evidence, those who reject the return argue that Jesus’ speaking in maturity could have occurred during his first earthly life. The significance of this speaking may also lie in its content, his teaching of the Book and wisdom, and his delivery of the message, rather than merely his ability to speak.

Another group, who believe Jesus continued his earthly life after the crucifixion episode, consider this same verse evidence that he lived to reach maturity and middle age, without believing in his return at the end of time.

9. The finality of prophethood and Jesus’ return

Some who reject the return cite Al-Ahzab 33:40 and Muhammad’s being the Seal of the Prophets, and consider the return of a Prophet preceding Muhammad incompatible with the finality of prophethood.

Those who affirm the return answer that Jesus had already received prophethood before Muhammad, and that his return would not mean receiving new prophethood or the revelation of another book or prescribed way.

The disagreement here therefore concerns how the finality of prophethood is understood and its relation to the possible return of a Prophet from the past.

10. Jesus’ return in Christianity and the absence of an explicit Qur’anic rejection

Another argument offered by those who affirm Jesus’ return is the presence of this teaching in Christian texts predating the revelation of the Qur’an.

Matthew 24:30, Mark 13:26, and Luke 21:27 speak of the Son of Man coming with power and glory. In John 14:3, Jesus also promises his disciples that he will come again.

In Acts 1:11, after the account of Jesus being taken up to heaven, his disciples are told that he will return in the same manner in which he ascended.

Hebrews 9:28 speaks of the Messiah appearing a second time, and 1 Thessalonians 4:16 of his descending from heaven.

Belief in Jesus’ return was therefore a longstanding Christian belief, widespread among Christians before the revelation of the Qur’an.

Those who affirm the return draw attention to the fact that the Qur’an explicitly rejects certain prevalent beliefs about Jesus. An-Nisa 4:157 rejects the claim that Jesus was killed and crucified; An-Nisa 4:171 rejects making divinity threefold and attributing a child to God; Al-Maidah 5:72 rejects treating the Messiah as God; and Al-Maidah 5:73 rejects describing God as the third of three.

Why, then, despite addressing Christian beliefs about Jesus and describing his fate, does the Qur’an not explicitly reject belief in his return?

Noting that the Qur’an describes itself in Aal Imran 3:3 as confirming the earlier scriptures, they consider the absence of such a rejection, together with the verses on taking, raising, the People of the Book’s belief, and Jesus’ connection with the Hour, as evidence strengthening the possibility of his return.

Those who reject the return respond that the absence of an explicit rejection does not necessarily mean affirmation. The Qur’an has not listed every belief among the People of the Book individually or pronounced judgment on each one.

Furthermore, the Christian teaching of Jesus’ return is connected with beliefs such as his death on the cross, his resurrection, and his role in the final judgment. This entire framework cannot be accepted without assessing it against the Qur’an. Conversely, rejecting part of an account does not prove every element of it false.

As we discussed in Mu’min or Muslim?, the Qur’an engages with the People of the Book and the scriptures in their possession. Confirmation of earlier scriptures should be understood neither as unconditional acceptance of everything in them nor as total denial of the divine guidance they contain. In Al-Maidah 5:48, besides confirming the earlier scriptures, the Qur’an describes itself as muhaymin over them.

Questions that remain

After considering the evidence of those who affirm and those who reject Jesus’ return, questions remain about the wisdom of God’s special arrangement for Jesus.

In several verses, including Aal Imran 3:21 and Aal Imran 3:183, the Qur’an reports that Prophets and Messengers were killed by their opponents. Receiving prophethood and a mission has not always meant protection from being killed by oppressors.

Why, then, did God thwart the opponents’ plans in a special way in Jesus’ case and rescue him from being killed and crucified? Why, in response to the claim that Jesus was killed, did He not merely state that He rescued him, but specifically speak of raising him to Himself?

If Jesus was to continue an ordinary life elsewhere after being rescued from his opponents’ plans and eventually die a natural death in obscurity, what was the particular wisdom of this rescue?

If Jesus’ rescue was intended to allow him to continue his mission or carry out another task, why does the Qur’an give no account of his life, residence, and activities after the crucifixion episode?

Is there a connection between this special divine arrangement and Jesus’ other exceptional characteristics, from the manner of his creation to his support through Spirit of The Holy and the signs bestowed upon him?

On the other hand, if Jesus will return to earth for a future mission, why, among the many verses concerning the Hour and Resurrection, has God not described this mission and his return as clearly as He described his first mission?

Of course, saving a person from unjust killing is not pointless even if that person later dies naturally. Yet the combination of expressions the Qur’an uses about Jesus’ fate invites us to reflect on the particular wisdom of this event.

Do we possess definitive answers to these questions?

Our view

Before judging between these positions, it is better to return to the preceding verse, in which God speaks of His planning.

God could have stated clearly when Jesus died, whether he was raised with his body, where he is now, and whether he will return to earth. Had He answered all these questions, the unknown dimensions of this arrangement would also have become clear to us.

Yet, in describing Jesus’ fate, the Qur’an draws our attention to the distinction between knowledge and conjecture:

وَقَوْلِهِمْ إِنَّا قَتَلْنَا الْمَسِيحَ عِيسَى ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَٰكِنْ شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ ۚ وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ لَفِي شَكٍّ مِنْهُ ۚ مَا لَهُمْ بِهِ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِلَّا اتِّبَاعَ الظَّنِّ ۚ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ يَقِينًا

An-Nisa 4:157

And because of their saying, “We killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the Messenger of The God,” whereas they neither killed him nor crucified him, but the matter was made unclear to them. Indeed, those who disagreed about him are in doubt; they have no knowledge of it, only the following of conjecture; and certainly they did not kill him.

Given such a warning against following conjecture, is it appropriate for us, too, to place our conjecture in the position of knowledge concerning the undisclosed dimensions of this very event?

It is striking that Surah Aal Imran begins by teaching us how to approach clear foundational and ambiguous verses, and then repeatedly confronts the Qur’an’s researcher and translator with questions whose definitive answers are not available to them. From the disconnected letters at the beginning of the surah to the question of Mary’s prophethood discussed in our reflection on Aal Imran 3:45, and now Jesus’ fate, opportunities arise to put that same lesson into practice.

In the reflection on Aal Imran 3:7, we explained that those firmly grounded in knowledge are not those who claim to know all the Qur’an’s mysteries. They believe in both clear and ambiguous verses and leave knowledge of the ultimate interpretation of the ambiguous verses to God.

In our view, the nature of Jesus’ taking and raising, and the question of his return, should be examined in the same way.

We believe in Jesus’ being taken, his raising to God, and the failure of his opponents’ plans because the Qur’an states these things. Taken together, the evidence we have examined makes Jesus’ return a possibility worth considering, but we possess no proof that would make it a certain truth or an undeniable proposition. Likewise, the evidence of those who reject the return does not suffice to rule it out definitively.

Jesus’ opponents imagined that their plans had determined his fate and that of his mission, but God had His own plan. Must we know every secret of God’s planning in order to believe in it?

We should neither narrow the meanings of tawaffi and raising to prove Jesus’ death nor fill the unspoken dimensions of this event with reports and our own conjectures to prove his bodily return. We have examined this methodological issue in detail in When Qur’anic Silence Is Filled with Reports.

Accepting the limits of our knowledge is one aspect of faith in the unseen and of truly submitting to God. We may research, reflect, and evaluate different positions, but we have no right to turn the tentative conclusion of our research into a binding belief for others.

Neither belief in Jesus’ return nor rejection of it can be made a condition of people’s faith and submission without clear Qur’anic proof.

Who are Jesus’ followers?

Discussion of Jesus’ taking, raising, and return must not distract us from another promise God makes in this same verse:

«وَجاعِلُ الَّذينَ اتَّبَعوكَ فَوقَ الَّذينَ كَفَروا إِلىٰ يَومِ القِيٰمَةِ»

Christians consider themselves followers of Jesus, and Muslims, too, claim to follow him through belief in him and affirmation of his mission. Within both communities there are those who regard themselves as superior to the other. But does God’s promise in this verse authorize such a claim?

The two groups God sets against each other are not Christians and Muslims, but those who followed Jesus and those who disbelieved.

As we examined in the reflection on Aal Imran 3:53, following the Messenger is connected with faith in the message God entrusted to him. How can those who make Jesus their deity or lord regard this as following Jesus, when Jesus himself called people to serve the One Lord in Aal Imran 3:51?

In our study of kufr, we also explained that disbelief should not be reduced to belonging to another religion. Just as a person may call themselves Muslim while resisting a truth that has become clear to them, association with Jesus does not prove adherence to his message.

In the reflection on Aal Imran 3:52, we read that Jesus’ disciples called themselves muslim. In Qur’anic language, being muslim means submitting to God, not membership in a particular religious community. The designation muslim therefore cannot be taken away from Jesus’ disciples and assigned exclusively to Muhammad’s followers, just as following Jesus is not restricted to those who call themselves Christian.

The superiority of Jesus’ followers and its historical manifestation

To understand the superiority of Jesus’ followers, we must again consider the verse’s context. Jesus’ opponents planned to eliminate him, but God not only thwarted their plans; He also speaks of supporting Jesus’ followers:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُونُوا أَنْصَارَ اللَّهِ كَمَا قَالَ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ لِلْحَوَارِيِّينَ مَنْ أَنْصَارِي إِلَى اللَّهِ ۖ قَالَ الْحَوَارِيُّونَ نَحْنُ أَنْصَارُ اللَّهِ ۖ فَآمَنَتْ طَائِفَةٌ مِنْ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ وَكَفَرَتْ طَائِفَةٌ ۖ فَأَيَّدْنَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا عَلَىٰ عَدُوِّهِمْ فَأَصْبَحُوا ظَاهِرِينَ

As-Saff 61:14

O you who believe, be helpers of The God, just as Jesus son of Mary said to the disciples, “Who are my helpers toward The God?” The disciples said, “We are helpers of The God.” Then a group of the Children of Israel believed and a group disbelieved; so We supported those who believed against their enemy, and they prevailed.

In this verse, God speaks of the actual fulfillment of His support for the believers. Those who denied Jesus could not end his call. His mission extended beyond the time and place of his own life and reached later generations throughout history.

Centuries after Jesus, the Qur’an also affirmed his mission, rejected his opponents’ slander, and reminded people anew of his teachings about God’s oneness.

God’s promise concerning the superiority of Jesus’ followers should therefore not be restricted to their fate in the hereafter. The continuation of Jesus’ call through history, the failure of his opponents’ plans, and the believers’ prevailing over their enemies are worldly manifestations of God’s support for his followers.

But can Christianity’s historical expansion, an increase in the number of churches, the rise of Christian or Muslim governments, and their military, economic, and civilizational advances be taken as a definitive measure of the superiority of Jesus’ followers?

In Aal Imran 3:140, God speaks of days alternating among people. Victory and defeat, strength and weakness, and the rise and decline of societies throughout history do not belong exclusively to one religious community.

If political and military power were the measure of superiority before God, we would have to consider Pharaoh superior to Moses at the height of his power, and the ruler who disputed with Abraham about his Lord superior to Abraham.

Al-Hujurat 49:13 identifies taqwa as the measure of human honor before God. Nor can the extent of people’s taqwa, honesty, and sincerity be determined from their religious label.

God addresses religious claims clearly in another verse:

لَيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ وَلَا أَمَانِيِّ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ ۗ مَنْ يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا يُجْزَ بِهِ وَلَا يَجِدْ لَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

An-Nisa 4:123

It is not according to your wishes, nor the wishes of the People of the Book. Whoever does evil will be recompensed for it and will find no protector or helper for themselves apart from The God.

The Qur’an does not set two different standards for the People of the Book and its own audience. Neither can claim exemption from responsibility for their deeds on the basis of a religious name.

If someone considers themselves a follower of Muhammad but lies, oppresses people, violates the rights of those in need, and breaks their covenant, while a follower of Jesus surpasses them in honesty, justice, kindness, and faithfulness to promises, on what Qur’anic principle could the first person claim superiority over the other?

In Aal Imran 3:113, God explicitly says that the People of the Book are not all alike. Aal Imran 3:114 speaks of those among them who believe in God and the Last Day, enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and hasten to good deeds. Aal Imran 3:115 promises that no good they do will be disregarded.

These verses occur in the very surah that contains the promise concerning the superiority of Jesus’ followers. We must therefore not interpret the present promise in a way that makes people’s goodness and wrongdoing dependent upon their religious label.

Diverse prescribed ways and the field of competition among their followers

One root of religious claims to superiority is confusing din with shariah. As we discussed in Din, Islam, and Shariah, the unity of religion’s foundation does not mean that all the rulings, rituals, and prescribed ways God established for different communities are identical.

God says:

وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَمُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ ۖ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ بِمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ عَمَّا جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْحَقِّ ۚ لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا ۚ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ فِي مَا آتَاكُمْ ۖ فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ ۚ إِلَى اللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ جَمِيعًا فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

Al-Maidah 5:48

And We sent down to you the Book in truth, confirming the scripture before it and as muhaymin over it. So judge among them by what The God has sent down, and do not follow their desires by turning away from the truth that has come to you. For each of you We appointed a prescribed way and a path. Had The God willed, He would have made you one community, but He tests you in what He has given you; so compete in good deeds. To The God you will all return, and He will inform you concerning that over which you differed.

In this verse, differences in the prescribed ways and paths God established for people are presented as part of divine will and trial. God could have made everyone one community, but did not. Each community is responsible for what God has given it.

Legitimate differences among divinely prescribed ways must be distinguished from human deviations from God’s teachings. Accepting diversity in prescribed ways does not mean affirming every belief and ruling people have attributed to God and His Messengers throughout history.

But after describing this diversity, what kind of competition does God invite people to pursue? Does He ask them to struggle against one another to prove their own religious community’s superiority, or to compel others to accept their prescribed way?

No; God has defined the field of competition: striving to surpass one another in good deeds.

We examined this same principle in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:148, in the context of differing directions of worship. Differences in the direction of worship should not distract people from worship’s purpose and their moral responsibility. In Al-Baqarah 2:177, God likewise does not confine righteousness to facing east or west, but speaks of faith, giving, fulfilling covenants, and steadfastness in hardship.

If Muslims, Christians, Jews, and other communities wish to surpass one another in something, why not compete in reducing poverty, helping the sick, expanding knowledge, respecting human rights, establishing justice, and supporting the oppressed?

How good it would be if a Muslim, on seeing a Christian’s kindness, sought to do more good themselves rather than looking for a reason to devalue that person’s action, and if a Christian adopted the same approach toward a Muslim’s kindness.

The Qur’an does not ask us to ignore our differences in belief or abandon inquiry into truth and falsehood. But disagreement in belief does not authorize injustice, disregard for others’ goodness, or contempt for them. In Al-Maidah 5:8, even hostility toward a group does not justify departing from justice.

God’s final judgment

In the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:113, we previously encountered an example of religious disputes:

وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ لَيْسَتِ النَّصَارَىٰ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ وَقَالَتِ النَّصَارَىٰ لَيْسَتِ الْيَهُودُ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ وَهُمْ يَتْلُونَ الْكِتَابَ ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ قَالَ الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ مِثْلَ قَوْلِهِمْ ۚ فَاللَّهُ يَحْكُمُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

Al-Baqarah 2:113

The Jews said, “The Christians stand on nothing,” and the Christians said, “The Jews stand on nothing,” while they recite the Book. Those who do not know also said something similar. The God will therefore judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they differed.

If we Muslims, merely because we associate ourselves with Muhammad, also regard every other community as having no religious worth and claim superiority and salvation exclusively for ourselves, have we not repeated the very conduct the Qur’an criticizes in this verse?

In Al-Baqarah 2:111, the Qur’an challenges claims of exclusive possession of Paradise. In Al-Baqarah 2:112, instead of introducing another religious community as its exclusive owner, it makes submission to God and doing good the criterion. We have examined this in detail in Paradise Is Not the Monopoly of One Sect or One Prescribed Way.

Now consider the similarity between the endings of three verses. In Al-Baqarah 2:113, judgment concerning religious differences is left to God. In Al-Maidah 5:48, after describing diverse prescribed ways and inviting people to compete in good deeds, everyone’s return and the disclosure of the truth about their differences are attributed to God.

The present verse likewise ends with the same message:

«ثُمَّ إِلَىَّ مَرجِعُكُم فَأَحكُمُ بَينَكُم فيما كُنتُم فيهِ تَختَلِفونَ»

Then your return is to Me, and I will judge between you concerning that over which you differed.

What an instructive ending for a verse that has itself been the subject of many disagreements throughout history.

Concerning Jesus’ fate, we must not substitute conjecture for knowledge; concerning the superiority of Jesus’ followers, we must not place ourselves in God’s position as judge.

Rather than spend our lives disputing Jesus’ return or non-return and trying to prove our own religious community’s superiority, it is better to strive to surpass one another in practicing the teachings that Jesus, Muhammad, and the other Messengers brought from the same One God.

God has specified our task: competing in good deeds; and He has reserved final judgment for Himself.

And God knows best.
