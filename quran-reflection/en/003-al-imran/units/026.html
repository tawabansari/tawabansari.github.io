Al-Imran 3:26

ۖ ۖ

Say: “O The God, Owner of all dominion and authority! You give dominion to whomever You will and take it back from whomever You will; You honor whomever You will and bring low whomever You will. Good is in Your hand; indeed, You have power over all things.”

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, The God spoke of the just reckoning of all human beings. Now He commands Muhammad to attribute true ownership of dominion and the source of power and honor to The God. No human being is the independent and absolute owner of what they possess.

“مَالِكَ الْمُلْكِ” has a broader meaning than owner of kingship or monarchy. Mulk in the Qur’an is connected with ownership, property, discretion, and the sphere of authority; political rule is only one of its instances. If we understand this verse as concerning kings alone, we restrict its message to a small group of people, whereas every human being possesses some measure of ownership and discretion.

A shepherd owns a flock; someone owns a house or land; parents bear responsibility for managing a family; a municipality has authority to administer a city; and a ruler governs a wider territory. A person may be subject to someone else’s command in one situation and hold authority and make decisions in another. The important question is not only how much dominion a person has, but what they do with what is at their disposal. A shepherd can care well for the flock or let it perish; a wealthy person can use their property to build and help others or make it an instrument of oppression; and an officeholder can serve justly or misuse power for personal advantage.

The absolute sovereignty of The God cannot be understood by the standards of earthly rulers’ power, rivalry, and dependence. This is one of the philosophical themes of “Subhan and Tasbih in the Qur’an”.

The phrase “مَن تَشَاءُ” occurs four times in the verse. Divine will is understood in light of the order and established patterns of existence that The God Himself created and governs, rather than as an arbitrary will that sets aside the laws of creation on each occasion. In Fatir 35:43, the Qur’an speaks of the absence of replacement or alteration in God’s established way. God does not break covenants, and Aal Imran 3:9 likewise states that He does not fail His promise. This relationship between divine will, the laws of creation, and God’s enduring patterns is examined in detail in Divine Permission in the Qur’an, and especially in Part Two of the study of shafāʿah: divine permission and the operative laws of existence.

Within this very order, human beings possess will and choice, and what they acquire also depends on their own choices and conduct. Ar-Rad 13:11 states that God does not change a people’s condition until they change what is within themselves. Al-Anfal 8:53 likewise states that God does not change a blessing He has bestowed on a people until they change what is within themselves. Thus, gaining or losing property, power, and position is not separate from human conduct and choices, although not all circumstances and outcomes are under people’s control. God’s will does not negate human choice, and the occurrence of an action within the created order does not mean that God morally approves of it. This subject is explored in Qada and Qadar: Free Will or Determinism?.

In Al-Baqarah 2:247, Talut’s selection for rule is connected with his knowledge and ability. By contrast, Al-Baqarah 2:258 speaks of a ruler to whom God had given dominion, but whose possession of power led him to pride and disputation with Abraham about lordship. Thus, attaining power and property does not establish the rightness of their possessor or God’s approval of that person’s conduct. An event’s occurrence within the created order is not the same as its being morally commendable.

The same care is necessary regarding “وَتُعِزُّ مَن تَشَاءُ وَتُذِلُّ مَن تَشَاءُ”. Honor must not be equated solely with wealth and power, nor humiliation solely with poverty and weakness. In Al-Fajr 89:15 and Al-Fajr 89:16, the Qur’an challenges the assumption of a person who regards abundant provision as a sign of being honored and restricted provision as a sign of being humiliated. Someone may be wealthy and powerful, yet lose their worth and standing through oppression and arrogance, while another person, without wealth or power, possesses dignity through integrity and steadfastness.

“بِيَدِكَ الْخَيْرُ” attributes the source of good to God, and “إِنَّكَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ” recalls His all-encompassing power. However much property, knowledge, and power a human being acquires, they neither created the laws of existence nor can they guarantee the lasting possession of what they hold.

In Aal Imran 3:8, those firmly grounded in knowledge, despite having received guidance, ask their Lord not to let their hearts deviate after being guided. They are not proud of their knowledge and faith, nor do they consider themselves immune to slipping. The same humility is essential for everyone who possesses dominion and discretion. People must recognize that their property and power are neither evidence of inherent superiority nor a guarantee that their position will endure; they are an opportunity for whose use they will be held responsible.
