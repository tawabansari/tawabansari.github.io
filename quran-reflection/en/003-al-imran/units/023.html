Al-Imran 3:23

Have you not looked at those who were given a portion of the Book? They are called to the Book of The God so that it may judge between them; then a group of them turns away, while they are averse.

Our Reflection

In Aal Imran 3:19, The God identified religion as Islam, meaning submission to truth, and spoke of those who differed out of transgression after knowledge had reached them. Aal Imran 3:23 now presents another example of this defiance: people who have received a portion of divine scripture, yet some of whom turn away when called to accept that same Book’s judgment. The problem here is not lacking scripture or being entirely unaware of it, but being unwilling to accept the authority of the Book they claim to follow.

The difference between the verse’s two expressions is meaningful. First it says, “أُوتُوا نَصِيبًا مِنَ الْكِتَابِ”—they were given a portion of the Book. Then it says, “يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَىٰ كِتَابِ اللَّهِ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَهُمْ”—they are called to the Book of The God so that it may judge between them. Nasib means a share or portion. Possessing a portion of the Book, however valuable, does not mean encompassing all its knowledge and rulings. What a person has learned or understood from it must be assessed against the whole of its guidance and criteria. One cannot accept a few passages that agree with one’s beliefs and interests and then make that limited understanding the measure for judging the rest of the Book. The Book of The God must judge human interpretations; people must not allow only those parts they have already accepted to exercise judgment.

This is the danger the Qur’an exposes in Al-Baqarah 2:85 by criticizing belief in one part of the Book and disbelief in another. An-Nur 24:48, An-Nur 24:49, An-Nur 24:50, and An-Nur 24:51 describe conduct closely resembling Aal Imran 3:23: a group turns away when called to divine judgment, but if the right is in their favor, they come obediently. In contrast to this selective approach, believers are described as accepting and obeying judgment based on truth. The issue, then, is not merely knowing a few rulings or respecting the name of the Book. It is whether a person is willing to accept the same criterion they profess to believe in when disputes arise, even against their own interests.

The Qur’an says, “ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّىٰ فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ وَهُمْ مُعْرِضُونَ.” After being invited to the Book’s judgment, a group turns its back and remains averse. This contradicts submission to truth: instead of assessing their beliefs and disputes by the Book, they withdraw from accepting its judgment. At the same time, the qualification “فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ” must be preserved. The Qur’an criticizes the conduct of a group among them, not everyone who has received a portion of the Book. Nor does the verse itself name the person issuing the invitation or specify the dispute. Its central concern is how people respond to an authority they claim to follow.

This warning is not confined to recipients of scripture in the past. Today, too, someone may have read the Qur’an for years, cite many verses, and consider themselves a follower of the Book of The God. Yet as soon as a verse challenges an inherited interpretation, a religious leader’s ruling, or a personal interest, they may seek a way to set aside its judgment instead of reconsidering their belief. In such a situation, the person has no longer submitted to the Book; they want the Book to follow their interpretation.

Submission to truth becomes evident when a person is willing to assess their knowledge and beliefs against all the Book’s relevant criteria and correct an error wherever they recognize it. Someone who accepts only the rulings that agree with their desires has benefited from the Book, but has not yet properly accepted its authority to judge.

In the next verse, the Qur’an shows what belief can give a person such confidence that, despite receiving a portion of the Book, they turn away from its judgment.
