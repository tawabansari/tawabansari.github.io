Al-Imran 3:45

When the angels said: O Mary, indeed The God gives you glad tidings of a word from Him whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, honored in this world and the Hereafter and among those brought near.

Our Reflection

In Aal Imran 3:39, we read that John was described, before his birth, as confirming a word from The God. That word is now identified by name: the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary.

Before Jesus is born, The God informs Mary of her child’s name and his standing in this world and the Hereafter. The Quran does not merely speak of Jesus being created through a word from The God; it describes Jesus himself as a word from Him. In An-Nisa 4:171, Jesus is also called The God’s Messenger and His word, which He conveyed to Mary.

In Arabic, there are two theories concerning the derivation of “Messiah”. Some derive it from m s h, connecting it with Jesus being anointed with blessing or touching the sick and healing them by The God’s permission. Others derive it from s y h, which means journeying and traveling through the land. According to the second theory, the letter m is not part of the root.

From the perspective of historical linguistics, Messiah is related to Hebrew mashiah and Aramaic and Syriac meshiha. The title meant “anointed”, and in the Israelite tradition, anointing with oil signified selection and legitimacy for a special responsibility, such as kingship or priesthood. According to one reading, Jesus received his blessing and legitimacy directly from The God, rather than from priests and religious authorities.

In Jewish tradition, the concept of the Messiah became associated with the expectation of a chosen descendant of David who would gather the scattered Israelites, restore their rule, and establish conditions for peace and justice. Adherents of mainstream Judaism still await such a Messiah; they have neither accepted Jesus as the promised Messiah nor accepted Muhammad’s mission as applying to them.

Christians regard Jesus as that promised Messiah. In Trinitarian Christianity, however, his standing is also associated with belief in his divinity and his being the Son of God. The Quran recognizes the title Messiah for Jesus but denies his divinity, presenting him as The God’s Messenger to the Children of Israel, confirming the Torah and announcing a Messenger after him (An-Nisa 4:171, Al-Maidah 5:72, and As-Saff 61:6).

“وَجيهًا فِى الدُّنيا وَالـٔاخِرَةِ” speaks of Jesus’ honor, esteem, and standing in both worlds. The same description is used of Moses in Al-Ahzab 33:69, where The God clears him of what others attributed to him and speaks of his honorable standing before Him. The standing The God grants a person does not depend on people’s judgments about that person.

Jesus is also described as one of the “muqarrabeen”: those granted a special standing before The God. In Al-Waqiah 56:10 and Al-Waqiah 56:11, the foremost are likewise counted among those brought near, and An-Nisa 4:172 mentions angels brought near. This nearness expresses standing, not spatial distance from The God.

In reflecting on this verse, we focus mainly on clear and ambiguous verses and how we approach them. God willing, we leave a fuller discussion of the Messiah, Jesus’ standing, his creation, and his mission to reflections on other verses.

In the reflection on Aal Imran 3:7, we discussed clear and ambiguous verses; in the reflection on Aal Imran 3:13, we encountered an example of verses that allow different interpretations. Perhaps one wisdom behind ambiguous verses in the Quran is to place people before a test and reveal what lies within them. Faced with such verses, one person sees the cup as half empty and another as half full.

One subject long debated among Quran researchers is Mary’s prophethood. Some regard Mary as a Prophet; others, while accepting her distinguished standing and reception of divine news, do not accept her prophethood. We will examine the evidence and arguments of both groups below.

Arguments against Mary’s prophethood

First: In the Quran, The God explicitly describes figures such as Abraham, Moses, Ishmael, and Idris as Prophets, but does not directly apply the title Prophet to Mary. Opponents hold that, if Mary were a Prophet, The God would have explicitly mentioned her prophethood as He did for other Prophets.

Second: In Al-Maidah 5:75, The God says:

مَا المَسيحُ ابنُ مَريَمَ إِلّا رَسولٌ قَد خَلَت مِن قَبلِهِ الرُّسُلُ وَأُمُّهُ صِدّيقَةٌ ۖ كانا يَأكُلانِ الطَّعامَ ۗ انظُر كَيفَ نُبَيِّنُ لَهُمُ الـٔايٰتِ ثُمَّ انظُر أَنّىٰ يُؤفَكونَ

Al-Maidah 5:75 — The Messiah son of Mary was only a Messenger; Messengers had passed away before him, and his mother was a woman of great truthfulness. They both ate food. See how We make the signs clear to them; then see how they are turned away from the truth.

Opponents of Mary’s prophethood argue that, when describing Jesus and his mother together in this verse, The God calls Jesus a Messenger and Mary a siddiqah. They also cite An-Nisa 4:69, which mentions Prophets, the truthful, witnesses, and the righteous separately. On this basis, they count Mary among the truthful rather than the Prophets.

Third: In Surah Maryam, after recounting the stories of Zechariah, John, Mary, Jesus, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Aaron, Ishmael, and Idris, The God says:

أُولٰئِكَ الَّذينَ أَنعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيهِم مِنَ النَّبِيّينَ مِن ذُرِّيَّةِ ءادَمَ وَمِمَّن حَمَلنا مَعَ نوحٍ وَمِن ذُرِّيَّةِ إِبرٰهيمَ وَإِسرٰءيلَ وَمِمَّن هَدَينا وَاجتَبَينا ۚ إِذا تُتلىٰ عَلَيهِم ءايٰتُ الرَّحمٰنِ خَرّوا سُجَّدًا وَبُكِيًّا

Maryam 19:58 — Those are the Prophets upon whom The God bestowed favor: from Adam’s descendants, from those We carried with Noah, from the descendants of Abraham and Israel, and from those We guided and chose. When the verses of the Entirely Merciful were recited to them, they fell down prostrating and weeping.

Opponents of Mary’s prophethood believe that “أُولٰئِكَ” in this verse refers to the Prophets mentioned in the surah, rather than every figure whose story has been told. They regard the absence of an explicit declaration of Mary’s prophethood in the preceding verses as grounds for excluding her from the Prophets mentioned here.

Fourth: One of the most important verses cited by opponents of women’s prophethood is:

وَما أَرسَلنا قَبلَكَ إِلّا رِجالًا نوحى إِلَيهِم ۖ فَسـَٔلوا أَهلَ الذِّكرِ إِن كُنتُم لا تَعلَمونَ

Al-Anbiya 21:7 — Translation according to the reading of those who oppose women’s prophethood: And before you We sent only men to whom We revealed; so ask the people of remembrance if you do not know.

The same theme appears in An-Nahl 16:43 and Yusuf 12:109. Opponents infer from “رِجالًا” in these verses that The God chose men, rather than women, for messengership and prophethood.

Fifth: Opponents do not consider receiving revelation or speaking with angels sufficient, on its own, to establish prophethood. They cite revelation to Moses’ mother in Al-Qasas 28:7, revelation to Jesus’ disciples in Al-Maidah 5:111, and even the use of revelation concerning the honeybee in An-Nahl 16:68.

They distinguish prophetic revelation from inspired revelation and regard Mary’s reception of revelation as the latter. Some also consider a mission to guide and reform society a condition of prophethood, and believe that no such mission is stated for Mary in the Quran.

Arguments for Mary’s prophethood and responses to opponents

First: Supporters of Mary’s prophethood believe that the absence of the title Prophet beside a person’s name does not negate that person’s prophethood. In this same Surah Maryam, Zechariah and John are not given the title Prophet after being mentioned, although we know of their prophethood from other Quranic verses.

Al-Anam 6:85 names Zechariah and John among the chosen figures, and Al-Anam 6:89 speaks of granting the Book, judgment, and prophethood to the figures mentioned. Thus, failing to state a quality explicitly in recounting someone’s story does not mean that the person lacks that quality.

Second: Calling Mary a siddiqah does not negate her prophethood either, because the Quran itself says of Abraham:

وَاذكُر فِى الكِتٰبِ إِبرٰهيمَ ۚ إِنَّهُ كانَ صِدّيقًا نَبِيًّا

Maryam 19:41 — And remember Abraham in the Book; indeed, he was a man of great truthfulness and a Prophet.

It also says of Idris:

وَاذكُر فِى الكِتٰبِ إِدريسَ ۚ إِنَّهُ كانَ صِدّيقًا نَبِيًّا

Maryam 19:56 — And remember Idris in the Book; indeed, he was a man of great truthfulness and a Prophet.

Being truthful and being a Prophet are therefore not mutually exclusive qualities. The separate mention of the truthful and the Prophets in An-Nisa 4:69 does not mean that one person cannot possess both qualities at once.

Third: Unlike their opponents, supporters of Mary’s prophethood regard Maryam 19:58 as one of its most important pieces of evidence.

In this same surah, The God introduces Mary with “وَاذكُر فِى الكِتٰبِ”, the same expression used to introduce Abraham, Moses, Ishmael, and Idris.

After recounting these figures’ stories, The God refers to them with “أُولٰئِكَ” and speaks of the Prophets upon whom He bestowed favor.

In Al-Fasl fi al-Milal wa al-Ahwa wa al-Nihal, Ibn Hazm of Andalusia cites this verse and does not consider it permissible to exclude Mary from the figures to whom it refers without evidence.

Supporters of Mary’s prophethood ask: On the basis of which expression within this surah can Mary be excluded from those to whom “أُولٰئِكَ” refers?

Fourth: Supporters of women’s prophethood examine their opponents’ reading of Al-Anbiya 21:7, An-Nahl 16:43, and Yusuf 12:109 from several angles.

In the context of Surah Al-Anbiya, the disputed issue is the Messengers’ humanity as opposed to the expectation that they should be angels, rather than whether they are women or men. In Al-Anbiya 21:3, opponents object to Muhammad being human; in Al-Anbiya 21:8, The God explains that earlier Messengers also ate food and were not immortal.

Arabic also has a convention known as masculine predominance over the feminine, whereby a masculine expression can refer to a group containing women and men. Mary herself is counted among “القانتین” in At-Tahrim 66:12, and Al-Araf 7:27 uses “أَبَوَيْكُمْ” of Adam and his wife.

This convention is not limited to pronouns and adjectives. In Lisan al-Arab, under r j l, Ibn Manzur transmits from Ibn al-Arabi an example of “الرجلان” referring to a man and his wife through this convention.

In Al-Mizan, commenting on the verses concerning the people of the Heights, Tabataba’i also discusses the possibility of “رجال” referring to a group that includes women through masculine predominance. In examining this usage, he also refers to An-Nahl 16:43.

In Al-Tahrir wa al-Tanwir, under Al-Araf 7:46, Ibn Ashur explicitly states that mentioning rijal in this verse does not necessarily mean that there are no women among the people of the Heights.

Accordingly, in light of this possible Arabic usage and the context of the verses under discussion, supporters of women’s prophethood translate Al-Anbiya 21:7 as follows:

“And before you We sent only human beings to whom We revealed; so ask the people of remembrance if you do not know.”

They also emphasize the semantic distinction between Prophet and Messenger. Prophet is associated with receiving and communicating divine news, whereas a Messenger is someone sent with a commission. Although Al-Hajj 22:52 speaks of sending both, none of the three verses cited by opponents directly states the title Prophet or the conditions of prophethood.

Fifth: Supporters of Mary’s prophethood point to the similarity between The God’s manner of communicating with Zechariah and with Mary.

In Aal Imran 3:39, the angels tell Zechariah that The God gives him glad tidings of John and inform him of his child’s future standing. In the present verse, the angels use the same structure to give Mary glad tidings of Jesus and inform her of her child’s name and future standing.

“Nabi” is connected with the n b a word family and the concept of news and knowledge. In this verse, Mary is made aware of the unseen.

Supporters of Mary’s prophethood ask: On the basis of which Quranic verse do we distinguish these two similar ways of receiving news from The God, calling one prophetic revelation and the other inspiration?

Ibn Hazm even regards revelation to Moses’ mother as evidence of women’s prophethood. He argues that The God’s command to her to place her child in the water, together with His promise to return him, was not merely an inner feeling. It was a specific command and news of the future upon which she acted with confidence.

Al-Qurtubi also defends Mary’s prophethood in his commentary on Aal Imran 3:42, regarding her reception of revelation from The God through an angel as evidence of it.

In addition to these responses, supporters of Mary’s prophethood cite further evidence:

1. The God chose Mary, purified her, and chose her above the women of all peoples (Aal Imran 3:42).

2. Angels spoke to Mary and conveyed commands from The God to her (Aal Imran 3:43).

3. Angels informed Mary of future events (Aal Imran 3:45).

4. The God sent His Spirit to Mary (Maryam 19:17).

5. That Spirit identified himself as the Messenger of Mary’s Lord (Maryam 19:19).

6. The God breathed of His Spirit into Mary and made her and her son a sign for all peoples (Al-Anbiya 21:91).

7. Mary is presented as an example for believers, women and men alike (At-Tahrim 66:12).

Our view

The importance of discussing Mary’s prophethood lies in challenging a taboo within a patriarchal tradition and a misogynistic society in whose jurisprudence and rulings women are widely barred from being imams, leaders, or judges. In such a society, even raising the possibility of a woman’s prophethood can open the way to reconsidering many beliefs and rulings imposed on women in the name of religion.

Yet the importance of not declaring categorically that Mary was or was not a Prophet lies in our submission to the Quranic text and in not imposing our individual views on The God’s will.

Had The God wished there to be no disagreement about Mary’s prophethood, He could have said in one short sentence that Mary was a Prophet or that she was not, ending all these disagreements. In our view, leaving such questions open in the Quran is purposeful and shows us one wisdom behind its ambiguous verses.

After thought, reflection, and examination of Quranic evidence, do we impose our own view on the Quran? Or do we believe what The God says in Aal Imran 3:7, acknowledge that no one but Him knows its interpretation, and say, like those firmly grounded in knowledge:

“ءامَنّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِن عِندِ رَبِّنا” — We believe in it; all is from our Lord.

Just as we should not deny Mary’s prophethood to preserve patriarchal traditions, neither should we categorically attribute her prophethood to The God in order to challenge those traditions.

Moderation does not mean standing halfway between two opposing claims; it means assessing both by the same standard. Mary’s being a woman is neither, on its own, proof of her prophethood nor grounds for denying it.

Thinking and reflecting on the Quran is our duty; imposing the result of our thinking upon it is not submission to The God.

The God knows best.
