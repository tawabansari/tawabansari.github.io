Al-Imran 3:27

ۖ ۖ

You cause night to enter day and day to enter night; You bring the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living; and You provide for whomever You will without reckoning.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, God was presented as the Owner of dominion, with authority to give and take it back. This verse shows that same power in the transformations of creation: night and day enter into one another, life emerges from death, and death likewise emerges from life. The same connection between the governance of night and day and divine ownership appears in Fatir 35:13.

“تولِجُ الَّيلَ فِى النَّهارِ وَتولِجُ النَّهارَ فِى الَّيلِ” does not merely describe the succession of night and day. Night and day are two different conditions that continually replace one another and, as they shorten and lengthen through the year, advance into one another. Night ends the daylight, but does not end creation or life, just as night recedes when day arrives. Both belong to a single order of creation, and each has its place and benefit.

“وَتُخرِجُ الحَىَّ مِنَ المَيِّتِ وَتُخرِجُ المَيِّتَ مِنَ الحَىِّ” places a deeper transformation before us. God brings forth life where no sign of life is visible, while the living being eventually encounters death. In Ar-Rum 30:19, the same expression is connected with the earth being brought to life after its death. Life and death are not two domains independent of God’s power; both are under His authority.

The Qur’an also uses the imagery of death and life to describe a person’s inner transformation. In Al-Anam 6:122, it speaks of someone who was dead, whom God brought to life and gave a light by which to move among people. This usage also opens a way to reflect on human awakening, understanding, and guidance. For a broader examination of life and death, see Life and Death in the Qur’an.

These transformations also offer lessons for human life. We judge many events only by their present condition, considering one thing good and another evil while remaining unaware of what will emerge later. Sometimes a hardship leads a person to new understanding, ability, or opportunity; sometimes a blessing or power that appears good leads to harm and ruin through a person’s wrong conduct. This is the point raised in Al-Baqarah 2:216: we may dislike something that is good for us, or love something that is bad for us. This does not mean that every suffering necessarily ends in good, or every blessing ultimately becomes harmful. Rather, a person must not mistake their limited knowledge of a situation for knowledge of all its consequences.

The ending of the verse, “وَتَرزُقُ مَن تَشاءُ بِغَيرِ حِسابٍ”, connects all these transformations with God’s provision. Rizq is not limited to food, money, and possessions. Health, knowledge, ability, security, opportunities to work, tranquility, guidance, and the various resources that make human life and growth possible can also be forms of provision. Someone may possess great wealth yet lack health or peace, while another with fewer possessions enjoys blessings whose value cannot be measured in money.

God’s provision is not limited to worldly life. Ghafir 40:40 speaks of those who have believed and done righteous deeds being provided for without reckoning in Paradise. Thus, “بِغَيرِ حِسابٍ” is used both for worldly provision and for the gift of the Hereafter.

“بِغَيرِ حِسابٍ” does not make God’s generosity dependent on limited human calculation. We usually measure resources by existing assets, the amount of work, costs, and an expectation of return; God’s provision does not fit within this narrow framework. He can make resources and blessings available through avenues a person has not foreseen. Divine giving without reckoning does not mean a lack of wisdom or order: in At-Talaq 65:3, provision from an unexpected source is mentioned alongside God’s setting a measure for everything.

Thus, just as night and day, life and death, and the giving and taking of dominion are under God’s authority, His ways of providing are not confined to our limited calculations. A person should not pass definitive judgment on the entire future of a situation, or on the extent of the Lord’s power and generosity, merely from outward appearances.

Cross-references
  • Aal Imran 3:26 — Ownership of dominion, giving and taking authority, and good being in God’s hand.
  • Al-Hajj 22:61 — Night entering day and day entering night, with the same verb yuliju.
  • Fatir 35:13 — The connection between night and day entering one another, the governance of creation, and divine ownership.
  • Al-Anam 6:95 — Splitting grain and seed, and bringing forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living.
  • Ar-Rum 30:19 — Bringing forth the living from the dead and reviving the earth after death.
  • Al-Anam 6:122 — Spiritual death and revival in relation to guidance and light on the human path.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:216 — The limits of human knowledge about the good or harm of what one loves or dislikes.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:212 — God providing without reckoning for whomever He wills.
  • Aal Imran 3:37 — Mary’s provision and the repeated expression of provision without reckoning.
  • Ghafir 40:40 — Provision without reckoning in Paradise for believers who do righteous deeds.
  • At-Talaq 65:3 — Provision from an unexpected source, alongside divine determination of measure.
