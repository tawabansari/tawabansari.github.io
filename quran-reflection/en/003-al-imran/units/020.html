Al-Imran 3:20

ۗ ۚ ۖ ۗ

So if they argue with you, say: I have submitted my face to The God, as have those who follow me. And say to those who were given the Book and to the ummiyyin: Have you submitted? So if they submit, they have indeed found guidance; and if they turn away, only conveying the message is your responsibility; and The God sees His servants.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, we read that religion with The God is Islam. Now, through the three forms “أَسْلَمْتُ,” “ءَأَسْلَمْتُم,” and “أَسْلَمُوا,” The God makes its meaning clearer: submitting to Him, rather than merely accepting the name of a religion. This connection is examined in detail in “Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Qur’an”.

“فَإِن حاجّوكَ فَقُل أَسلَمتُ وَجهِىَ لِلَّهِ وَمَنِ اتَّبَعَنِ”

“So if they argue with you, say: I have submitted my face to The God, as have those who follow me.”

In response to others’ arguments, The God commands Muhammad to state his position clearly: I have submitted my face to The God.

“Wajh” means a person’s face, and in such a usage it also conveys their orientation and purpose. Submitting one’s face to The God means aligning one’s desires, goals, and conduct with His guidance, even when accepting truth conflicts with one’s interests or previous beliefs.

This is the same path as Abraham’s: in Al-Anam 6:79 he turned his face toward the Creator of the heavens and the earth, and in Al-Baqarah 2:131 he declared his submission to the Lord of all worlds. Al-Baqarah 2:112 also presents submission of one’s face to The God, together with doing good, in response to claims of exclusive possession of Paradise. The connection between these verses is examined in “Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Qur’an”.

Muhammad likewise describes himself as submitting to The God and places his followers on that same path. Thus, the purpose of following the Messenger of The God is not submission to his person or to a religious institution, but following the guidance to which he himself has submitted.

“وَقُل لِلَّذينَ أوتُوا الكِتٰبَ وَالأُمِّيّـۧنَ ءَأَسلَمتُم”

“And say to those who were given the Book and to the ummiyyin: Have you submitted?”

Two groups are addressed here: those who were given the Book and the ummiyyin.

The expression “الأُمِّيّـۧنَ” requires careful attention. In this verse, the Qur’an contrasts them with “الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ.” Yet to understand ummi, we must also consider the word’s other uses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:78, we read:

وَمِنهُم أُمِّيّونَ لا يَعلَمونَ الكِتٰبَ إِلّا أَمانِيَّ وَإِن هُم إِلّا يَظُنّونَ

“And among them are ummiyyun who have no knowledge of the Book except fancies; they do nothing but suppose.” — Al-Baqarah 2:78

This verse speaks of ummiyyun among people who possessed scripture, but who themselves lack firm knowledge of it. The Qur’an does not say that they cannot read and write; it says that they do not know the Book and that their understanding is limited to amani and supposition.

This distinction matters. Someone may be able to read and write, and may even have read extensively, yet be unable to distinguish knowledge from their own assumptions. They may speak confidently on a subject they do not know adequately, accept claims they find appealing, and defend familiar beliefs instead of weighing evidence.

Such a person is not necessarily incapable of thought; the problem is that they have put supposition in the place of knowledge. This is the condition Al-Baqarah 2:78 describes concerning a group of ummiyyun.

On the other hand, the use of ummiyyin in Aal Imran 3:75 and Al-Jumua 62:2 shows that this designation can also refer to people contrasted with recipients of earlier scripture. In studying this word, therefore, we must consider both a person’s relationship to the Book and their knowledge of it, rather than treating ummi as simply equivalent to illiteracy in every verse.

These distinctions and Qur’anic uses will be examined in the separate study “Ummi and Ummiyyin in the Qur’an: Their Relationship to the Book, Knowledge, and Amani.”

Now the Messenger’s question to both groups is the same:

“ءَأَسلَمتُم” — Have you submitted?

Possessing the Book and truly knowing it are not the same, just as knowing truth and submitting to it are not identical. In Aal Imran 3:19, we read of people who differed out of transgression after knowledge had reached them. In Al-Baqarah 2:78, we also encountered people relying on amani and supposition instead of knowledge of the Book.

In both situations, the Qur’an’s invitation is to submit: to make truth the criterion, rather than one’s desires, prejudices, and assumptions.

“فَإِن أَسلَموا فَقَدِ اهتَدَوا”

“So if they submit, they have indeed found guidance.”

This sentence clarifies the relationship between submission and guidance. Guidance is not merely hearing the message or knowing the path; a person must accept truth and walk in its direction.

In Al-Baqarah 2:135, groups called on people to become Jews or Christians to find guidance, but the Qur’an responded by presenting the way of Abraham, the hanif. In Al-Baqarah 2:137, guidance is likewise connected with accepting faith, rather than changing one’s religious name.

Abraham, who lived centuries before Muhammad, is also described in Aal Imran 3:67 as hanif and muslim. Islam therefore cannot be monopolized by a sect, people, or historical community.

Today, too, a group may claim to be the sole true representative of Islam and make accepting its denomination and leaders a precondition for others’ guidance. Yet no sect owns Islam or serves as the gatekeeper of guidance. What brings a person to guidance is genuine submission to The God, not merely bearing the title Muslim or following a religious denomination.

This submission must also become evident in conduct. Someone unwilling to correct their belief or behavior in the face of clear evidence cannot consider themselves exempt from the test of submission merely by claiming to be religious.

“وَإِن تَوَلَّوا فَإِنَّما عَلَيكَ البَلٰغُ”

“And if they turn away, only conveying the message is your responsibility.”

After setting out the path of guidance, The God also raises the possibility that people may turn away and clarifies the Messenger’s responsibility.

Muhammad is commissioned to convey the message, not to force people to accept it. In Al-Baqarah 2:272, he is reminded that guiding people is not his responsibility. In Ash-Shura 42:48, after mentioning the audience’s turning away, the Messenger’s responsibility is again limited to conveying the message.

This is the same principle expressed in Al-Baqarah 2:256 by denying compulsion in religion. Genuine faith and submission arise from conscious acceptance, not fear and coercion.

If Muhammad himself has no right to impose faith on people, no religious leader, sect, or government can compel people to accept a belief in the name of Islam.

“وَاللَّهُ بَصيرٌ بِالعِبادِ”

“And The God sees His servants.”

The God sees the reality of His servants’ choices and conduct. He knows who has submitted through understanding, who has recognized truth and turned away from it, and who remains caught in their own assumptions and suppositions.

For this reason, no human being can pass definitive judgment on the reality of another person’s faith and ultimate destiny solely on the basis of their religious name.

Muhammad conveys the message, people are responsible for accepting it or turning away, and final judgment belongs to The God. Guidance is connected with submission to truth—not claims to ownership of religion, the accumulation of knowledge without action, or suppositions that a person has accepted in place of truth.

  • Al-Jumua 62:2: A Messenger raised from among the ummiyyin to recite the signs, purify, and teach the Book and wisdom.
