Al-Imran 3:35

ۖ

When Imran’s wife said: My Lord, I have vowed what is in my womb to You, freed and dedicated; so accept it from me. Surely You are the Hearing, the Knowing.

Our Reflection

In Aal Imran 3:33, The God named Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of Imran among those He chose, and in Aal Imran 3:34 He spoke of the continuity of generations. Now, as the story of Imran’s family begins, the Quran first places before us the words and decision of a woman: Imran’s wife, who vows the child in her womb to The God.

This choice in the Quran’s account matters. Imran’s wife speaks to her Lord herself, makes the vow herself, and herself asks that it be accepted. Although the Quran identifies her as “Imran’s wife,” it does not attribute her decision of faith to her husband. What it presents about this woman is her own faith, intention, and commitment.

We see this approach elsewhere in the Quran as well. In At-Tahrim 66:10, the wives of Noah and Lot are held responsible for their own conduct despite living alongside two righteous servants; in At-Tahrim 66:11, Pharaoh’s wife, despite living alongside an oppressive ruler, is presented as an example of faith for all believers. A woman’s worth and responsibility in faith therefore do not depend on her husband’s position.

Women’s role is also prominent in the story of Moses. In Al-Qasas 28:7, The God speaks of revealing to Moses’ mother and of her role in protecting her child. Here too, a story that leads to Mary and then Jesus begins with the decision of faith made by Mary’s mother. A woman in this account is not merely a figure alongside men; her own conduct and decision form part of the story of those divinely chosen.

Imran’s wife says: “إِنّى نَذَرتُ لَكَ ما فى بَطنى مُحَرَّرًا,” meaning “I have vowed what is in my womb to You, freed and dedicated.”

A vow is a commitment that a person voluntarily undertakes before The God. The word “مُحَرَّرًا” also speaks of freeing and dedicating. Here it does not mean freeing a slave; the mother wishes to set her child apart from ordinary personal attachments and expectations and dedicate the child to worshipping and serving The God.

The vow of Imran’s wife is not a bargain with The God. She does not ask for a child, wealth, or a new position in return for her vow; rather, she offers what she carries within her to her Lord with sincerity.

But an important question arises here: Does a mother have the right to vow a child who has not yet been born and cannot express consent for an entire lifetime, determining that child’s future without the child’s own choice?

We must seek the answer in the meaning of “مُحَرَّرًا” and in the continuation of the story.

The mother wishes to free her child from personal attachments and expectations, not to remove the child from the family’s ownership and place the child under her own ownership or that of the custodians of a place of worship. A child is not a means of fulfilling the parents’ wishes, providing for their future, or preserving the family’s reputation. The child is an independent human being with a life and responsibility of their own before the Lord.

Furthermore, the mother’s vow alone does not remove the child’s right to choose and believe. The principle of no compulsion in religion is clear in Al-Baqarah 2:256. No parent may invoke a vow to force a child to accept a particular belief, worship, or destiny and call it The God’s command.

The Quran itself makes clear, as the story continues, the outcome of the mother’s pure intention and The God’s acceptance. In Aal Imran 3:37, The God accepts Mary with a good acceptance and nurtures her with a good growth. The fruit of this sincere vow and divine acceptance is the upbringing of a girl who, by her own will and choice, attains faith, purity, truthfulness, and worthiness, rather than through force and coercion. The God chooses and purifies Mary (Aal Imran 3:42) and calls her a woman of truth (Al-Maidah 5:75). In Aal Imran 3:45, He also conveys to Mary, through the angels, important news about the birth of Jesus. This reception of divine news, or “نَبَأ,” connects with our study of the concept of “Prophet.” Another point is that in Surah Maryam, The God recounts Mary’s story and then names Prophets such as Abraham, Moses, Ishmael, and Idris. After this sequence, He says in Maryam 19:58:

“أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ مِنْ ذُرِّيَّةِ آدَمَ وَمِمَّنْ حَمَلْنَا مَعَ نُوحٍ وَمِنْ ذُرِّيَّةِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْرَائِيلَ وَمِمَّنْ هَدَيْنَا وَاجْتَبَيْنَا ۚ إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُ الرَّحْمَٰنِ خَرُّوا سُجَّدًا وَبُكِيًّا”

“These are those whom The God has favored: from among the Prophets, from the descendants of Adam, from those We carried with Noah, from the descendants of Abraham and Israel, and from those We guided and chose. When the signs of the Entirely Merciful were recited to them, they fell down in prostration and weeping.”

Thus, the mother’s pure intention is met with good divine acceptance, and Mary grows in purity and worthiness. But she is not a figure without choice who merely carries out her mother’s vow. In Aal Imran 3:43, Mary herself is addressed by a divine command, and in At-Tahrim 66:12, The God praises her own faith, affirmation, and obedience. Mary’s worthiness and sincerity therefore develop in continuity with her mother’s pure intention and The God’s good acceptance and nurturing, and become manifest in Mary’s own faith and conduct—not through force and coercion.

Another lesson of this story concerns parents’ responsibility to nurture and protect their children.

Today, some parents entrust their children from an early age to mosques, religious schools, educational centers, or boarding institutions to gain knowledge and religious education. Yet sometimes, after placing a child there, they cease to inquire about the child’s situation and do not know who is caring for the child or how the child is being treated.

Media reports describe beatings, humiliation, abuse, and even rape and sexual abuse of children by some clerics, instructors, and staff at religious and educational institutions. Such conduct is oppression and a betrayal of the trust placed in them. The titles “cleric,” “teacher,” “memorizer of the Quran,” or “mosque servant” do not in themselves guarantee anyone’s purity, suitability, or trustworthiness.

As Mary’s story continues, the Quran does not stop at the mother’s vow and divine acceptance. In Aal Imran 3:37, it speaks of Mary’s good upbringing and her being placed in Zechariah’s care. Zechariah is a known figure in the Quran: in Al-Anam 6:85 he is named among the righteous, and in Aal Imran 3:40 he speaks of his old age. In Aal Imran 3:44, the question of who would take responsibility for Mary’s care is raised separately.

This part of the story offers a clear lesson for parents today: a pure intention to provide religious education is not enough; the caregiver’s suitability, a sound environment for upbringing, and protection of the child also matter.

Parents must know who is caring for their child, how instructors treat the child, whether the child’s physical and psychological safety is protected, and whether the child can report mistreatment without fear of threats or punishment. Entrusting a child to a religious or educational institution does not end the parents’ responsibility to follow up on the child’s situation and provide protection.

No place of worship should become a place where a child is deprived of freedom, dignity, and safety in The God’s name. A child raised to serve The God must not become trapped in servitude to, and fear of, people who claim to represent religion.

After stating her vow, Imran’s wife says: “فَتَقَبَّل مِنّى,” meaning “so accept it from me.”

She does not regard making the vow as an assured merit for herself; rather, she asks her Lord to accept it. This humility shows that the worth of an act does not lie merely in announcing that it has been done; intention and its acceptance before The God also matter.

The verse ends with “إِنَّكَ أَنتَ السَّميعُ العَليمُ,” meaning “surely You are the Hearing, the Knowing.”

At the end of the preceding verse, The God was also described as “سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ.” Now Imran’s wife addresses her Lord with those same two attributes. This prayer also connects with the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael in Al-Baqarah 2:127: while raising the foundations of The God’s House, they ask Him to accept their work using these same two attributes.

Imran’s wife believes that The God hears her words and knows the intention behind her vow. She entrusts her child to her Lord and asks for acceptance; as the story continues, we see this request answered through Mary’s good acceptance and upbringing.
