Al-Imran 3:29

ۗ ۗ

Say: If you conceal what is in your breasts or disclose it, The God knows it; and He knows what is in the heavens and what is on earth; and The God has power over every thing.

Our Reflection

This verse states one of the fundamental principles for knowing The God in the Quran: a principle that not only recalls His knowledge and power but also gives people a criterion for distinguishing truth from falsehood. Through this principle, anyone can assess many claims, reports, and beliefs about The God against the Quran and discern whether they are sound.

“إِن تُخفوا ما فى صُدورِكُم أَو تُبدوهُ يَعلَمهُ اللَّهُ”: The God knows what a person conceals within and what they disclose. “Ṣudūr” is the plural of “ṣadr,” and here refers to the location of a person's inward affairs, including thoughts, intentions, desires, beliefs, and secrets. His knowledge is therefore not limited to outward words and actions; even what has not yet been spoken is not hidden from Him.

In the preceding verse, believers were warned against accepting the authority and protective allegiance of opponents of truth, with an exception for those compelled to protect themselves in danger. This verse now makes clear that The God knows the inward reality of both situations. In Al-Mumtahanah 60:1, too, in the context of prohibiting taking enemies as allies, The God speaks of His knowledge of what people conceal or disclose.

The verse then extends the scope of this knowledge: “وَيَعلَمُ ما فِى السَّمٰوٰتِ وَما فِى الأَرضِ”. His knowledge is not confined to human beings or the earth; everything in the heavens and earth lies within it. No distance, place, covering, or concealment can put anything beyond His awareness.

This truth is also stated in Aal Imran 3:5. In Al-Baqarah 2:33, The God speaks of His knowledge of the unseen of the heavens and earth and of what created beings reveal or conceal. Ta-Ha 20:7 likewise emphasizes that He knows the secret and what is even more hidden.

In Al-Mujadilah 58:7, this all-encompassing knowledge is connected with His awareness of people's most confidential conversations. In Fussilat 41:22, the Quran also rebukes those who thought that The God did not know much of what they did. These verses show that being hidden from human eyes does not mean being hidden from Him.

How can this principle serve as a means of distinguishing truth from falsehood in everyday life?

We offer several practical examples:

1: In the Torah as transmitted today, Genesis 3:7–11 recounts that after eating from the tree, Adam and his wife became aware of their nakedness, made coverings, and hid among the trees when they heard The God in the garden. He then called Adam and asked where he was and who had told him he was naked.

Let us assess this account against the principle of Aal Imran 3:29: would The God, who knows a person's most hidden thoughts, need to enter the garden and search for Adam to learn where he was or what he had done? Could someone escape His knowledge by hiding among the trees?

2: Another example is the story of Jacob and Esau in Genesis 27:18–35. In this account, Jacob presents himself as his brother Esau and, by deceiving their blind father Isaac, obtains the blessing intended for Esau.

If this story is taken to mean that divine choice, blessing, and determination can also be stolen through human trickery, we must ask: can The God, who knows Jacob's hidden intention and has power over everything, be deceived like a human being? Isaac may have been unaware of the truth, but The God neither lacked knowledge of Jacob's purpose nor lost His authority to bestow and determine through someone's deception. Deceiving a human being is not the same as deceiving The God.

3: Another example is the claim that an intermediary is necessary to convey prayers to The God. People are sometimes told that for their prayer to be accepted, they must first appeal to a holy person or another being who will convey their request to Him.

Using this verse as a criterion, we may ask: what human need is unknown to The God, requiring someone else to inform Him? If He knows what is still hidden within a person's breast, how could He need an intermediary to inform Him of a prayer?

In Al-Baqarah 2:186, The God describes Himself as near and speaks directly of answering His servants' prayers. In Yunus 10:18, some people describe other beings as their intercessors with Him, and the Quran asks whether they would inform Him of something in the heavens or earth that He does not know.

That question is directly connected with Aal Imran 3:29. Before a prayer is spoken, The God knows the person's need and intention. No intermediary can bring a truth to His attention for the first time. For a fuller examination, see Shafaʿah in the Quran.

4: This principle also applies to assessing fabricated reports attributed to Muhammad in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. For example, Sahih al-Bukhari 1145 transmits a report from Abu Hurayrah describing the Lord descending to the lowest heaven each night during its final third and asking who calls upon Him for an answer, requests something for Him to grant, or seeks His forgiveness.

This report connects a descent to the lowest heaven at a particular time with an invitation to prayer and seeking forgiveness. According to Aal Imran 3:29, however, The God knows His servants' needs and prayers at every time and place. He does not need to descend from one location to another to become aware of someone's request or hear their prayer. No hour of the day or night increases His knowledge, and no spatial distance can limit His access to His servants.

In Ash-Shura 42:11, the Quran explicitly states that nothing is like Him. In Al-Hadid 57:4, it speaks of His being with people wherever they are. Together with His all-encompassing knowledge, these verses provide a criterion for assessing portrayals that represent Him as a being confined by location and movement.

Thus, such a report cannot justify a picture of The God as needing to move through the heavens or await a particular time to draw near to His servants, hear their prayers, or know their needs. The criterion for knowing Him is the all-encompassing knowledge and power stated in the Quran.

Similar reports appear in Sahih Muslim 758a, Sahih Muslim 758b, Sahih Muslim 758c, and Sahih Muslim 758d.

We assess this narration and other examples of attributing created beings’ limitations to The God against the Qur’anic principle of divine transcendence in “Subhan and Tasbih in the Qur’an”.

The verse's ending, “وَاللَّهُ عَلىٰ كُلِّ شَىءٍ قَديرٌ”, connects His all-encompassing knowledge with His power. The root Q D R relates to ability, measure, and determining measure; here, “qadīr” emphasizes His comprehensive ability. He is not merely aware of and observing creation; power and ability also belong to Him. No being can make Him ignorant through concealment or deprive Him of authority and sovereignty through trickery.

Aal Imran 3:26 and Aal Imran 3:27 describe His ownership of dominion, His granting and withdrawing authority, the transformations of creation, and His provision. This verse makes His all-encompassing knowledge and ability clear. Together, these descriptions offer an understanding of The God against which other claims about Him can be assessed.

For this reason, the verse is not merely a warning about human concealment; it provides a criterion for knowing The God and discerning whether statements attributed to Him are sound. Every report or belief that makes His knowing, hearing, or becoming aware dependent on an intermediary, place, or time must be assessed against this Quranic principle.

The same God from whose knowledge nothing is hidden also knows human intentions and actions. The next verse, Aal Imran 3:30, speaks of the day when a person finds what they have done present before them. His all-encompassing knowledge is the foundation of this accountability.
