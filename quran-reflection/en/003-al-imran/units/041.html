Al-Imran 3:41

ۖ ۗ

He said: My Lord, appoint a sign for me. He said: Your sign is that you will not speak to people for three days except through gestures; and remember your Lord often, and glorify Him in the evening and morning.

Our Reflection

After receiving the good news of John’s birth, Zechariah asked his Lord for a sign. The God made his sign three days of not speaking to people except through gestures, and commanded him to remember his Lord often and glorify Him in the evening and morning.

The command to remember abundantly and glorify in the morning and evening is not exclusive to Zechariah; in Al-Ahzab 33:41 and Al-Ahzab 33:42, the same command is given to all believers. We too should not remember our Lord only in times of need, but should also remember Him often and glorify Him after our prayers are answered and we receive blessings.

Commentary

As we examined in the reflections on Aal Imran 3:3 and Aal Imran 3:4, the Quran confirms the earlier scriptures and serves as a criterion in relation to them. Furqan means distinguishing truth from falsehood. In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Quran is likewise described as confirming and exercising oversight over the earlier scriptures: a guardian and a standard for judging them.

The Quran recounts accounts shared with the Torah and Gospel with a clear purpose. For example, Jesus is presented in the Gospel as the Son of God, whereas the Quran calls him Jesus son of Mary and denies that The God has offspring. By contrast, concerning the second coming of Christ discussed in the Gospel, the Quran neither explicitly rejects nor affirms it.

When the Quran recounts details differently from earlier scriptures, we should therefore reflect on the significance of that difference. One purpose of these corrections is to remove accusations against The God’s Prophets.

In Luke 1:18–20, Zechariah is said not to have believed the angel’s words and, for that reason, to have been deprived of speech until after John’s birth. The Quran, however, makes clear that Zechariah’s silence was not punishment for unbelief, but a sign he himself had requested from his Lord. In Maryam 19:10, the duration of this sign is given as three nights while Zechariah was sound. Just as Abraham in Al-Baqarah 2:260 asked to see how the dead are brought to life so that his heart might be reassured, Zechariah’s seeking reassurance did not mean that he lacked faith.

This is not the only instance in which the Quran clears a Prophet of an accusation. In Al-Ahzab 33:69, The God explicitly speaks of clearing Moses of what people said and warns believers against repeating such conduct.

In the Torah, Genesis 27:18–36 attributes to Jacob the deception of his blind father, Isaac, to obtain the blessing his father intended for his brother. Likewise, Exodus 32:1–6 presents Aaron as responsible for making the calf and preparing the way for calf worship. The Quran, however, clears Aaron of this accusation, places responsibility upon the Samiri, and speaks of Aaron’s efforts to restrain the people from calf worship (Ta-Ha 20:85 and Ta-Ha 20:90).

Unfortunately, this error has also entered some Muslim hadith and narrative sources. In his commentary on Sad 38:24, al-Tabari transmits a report through Yunus ibn ʿAbd al-Aʿla, from Ibn Wahb, from Ibn Lahiʿah, from Abu Sakhr, from Yazid al-Raqashi, from Anas ibn Malik, attributed to the Messenger of The God. According to it, David ordered that the husband of a woman he admired be placed in a position that would lead to his death.

The antecedents of this story can be found in Israelite narratives, including 2 Samuel 11:2–27. Even by the assessment of hadith scholars, this report lacks a reliable chain of transmission, and the Quran does not attribute such a crime to David.

Believers must not only refrain from accusing The God’s Prophets and speaking about what they do not know (Al-Isra 17:36), but must also observe the principle of not discriminating or making divisions among the Prophets. We cannot honor one Prophet while repeating and promoting accusations against another. We must apply the same standard to our own hadiths and narratives that we apply when evaluating accounts in the Torah and Gospel. That standard is the Quran.
