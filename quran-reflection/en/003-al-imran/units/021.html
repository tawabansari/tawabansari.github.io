Al-Imran 3:21

Indeed, those who disbelieve in the signs of The God, kill the Prophets without right, and kill those among the people who command justice—give them tidings of a painful punishment.

Our Reflection

In Aal Imran 3:18, The God was described as upholding justice. Aal Imran 3:19 identified religion with Him as Islam—submission to The God and truth—and in Aal Imran 3:20, Muhammad was commissioned to convey the message to people. Even if his audience did not accept his invitation, his responsibility was delivery, not forcing them to believe. Now, in Aal Imran 3:21, the Qur’an speaks of those who stand directly against these principles: they deny the signs of The God, kill the Prophets without right, and take the lives of people who call others to justice.

This continuity reveals the contrast between Islam and rebellion against truth. Submission to The God is not merely a claim of faith or belonging to a religious community. When truth conflicts with a person’s desires, prejudices, or interests, their conduct reveals what they submit to. Someone who considers themselves a follower of divine religion yet uses force and violence to silence truth has, in practice, put their own will and power above truth. Disbelief in the signs in this verse is accompanied by two grave crimes that target the conveyance of guidance and the establishment of justice.

The Qur’an first speaks of killing the Prophets “بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ”—without right. The Prophets bore divine knowledge and tidings and called people to the guidance of The God. Yet those who found that invitation contrary to their desires sometimes acted to destroy the Prophet himself instead of responding to the message. In Al-Maidah 5:70, the Qur’an recalls Messengers who, whenever they brought something contrary to a group’s desires, were either denied or killed. This example shows how opposition to truth, when joined with rebellion and the pursuit of power, can move from denying the words to eliminating the speaker. Of course, not everyone who rejected an invitation was a killer or wanted to kill; the verse condemns those who committed such aggression.

The qualification “بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ” emphasizes the injustice of these killings; it does not suggest the possibility of rightfully killing the Prophets. Al-Baqarah 2:61, Al-Baqarah 2:91, and An-Nisa 4:155 also speak of killing the Prophets without right. The Qur’an does not give the names and stories of all the Prophets who were killed, but understanding the verse’s message does not require inventing names or stories from other sources. Its point is clear: those who bore divine guidance were targeted for killing instead of having their words heard and considered.

The verse does not stop at this crime. By repeating “وَيَقْتُلُونَ,” it identifies another group of victims: “الَّذِينَ يَأْمُرُونَ بِالْقِسْطِ مِنَ النَّاسِ”—people who command justice. The repetition of the verb matters. Killing those who call for justice is not a minor issue on the margins of killing the Prophets; the Qur’an states it separately and includes it in the same severe condemnation. These people are not necessarily Prophets or holders of religious office. They may be ordinary citizens, workers, scholars, honest judges, or people who stand against oppression and ask others to respect people’s rights. The value of their call does not depend on their position, wealth, or power, but on the justice and right they defend.

The connection with Aal Imran 3:18 is clear. The God Himself upholds justice, and Al-Hadid 57:25 presents establishing justice through people as one purpose of sending the Prophets and Messengers with the Book and the balance. Thus, qist is not solely a matter of divine judgment; it is also a responsibility people must undertake in their social relations. In An-Nisa 4:135, the Qur’an commands upholding justice and truthful testimony, even against oneself or one’s relatives. In Al-Maidah 5:8, hostility toward a group must not cause a departure from justice. Commanding justice can therefore mean demanding the rights of the oppressed, exposing corruption, seeking fair judgment, and standing against someone who uses power to violate others’ rights.

One telling Qur’anic example is the believing man from Pharaoh’s household who, in Ghafir 40:28, opposes the intention to kill Moses. He asks those who have made that decision whether they would kill a man merely for saying that his Lord is The God, when he has also brought clear evidence. He is not a Prophet; he is a person among the people who, before the ruling power, defends a caller’s right to speak and the sanctity of his life. The Qur’an does not report this man’s killing here, but his stand clearly illustrates human responsibility in the face of oppression. Truthful speech must not be treated as worthless merely because its speaker lacks power or position.

Here the connection with freedom of expression and the right to convey a message becomes apparent. In Aal Imran 3:20, Muhammad was commissioned to deliver the message; his audience could accept the invitation or turn away. In Aal Imran 3:21, however, we encounter people who did not stop at rejecting the message but attacked the lives of its bearers. There is a great difference between disagreeing with speech and preventing it from being expressed. A person may disagree with an invitation, ask the speaker for evidence, or state an opposing view; disagreement in belief does not authorize killing, threatening, or silencing the speaker. If speaking and listening are blocked, how can people recognize truth and make an informed choice about it?

This is directly connected with the principle of no compulsion in religion. In Al-Baqarah 2:256, the Qur’an rejects religious coercion. Just as Muhammad had no right to force people to believe, his opponents had no right to use force to prevent him from delivering his message. Compelled acceptance of a belief and compelled silence both deprive people of freedom to understand and choose. In Az-Zumar 39:17 and Az-Zumar 39:18, the Qur’an praises those who listen to speech and follow the best of it. Such choice has no meaning without the opportunity to listen, question, and evaluate. This connection is examined in detail in Forqan’s study “There Is No Compulsion in Religion; the Messenger’s Duty Is Delivery”, particularly the section “Delivery is impossible without freedom.”

We also see an example of this treatment of message delivery in the early period of Muhammad’s invitation in Mecca. Before the political community of Medina was formed, he had neither a government nor an army with which to force people to accept his invitation. His principal means were revelation, speech, and conveying the message, yet his opponents did not stop at rejecting his words. In Al-Anfal 8:30, the Qur’an speaks of a plot to detain Muhammad, kill him, or drive him from his land. This verse does not report Muhammad’s killing; it shows that, to end his invitation, some people considered actions ranging from detention and exile to murder. Such conduct directly opposed the mission of delivery without compulsion assigned by The God.

In social life, silencing those who seek justice does not always begin with killing. Someone may first be threatened for exposing corruption, prevented from speaking, imprisoned, or deprived of work and a livelihood. Sometimes killing is then used to end their pursuit of justice. Aal Imran 3:21 itself explicitly speaks of killing, and threats, imprisonment, or censorship must not be treated as literally identical to killing. Yet these actions can share an aim: preventing the communication of words that call oppressive power to account and demand respect for others’ rights. Killing is the most extreme form of this assault, taking not only the speaker’s freedom of expression but also their life.

This verse is a particularly serious warning to those who deny people the right to protest oppression in the name of religion or preserving order. If The God upholds justice and calls people to uphold it, how can anyone punish people seeking justice in His name? No ruler, community, or religious leader can treat a mere claim to being right as permission to kill or silence those who ask for evidence, fairness, and accountability. Of course, not every claim made in the name of justice is automatically correct; it too must be assessed through evidence and the criterion of justice. But the response to an incorrect claim is evidence and fair examination, not taking the speaker’s life or liberty merely for expressing an opinion.

The verse ends with the command “فَبَشِّرْهُم بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ.” Bushra ordinarily announces good news, but here it is paired with painful punishment, creating a forceful contrast. Those who try to silence the message of truth and the voice of justice through killing are themselves confronted with news of painful punishment. This command likewise directs Muhammad to announce the consequence of their conduct; it is not permission for him to use the same methods of violence and coercion against opponents in this world.

Continuing the discussion of Islam, delivery, and justice, Aal Imran 3:21 draws a clear boundary between submitting to truth and rebelling against it. Submission is not established merely by invoking the name of The God or claiming to follow His religion. Someone who denies truth because it conflicts with their interests and resorts to violence to silence those calling for justice has, in practice, submitted to their own desires and power, rather than to truth.

Establishing justice in society requires people who can speak truth, defend others’ rights, and call those in power to justice without losing their lives and freedom for that very call. Killing the Prophets and those who command justice is not merely an assault on the lives of a few individuals; it attacks the path of guidance, reform, and the establishment of the justice that The God Himself upholds.
