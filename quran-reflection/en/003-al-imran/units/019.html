Al-Imran 3:19

ۗ ۗ

Indeed, religion with The God is submission; and those who were given the Book differed only after knowledge had reached them, out of transgression among themselves. And whoever disbelieves in the signs of The God, indeed, The God is swift in reckoning.

Our Reflection

In the preceding verse, those possessing knowledge testified alongside the angels to the oneness of The God. The Qur’an now clarifies the reality of religion and speaks of people who, despite receiving the Book and attaining knowledge, differed among themselves out of transgression. This connection raises an important question: how can a person recognize truth yet put their own desires above following it?

“إِنَّ الدِّينَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ الْإِسْلَامُ”

“Indeed, religion with The God is submission.”

“Din” comes from the root د ي ن, associated with obedience, commitment, judgment, and accountability. “Islam” comes from the root س ل م and is the verbal noun of “أَسْلَمَ,” here meaning to submit and yield to the command of The God.

Submission to The God does not arise from helplessness or compulsion; it is the conscious acceptance of truth and living by the guidance He has sent. Having recognized what is right, a person freely decides whether to follow it or give their own desires priority over it.

Islam in this verse is not the proper name of a historical religious denomination, but the reality of servitude and submission to The God. In Aal Imran 3:67, the Qur’an describes Abraham as “مُسْلِمًا,” although he lived centuries before Muhammad. In Al-Baqarah 2:133, Jacob and his children also speak of their submission to that same God.

Ash-Shura 42:13 presents the foundation of divine religion as one in the missions of Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad. Yet Al-Maidah 5:48 speaks of different ways and prescribed paths for different communities and calls them to vie with one another in good deeds.

Thus, the foundation of divine religion is one, although the prescribed paths and ways established for different communities may differ. What matters is faith, real submission to The God, and human conduct, not merely the religious name one adopts. In Al-Baqarah 2:62 and Al-Maidah 5:69, the Qur’an likewise identifies faith in The God and the Last Day, together with righteous action, as the criterion for reward and salvation.

This meaning also matters in Aal Imran 3:85, where The God states that no religion other than Islam will be accepted. To understand the connection between these verses and the distinctions among religion, Islam, prescribed paths, and religious identities, see “Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Qur’an”.

“وَمَا اخْتَلَفَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ إِلَّا مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَهُمُ الْعِلْمُ بَغْيًا بَيْنَهُمْ”

“Those who were given the Book differed only after knowledge had reached them, out of transgression among themselves.”

It is striking that a person may sometimes recognize truth yet be unwilling to act on it. The problem is not always ignorance of the path; sometimes the path is clear, but a person does not wish to follow it because accepting truth may threaten their power, wealth, position, or inherited beliefs.

The Qur’an uses the expression “بَغْيًا بَيْنَهُمْ” for this behavior.

The root ب غ ي in the Qur’an is associated both with seeking and pursuing and with transgression and going beyond a limit. In this verse, baghy points to excessive ambition and the pursuit of superiority that lead a person beyond the bounds of what is right.

In Al-Baqarah 2:213, we also read that The God raised up Prophets and sent the Book with them to judge between people in truth. Yet the very people who had received the Book differed about it out of transgression after clear evidence had reached them. Ash-Shura 42:14 and Al-Jathiya 45:17 recall the same behavior.

The important point is that possessing knowledge does not guarantee acting upon it. Knowledge can illuminate the path, but it does not compel a person to walk it.

Someone may have spent years reading the Book of The God, speaking about justice, and calling others to avoid oppression. Yet when practicing that same justice threatens their own power or interests, they may turn away from it. In such a situation, the problem is not a lack of knowledge: the person knows what is right but is unwilling to pay the price of remaining faithful to it.

This danger is not confined to followers of earlier scriptures. Anyone who reads the Qur’an and speaks in the name of religion must guard against falling into the same behavior. In Al-Hadid 57:16, believers are also warned not to become like some earlier recipients of the Book whose hearts hardened with the passage of time.

When truth is sacrificed to competition for power and religious superiority, disagreement can move beyond discussion and debate into division, hostility, and oppression. In Al-Anam 6:159 and Ar-Rum 30:32, the Qur’an speaks of those who divided their religion and split into separate groups, each group pleased with what it possesses.

“وَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ”

“And whoever disbelieves in the signs of The God—indeed, The God is swift in reckoning.”

Here the warning extends beyond the recipients of the Book to all human beings: “وَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ”—whoever disbelieves in the signs of The God.

Kufr in this context is associated with denying and concealing truth. Someone who recognizes the divine signs yet knowingly acts against them to protect their own desires and interests must understand that their conduct does not escape divine reckoning.

The God describes Himself as “سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ.”

“Sarīʿ” comes from the root س ر ع, meaning speed and the absence of slowness; “hisab” comes from the root ح س ب, associated with counting, assessing, examining, and accountability.

The God does not need time to pass in order to know and reckon human deeds. No action or intention is hidden from His knowledge. A person may do something and imagine for years that it has had no consequences, but not seeing a result does not mean that the deed has gone unaccounted for.

We also live in a world where our conduct is not without effect. Each choice can set a chain of consequences in motion, even if its effects become apparent only gradually.

For example, someone who conceals a truth to preserve their power may initially get what they want. But others make decisions based on that false information, trust disappears, and disagreement and hostility may arise. Like the first domino that sets the others moving as it falls, a deed can have consequences beyond what a person initially imagined.

The result of an action may become apparent late, but divine reckoning is not subject to that delay. In Ar-Rum 30:41, the Qur’an also connects the corruption that has appeared on land and at sea with what human beings have done. People experience some consequences of their conduct in this world, even if not all its effects and outcomes become apparent to them.

Divine reckoning is not only about punishment. In Al-Baqarah 2:202, a person’s share of what they have earned appears alongside the description “سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ.” In Aal Imran 3:199, the same description is connected with the reward of those who believe and do not sell the signs of The God for a small price. The final reckoning and complete judgment of The God in the Hereafter also remain in place.

Religion with The God is submission to truth. Whoever recognizes truth is responsible for remaining faithful to it, even when accepting it conflicts with their interests and desires. Knowledge, scripture, and a religious name cannot replace this responsibility. The God is swift in reckoning, and no good or bad deed, however hidden or late its consequences appear, lies beyond His knowledge and reckoning.

Cross-references
  • Aal Imran 3:18: Those possessing knowledge testify to the oneness of The God, who is described as upholding justice.
  • Aal Imran 3:20: The verbs aslamtu, a-aslamtum, and aslamu directly continue the subject of submission.
  • Aal Imran 3:67: Abraham is described as muslim before the formation of later religious identities.
  • Aal Imran 3:85: The rejection of religion other than Islam and its connection with submission to The God.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:62: Faith in The God and the Last Day, with righteous action, as the criterion for divine reward.
  • Al-Maidah 5:69: The same criterion of faith and righteous action among different communities.
  • Ash-Shura 42:13: The common foundation of divine religion in the missions of the Prophets and the command to uphold it.
  • Al-Maidah 5:48: Different prescribed paths and ways for communities, with a call to vie in good deeds.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:213: The Book sent for judgment in truth, and its recipients differing out of transgression after clear evidence arrived.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:253: Disagreement and fighting after clear evidence reached the followers of the Messengers.
  • Ash-Shura 42:14: Division and disagreement after knowledge arrived, owing to transgression.
  • Al-Jathiya 45:17: Clear evidence followed by disagreement out of transgression.
  • Al-Hadid 57:16: Warning believers against hardened hearts, as among some earlier recipients of the Book.
  • Al-Anam 6:159: Dividing religion and becoming separate groups.
  • An-Nisa 4:123: Religious wishes do not replace responsibility for one’s conduct.
  • Al-Baqarah 2:202: A person’s share of what they have earned and the description of The God as swift in reckoning.
  • Aal Imran 3:199: The God’s swift reckoning in connection with the reward of believers.
  • Ar-Rum 30:41: Corruption becoming apparent on earth because of human conduct.
