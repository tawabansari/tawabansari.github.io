Al-Imran 3:7

ۖ ۗ ۗ ۗ

He is the One who sent down the Book to you; some of it consists of firm, clear verses, which are the mother of the Book, and others are ambiguous. But those whose hearts contain deviation follow its ambiguous passages, seeking discord and seeking their ultimate interpretation; and no one knows its ultimate interpretation except The God. And those firmly grounded in knowledge say: We believe in it; all is from our Lord. And none take heed except those possessed of understanding.

Our Reflection

This is one of the Quran’s most foundational verses: it speaks not only about the Book’s contents, but also clarifies how to understand it and distinguish a sound reading from an unsound one. Here God teaches human beings how to approach the Quran’s clear and ambiguous passages, what to take as their standard, and where to acknowledge the limits of their knowledge.

“هُوَ الَّذِى أَنْزَلَ عَلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ”

“He is the One who sent down the Book to you.”

These words recall that the Book’s source is The God. It was sent down to Muhammad, who receives and conveys revelation. The standard for recognizing the divine message is therefore the Book God has sent down, rather than the beliefs, wishes, and reports that people subsequently added to it or imposed upon it.

“مِنْهُ آيَاتٌ مُحْكَمَاتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ”

“Some of it consists of firm, clear verses, which are the mother of the Book, and others are ambiguous.”

“Muhkamat” comes from H K M, a root associated with firmness, restraining disorder, establishing order, and sound judgment. The clear verses have firm and clear meanings and can serve as a foundation for understanding and judgment.

Still more significant is the expression “هُنَّ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ”.

“Umm” means mother in Arabic and, by extension, an origin, source, or point of reference upon which other things depend or to which they return. Through this expression, the Quran identifies the place of the clear verses: they are the mother and foundation of the Book—the standard by which its other parts are assessed and understood.

These clear verses encompass the evident principles and limits of religion: divine oneness, faith, justice, individual responsibility, the inviolability of human life, freedom of belief, the rights of women and men, equitable retribution, inheritance, fasting, prayer, and the other rulings and principles stated in the Quran.

For example, when the Quran states the principle of no compulsion in religion in Al-Baqarah 2:256, an ambiguous report or tentative interpretation cannot justify forcing people to accept faith. Likewise, when An-Nisa 4:135 commands upholding justice even against oneself, one’s parents, and relatives, no ethnic, religious, or political affiliation can authorize setting that principle aside.

The existence of clear verses does not mean that people understand all their details without knowledge of language, context, and the relationships between verses. Research remains necessary for more precise understanding. But no tentative interpretation or outside source has the right to arbitrarily alter the clear, well-supported meaning of the verses or replace their rulings with others.

By contrast, “mutashabihat” comes from SH B H, denoting resemblance and similarity. Similarity can sometimes bring coherence and recognition, and at other times make it difficult to distinguish meanings or the reality of things.

In this verse, the ambiguous passages stand in contrast to the clear ones: their understanding may involve multiple layers, or the ultimate reality and manner of what they describe may not be fully evident to human beings. Certain expressions concerning the unseen, the nature of the hereafter, and broad depictions of creation can be considered from this perspective.

The presence of ambiguous verses does not mean that the Quran is incomplete. God has made the clear verses the Book’s foundation for human guidance. To recognize divine oneness, justice, goodness, responsibility, and the clear limits of religion, people do not first have to know every secret of the unseen, the manner of creation, or the ultimate reality of every ambiguous expression.

Nor are the ambiguous verses worthless or outside the Book. They too come from the same divine source and can be studied and reflected upon. The distinction is that a tentative reading of ambiguous verses must not govern the clear verses.

We have examined this principle in detail in “The authority of clear foundational verses”.

“فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ ابْتِغَاءَ الْفِتْنَةِ وَابْتِغَاءَ تَأْوِيلِهِ”

“But those whose hearts contain deviation follow its ambiguous passages, seeking discord and seeking their ultimate interpretation.”

Here God reveals the central danger: instead of making the clear verses the standard for understanding the ambiguous ones, people may select passages capable of different readings and use them to establish their own beliefs and desires.

“Zaygh,” from Z Y GH, means inclining and deviating from a path. Here it concerns deviation of the heart: a person’s inner orientation shifts away from seeking truth toward other desires and purposes.

The Quran identifies two purposes behind this pursuit: “ابْتِغَاءَ الْفِتْنَةِ” and “ابْتِغَاءَ تَأْوِيلِهِ”—seeking discord and seeking ultimate interpretation.

“Fitnah” has various uses in the Quran, from trial and pressure to persecution, misleading others, and disorder. In this context, seeking fitnah is connected with exploiting ambiguous passages to mislead, create division, and disrupt the pursuit of truth.

Yet not every question, difference of interpretation, or investigation of ambiguous verses indicates deviation of the heart. The Quran condemns those who follow ambiguous passages with a deviant orientation to achieve their own purposes. Someone sincerely seeking truth may make a mistake, fail to understand a meaning, or propose a new possibility. That is different from deliberately setting aside clear truth to impose a preferred interpretation on the Quran.

The history of religious interpretation offers examples of this danger. Different groups may all cite the Quran while reaching completely different, even contradictory conclusions. Merely finding a verse to support a statement does not establish that the reading is sound. One must ask whether it accords with the clear verses and the rest of the Quran, or has been constructed to prove a previously accepted belief.

In Al-Baqarah 2:78, the Quran speaks of people without real knowledge of the Book who are caught in wishful notions and conjecture. In Al-Baqarah 2:111, it calls the claim that salvation belongs exclusively to one community “their wishful notions” and demands proof. These verses recall that wishes and inherited beliefs, however dear to people, cannot replace evidence.

One clear example with grave consequences is rajm, or stoning.

In An-Nur 24:2, the Quran prescribes one hundred lashes for a woman and man who commit zina. In An-Nisa 4:25, it speaks of halving the punishment in a specified situation. One hundred lashes can be halved, but stoning to death cannot.

Nevertheless, much report-based jurisprudence prescribes stoning for a married person who commits zina. To justify it, some have even claimed that a verse about stoning once existed but was supposedly removed from the Quran’s text.

The fundamental question is: can the Quran’s existing, clear ruling be altered on the basis of a report about an absent verse, and that report then made a source of legislation?

If the clear verses are the mother of the Book, outside reports and claims must be assessed against the Quran’s clear ruling. An existing verse must not become subordinate to a report that is itself absent from the Quran. This is more than a theoretical disagreement: its outcome may determine a judgment concerning a human life.

We have examined this issue in “Stoning: a Quranic ruling or a ruling derived from reports?”.

The same principle matters when verses are claimed to be abrogated. Anyone claiming that a Quranic verse is no longer valid must provide clear Quranic evidence. An apparent difference between two verses, failure to understand their context, or the existence of a report does not in itself prove that God has annulled a verse’s ruling. For a broader discussion, see “Abrogation in the Quran”.

The danger of seeking discord through ambiguous passages is therefore not confined to theoretical and philosophical debates. When tentative readings are imposed upon people as definitive divine rulings, they may lead to deprivation, oppression, declarations of unbelief, division, and even the taking of human lives.

“وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُ إِلَّا اللَّهُ”

“And no one knows its ultimate interpretation except The God.”

“Ta’wil” belongs to the root A W L and is connected in the Quran with a matter’s return to its reality, reference, or outcome. It should not be equated with explaining and translating words in every occurrence.

For example, in Yusuf 12:100, when Joseph’s earlier dream comes true, he speaks of its ta’wil. There it is more than a mental explanation of a dream: it is the realization of what had previously been seen in a vision.

Al-Araf 7:53 likewise speaks of the day when the ta’wil of what was previously stated arrives and the reality of the divine promise becomes apparent. In Yunus 10:39, the Quran condemns those who deny something before encompassing it in knowledge and before its ta’wil has arrived.

These usages show a distinction between understanding a statement and knowing its ultimate reality and outcome.

In Aal Imran 3:7, ta’wil is discussed in connection with ambiguous verses. A person may understand part of such a verse’s meaning, investigate it, or recognize a new dimension as knowledge grows. But that understanding is not equivalent to encompassing its true and ultimate ta’wil.

This does not prohibit reflection or mean that people cannot understand anything of ambiguous verses. The Quran itself speaks of teaching ta’wil to human beings, as in Joseph’s story. We must therefore distinguish between knowledge God grants to people and a claim to complete knowledge of the ultimate ta’wil of ambiguous passages.

For example, knowledge of celestial motion and the structure of the universe may lead a cosmologist to a new possibility concerning certain Quranic depictions of creation. A physician or biologist, through more precise knowledge of embryonic development, may better appreciate subtleties in verses about human creation. Such research can be valuable and broaden understanding. Yet no scientific theory or philosophical reading can be presented as the definitive and ultimate ta’wil of God’s words without sufficient evidence.

This boundary neither obstructs research nor makes knowledge worthless. It keeps people from turning their limited understanding into an unchangeable truth.

“وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِنْ عِنْدِ رَبِّنَا”

“And those firmly grounded in knowledge say: We believe in it; all is from our Lord.”

This is one of the Quran’s most beautiful descriptions of the relationship between knowledge and faith.

“Al-rasikhun” comes from R S KH, denoting becoming firmly established and taking a secure place. Those firmly grounded in knowledge have an understanding that is established within them, rather than superficial and fleeting.

But the Quran does not identify claiming to know everything as the sign of this firmness. They say: “آمَنَّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِنْ عِنْدِ رَبِّنَا”—We believe in it; all is from our Lord.

These words clarify two truths.

First, those firmly grounded in knowledge recognize that both the clear and ambiguous verses come from God. They neither deny ambiguous verses because their ultimate ta’wil is unclear nor sacrifice the clear verses to their own tentative readings.

Second, firmness in knowledge is accompanied by intellectual humility. A person truly grounded in knowledge distinguishes knowing from not knowing. Such a person does not have to invent a definitive answer to every question to preserve scholarly standing, reputation, or religious authority.

In An-Nisa 4:162, the Quran also speaks of those firmly grounded in knowledge among the People of the Book who believe in the revelation sent down. Firmness in knowledge is therefore not a title exclusive to a sect, religious class, or group of officeholders.

Someone who has studied the Quran for years may be mistaken on a matter, while a person just beginning research may discover valid evidence. The standard is not people’s names, experience, or rank, but knowledge, evidence, honesty, and willingness to correct a reading in the face of truth.

Moreover, “all is from our Lord” means that all the clear and ambiguous verses come from the Lord—not that all the interpretations people have constructed from them also come from God.

This distinction is fundamental. The sanctity of revelation must not be transferred to human readings of revelation. The Quran is divine speech, but our translations, reflections, explanations, and readings result from human effort. They must always remain open to examination and correction against the text and new evidence.

At Forqan, we face the same responsibility. However extensive our research into Quranic roots, verses, concepts, and principles may be, it does not claim ownership of truth or knowledge of the ultimate ta’wil of all ambiguous passages. We must assess our readings against the clear verses, context, and the network of Quranic verses, and correct any error that becomes evident.

This approach also forms the basis of our work on the “Translation and reflection methodology” page.

“وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّا أُولُوا الْأَلْبَابِ”

“And none take heed except those possessed of understanding.”

The verse concludes by recalling the place of reason.

“Al-albab” is the plural of “lubb,” a word denoting the core and pure, essential part of something; in human usage it refers to reason and the faculty of understanding.

At the close of this verse, the Quran speaks not merely of abundant knowledge or a powerful memory, but of people capable of thinking, distinguishing, assessing, and taking heed.

A person of understanding can distinguish God’s words from human readings, knowledge from conjecture, clear from ambiguous, and honest research from pursuing interpretation to prove personal wishes.

Such a person neither fears asking questions nor feels ashamed to say “I do not know.” Human understanding can grow, but no amount of knowledge places a person in the position of The God’s limitless knowledge.

Ultimately, this verse places a great responsibility upon us all—especially those who speak about religion, translate, write interpretations, issue religious rulings, or invoke the Quran to judge matters affecting others’ rights and lives.

The Quran’s clear verses must be the standard for assessing ambiguous passages, reports, religious beliefs, and all human readings. They must not be set aside to preserve an inherited belief or a preferred interpretation.

The Quran must not be made subordinate to our beliefs; our beliefs must be assessed and corrected against the Quran.
