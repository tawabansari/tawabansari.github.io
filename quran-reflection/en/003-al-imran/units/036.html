Al-Imran 3:36

ۖ

Then, when she gave birth to her, she said: My Lord, I have given birth to her, a girl; and The God knows best what she has given birth to, and the boy is not like the girl. And I have named her Mary, and I place her and her descendants in Your protection from the outcast Satan.

Our Reflection

In Aal Imran 3:35, Imran’s wife vowed the child in her womb to worship and serve The God. Now the child has been born, but contrary to what the mother may have expected, the baby is a girl.

The Quran reports the mother’s words as follows: “رَبِّ إِنّى وَضَعتُها أُنثىٰ,” meaning “My Lord, I have given birth to her, a girl.”

To understand this mother’s possible concern, we must consider the social and religious conditions of her time. In the tradition of the Children of Israel, the formal priesthood was centered on men: in Exodus 28:1, priestly responsibility is entrusted to Aaron and his sons. Perhaps Imran’s wife, influenced by these customs, also expected to give birth to a boy who would serve in a place of worship, and was now concerned that she could not fulfill her vow as she had imagined. Yet the Quran does not say that she became averse to her daughter or rejected her. Her possible concern relates to the child’s place in religious service; it does not establish that she considered her daughter worthless.

But immediately after the mother’s words, the Quran says:

“وَاللَّهُ أَعلَمُ بِما وَضَعَت,” meaning “and The God knows best what she has given birth to.”

In the Hafs reading, “بِمَا وَضَعَتْ” is read in the third person, and the sentence states The God’s knowledge of the newborn child. In the readings of Shuʿbah from ʿAsim, Ibn ʿAmir, and Yaʿqub, however, “بِمَا وَضَعْتُ” is in the first person. In this reading, the mother herself says: “and The God knows best what I have given birth to.”

In both readings, The God’s knowledge exceeds the mother’s understanding and expectations. In Ar-Rad 13:8, we also read that The God knows what every female carries in her womb. The mother has just learned that her child is a girl, but The God already knew her reality and future. The mother does not yet know what place this girl will have in the history of human guidance. As the story continues, we see that this very girl receives a good acceptance and a good upbringing (Aal Imran 3:37). Being a boy was therefore not a condition of divine acceptance, and limited human expectations could not be the criterion for The God’s choice.

Then we read:

“وَلَيسَ الذَّكَرُ كَالأُنثىٰ,” meaning “and the boy is not like the girl.”

The order of the words matters. The Quran does not say “the girl is not like the boy,” but “the boy is not like the girl.”

If we take this sentence as a continuation of the mother’s speech, she is speaking of the difference between the boy she expected and the girl who has been born, in the context of religious service. But if we read it as continuing the divine statement “وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا وَضَعَتْ,” another meaning comes to the fore: the boy the mother had expected was not like this girl, because The God knew Mary’s special standing while the mother did not yet know it.

In either reading, the comparison concerns the expected boy and the newborn girl in this story, rather than issuing an enduring judgment that all men are superior to all women.

The continuation of the story shows the prominent place this very girl came to hold in the Quran’s account of guidance.

In the Quran, which does not name Ali, Abu Bakr, Umar, and Uthman as the historical figures in question, Mary’s name appears 34 times, whereas Muhammad’s name appears four times and Ahmad’s once. Of course, the frequency of a person’s name does not in itself establish that person’s superiority before The God. The comparison shows the great place a woman came to occupy in the narrative of divine guidance in the face of a society with structures centered on men.

The Quran dedicates a surah to Mary’s name, speaks of her being chosen and purified (Aal Imran 3:42), calls her a woman of truth (Al-Maidah 5:75), conveys news of Jesus’ birth to her through the angels (Aal Imran 3:45), and presents her and her son as “a sign for all peoples” (Al-Anbiya 21:91). In Al-Hujurat 49:13, taqwa is the criterion of honor before The God, rather than gender, descent, or social position. Where taqwa and being a sign for guidance matter, a woman such as Mary can have a greater place in the Quran’s narrative of guidance even compared with thousands of unnamed Prophets. This is not a final ranking of people before The God. Rather, it shows that while many divine Messengers are not named in the Quran, Mary is presented to the world through her name, story, faith, and conduct.

This story also offers an important lesson for parents today. A family that has placed all its hopes in the birth of a boy may not yet know what talents and future its daughter will have. In Ash-Shura 42:49, The God presents girls and boys alike as His gifts, mentioning girls before boys. This precedence does not mean that girls are inherently superior; rather, the birth of a girl is also a divine gift, not a deprivation or a sign of the family’s lesser worth. Yet in some families, the birth of a boy is still greeted with joy, while the birth of a girl brings sorrow or blame upon the mother—conduct condemned in An-Nahl 16:58 and An-Nahl 16:59.

Imran’s wife continues:

“وَإِنّى سَمَّيتُها مَريَمَ,” meaning “and I have named her Mary.”

Despite her possible concern that her child is a girl, the mother does not reject her. The same woman who personally made the vow in the preceding verse now chooses her daughter’s name herself and continues speaking with her Lord.

Then the mother’s prayer continues:

“وَإِنّى أُعيذُها بِكَ وَذُرِّيَّتَها مِنَ الشَّيطٰنِ الرَّجيمِ,” meaning “and I place her and her descendants in Your protection from the outcast Satan.”

The word “أُعِيذُهَا” is from the root ʿ W Dh. The mother does not seek refuge only for herself, but places her daughter and her future descendants in The God’s protection—a line from which Jesus will later come. Thus, even if she was initially concerned that the child was a girl, she now prays with hope for that very girl’s future and descendants.

Seeking refuge from Satan also connects with the vow in the preceding verse. The mother has dedicated her child to serving The God, and now asks that same Lord to protect her and her descendants from temptation, deception, and misguidance. Interestingly, in Maryam 19:18, Mary herself uses the same word family, ʿ W Dh, when faced with an unfamiliar person, and seeks refuge in the Entirely Merciful. The mother sought protection for her daughter, and Mary too seeks refuge in her Lord with her own awareness and will.

The verse ends with the mother’s prayer, and the divine response becomes clear in the next verse. The mother may have expected a boy to serve The God, but The God accepted that very girl with a good acceptance—a girl who, through her faith and worthiness, became one of the most prominent signs of guidance in the Quran.

Cross-references
  • Aal Imran 3:35 — Imran’s wife’s vow and her dedication of the unborn child to serving The God, setting the context for her possible concern here.
  • Aal Imran 3:37 — Mary’s good acceptance and upbringing: the divine response to the mother’s vow and concern.
  • Aal Imran 3:42 — Mary’s selection and purification, revealing a standing her mother did not know at her birth.
  • Aal Imran 3:43 — The command to Mary herself to worship and bow; her service to The God despite being a girl.
  • Aal Imran 3:45 — The angels’ announcement of Jesus’ birth to Mary, continuing the story of the vowed child.
  • Aal Imran 3:195 — No woman’s or man’s deeds are lost before The God; “some of you are from others” affirms that the worth of conduct does not depend on gender.
  • Al-Maidah 5:75 — Mary as a woman of truth, showing her worthiness and truthfulness.
  • Ar-Rad 13:8 — The God’s knowledge of what every female carries, directly related to His knowing best what she gave birth to.
  • An-Nahl 16:58 — The sorrow and altered faces of some on hearing of a girl’s birth, illustrating a wrong attitude toward a child’s gender.
  • An-Nahl 16:59 — Condemnation of the conduct and judgment of those who regarded a girl’s birth as disgraceful.
  • An-Nahl 16:97 — A good life connected with faith and righteous conduct, whether one is male or female.
  • Al-Anbiya 21:91 — Mary and her son as a sign for all peoples, and their place in divine guidance.
  • Ash-Shura 42:49 — Girls and boys as divine gifts, with girls mentioned first.
  • Al-Hujurat 49:13 — Taqwa as the criterion of human honor before The God, rather than gender or descent.
  • At-Tahrim 66:12 — Mary’s own faith, affirmation, and obedience; her worthiness beyond her mother’s vow, without removing her choice.
  • Maryam 19:18 — Mary herself seeking refuge in the Entirely Merciful, connected with her mother’s prayer for her protection.
  • Al-Anam 6:112 — Devils among humans and jinn and their deceptive embellishment, showing the scope of the danger from which people seek refuge in The God.
