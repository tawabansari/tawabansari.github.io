Al-Imran 3:28

ۖ ۗ ۗ

Believers must not take disbelievers as awliya instead of believers; whoever does so has no connection with The God in anything, except when taking precautions to protect yourselves against them. The God warns you of Himself; and the ultimate return is to The God.

Our Reflection

People form many kinds of relationships: they make friends, establish families, trade, enter agreements, and sometimes rely on others' support for security or power. These relationships are not all alike. Someone may be a friend without abandoning their beliefs and principles; alternatively, they may become so dependent on another's power and wishes that they set aside a truth they know in order to win that person's approval.

Aal Imran 3:28 warns about such a choice. Believers must not take disbelievers as their awliya instead of believers and thereby violate their loyalty to truth. The verse also leaves room for precaution and self-protection for someone in danger, and closes by recalling human accountability before The God.

To understand this instruction, we must first understand “believer,” “disbeliever,” and “wali.” Misunderstanding these words has led to turning this verse into a prohibition on friendship with followers of other religions, excluding all Jews and Christians from faith, and stirring up hostility in the name of religion.

“Al-muʾminūn” means believers. Faith in the Quran is connected with belief in The God and the Last Day, acceptance of truth, faith in divine revelation, and righteous action. Believer is not a title exclusive to one nation or religious community.

In Al-Baqarah 2:62, the Quran names Jews, Christians, and Sabians alongside those who have believed, and identifies faith in The God and the Last Day and righteous action as the criterion for divine reward. Al-Maidah 5:69 states the same criterion.

Aal Imran 3:113 explicitly says that the People of the Book are not all alike. Aal Imran 3:114 speaks of some among them who believe in The God and the Last Day, enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and hasten to good deeds. Aal Imran 3:199 also describes People of the Book who believe in The God, the revelation sent to Muhammad, and what was sent to themselves.

How, then, can all Jews and Christians be called disbelievers and excluded from faith when the Quran itself affirms believers among them?

“Muslim” in the Quran likewise concerns submission to The God and is not confined to the community subsequently known by that name. Abraham is called a Muslim in Aal Imran 3:67, and Al-Hajj 22:78 recalls that this designation existed previously. Al-Hujurat 49:14, meanwhile, distinguishes professed submission from faith that has entered the heart.

Being called Muslim therefore does not by itself make someone a believer, just as being called Jewish or Christian does not automatically exclude someone from faith. These distinctions are examined in Believer or Muslim? and Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Quran.

“Al-kāfirīn” is likewise not a general designation for all religious or political opponents. The root K F R relates to covering and concealing. Kufr, in the context of denying truth, concerns turning away from and concealing truth whose evidence has become clear to a person. Someone who has not yet recognized truth or is sincerely seeking it is not a conscious denier merely because they lack a particular religious label.

This surah itself gives examples of conduct characterized by kufr. In Aal Imran 3:11, Pharaoh's people deny divine signs. An-Naml 27:14 describes people denying signs out of injustice and arrogance despite their inward certainty.

Aal Imran 3:19 speaks of disagreement after knowledge has arrived, arising from transgression among people. Aal Imran 3:21 describes those who deny divine signs, kill Prophets without right, and kill those calling for justice. In Aal Imran 3:23, a group of scripture-holders turn away when summoned to the judgment of The God's Book.

In these examples, kufr concerns denying truth, opposing justice, transgressing after knowledge has arrived, and turning away from guidance; it is not simply the name of a religion or people.

We now come to the verse's central word: “awliyāʾ.”

It comes from the root W L Y, relating to nearness and connection. Depending on context, this connection can include protection, alliance, guardianship, mutual responsibility, dependence, following, or accepting another's authority. “Wali” therefore cannot always be reduced to “friend.”

To clarify the distinction, imagine someone with a Christian neighbor whom they visit, help during illness, and work with in community affairs. They may be close friends without handing over their beliefs or standards of judgment to that neighbor.

Another person may feel no affection for someone powerful, yet become so obedient to them that they abandon a known truth to preserve their position, wealth, or protection. Here there is dependence and acceptance of wilayah even without emotional friendship.

In Al-Baqarah 2:257, The God is the believers' wali, bringing them from darkness into light. Conversely, the awliya of disbelievers are taghut, bringing them from light into darkness. Wilayah here concerns the course of guidance and the direction of human life.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 likewise places rejection of taghut alongside faith in The God. Al-Araf 7:3 commands following what the Lord has revealed and not accepting other awliya instead of Him.

Wilayah is not limited to religious authority. In At-Tawbah 9:71, believing men and women are awliya of one another, a relationship involving mutual responsibility, enjoining right, and forbidding wrong. Wilayah can therefore also encompass social protection, alliance, and assistance.

In Aal Imran 3:28, “min dūni al-muʾminīn” is particularly important: taking disbelievers as awliya “instead of believers.” The issue is withdrawing one's support, loyalty, and direction from people of faith and truth and giving these to those who stand against it.

An-Nisa 4:139 connects this with seeking honor among disbelievers. Are they seeking honor there, the Quran asks, when all honor belongs to The God? This connects with Aal Imran 3:26, where granting dominion and honor belongs to Him.

In pursuing power, wealth, status, or security, people may become so dependent on others that they abandon their principles to satisfy others' demands. Such wilayah can be political, social, economic, or religious; it cannot be restricted to military alliances.

Does this instruction, however, prohibit friendship, companionship, marriage, family ties, and cooperation with Jews, Christians, and followers of other religions?

The Quran itself has issued no such ruling.

Al-Maidah 5:5 permits the food of the People of the Book and marriage to their chaste women under the conditions stated. Marriage is not a passing relationship: it creates family ties involving love, trust, responsibility, and kinship.

Al-Maidah 5:82 speaks of people calling themselves Christians who are nearest in affection to believers. Al-Mumtahanah 60:7 even raises the possibility of affection developing between former enemies.

In Al-Mumtahanah 60:8, The God does not forbid kindness and justice toward those who have neither fought on account of religion nor expelled people from their homes. The following verse, Al-Mumtahanah 60:9, prohibits taking as awliya those who have fought on religious grounds, driven people out, or assisted in their expulsion.

These verses distinguish difference of belief from active hostility. A different belief does not itself justify enmity or injustice. This is examined in the principle of kindness and justice toward people of other religions.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:1 also addresses people aiding enemies who expelled believers because of their faith, by conveying information and expressing affection to them. This differs from ordinary friendship because it involves practical support for those acting against believers.

Joseph's story also illustrates a believer participating in society's administration. In Yusuf 12:54, Egypt's king trusts Joseph and brings him close. In Yusuf 12:55, Joseph requests responsibility for the land's storehouses. Yusuf 12:76 also distinguishes Joseph's position in Egypt's administration from the king's law. The Quran does not explicitly identify the king's beliefs, but it describes Joseph's cooperation in administering the land.

In early Islamic history, some followers of Muhammad also took refuge with the Christian king of Abyssinia and received his protection. Under the Constitution of Medina, groups of Jews participated in social and defensive agreements with Muhammad's community while retaining their religious differences.

Offering refuge, friendship, trade, marriage, administrative cooperation, and just social agreements therefore cannot be called prohibited wilayah solely because religions differ. We must examine their basis and whether they lead someone to deny truth, commit injustice or betrayal, or abandon their principles.

Al-Maidah 5:51 is another verse used to prohibit friendship with Jews and Christians and exclude them all from faith. Since it is closely connected with Aal Imran 3:28, its message also needs to be understood correctly.

Al-Maidah 5:51 addresses believers and prohibits taking Jews and Christians as awliya. It says that some of them are awliya of others and that whoever among the believers accepts their wilayah belongs to them. It then warns that The God does not guide unjust people.

Does this mean that no Jew or Christian can be a believer and that every relationship with them is forbidden?

Surah Al-Maidah itself rejects that reading. Al-Maidah 5:69, in the very surah that names Jews and Christians in Al-Maidah 5:51, makes faith in The God and the Last Day and righteous action the criterion among religious communities. Al-Maidah 5:82 and Al-Maidah 5:83 describe people calling themselves Christians who have affection for believers and, on hearing revelation, recognize its truth and profess faith. Aal Imran 3:113, Aal Imran 3:114, and Aal Imran 3:199 also affirm faith and righteous conduct among the People of the Book.

“Jews” and “Christians” name religious and social communities, whereas “believer” describes a person's faith. Naming a community in relation to the verse's believing addressees does not deny faith to every member of that community.

The verses before and after Al-Maidah 5:51 also matter for understanding wilayah.

Al-Maidah 5:43 speaks of people who approach Muhammad for judgment despite having the Torah and The God's judgment within it, then turn away. Al-Maidah 5:44 describes the Torah as containing guidance and light and discusses the responsibility to judge by it.

Al-Maidah 5:46 presents Jesus with the Gospel, confirming the Torah before him. Al-Maidah 5:47 calls the people of the Gospel to judge by what The God revealed within it.

In Al-Maidah 5:48, the Quran is then described as confirming the scripture revealed before it and as muhaymin over it: a guardian, witness, and criterion for assessing it. The same verse commands judging by divine revelation and not following others' desires against the truth that has arrived.

Al-Maidah 5:49 continues the warning: judge by revelation and do not follow desires that could turn you away from part of what The God has revealed. Al-Maidah 5:50 then rebukes seeking the judgment of ignorance instead of divine judgment.

After these verses, Al-Maidah 5:51 addresses taking Jews and Christians as awliya.

This continuity indicates that an important instance of prohibited wilayah is entrusting religious authority to those who place their wishes and rulings above revealed guidance. The issue is not merely personal affection or social contact: it involves following, judgment, and loyalty to the source of guidance.

The following verses clarify further dimensions. Al-Maidah 5:52 describes people hastening to join others out of fear of misfortune, a behavior related to dependence and seeking support. Al-Maidah 5:55 presents the wilayah of The God, His Messenger, and believers; Al-Maidah 5:57 prohibits taking those who mock religion and treat it as a game as awliya.

Wilayah thus involves religious authority, following, support, dependence, and practical direction, and cannot be reduced to ordinary friendship.

Al-Baqarah 2:120 clarifies this further. Muhammad is warned that Jews and Christians will not be pleased with him until he follows their religious way. Divine guidance is then identified as true guidance, with a warning that if he follows their desires after knowledge has come, he will have no protector or helper against The God.

Muhammad was not commissioned to set aside the truth he had received and submit to other religious communities' authority to win their approval.

The same principle appears in Al-Maidah 5:48 and Al-Maidah 5:49. The Quran confirms previous scriptures but is muhaymin over them. In religious judgment, rulings and accounts attributed to earlier scriptures must therefore be assessed by the Quran, rather than allowing other rulings and reports to govern it.

This issue is not confined to Muhammad's relationship with the People of the Book during revelation. In subsequent history, some reports and rulings derived from earlier traditions entered Muslim interpretation and legal systems through Israʾiliyyat, hadith, and other human sources.

Israʾiliyyat are reports and accounts from the religious heritage of the Children of Israel that entered later interpretive and narrative traditions. Sometimes they supplied details about which the Quran says nothing: the name of Adam's wife, the name of the angel of death, the identity of Moses' father-in-law, some accounts of the Kaaba's earlier history, and details about the Companions of the Cave.

More importantly, some religious rulings also entered legal systems from other sources and were then made binding in the name of Islamic law.

Stoning is a clear example. In the legal tradition of the Children of Israel, stoning was prescribed for certain offenses. The Quran, however, states the punishment for zina in An-Nur 24:2 and does not prescribe stoning as a punishment. Nevertheless, stoning became a prescribed legal penalty in much later report-based jurisprudence.

Execution for apostasy also entered later legal systems as a religious punishment, although the Quran does not prescribe it for merely changing one's belief. Turning zakat into an instrument of religious taxation and political power, adding onerous obligations, and attributing human rulings to The God are further topics examined in Forqan's studies.

The central question is this: why should a ruling that The God has not prescribed in the Quran be imposed upon it through reports and tradition, and then made binding on people as a divine command?

This is the danger of reversing authority. The Quran, described in Al-Maidah 5:48 as muhaymin over previous scriptures, is in practice subordinated to rulings and accounts from other sources. Instead of making The God's Book the criterion, people make it subject to others' authority.

Al-Araf 7:3 commands following what the Lord has revealed and prohibits following other awliya instead of Him. At-Tawbah 9:31 condemns turning scholars and monks into lords alongside The God. Ash-Shura 42:21 speaks of partners legislating religious rules without His permission, and Yunus 10:59 questions attributing permissions and prohibitions to Him without His authorization.

This is the issue already encountered in Aal Imran 3:23: some scripture-holders are summoned to The God's Book for judgment but turn away. If people call the Quran their book of guidance yet give human sources priority over its verses when they conflict, they have in practice set aside its authority.

Forqan examines this in When Quranic Silence Is Filled with Reports, Stoning: A Quranic Ruling or One Derived from Reports?, Zakat and Its Historical Distortion, Halal and Haram in the Quran, and the principle of the Quran's completeness and sufficiency for religious guidance.

The principle of revelation as the authority for religious judgment and the principle of avoiding unfounded religious authority examine the same issue through Quranic principles.

Religious wilayah is only one dimension of wilayah, but a particularly important one: people may think they are following The God while in practice accepting another authority over His Book.

After prohibiting this wilayah, Aal Imran 3:28 says: “وَمَن يَفعَل ذٰلِكَ فَلَيسَ مِنَ اللَّهِ فى شَىءٍ”: whoever does this has no connection with The God in anything.

This warning shows that choosing wilayah is not merely an outward matter. By choosing their source of guidance, their support, and the direction of their loyalty, people show in practice what they place above other things.

Someone who knowingly turns to the wilayah of those opposed to truth for honor, power, or gain, abandoning divine guidance, cannot justify that choice by a claim of faith or religious status.

Al-Anam 6:159 also speaks of those who divide their religion into factions and tells His Messenger that he has no connection with them in this matter. Group affiliations and religious claims cannot take the place of loyalty to truth.

After this warning, the verse states an important exception: “إِلّا أَن تَتَّقوا مِنهُم تُقىٰةً”: except when taking precautions to protect yourselves against them.

This expression comes from the root W Q Y, relating to keeping, protecting, and shielding against harm.

The term “taqiyyah” arose from this same word family. In religious usage, it means concealing one's belief or religious affiliation in danger to protect one's life, security, or faith, and sometimes being compelled to act outwardly against one's inward belief.

Imagine someone under an oppressive government whose disclosed belief could lead to imprisonment, torture, or death. They conceal it to preserve their life. This differs from freely abandoning loyalty to truth for status or gain.

Taqiyyah is not exclusive to one denomination, but became prominent in Shiʿi history, particularly among the Imami Shiʿa. At various times, followers faced pressure, persecution, and restrictions from opposing governments, and concealing their beliefs could protect life and security.

Taqiyyah also acquired considerable importance in reports attributed to Shiʿi Imams and an extensive place in that tradition's theological and legal discussions. Yet we must distinguish the necessity the Quran recognizes for human protection from turning taqiyyah into a permanent method or independent pillar of religious life. The Quran itself is the criterion for assessing any religious ruling.

The Quran presents a clear example of concealed faith in the believer from Pharaoh's people. Ghafir 40:28 describes a man in Pharaoh's household concealing his faith, within a power structure threatening Moses with death.

When Pharaoh intended to kill Moses, however, the man defended him and objected to killing someone who said his Lord was The God and had brought clear evidence.

In Ghafir 40:38, the same man calls people to follow the right way. In Ghafir 40:42, he rejects their invitation to association and disbelief; in Ghafir 40:44, he entrusts his affair to The God. Finally, Ghafir 40:45 reports that The God protected him from their plots.

Despite concealing his faith, this man is called a believer in the Quran. His concealment served self-protection, not acceptance of Pharaoh's beliefs. When defending Moses became necessary, he spoke openly for truth.

Another example is the Companions of the Cave. In Al-Kahf 18:19, the young people send one companion to the city for food, instructing him to act cautiously and not disclose their presence to anyone.

Al-Kahf 18:20 gives the reason: if their opponents discover them, they may stone them or force them back to their former religion. These young people were hiding to preserve their lives and faith. Concealment protected them from a threat.

An-Nahl 16:106 likewise distinguishes someone compelled while their heart remains secure in faith from someone who freely opens their breast to disbelief. Coercion matters when judging behavior; compelled outward conduct cannot be equated with free choice.

Fear of oppressive power is not disregarded in Moses and Aaron's story either. In Ta-Ha 20:43 they are sent to Pharaoh, and in Ta-Ha 20:44 instructed to speak gently. In Ta-Ha 20:45 they express concern over Pharaoh's dangerous behavior; in Ta-Ha 20:46 The God reassures them that He is with them.

An-Nisa 4:97, An-Nisa 4:98, and An-Nisa 4:99 likewise distinguish those able to leave an oppressive environment from genuinely powerless people who can find no escape.

These examples show that the Quran considers danger, coercion, ability, and actual circumstances. Taking precautions for protection is not the same as freely choosing denial of truth or support for oppression.

Can this protective exception, however, become a permanent religious method? Can taqiyyah authorize lying, deception, breach of trust, or broken agreements? To answer, we must assess it against clear Quranic principles, also studied independently in Forqan's Quranic principles.

The following example is hypothetical and applies to any person or group seeking to justify deception and broken agreements in the name of protecting belief; it does not attribute such conduct to all followers of any particular denomination.

Suppose a group enters agreements of friendship, cooperation, or mutual defense with people of another religion. For years, they benefit from their trust, protection, and resources while presenting themselves as loyal friends and allies. On acquiring greater power, however, they say: “We regarded you as enemies from the beginning; our friendship and commitments were never genuine—we were practicing taqiyyah.”

At-Tawbah 9:119 commands believers to practice taqwa and be with the truthful. Al-Ahzab 33:70 calls for sound, upright speech; Al-Hajj 22:30 prohibits false speech. These instructions are also discussed in the principle of truthfulness and the principle of not lying.

Al-Baqarah 2:8 and Al-Baqarah 2:9 describe people professing faith while their conduct contradicts their claim and they seek to deceive. As-Saff 61:2 and As-Saff 61:3 also condemn saying what one does not do. These verses underlie the principle of avoiding hypocrisy and the principle of consistency between words and deeds.

Al-Maidah 5:1 commands believers to fulfill agreements. An-Nahl 16:91 calls for honoring covenants once made and not breaking confirmed oaths. Al-Isra 17:34 recalls that people will be questioned about their commitments. These instructions are examined in the principle of keeping covenants, agreements, and promises.

An-Nahl 16:92 is even more explicit. It condemns unraveling a firm agreement like thread undone after being spun, and turning oaths into a means of deception, especially when one group becomes more powerful or numerous than another.

An-Nahl 16:94 again prohibits turning oaths into a means of deception and recalls its consequences.

An-Nisa 4:58 commands returning trusts to their owners and judging justly among people. Al-Anfal 8:27 prohibits believers from knowingly betraying The God, the Messenger, and their trusts. These underlie the principle of trustworthiness and the principle of not betraying trust.

Al-Baqarah 2:42 prohibits mixing truth with falsehood and knowingly concealing truth. Al-Baqarah 2:283 condemns withholding testimony and attributes its sin to the heart. These instructions are discussed in the principle of not concealing truth.

Concealing belief to escape torture or death differs from concealing truth needed to uphold another person's rights. The former protects someone against oppression; the latter may deprive another of their rights.

Al-Anfal 8:58 commands that, if betrayal by a group is feared, the agreement must be returned to them openly and on equal terms, for The God does not love betrayers.

An-Nisa 4:135 commands believers to uphold justice even against themselves, their parents, or relatives. Al-Maidah 5:8 warns against letting hostility toward a group deflect them from justice. These instructions appear in the principle of justice.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 permits and approves kindness and fairness toward those who neither fought on religious grounds nor expelled people. Al-Baqarah 2:190 prohibits transgression even in war. This is discussed in the principle of no transgression even against enemies.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 rejects compulsion in religion. In Yunus 10:99, even Muhammad is asked whether he would compel people to become believers. Al-Kahf 18:29 leaves the choice of faith or disbelief to the person, together with responsibility for its consequences.

These verses ground the principle of no compulsion in religion and the principle of freedom of belief.

Al-Maidah 5:2 commands cooperation in goodness and taqwa and prohibits cooperation in sin and aggression. Even religious and political cooperation must therefore be assessed against the principle of cooperating in goodness and the principle of not cooperating in wrongdoing, rather than the name of the participating community.

Does this mean every concealment is sinful and people must disclose their beliefs even under torture or threat of death?

No. Aal Imran 3:28 itself states the protective exception. An-Nahl 16:106 distinguishes compulsion from free choice. On necessity, Al-Baqarah 2:173 shows that an otherwise prohibited act may become permissible under compulsion, provided necessity's limits are not exceeded. This is examined in the principle of recognizing necessity and emergency.

The principle of distinguishing intention, error, and coercion likewise explains that deliberate conduct is not judged identically to conduct arising from error or compulsion.

Taqiyyah in Aal Imran 3:28 is thus an exception for protection against danger, not a general instruction to live behind a mask or permission for lying, deception, betrayal, and broken agreements.

If danger continues, the need for precaution may also continue. But this exception cannot be made a permanent habit or independent pillar of religion, used to set aside honesty, justice, and fidelity to agreements.

The believer from Pharaoh's people concealed his faith for protection; the Companions of the Cave hid to escape persecution and compulsion; An-Nahl 16:106 distinguishes the compelled person from someone freely accepting disbelief. None of these examples presents deceiving people to gain power and then betraying them as legitimate taqiyyah.

Taqiyyah protects a person against oppression; it is not a cover for becoming an oppressor and betrayer.

Aal Imran 3:28 closes: “وَيُحَذِّرُكُمُ اللَّهُ نَفسَهُ وَإِلَى اللَّهِ المَصيرُ”: The God warns you of Himself, and the ultimate return is to The God.

This warning follows the discussion of wilayah and the protective exception. People may justify their conduct before others through claims of faith, friendship, agreements, or taqiyyah, but the reality of their choice is not hidden from The God.

Aal Imran 3:29 recalls that whatever people conceal in their breasts or disclose is known to Him. Aal Imran 3:30 repeats the warning alongside a reminder of His compassion.

The issue is therefore not merely the outward appearance of relationships, but the intention, loyalty, and conduct behind them.

Someone may be friends with a person of another religion while remaining faithful to truth. Another may call themselves a believer and follower of the Quran yet set its Book aside for power, gain, or obedience to human authority. Community labels and outward religiosity do not determine the reality of these behaviors.

The Quran prohibits wilayah that leads people away from divine guidance toward denial, injustice, betrayal, and following authority opposed to truth. Conversely, it recognizes kindness, justice, fidelity to agreements, and healthy human relationships even with people of different beliefs.

Ultimately, all people return to The God and are responsible for their choices and deeds.
