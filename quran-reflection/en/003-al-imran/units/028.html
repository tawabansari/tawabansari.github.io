Al-Imran 3:28

ۖ ۗ ۗ

Believers must not take disbelievers as awliya instead of believers; whoever does so has no connection with The God in anything, except when taking precautions to protect yourselves against them. The God warns you of Himself; and the ultimate return is to The God.

Our Reflection

This is one of the verses whose message has been inverted by distorting three fundamental words: “kafir” has been turned into a name for every non-Muslim; “believer” has been monopolized by religious communities that made the name Muslim their exclusive identity; and “wali” has been translated as “friend.” These three betrayals of Qur’anic concepts have turned a warning about wilāyah and loyalty to truth into an instrument of hatred, division among people, and religious hostility.

To understand the verse, we must first learn these three concepts from the Qur’an itself.

“الْمُؤْمِنُونَ” is not an exclusive name for Muhammad’s followers or for one religious community. Faith in the Qur’an is connected with belief in The God, the Last Day, acceptance of truth, belief in divine revelation, and righteous action. Being a believer is not the monopoly of any people or denomination; a Jew or a Christian can also be a believer.

In Al-Baqarah 2:62 and Al-Maidah 5:69, the Qur’an names Jews, Christians, and Sabians alongside those who have believed, and identifies belief in God, the Last Day, and righteous action as the criterion for reward. Aal Imran 3:113 and Aal Imran 3:114 explicitly state that the People of the Book are not all alike: among them are those who believe in God and the Hereafter, enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and hasten to good deeds. Aal Imran 3:199 also identifies People of the Book who believe in God and the revelation sent down.

How, then, can all Jews and Christians be called disbelievers when the Qur’an itself affirms the presence of believers among them?

“Muslim” in the Qur’an likewise speaks of submission and is not confined to one historical community. Aal Imran 3:67 describes Abraham as muslim, and Al-Hajj 22:78 recalls that this designation existed previously. Al-Hujurat 49:14 distinguishes a declaration of submission from the reality of faith established in the heart. Thus, bearing the name Muslim does not by itself make someone a believer, just as being called Jewish or Christian does not automatically exclude a person from faith. This distinction is examined in “Believer or Muslim?” and “Dīn, Islām, and Sharīʿah in the Qur’an”.

“الْكَافِرِينَ” is not a general name for religious and political opponents either. The root K F R concerns covering and concealing. Kufr, in the context of denying truth, is concealing a truth whose evidence and signs have become clear to a person. Someone who has not yet recognized the truth or is sincerely seeking it does not become a disbeliever merely by lacking a religious label.

To recognize the conduct of those who reject truth, we need only consult the preceding verses of this surah. Aal Imran 3:11 presents Pharaoh’s people as an example of those who denied God’s signs. An-Naml 27:14 says that they denied the signs while inwardly certain of them—a denial arising from injustice and self-exaltation.

Aal Imran 3:19 speaks of disagreement out of transgression after knowledge had arrived. Aal Imran 3:21 describes people who reject God’s signs, kill the Prophets unjustly, and eliminate those who call for equity. In Aal Imran 3:23, a group of those given the Book turns away when called to the judgment of the Book of The God.

These are examples of the conduct of kufr within this surah’s context: denying truth, opposing justice, rebelling after knowledge has arrived, and turning away from the judgment of the Book of The God—not the name of a religion or community.

The third word is “أَوْلِيَاءَ”. The root W L Y concerns nearness and continuity: a relationship that, depending on context, can encompass support, alliance, undertaking responsibility, following, dependence, or accepting authority. “Wali” cannot be translated as “friend” in every verse.

One of the clearest Qur’anic passages for understanding wilāyah is Al-Baqarah 2:257. There, The God is the wali of believers, bringing them from darkness into light; by contrast, the awliya of those who reject truth are Taghut, bringing them from light into darkness.

This contrast is deeply significant. Wilāyah here is not merely affection, support, or a close relationship; it is connected with guidance, orientation, and the path a person chooses in life. Divine wilāyah leads toward light, while the wilāyah of Taghut draws toward darkness. Al-Baqarah 2:256 likewise places rejection of Taghut alongside faith in God. In Al-Araf 7:3, the command to follow what the Lord has revealed stands directly against following other awliya.

One of the most important functions of wilāyah, therefore, is accepting and following a source of guidance. In choosing a wali, a person does not merely establish a relationship; they may also entrust that figure with authority over their orientation, judgments, and decisions.

Wilāyah in the Qur’an is not limited to religious authority, however. At-Tawbah 9:71 describes believing men and women as awliya of one another, connecting this relationship with mutual responsibility, enjoining what is right, and restraining what is wrong. Wilāyah can also include social support, alliance, and assistance.

A person may love someone, cooperate and trade with them, yet not entrust them with authority over their decisions and religious orientation. Conversely, a person may feel no emotional affection for someone but become so subject to that figure’s power, commands, and authority that they have effectively made that person their wali. Affection that involves supporting oppression, disclosing secrets, or aiding enemies is no longer merely a private feeling; Al-Mumtahanah 60:1 addresses such a situation.

The expression “مِن دُونِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ” is also highly important: taking disbelievers as awliya “instead of believers.” The issue is not simply establishing a relationship; it is withdrawing support and loyalty from people of faith and truth and entrusting them to those who stand against it.

An-Nisa 4:139 repeats this expression and asks whether they seek honor among the disbelievers when all honor belongs to God. This question connects with Aal Imran 3:26, where the granting of dominion and honor was placed under God’s authority. To obtain power, wealth, status, or others’ support, a person may abandon loyalty to truth and submit to those who stand against it.

This wilāyah can be political, social, economic, or religious; it cannot be restricted to military alliances alone.

Those whose hearts contain deviation, in the language of Aal Imran 3:7, have used Al-Maidah 5:51 in isolation from its context to portray Jews and Christians indiscriminately as faithless and to promote hostility and hatred toward them. Yet the Qur’an itself speaks of faith among the People of the Book in Al-Maidah 5:69 and Aal Imran 3:199. To understand Al-Maidah 5:51 correctly, we must read the preceding and following verses together. This examination also clarifies wilāyah.

Al-Maidah 5:43 and Al-Maidah 5:44 concern the Torah and judging according to God’s judgment within it. Al-Maidah 5:46 and Al-Maidah 5:47 present Jesus with the Gospel, confirming the Torah before it. Then, in Al-Maidah 5:48, the Qur’an is sent down, confirming the earlier scriptures and being muhaymin over them: a guardian and witness over them and a criterion in judgment.

Al-Maidah 5:49 commands judging by what God has revealed and not following others’ desires, lest they turn people away from part of the revelation. Al-Maidah 5:50 condemns seeking the judgment of ignorance, and immediately afterward Al-Maidah 5:51 raises the question of taking Jews and Christians as awliya.

This continuity becomes clearer with Al-Baqarah 2:120. There, God warns Muhammad that Jews and Christians will not be pleased with him until he follows their religious way; He then identifies God’s guidance as the only true guidance and warns that if he follows their desires after knowledge has reached him, he will have neither wali nor helper against God.

This verse is crucial to understanding wilāyah. On one side stands following divine guidance; on the other, following others’ religious way and desires to gain their approval. The message is clear: Muhammad was not commissioned to abandon the truth that had reached him and submit to other religious communities’ authority in order to please them.

Al-Maidah 5:48 and Al-Maidah 5:49 repeat this principle: do not follow their desires against the truth that has reached you, and let what God has revealed be the criterion of judgment.

The connection with Al-Maidah 5:51 is now clearer. Before discussing wilāyah, the passage addresses judgment according to revelation, avoiding submission to others’ desires, and preserving the authority of the Book of The God. Thus, one important instance of forbidden wilāyah is entrusting religious authority to those who place desires, distortions, and human rulings above revelation instead of judging by the Book of The God.

The following verses clarify further dimensions. Al-Maidah 5:52 speaks of people who rush to join others for fear of calamity; Al-Maidah 5:55 presents the wilāyah of God, His Messenger, and believers; and Al-Maidah 5:57 prohibits taking as awliya those who treat religion as mockery and play. The ending of Al-Maidah 5:51 also connects such wilāyah with injustice. Wilāyah is therefore not simply ordinary friendship; it also concerns following, support, loyalty, authority, and practical orientation.

The same Qur’an permits the food of the People of the Book and marriage to their chaste women under the prescribed conditions in Al-Maidah 5:5. Al-Maidah 5:82 speaks of the closeness in affection to believers of a group who call themselves Christians. Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 does not prohibit kindness and justice toward those who have neither fought people over religion nor driven them from their homes. But Al-Mumtahanah 60:9 prohibits wilāyah with those who have fought, expelled people from their homes, or assisted in that expulsion.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:7 also speaks of the possibility of affection arising between people who were previously enemies. Prohibiting wilāyah does not therefore mean perpetual enmity and severing human relations.

Using Al-Maidah 5:51 or Aal Imran 3:28 to prohibit friendship with all Jews, Christians, and people of other beliefs imposes a meaning on the Qur’an that is inconsistent with its interconnected verses.

Qur’anic and historical evidence further clarifies this truth. In Yusuf 12:54 and Yusuf 12:55, the king of Egypt grants Joseph standing and trust, and Joseph requests responsibility for the land’s storehouses. Yusuf 12:76 also distinguishes Joseph’s position in Egypt’s government from the king’s law. The Qur’an does not explicitly specify the king’s belief, but it describes Joseph’s cooperation in administering the land.

Historical accounts likewise relate that, before the migration to Medina, some of Muhammad’s followers sought refuge with the Christian king of Abyssinia and received his protection. Under the Charter of Medina, groups of Jews also participated in social and defensive agreements with Muhammad’s community while retaining their religious distinctiveness. These examples show that seeking refuge, administrative cooperation, and even alliance with people of other beliefs do not automatically constitute forbidden wilāyah.

“وَمَن يَفعَل ذٰلِكَ فَلَيسَ مِنَ اللَّهِ فى شَىءٍ” is a very grave warning. Someone who chooses the wilāyah of those who stand against truth instead of remaining loyal to it effectively violates their bond with divine wilāyah. The label of faith, religious office, political power, and claims of devotion cannot justify this choice. Al-Anam 6:159 uses a similar expression of dissociation concerning those who divided their religion and became factions.

What, then, is the verse’s exception concerning taqiyyah?

“إِلّا أَن تَتَّقوا مِنهُم تُقىٰةً” derives from W Q Y: protecting, taking a shield, and guarding oneself from harm. Here the Qur’an makes an exception for a situation in which danger compels a person to take precautions for self-protection. This is the expression later known in religious discussions as “taqiyyah.”

Does the Qur’an describe concealing faith? Yes. Ghafir 40:28 speaks of a believing man from Pharaoh’s household who concealed his faith. But when Pharaoh intended to kill Moses, that man did not remain silent: he opposed the regime’s decision and defended Moses.

In Ghafir 40:38, he openly calls people to the right path; in Ghafir 40:42, he criticizes their call to association and disbelief; in Ghafir 40:44, he entrusts his affair to God; and in Ghafir 40:45, God protects him from his opponents’ schemes.

Thus, the Qur’an calls a man who concealed his faith a “believer,” but his conduct does not end in permanent concealment. When defending truth becomes necessary, he breaks his silence and speaks against Pharaoh’s power.

The conduct of Moses and Aaron is also instructive. In Ta-Ha 20:43, God commissions them to go to Pharaoh. In Ta-Ha 20:45, they express concern about the danger he poses, but God reassures them in Ta-Ha 20:46, and in Ta-Ha 20:47 they continue their mission of conveying the message and demanding the release of the Children of Israel. They were even commanded to speak gently to Pharaoh (Ta-Ha 20:44), rather than abandon the declaration of truth.

An-Nahl 16:106 distinguishes someone who is compelled while their heart remains at peace in faith from someone who freely turns to disbelief. An-Nisa 4:97, An-Nisa 4:98, and An-Nisa 4:99 also address migration from an oppressive environment and some people’s genuine inability to migrate.

These verses show that protecting one’s life against a real threat is not the same as freely choosing the wilāyah of falsehood. Taqiyyah here is an exception protecting a person in danger, not permission to conceal truth permanently, deceive others, or turn lying and expediency into a permanent religious method.

The verse ends with “وَيُحَذِّرُكُمُ اللَّهُ نَفسَهُ وَإِلَى اللَّهِ المَصيرُ”. God warns people of Himself and recalls that everyone’s ultimate return is to Him. Aal Imran 3:29 makes clear that God knows what people conceal or reveal within their breasts; Aal Imran 3:30 repeats this warning alongside a reminder of God’s kindness.

This warning also matters in religious life today. One of the most dangerous forms of wilāyah arises when, in the name of following religion, people set aside the authority of the Book of The God and entrust final judgment to human beings and their writings.

Al-Araf 7:3 commands following what the Lord has revealed, not other awliya instead of Him. At-Tawbah 9:31 condemns taking scholars and monks as lords alongside God. Ash-Shura 42:21 criticizes partners who prescribe religious rulings without God’s permission, while Yunus 10:59 questions attributing permission and prohibition to God without His authorization.

In later history, Israelite reports and other human sources entered Muslim interpretation and religious law. Sometimes external information filled the Qur’an’s silence about narrative details: the name of Adam’s wife, the name of the angel of death, the identity of Moses’ father-in-law, accounts about the Kaaba’s earlier history, and invented details about the Companions of the Cave. Sometimes rulings the Qur’an had not prescribed became binding in the name of Islamic law.

Clear examples include stoning and execution for apostasy: rulings with counterparts in the extant Torah that the Qur’an did not prescribe as penalties, but which later entered Islamic jurisprudential systems. Turning zakat into an instrument of religious taxation and political power, monopolizing religion within one community, and adding burdensome obligations are further examples examined in Forqan’s studies.

These are only examples of the danger of handing revelation’s authority over to human sources. They are examined in “When Qur’anic Silence Is Filled with Reports: Isra’iliyyat, Hadith, and the Inability to Say ‘We Do Not Know’”, “Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Rule Derived from Reports?”, and “Zakat and Its Historical Distortion”.

The central issue here is not which people or book a report comes from. It is why something God has not prescribed as a ruling in the Qur’an should be imposed on people as a divine command. The Qur’an is described as muhaymin over earlier scriptures; rulings from other books or later writings therefore cannot be made to govern it.

This danger connects directly with Aal Imran 3:23: a group is invited to the judgment of the Book of The God, yet turns away. If someone calls the Qur’an their book of guidance but, in disagreement, puts human sources’ rulings above its clear verses, they have effectively set aside its authority. The underlying principle is examined in “The Qur’an’s Completeness and Sufficiency for Religious Guidance”.

The message of Aal Imran 3:28 is therefore neither hostility toward other religions’ followers nor a prohibition on human relationships. The issue is loyalty to truth, preserving the independence of faith, and refusing a wilāyah that leads a person away from divine judgment toward denial, injustice, and following authorities opposed to truth.

The same Qur’an that prohibits such wilāyah also commands kindness, justice, and fidelity to covenants. People do not become believers by the name of their community or denomination, nor automatically become disbelievers by another community’s name. The criterion is the reality of faith, human conduct, and loyalty to guidance and justice.
