Introduction

Why do people turn to hadith—reports of what Muhammad is said to have spoken, done, or approved—when the Qur’an presents itself as divine guidance? Familiar questions quickly follow: Without those reports, how would anyone pray, calculate charitable contributions, or perform the pilgrimage? What would obedience to the Messenger mean? Would rejecting the religious authority of hadith also mean rejecting Muhammad, Muslim history, or the worship practiced by Muslim communities?

This study examines those questions individually. Its purpose is to establish what each kind of evidence can support. Revelation, a historical report, a legal scholar’s interpretation, evidence from Arabic usage, and a practice learned within a community are different kinds of evidence. Problems arise when those distinctions disappear and a human report becomes a binding command of God without the necessary argument.

An adequate response must address the strongest case for the authority of the Prophet’s teaching: commands to obey the Messenger, his responsibility to explain revelation, reliable oral transmission, and practices transmitted widely across generations. Saying that the Qur’an does not name a later hadith collection cannot settle those arguments. We must examine the steps by which a report becomes a religious obligation binding on people in every age, and identify any step that still requires evidence.

The practical questions discussed here have fuller treatments in Forqan’s individual studies. The answers below provide an argument and a route to that further reading. They connect those studies without replacing them.

How the evidence is assessed

Our starting point is to distinguish what the Qur’an expressly says, what a reader infers from its passages, and what people learn through history, communal instruction, or independent judgment. Anyone presenting a requirement as God’s command for everyone must establish the divine authorization for that claim (Yunus 10:59; Ash-Shura 42:21; An-Nahl 16:116). Historical investigation may establish that something happened. It does not, by itself, establish that the event created a permanent religious obligation.

Throughout this study, “explicit Qur’anic text” means a command or statement expressed in the text; “Qur’anic interpretation” means a conclusion reached by considering passages together; and “human practice or judgment” covers historical, educational, administrative, and experiential decisions. An interpretation is not automatically the wording of a verse. Equally, a useful human practice does not become invalid simply because the Qur’an does not specify every detail of it.

“Independent legislative authority” also needs a precise meaning. The dispute is not limited to whether anyone may legislate without God. A defender of the Prophet’s normative teaching may argue that God authorized binding rules conveyed outside the Qur’an. The question is then what establishes that authorization, which reports convey it reliably, and how their binding scope is determined. Calling a rule “prophetic” cannot itself answer those questions, but neither can merely observing that it is absent from the Qur’an.

The central argument

The Qur’an addresses prayer, charitable responsibility, fasting, and pilgrimage. It sets out their purposes, important limits, and many practical features. It does not present every disputed detail of later legal schools as an unchanging divine requirement. People can learn prayer through instruction, organize charitable giving, manage a pilgrimage with modern resources, and consult language and history without making every practical arrangement a further source of revelation.

The guiding question is therefore: Which parts of this practice are God’s command, which are human understanding or implementation, and what justifies making them compulsory for others? A tradition’s usefulness and familiarity matter, but they do not settle its legislative authority.

The Qur’an, the Messenger, and the authority of reports

1. Is the Qur’an incomplete without hadith? #

The completeness of guidance is different from an exhaustive record of technical procedures and historical facts. The Qur’an describes itself as an explanation of everything within its guiding purpose (An-Nahl 16:89), as a Scripture set out in detail (Al-An'am 6:114), and asks whether the revelation recited to its audience is not sufficient for them (Al-'Ankabut 29:51). Someone arguing that further binding legislation is indispensable must identify the obligation and establish the authority through which it becomes God’s requirement.

This argument should not rest on the word “complete” alone. The Qur’an asks whether God has authorized people’s prohibitions (Yunus 10:59) and challenges religious legislation made without His permission (Ash-Shura 42:21). The issue is who may fill an alleged gap with a command attributed to God. A historical explanation or a practical method may be valuable; usefulness alone does not supply legislative permission. Conversely, the claim that God delegated such authority must be examined on its evidence rather than dismissed merely because the resulting rule is outside the Qur’an.

2. If the Qur’an explains everything, why does it not list every unit of prayer or every business procedure? #

The scope of an explanation depends on its subject. A book of guidance, responsibility, and worship need not be an encyclopedia of medicine, engineering, or every future commercial arrangement. Clear principles can coexist with practical decisions left to human knowledge. The absence of a particular table proves a deficiency only after it has been shown that God required that table as an indispensable part of His guidance.

The statement that God has explained what He has forbidden (Al-An'am 6:119) should be considered here. Identifying religious prohibitions is different from recording every possible way of organizing society. People may choose tools, accounting methods, transport, and administrative procedures. That freedom does not authorize them to turn their choices into immutable words of God. The distinction preserves both the authority of revelation and the legitimate work of practical judgment.

3. Does “obey God and obey the Messenger” require us to obey every hadith? #

The Qur’an commands obedience to the Messenger. A later report about him, however, must first be shown to convey his words or actions accurately. The commands in An-Nur 24:54, Al-Ma'idah 5:92, and At-Taghabun 64:12 connect obedience with the Messenger’s responsibility to deliver the message clearly. Applying them to a particular report requires an argument about both its authenticity and the nature of the instruction it conveys. Repeating the verb “obey” does not authenticate a later collection.

The stronger objection is that the Messenger also issued spoken instructions during his lifetime. That possibility must be taken seriously. Three questions follow: Did he give this instruction? Does the surviving report reliably transmit it? Was it a permanent religious requirement or an instruction for particular circumstances? Passages such as An-Nisa 4:65 and Al-Ahzab 33:36 establish his authority to judge and command; they do not automatically answer all three questions about every subsequent report. His declared commitment to follow revelation also matters (Yunus 10:15; Al-An'am 6:50; Al-Ahqaf 46:9). The person claiming a binding rule today must establish how that rule follows from the authority described in those passages.

4. Does the Messenger’s duty to explain revelation make hadith a necessary second source? #

In An-Nahl 16:44, the Messenger is to make clear what has been sent down to people. Teaching and explaining revelation are real activities. But the existence of those activities does not automatically authenticate every later account of them or establish a second body of legislation. The Qur’an’s own explanatory character must also be considered (An-Nahl 16:89).

The object of the explanation in An-Nahl 16:44 is the revelation given to people. Other passages describe the Messenger’s responsibility as clear delivery (Al-Ma'idah 5:99; An-Nur 24:54; An-Nahl 16:82). These statements do not erase his teaching role. They do require an argument before explaining revelation is equated with producing additional permanent laws. If a particular oral explanation is said to establish such a law, its transmission, meaning, and continuing scope must be demonstrated. This applies even where someone accepts only selected reports rather than an entire collection.

5. What about “whatever the Messenger gives you, take it”? #

The full passage matters. In Al-Hashr 59:7, the immediate subject is the distribution of fay’ (property acquired from an opposing community without the usual battle division), with the stated aim of preventing wealth from circulating only among the rich. The Messenger’s directions concerning that property are authoritative. The passage does not, by itself, establish that every later report preserves an authentic, permanent command.

Even if the instruction to obey has a broader application than this distribution, a general principle of obedience cannot authenticate a particular historical account. Two arguments must remain distinct: that the Messenger should be obeyed, and that a specific reported rule is genuinely his and binding now. Generalizing the verse’s principle still leaves questions about the report’s origin, meaning, audience, and duration. The social purpose stated within the verse should not disappear while its final instruction is invoked in another debate.

6. Do “the Book and wisdom” mean two separate sources: the Qur’an and hadith? #

The use of two nouns does not by itself establish two separate collections of texts. After a series of moral and practical commands in Al-Isra 17:22, Al-Isra 17:23, Al-Isra 17:24, Al-Isra 17:25, Al-Isra 17:26, Al-Isra 17:27, Al-Isra 17:28, Al-Isra 17:29, Al-Isra 17:30, Al-Isra 17:31, Al-Isra 17:32, Al-Isra 17:33, Al-Isra 17:34, Al-Isra 17:35, Al-Isra 17:36, Al-Isra 17:37, Al-Isra 17:38, the Qur’an calls those teachings part of the wisdom revealed to the Messenger (Al-Isra 17:39). Wisdom can therefore describe the content of the revelation itself. The reference to God’s verses and wisdom recited in the households in Al-Ahzab 33:34 must also be read in its setting.

The teachings preceding Al-Isra 17:39 concern worship of God alone, parents, financial duties, honest measures, and human life. Their description as revealed wisdom prevents us from treating hikmah (wisdom or sound judgment) as an automatic technical name for later hadith collections. The Book and wisdom are also associated with being sent down in Al-Baqarah 2:231 and An-Nisa 4:113, while wisdom is given to Luqman in Luqman 31:12. Each passage deserves its own linguistic analysis. Someone arguing for a separate normative source needs more than the presence of the word “and”; the proposed identification must be established from the text and its context.

7. Does “he does not speak from desire” make everything Muhammad said revelation? #

The opening of chapter 53 concerns the Messenger’s reliability and the message he receives (An-Najm 53:3, An-Najm 53:4). Its wording and pronouns must be read within that discussion. It cannot simply be assumed that every domestic conversation, personal choice, or administrative decision was therefore permanent revealed legislation. The Qur’an itself questions or corrects decisions associated with the Prophet in At-Tahrim 66:1 and At-Tawbah 9:43.

The Messenger declares that he follows what is revealed to him (Yunus 10:15). The hypothetical warning against inventing statements in God’s name is exceptionally severe (Al-Haqqah 69:44, Al-Haqqah 69:45, Al-Haqqah 69:46, Al-Haqqah 69:47). Alongside it, At-Tahrim 66:1, At-Tawbah 9:43, and the episode in 'Abasa 80:1, 'Abasa 80:2, 'Abasa 80:3, 'Abasa 80:4, 'Abasa 80:5, 'Abasa 80:6, 'Abasa 80:7, 'Abasa 80:8, 'Abasa 80:9, 'Abasa 80:10 show human conduct being examined or corrected by revelation. These passages require us to distinguish a messenger’s human conduct from the revelation he is commissioned to communicate. They also prevent the opening of chapter 53 from becoming a blanket guarantee for every later narrative attributed to him. Its own context must remain part of the argument.

8. If the Messenger is an excellent example, must every personal habit of his be obligatory? #

The description of the Messenger as an excellent example (Al-Ahzab 33:21) gives real importance to his faithfulness, perseverance, responsibility, and conduct. It does not, without further reasoning, make every food, garment, cleaning implement, or means of travel he used compulsory for everyone thereafter. Following an example and assigning a fixed religious obligation are different claims.

An argument for a particular practice should establish whether the example concerns a moral purpose, an act of worship, or a circumstance of ordinary life, and whether it supports obligation, recommendation, or permission. Respecting the Messenger is not a reason to leave those distinctions unexplained.

9. Did the Messenger not issue judgments during his lifetime? #

He did. His roles in conveying revelation, teaching, judging, and implementing divine commands are recognized in the Qur’an (An-Nisa 4:65; Al-Ma'idah 5:48, Al-Ma'idah 5:49). The separate question is whether a surviving report establishes a particular judgment accurately and whether that judgment was intended as a permanent rule for all people. Establishing an event, understanding its circumstances, and establishing its continuing legal scope are distinct tasks.

In An-Nisa 4:65, the Messenger judges actual disputes. In Al-Ma'idah 5:48, Al-Ma'idah 5:49, he is instructed to judge by what God has revealed rather than follow people’s desires. His judicial role therefore cannot be dismissed; neither does it establish legislation without divine authorization. A decision concerning a specific dispute is not necessarily a universal rule of worship. A report of it must be assessed for its subject, parties, circumstances, and intended application before the decision is imposed on later generations.

10. Do Al-A'raf 7:157 and At-Tawbah 9:29 not attribute permission and prohibition to the Messenger? #

They do, and those statements must be addressed rather than omitted. The Messenger’s authority belongs to his commission from God. The same Qur’an challenges religious legislation without divine authorization (Ash-Shura 42:21; Yunus 10:59). Attributing prohibition to the Messenger does not automatically establish that every report bearing his name has legislative authority. The reported rule and its scope still need evidence.

The description in Al-A'raf 7:157 also speaks of lifting burdens and restraints. Other passages describe restrictions imposed on the Children of Israel (An-Nisa 4:160; Al-An'am 6:146) and the lifting of some earlier prohibitions (Al-Imran 3:50). Permission and prohibition must be considered within that history of revelation. The reference to what God and His Messenger have forbidden in At-Tawbah 9:29 need not imply two independently sovereign legislators. It does raise a genuine question about the Messenger’s authorized role—a question that should be answered directly while keeping the authenticity of any particular later report a separate matter.

11. Could the Prophet have received guidance outside the recited Qur’an? #

Passages such as At-Tahrim 66:3 and Al-Baqarah 2:143 require a distinction between receiving information about a particular situation and establishing a second body of legislation for all future generations. Even if guidance beyond the recited text is accepted in a particular case, neither the wording of a later hadith collection nor its universal authority follows automatically.

The earlier prayer direction is mentioned in Al-Baqarah 2:143; At-Tahrim 66:3 describes God informing the Prophet of something; and Al-Hashr 59:5 describes the cutting of trees as permitted by God. These are serious evidence to consider in discussing particular divine communications or permissions. But establishing such a communication would still not establish that later collections preserve it without error, or that an instruction for an event was a permanent law. The possibility of a divine communication and the demonstrated authority of a specific surviving report are different propositions.

12. Does God’s promise to preserve the Reminder guarantee the preservation of hadith? #

The promise in Al-Hijr 15:9 concerns the revealed Reminder, whose immediate context also appears in Al-Hijr 15:6. Extending it to a particular body of reports requires an explanation of why those reports fall within its subject. Simply calling additional material “remembrance” or “revelation” does not establish that inclusion.

The Qur’anic word dhikr (remembrance, reminder, or recalled message) has different uses; context identifies its reference. Anyone claiming that the promise guarantees a defined set of hadith must identify that set and explain its relationship to the promise, including differences in wording and scholarly judgments of authenticity. Disagreement does not by itself show that every report is false. It does prevent the guarantee from being assumed without argument. The question remains even when a defender claims preservation only of authentic teachings rather than of every report ever circulated.

13. If a hadith is classified as authentic, why not treat it as the Prophet’s certain words? #

Sahih (classified as authentic under hadith scholarship’s criteria) is a scholarly assessment, not a divine declaration that human investigation cannot err. The classification does not, on its own, determine whether a report concerns a permanent command. Attribution, meaning, circumstances, and the relationship to clear Qur’anic passages must each be examined.

The historical tradition of hadith criticism itself includes scrutiny of reports in the collections of Bukhari and Muslim. Jonathan Brown’s study of al-Daraqutni documents such criticism. This does not make historical knowledge impossible. It shows why “judged authentic,” “certainly spoken,” and “divinely binding on everyone” should not be treated as interchangeable descriptions. Where a claim affects someone’s life, property, or freedom, a collection’s reputation cannot remove the need to justify the particular judgment being imposed.

14. If people transmitted both the Qur’an and hadith, why give them different weight? #

The involvement of human transmitters does not make all forms of transmission equivalent. Public recitation, memorization, writing, and continued communal use differ from the transmission of an individual report through disputed chains. Historical claims should be assessed by the nature and independence of their supporting evidence, rather than by the shared fact that people conveyed them.

Early Qur’anic manuscripts provide material evidence, and research into shared spelling features supports an early common written exemplar. The parchment of the Birmingham fragments was dated to 568–645 CE with 95.4 percent probability. That is a date for the animal material, not a direct measurement of when ink was put on it. Early note-taking is also relevant to hadith history. Evidence for the age of some reports, however, does not establish the collective, word-for-word transmission of every later collection. The comparison must be made at the level of the specific evidence and claim.

15. Does widespread transmission settle the authenticity of a hadith? #

Tawatur (transmission through numerous independent channels) is a claim that can be investigated. Naming it does not make every report widely and independently transmitted. We must ask which account is being discussed, through what routes it passed, and how much wording or meaning remained common. Even a firmly established historical practice still requires interpretation before all its details are treated as permanent divine requirements.

Transmission of exact wording, shared meaning, and communal practice are different matters. At what generation does the evidence begin? Which channels are genuinely independent? What is their common content? Later popularity does not prove extensive transmission from the Messenger’s lifetime. In prayer and pilgrimage especially, establishing that an action was taught and practiced does not automatically determine which features were obligatory, recommended, or circumstantial. Both the historical and normative questions deserve an answer.

16. Is living religious practice simply another form of hadith? #

A child can learn prayer by watching a family or congregation without first consulting a written report. Learning an established communal practice is different from authenticating every report in a collection, and different again from declaring every detail of the practice compulsory. These forms of evidence may inform one another, but they are not interchangeable.

A living practice can teach someone how to perform an act. Whether each feature of that performance is God’s binding requirement remains a further question. Preserving a familiar method does not require presenting every disagreement with it as disobedience to revelation.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

17. Were reports not written down by the earliest Muslim generations? #

Claims about early writing should be evaluated historically. The absence of a surviving manuscript does not prove that nobody wrote anything. Conversely, reports about a few early notebooks do not authenticate an entire later collection or establish its religious authority. The question is which identifiable material supports which particular claim.

Studies by Gregor Schoeler and Harald Motzki help investigate how written notes and oral transmission could coexist. The assertion that none of the Prophet’s companions ever wrote reports is therefore both unnecessary to this argument and insufficiently supported. The more useful inquiry concerns the evidence for a divinely mandated body of additional legislation, its intended preservation, and its boundaries. What instruction establishes its binding role for later generations, and what surviving text or transmission network supports the specific command now being attributed to it?

Further reading: Refuting the Claim That the Transmitters of the Qur’an and Hadith Are Equal

18. How should we treat reports forbidding the writing of anything besides the Qur’an? #

Reports of a prohibition on writing and reports permitting writing are both historical evidence to investigate. In a debate, they may reveal questions within the reporting tradition itself. They should not be used by accepting whichever report supports one’s position as certainly prophetic while dismissing the other without examination. The argument for the Qur’an’s authority need not depend on proving one contested report.

There is no need to establish that the Messenger permanently prohibited recording everything he said. Even if recording was permitted, permission to preserve information does not itself make every preserved statement a universal religious law. Equally, a report restricting writing needs its circumstances and scope examined. Neither side can bypass questions of authenticity and meaning simply because a report appears useful to its case.

Further reading: Refuting the Claim That the Transmitters of the Qur’an and Hadith Are Equal

19. Does criticism of conjecture make testimony, probability, and human information worthless? #

No. Human decisions often depend on evidence and degrees of confidence. The Qur’an discusses testimony and the investigation of reports (Al-Baqarah 2:282; Al-Hujurat 49:6). Reaching a practical judgment on available evidence is different from claiming that God certainly issued a permanent command. In the context of invented prohibitions, the Qur’an demands knowledge and challenges unsupported conjecture (Al-An'am 6:148).

Even the word zann (an assessment or belief whose force depends on context) does not always mean a weak guess: Al-Baqarah 2:46 and Al-Haqqah 69:20 use it in contexts of serious conviction. Thus the argument should not condemn every probabilistic judgment. Its target is the unsupported promotion of a human conclusion into a preserved divine statement. Evidence may justify action without making our conclusion infallible. The basis and limits of the claimed authority must be stated honestly.

20. Would one report contradicting the Qur’an make an entire collection false? #

No. One genuine contradiction would defeat a claim that every item in a collection is free from error or conflict with revelation. It would not show that every other report is false. Rejecting a blanket guarantee is different from assessing the historical value of each entry.

The Qur’an’s invitation to consider its coherence in An-Nisa 4:82 refers to the absence of much discrepancy. It does not say that a single error in any book makes every sentence in that book untrue. A real contradiction with a clear Qur’anic statement is relevant to the authority claimed for the particular report and to any claim of flawless authority for the collection. A defender who accepts only selected reports still deserves an answer at that level: why is this report authentic, what does it mean, and what authorizes its use as a binding rule? The broader critique cannot replace that examination.

Further reading: Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Rule Derived from Reports?

21. If a report repeats a Qur’anic truth, does that make the report revelation? #

A report may correctly affirm that God is one. The truth of that statement does not transfer the status of revelation to the report or the collection containing it. Correct content, historical authenticity, divine origin, and legislative authority are separate claims. Agreement with the Qur’an may support a statement’s acceptability, but it does not establish who originally said it or confer additional authority on everything transmitted alongside it.

22. Does this approach reject dictionaries, historical reports, and early Arabic poetry too? #

No. Arabic has a history, and evidence about its usage can help readers understand the Qur’an. A historical report may illuminate a possible setting or meaning without becoming a source of additional divine law. Using evidence to understand a text is different from assigning that evidence a right to legislate.

For example, investigating an ancient livestock term or the word families behind “wholesome” and “corrupt” may involve poetry, related Semitic languages, and dictionaries. The result must still be tested against the Qur’an’s actual usage. Such evidence supports a linguistic conclusion; it does not authorize a new punishment in Muhammad’s name. Linguistic judgments can affect interpretation, so they too require scrutiny, but their function should remain explicit. A source of information is not automatically a source of binding religious commands.

Further reading: Al-Mubin (clear and clarifying): the Qur’an and the Messenger’s delivery

23. Does “ask those who know” make religious scholars final authorities? #

Consulting informed people is different from giving them legislative power. The passages in An-Nahl 16:43 and Al-Anbiya 21:7 concern the human character of earlier messengers. They support seeking relevant knowledge; they do not make a scholar’s opinion immune from examination. An expert should explain the evidence and remain answerable to the text and to reasoned criticism. Respect for learning need not become a transfer of God’s authority to the learned.

Further reading: Imamah in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Example and the Question of Leadership

24. If the Qur’an is silent about a difficult question, must hadith supply the missing answer? #

Not necessarily. The detail may be irrelevant to a passage’s purpose, accessible through experience, or still unknown to us. Curiosity does not establish that a definite divine answer has been supplied elsewhere. An answer’s availability in a report also does not prove its authenticity.

Forqan’s studies of stories added to the Qur’an and of its opening letter sequences explore this boundary. Acknowledging what we do not know may be more faithful to the text than providing a confident but unsupported explanation. Silence is not, by itself, evidence either of deficiency or of an obligation to adopt a particular external account.

Further reading: When Qur’anic Silence Is Filled with Reports: Isra’iliyyat, Hadith, and the Inability to Say “We Do Not Know”

25. Can a hadith cancel a command that remains in the Qur’an? #

Naskh (a term used in later legal theory for the abrogation or supersession of a rule) requires careful examination in each relevant passage. Calling a report “unrecited revelation” neither proves its attribution nor establishes its power to suspend a Qur’anic command. Anyone claiming that a command no longer applies must provide the argument for that conclusion.

Passages such as Al-Baqarah 2:106 and An-Nahl 16:101 are central to the debate, but their terms and contexts must first be examined. What is being replaced, forgotten, or superseded, and what text identifies the change? A claim that a still-recited command has been canceled by a disputed report assigns considerable authority to that report. The report cannot simply supply its own contested authorization. The debate about stoning makes this problem particularly concrete; the detailed arguments belong in the dedicated studies of abrogation and punishment.

Further reading: Naskh (abrogation): examining claims that Qur’anic commands have been superseded

26. Do difficult passages and unexplained letter sequences prove that hadith is indispensable? #

The existence of passages requiring interpretation (Al-Imran 3:7), or opening letter sequences whose meaning is disputed, does not establish that a hadith collection supplies their certain explanation. Nor does the reporting tradition provide an uncontested prophetic explanation of every such sequence. A specific claim to solve a difficulty needs its own evidence.

Guidance can rest on clear teachings while readers acknowledge the limits of their knowledge elsewhere. Uncertainty should prompt inquiry. It does not authorize inventing an answer or treating a disputed explanation as certain merely because it has been transmitted.

Prayer, purification, and direction

27. Without hadith, how would anyone learn to pray? #

The Qur’an describes prayer as an actual, timed practice. It discusses purification, standing, bowing, prostration, recitation, remembrance, and collective worship (Al-Ma'idah 5:6; An-Nisa 4:102; Al-Baqarah 2:238, Al-Baqarah 2:239; Al-Jumu'ah 62:9, Al-Jumu'ah 62:10). People can also learn how to perform prayer through family and community instruction. Learning from others does not automatically make every detail of their legal school a divine obligation. Neither dismissing embodied prayer nor confining it to unsupported rules follows from the Qur’an’s authority.

The practical guidance includes preparation and cleanliness (Al-Ma'idah 5:6), facing the prayer direction (Al-Baqarah 2:144), standing attentively (Al-Baqarah 2:238), bowing and prostrating (Al-Hajj 22:77), recitation and remembrance (Al-Muzzammil 73:20; Ta-Ha 20:14), appointed times (An-Nisa 4:103), and accommodation of danger (Al-Baqarah 2:239; An-Nisa 4:102). A person can put these into practice through a familiar form of communal prayer. The additional claim requiring proof is that every difference in hand placement, recitation, concluding testimony, or number of units necessarily invalidates the prayer before God. Salat (the Qur’anic term whose uses include ritual prayer) is examined at greater length in the linked study.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

28. Where does the Qur’an prescribe two, four, four, three, and four units for the five daily prayers? #

It does not present that numerical table explicitly. These counts are familiar through the taught and repeated practice of Muslim communities. A rak‘ah (one unit in a sequence of standing, bowing, and prostration) can be learned practically. Following the familiar sequence is different from claiming that the Qur’an expressly declares every departure from it invalid.

Present-day familiarity does not itself establish an uninterrupted, undisputed history for every detail. Nevertheless, a person can perform the familiar prayer without first resolving every historical question. The issue is the evidence required to make each number a divine condition for rejecting another person’s worship. The Qur’an’s references to standing, bowing, prostration, recitation, purification, direction, and timing give prayer real structure. The absence of a numerical table does not erase that structure, just as recognizing the structure does not supply the missing table by inference alone.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

29. How can prayer times be known without a numbered timetable in the Qur’an? #

The Qur’an says that prayer is prescribed at appointed times (An-Nisa 4:103) and refers to periods of day and night in passages such as Hud 11:114, Al-Isra 17:78, and An-Nur 24:58. Those passages should be considered together. The dedicated study of prayer examines their relationship. One may reason from them and follow a community’s established schedule without first assuming that a particular school’s timetable is the literal wording of the Qur’an.

The instruction to glorify God before sunrise, before sunset, and during parts of the night also belongs in this discussion (Ta-Ha 20:130). Equating every instruction to glorify God with a particular obligatory ritual prayer, however, requires argument. Reading the passages together is more careful than extracting an entire timetable from one isolated phrase. Using observation, calendars, and clocks to implement a time-related command is an application of knowledge; it is not the creation of additional revelation.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

30. How would people know how to wash or perform dry purification before prayer? #

The Qur’an gives substantial instructions in Al-Ma'idah 5:6 and 4:43: washing, wiping, purification after sexual impurity, and the alternative using clean earth when the stated conditions apply. Wudu (the washing and wiping associated with preparation for prayer) and tayammum (purification using clean earth in the circumstances specified) therefore have a textual basis. Disputes about details must be examined through the wording and grammar of the passages.

In Al-Ma'idah 5:6, the instructions address the face, arms to the elbows, wiping the head, and the feet to the ankles, alongside purification after sexual impurity and the alternative involving clean earth. The grammatical treatment of the feet is part of the interpretive dispute; it should not be settled by pretending no dispute exists. Equally, disagreement does not mean the Qur’an provides no guidance. A hygienic preference or an additional practical arrangement should be identified as such rather than quietly made an independent divine condition.

31. How can the prayer direction be known? Is it merely symbolic? #

The commands to face the Sacred Mosque in Al-Baqarah 2:144 and Al-Baqarah 2:149, Al-Baqarah 2:150 give qiblah (the direction faced in prayer) a concrete geographical dimension. Its spiritual significance does not eliminate that direction. Forqan’s study of direction and orientation considers how bodily orientation relates to commitment and conduct; one should not be used to erase the other.

Identifying the mosque’s location and finding its direction from a particular place require geography and shared knowledge. Maps and direction-finding tools do not constitute a second revelation. They show how a command can establish a requirement while people use knowledge of the world to carry it out. The same distinction prevents practical geographical information from becoming an independent source of religious law.

32. What about the opening chapter, testimony, greetings, and hand placement in prayer? #

The Qur’an discusses recitation, remembrance, attentive speech, and humility. It does not supply one complete list of every formula and posture required by all legal schools. A person may follow a familiar method. The disputed step is making one school’s detailed arrangement the certain measure of every other person’s prayer without sufficient evidence.

The recitation of the opening chapter behind a prayer leader is one example: some approaches require it from the participant in each unit, while others prioritize listening in the relevant congregational setting. Passages about listening to recitation (Al-A'raf 7:204), reciting what is manageable (Al-Muzzammil 73:20), and conscious remembrance should inform the discussion. The same care applies to tashahhud (a seated testimony recited in prayer) and the closing greeting. “We have always done this” does not by itself establish that a different arrangement is rejected by God. The study of prayer treats these differences in greater detail.

33. How could the words of the call to prayer be determined without hadith? #

The Qur’an recognizes a call to prayer and to communal worship (Al-Ma'idah 5:58; Al-Jumu'ah 62:9). These passages do not give the complete wording of the familiar adhan (the public call announcing prayer) as an exclusive verbal formula. A community may learn and preserve an established form of announcement. Declaring every verbal variation sinful requires an additional argument.

A call has a practical purpose: people need to know when and where to gather. Preserving a recognizable form can serve that purpose. The Qur’anic recognition of the call, however, should be distinguished from a claim that only one exact wording is divinely valid. Differences between communal traditions should be assessed without assuming that one version is necessarily a heavenly text and all alternatives offenses. This answer draws an inference from the passages about the call; it does not replace a separate historical study of its development.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

34. Are Friday and congregational prayer known only through hadith? #

No. The Qur’an describes the call on Friday, leaving trade, and gathering for the remembrance of God (Al-Jumu'ah 62:9, Al-Jumu'ah 62:10). It also gives a practical account of collective prayer in danger (An-Nisa 4:102). These are direct evidence for communal worship. Questions about the precise order of a sermon or other arrangements should be distinguished from the underlying command and practice. The presence of disputes about implementation does not make the Qur’anic basis disappear.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

35. How should people pray while traveling, ill, or in danger? #

The Qur’an addresses travel, fear, and maintaining prayer under difficult circumstances (Al-Baqarah 2:239; An-Nisa 4:101, An-Nisa 4:102, An-Nisa 4:103). Worship is not detached from human circumstances and ability. Where the text gives a requirement, it should guide practice; where it does not specify every adjustment, people must exercise judgment in the conditions they actually face.

The account in An-Nisa 4:102 describes groups taking turns and precautions during danger. In Al-Baqarah 2:239, worship in fear is accommodated on foot or while riding. These instructions show that maintaining worship need not depend on an impossible outward form. Applying that principle to an ill person’s abilities requires reasoned judgment; it should be identified as application, rather than presented as an additional verse. Modern aids and practical adjustments do not become a new religion simply because the text does not name each one.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

36. Did prayer begin with Muhammad? #

No. The Qur’an associates prayer with Abraham (Ibrahim 14:40), Ishmael (Maryam 19:55), Jesus (Maryam 19:31), and Moses and his people (Yunus 10:87). The practice therefore has a history within the Qur’anic account before Muhammad’s mission. That does not prove that every detail was identical for all prophets. It shows continuity in worship while leaving particular forms and changes to be examined on their own evidence. Muhammad continued that path, and the Qur’an gives directions concerning it where its passages do so.

Further reading: Salat (prayer): meaning and practice in the Qur’an

37. Does this approach reduce prayer to a private wish and abandon its physical practice? #

No. Standing, bowing, prostration, purification, and the ordering of prayer are treated as real acts. The Qur’an also emphasizes remembrance of God (Ta-Ha 20:14) and restraint from shameful and wrongful conduct (Al-'Ankabut 29:45). Its spiritual and ethical purposes do not make bodily worship unreal. Conversely, completing bodily movements without attention to those purposes does not exhaust what establishing prayer involves. The fuller study examines the different uses of Salat while preserving the reality of ritual prayer.

Giving, financial responsibility, and fasting

38. Without hadith, can people understand and fulfill zakat? #

Zakat (a Qur’anic term connecting purification and growth with religious responsibility, including material giving) repeatedly appears alongside prayer. The Qur’an links giving with purification, responsibility toward others, and the claims of those in need (Al-Baqarah 2:43; At-Tawbah 9:103; Adh-Dhariyat 51:19). People can assess their resources and organize continuing support. The absence of a comprehensive table of later legal thresholds does not mean the purpose and duty are absent.

In At-Tawbah 9:103, the Messenger is instructed to receive a contribution from people’s wealth through which they are purified. This gives the discussion both a material and a spiritual dimension. Zakat should not be reduced to an inward feeling that leaves the needs of others untouched. Nor should the entire field of purification, charitable giving, and financial responsibility be reduced to a single percentage. Forqan’s dedicated study examines both reductions and the relationship between the relevant terms.

Further reading: Zakat (purification and material responsibility) and its historical distortion

39. Does the Qur’an establish a universal rate of 2.5 percent? #

The Qur’an does not state 2.5 percent as a universal and permanent rate for zakat. Such rates and their conditions belong to developed legal and reporting traditions. A rate may be used in a practical system of calculation, but its familiarity does not make it an explicit Qur’anic statement or exhaust a person’s responsibility toward those in need.

If a society sets a figure for administration or accountability, it should explain its legal and practical basis. An operational threshold for a fund is a different claim from an amount prescribed by God for all people in all circumstances. Economic conditions change, and needs must be assessed. The discussion of 2.5 percent is one example of the broader question: what evidence establishes the figure’s religious status, and how does an organized system actually fulfill the rights it is intended to serve?

Further reading: Zakat (purification and material responsibility) and its historical distortion

40. Without fixed rates, would giving become entirely optional? #

Discretion over implementation is not permission to disregard other people’s rights. The Qur’an addresses appropriate giving, the claims of those in need, and the concentration of wealth (Al-Baqarah 2:267; Adh-Dhariyat 51:19; Al-Hashr 59:7). Systems of calculation, public standards, and accountable administration can help implement those responsibilities. Their decisions should be distinguished from the direct wording of a divine command.

The workers who administer contributions are mentioned in At-Tawbah 9:60, while At-Tawbah 9:103 contains an instruction to receive contributions. Collective organization therefore has textual support. However, Al-Hashr 59:7 concerns a particular category of distributed property; using its concern about concentrated wealth as a wider ethical principle is different from extracting a zakat rate from it. That distinction protects both meaningful administration and honest attribution. A proposed tax or fund should not gain unchallengeable religious authority simply because a relevant verse is cited alongside it.

Further reading: Zakat (purification and material responsibility) and its historical distortion

41. Does the Qur’an identify recipients of charitable contributions? #

Yes. The recipients in At-Tawbah 9:60 include people in need, people burdened by debt, travelers, and those administering the contributions. Another passage addresses giving to parents, relatives, orphans, people in need, and travelers (Al-Baqarah 2:215). It is therefore inaccurate to say that the destination of financial assistance is left entirely to reports outside the Qur’an.

The wording in At-Tawbah 9:60 is sadaqat (charitable contributions), not the word zakat. Their relationship should be explained rather than assumed. The eight categories concern the poor, the needy, administrators, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, release from bondage, debtors, God’s cause, and the stranded traveler. Infaq (spending or giving from one’s resources) is another related term. Their uses and contexts help establish their relationship, but the distinctions should remain visible when quoting the text or drawing a practical conclusion.

Further reading: Zakat (purification and material responsibility) and its historical distortion · At-Tawbah 9:60

42. Must a government or religious class be the compulsory intermediary for giving? #

The reference to administrators in At-Tawbah 9:60 recognizes organized management. It does not itself establish a ruler’s personal ownership of contributions or a right to exploit them politically. Entrusted administration for the benefit of recipients differs from using religious funds to consolidate power or condemn dissenters.

The instruction to receive contributions in At-Tawbah 9:103 should be considered alongside At-Tawbah 9:60. Together they give a basis for collection and administration. Neither passage, by itself, gives every future government or professional religious class an unrestricted and unaccountable monopoly. An institution can be transparent, audited, and responsive to social needs. Its legitimacy must not be confused with a claim that its decisions are beyond criticism because it administers a religious obligation.

Further reading: Zakat (purification and material responsibility) and its historical distortion

43. How would people know about Ramadan fasting without hadith? #

The Qur’an prescribes fasting, names Ramadan, addresses illness and travel, and sets out eating, drinking, and the period of abstention (Al-Baqarah 2:183, Al-Baqarah 2:184, Al-Baqarah 2:185, Al-Baqarah 2:186, Al-Baqarah 2:187). In Al-Baqarah 2:187, the beginning is connected with the discernible dawn and the completion with night. The purpose of cultivating taqwa (attentive responsibility and guarding oneself against wrongdoing before God) is also stated. Fasting therefore has a substantial Qur’anic foundation; its existence and basic boundaries do not first appear in later collections.

Further reading: Taqwa (attentive responsibility before God) · Al-Baqarah 2:187

44. What is the difference between breaking a fast with dates as a custom, a recommendation, or a requirement? #

These are distinct claims. In Al-Baqarah 2:187, the Qur’an states the period of eating and drinking and the completion of the fast at night. It does not make dates a condition for a valid meal ending the fast, or prescribe a particular food as compulsory before dawn. A food can be familiar and suitable without being a universal divine requirement.

If a report is cited to establish a special religious merit for a particular food or practice, its authenticity and meaning should be examined at that level. Evidence for a recommendation should not silently become evidence that alternatives are sinful or invalidate the fast. This distinction avoids attributing a stronger claim to a tradition than its defender actually makes, while preserving the demand for evidence when a religious status is asserted.

Pilgrimage and its practices

45. How could anyone perform the pilgrimage without hadith? #

Hajj (the pilgrimage to the Sacred House) has concrete Qur’anic features: the House, Abraham’s invitation, ability to undertake the journey, timing, walking around the House, Safa and Marwah, remembrance, offerings, and restrictions during the pilgrimage state (Al-Imran 3:97; Al-Baqarah 2:125; Al-Baqarah 2:158; Al-Baqarah 2:196, Al-Baqarah 2:197, Al-Baqarah 2:198, Al-Baqarah 2:199, Al-Baqarah 2:200, Al-Baqarah 2:201, Al-Baqarah 2:202, Al-Baqarah 2:203; Al-Hajj 22:27, Al-Hajj 22:28, Al-Hajj 22:29, Al-Hajj 22:30, Al-Hajj 22:31, Al-Hajj 22:32, Al-Hajj 22:33, Al-Hajj 22:34, Al-Hajj 22:35, Al-Hajj 22:36, Al-Hajj 22:37). It is not merely a spiritual slogan. But neither does the existence of these features establish every movement and number used by a modern pilgrimage group as a divine condition.

Consider the elements together: Abraham’s commission (Al-Hajj 22:26, Al-Hajj 22:27), the House and movement around it (Al-Baqarah 2:125; Al-Hajj 22:29), Safa and Marwah (Al-Baqarah 2:158), restrictions during pilgrimage (Al-Baqarah 2:197; Al-Ma'idah 5:95, Al-Ma'idah 5:96), and remembrance and feeding people (Al-Hajj 22:28; Al-Hajj 22:36, Al-Hajj 22:37). Routes, crowd management, and practical instruction can be learned from experienced people. Those arrangements serve the pilgrimage without automatically becoming further conditions imposed by God for its acceptance. The dedicated study examines the distinction between the rite, its implementation, and people’s rights.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

46. How does the Qur’an establish who is able to undertake the pilgrimage? #

The requirement in Al-Imran 3:97 applies to those able to find a way to it. Ability involves more than an amount of money: a practicable route, security, bodily capacity, and real responsibilities affect whether a journey can be undertaken. The condition should not be erased by demanding a journey at the cost of severe harm or a family’s destitution.

The reference to a way in the verse concerns actual access and capacity. Transport, medical advice, and security information can help determine whether the condition is met. Their use is practical assessment rather than a new revelation. This reading also does not make ability an excuse for indifference: the person who has a safe route and sufficient capacity still faces the command. The obligation and its qualifying condition must both remain part of the account.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

47. How is the pilgrimage season known without hadith? #

The Qur’an refers to known months for pilgrimage (Al-Baqarah 2:197) and identifies lunar phases as markers of time for people and pilgrimage (Al-Baqarah 2:189). Timing therefore belongs to the text. Those passages do not list every day in a modern group’s itinerary, and a distinction is needed between the religious calendar and administrative travel decisions.

“Known months” indicates that the audience already recognized a calendar; it does not imply that timing is irrelevant. Historical and communal knowledge are relevant to identifying that calendar, while observation and calculation help determine current lunar dates. These are not all the same task. Using them does not automatically turn every timetable into revelation. Equally, referring only to modern travel arrangements would leave the historical question about the known season unanswered; that question belongs in the fuller study of pilgrimage.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

48. What about the two cloths and the places where pilgrims enter the restricted state? #

The Qur’an recognizes ihram (the consecrated state in which pilgrimage restrictions apply), including the prohibition of land hunting in that state (Al-Ma'idah 5:95, Al-Ma'idah 5:96), and prohibits sexual conduct, wrongdoing, and disputing during pilgrimage (Al-Baqarah 2:197). These passages do not themselves specify two white cloths or list the later legal entry points as the only valid arrangement.

An established form of dress and recognized entry points can provide order and continuity. The further question is what establishes each detail as a divine condition of acceptance, including its application to different bodies and circumstances. A miqat (a designated point or boundary for entering the pilgrimage state) may be known through a legal and historical tradition; that tradition’s normative claims need to be examined explicitly. Respect for a practical custom is not the same as proving every feature directly from revelation.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

49. Does the Qur’an prescribe seven circuits around the House? #

Tawaf (walking around the Sacred House as a pilgrimage rite) is expressly mentioned in Al-Baqarah 2:125, Al-Hajj 22:26, and Al-Hajj 22:29. Those passages do not explicitly state seven circuits or every detail of where to begin and how to move. Following an established seven-circuit practice and declaring all alternatives invalid before God are different claims.

Even if seven circuits are firmly established in familiar practice, the number cannot be quoted as the wording of Al-Hajj 22:29. At the same time, the absence of a number does not establish that any amount is necessarily sufficient, or that the known form should be discarded. A judgment of divine invalidity requires evidence about the rite’s conditions. That is the recurring distinction between performing a practice one has learned and assigning every detail an obligatory status that excludes all other forms.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

50. What does the Qur’an say about movement between Safa and Marwah? #

The two places are identified as signs or rites of God in Al-Baqarah 2:158. The verse therefore gives them a Qur’anic place in pilgrimage. The statement that there is no blame in going between them, however, needs analysis before every later rule is read into it. Sa‘y (the pilgrimage movement between Safa and Marwah) and its number of traversals should be examined within the whole account of pilgrimage.

The wording removes blame from the act. On its own, that does not establish every later condition as obligatory; nor does it settle that no obligation could be involved. The designation of the sites as God’s rites must also be considered. How the expression “no blame” functions elsewhere, the passage’s context, and the rite’s place within pilgrimage all matter. A conclusion requires more than translating one phrase and stopping there.

51. Where does the Qur’an require throwing a specified number of pebbles at the pillars? #

The pilgrimage passages do not expressly command throwing specified numbers of pebbles at three designated places. Nor does the Qur’anic account of Abraham’s trial present that action as a ceremony to repeat (As-Saffat 37:102, As-Saffat 37:103, As-Saffat 37:104, As-Saffat 37:105, As-Saffat 37:106, As-Saffat 37:107). A practice may carry historical and symbolic meaning without that meaning alone establishing a divine obligation, a penalty for omission, or invalidity of the pilgrimage.

If reports are offered as evidence for rami (the pilgrimage practice of throwing pebbles at designated sites), their precise claims matter. Do they describe a practice or convey a general instruction? What establishes their attribution and binding scope? A historical description, even if reliable, does not necessarily establish a condition of validity or a penalty. Meanwhile, the Qur’an’s explicit demands concerning remembrance, restraint from wrongdoing and conflict, and the treatment of people should not be displaced by an exclusive focus on disputed details.

52. Does sacrifice during pilgrimage come only from hadith? #

No. Offerings, remembrance of God’s name, and feeding people appear in Al-Baqarah 2:196, Al-Hajj 22:28, Al-Hajj 22:34, and Al-Hajj 22:36. Hady (an offering associated with pilgrimage) therefore has a textual basis. The statement in Al-Hajj 22:37 that neither flesh nor blood reaches God directs attention to the worshipper’s responsibility before Him. It does not make the material act unreal. Conversely, the conditions described for an offering should not be generalized without examination to every pilgrim in every situation. Both the practical rite and its stated purpose deserve attention.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

53. Must pilgrims use only fixed reported formulas for remembrance and prayer? #

The Qur’an emphasizes remembrance during pilgrimage (Al-Hajj 22:28; Al-Baqarah 2:198; Al-Baqarah 2:203) and gives an example of asking for good in this life and the next (Al-Baqarah 2:201). These passages do not prescribe the familiar talbiyah (the pilgrim’s spoken response to God’s call) or a separate fixed prayer for each circuit or pebble as the sole acceptable wording.

Pilgrims can use established, appropriate forms of remembrance and join a group’s recitation. The further claim is that every person must repeat precisely the same words, number, and language at a particular point or have their worship rejected. That moves from an agreed arrangement to a divine requirement. Evidence for the formula’s status and scope is needed at that point. Familiarity can support coordination; it cannot by itself prove exclusive validity.

Further reading: Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

54. What about Abraham’s station and prayer after walking around the House? #

The instruction to take Abraham’s station as a place of prayer appears in Al-Baqarah 2:125. The relationship between that location and worship therefore has a Qur’anic basis. The instruction does not, by itself, specify exactly two units immediately after every circuit sequence with all the conditions developed by legal schools.

Recognizing the place as Qur’anic does not require treating it as merely a later story, and distinguishing the later details does not require abandoning the familiar practice. A person can perform that practice while identifying accurately what the verse expressly commands and what is known through historical instruction or legal interpretation. Respect for the original command and scrutiny of additional requirements belong together.

Prohibitions, punishment, belief, and the unseen

55. How can permitted and forbidden food be identified without hadith? #

The Qur’an states food prohibitions in Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Ma'idah 5:3, Al-An'am 6:145, and An-Nahl 16:115. It also discusses hunting, slaughter-related conditions, mentioning God’s name, and the food of the People of the Book (Al-Ma'idah 5:4, Al-Ma'idah 5:5; Al-Ma'idah 5:96; Al-An'am 6:118, Al-An'am 6:119, Al-An'am 6:120, Al-An'am 6:121). These passages should be read together. A cultural preference or a jurist’s judgment does not automatically create another prohibition in God’s name. Halal (permitted) and haram (forbidden) concern religious status, which should not be confused with personal taste.

The reference to forbidding corrupt or foul things in Al-A'raf 7:157 must be considered alongside Yunus 10:59 and An-Nahl 16:116. Khabith (foul, corrupt, or bad in the relevant context) is not restricted to food in Qur’anic usage; its word family also appears in descriptions of property, land, conduct, and speech. A culturally disliked animal cannot simply be assigned to that category without an argument. Science can identify contamination or spoilage, but a finding about a food’s condition is different from declaring a species permanently forbidden to everyone. The distinction between halal and tayyib (good or wholesome in context) is examined in the dedicated study.

56. If the Qur’an does not list dogs or donkeys as forbidden food, must people eat them? #

No. A claim that something is not forbidden is not a command to consume it. People may avoid a food because of taste, health, culture, or circumstances without attributing a universal prohibition to God. A claim of religious prohibition must be assessed against all the relevant passages and conditions, not merely the absence or presence of an animal’s name.

The descriptions in An-Nahl 16:5, An-Nahl 16:6, An-Nahl 16:7, An-Nahl 16:8 mention eating from livestock and using horses, mules, and donkeys for transport and adornment. Naming one benefit does not necessarily exclude every other benefit: “you ride these” is not identical to “you must never eat these.” Equally, the absence of a species from a list does not remove conditions concerning how food is obtained and its fitness for consumption. The question is what establishes a divine prohibition, rather than whether a reader personally wishes to eat a particular animal.

57. What does the Qur’an say about mentioning God’s name over food and hunted animals? #

The issue is present in the text. The passages address eating what God’s name has been mentioned over (Al-An'am 6:118), avoiding what His name has not been mentioned over (Al-An'am 6:121), hunting with trained animals and mentioning His name (Al-Ma'idah 5:4), and offerings dedicated to someone other than God (Al-An'am 6:145). Although the verb “slaughter” does not occur in Al-An'am 6:118, the context cannot be ignored. Neither uncertainty about an industrial process nor a later spoken formula automatically resolves every case.

Three situations should be distinguished. Where an animal is known to have been offered to someone other than God, that dedication cannot be ignored. Where food falls within an expressed permission, such as the food of the People of the Book in Al-Ma'idah 5:5, remote suspicions should not automatically erase that permission; explicit prohibitions still remain relevant. Where details are unknown, ignorance is not itself proof of prohibition. Personal caution and declaring another person sinful are different responses. The context of invented restrictions in Al-An'am 6:138 and the connection between God’s name and sacrificial animals in Al-Hajj 22:28, Al-Hajj 22:34, and Al-Hajj 22:36 belong in the analysis. They do not establish that saying God’s name at the meal cancels any earlier prohibited condition.

58. Would rejecting hadith authority make intoxicating wine permissible? #

No. The Qur’an instructs people to avoid khamr (the intoxicating drink discussed in these passages) and gambling and describes their harmful effects (Al-Ma'idah 5:90, Al-Ma'idah 5:91; Al-Baqarah 2:219). A prohibition need not contain the specific word haram to express a serious command of avoidance. Extending the rule to other intoxicants requires an argument about meaning and effects, rather than pretending every modern substance is named in the verse.

The account of producing an intoxicating drink and good provision from dates and grapes in An-Nahl 16:67 describes human activity; it does not expressly declare intoxicating wine permitted. The restriction on approaching prayer while intoxicated in An-Nisa 4:43 must also be read alongside the command of avoidance. The combined evidence should not be used to erase that command. In reasoning about other intoxicants, loss of awareness and the passage’s stated harms matter. The descriptions of drinks in the next life concern different effects (As-Saffat 37:47; Al-Waqi'ah 56:19); their names alone cannot settle the treatment of intoxicants in this life.

59. What justifies forbidding gold and silk specifically to men? #

The Qur’an challenges the prohibition of God’s adornments and good provision (Al-A'raf 7:31, Al-A'raf 7:32). Its descriptions of gold and silk in the next life (Al-Hajj 22:23) do not, by themselves, establish all rules for this life. But a universal prohibition specifically for men also needs demonstrated divine authority. Arrogance, wastefulness, and ostentation should be examined as conduct rather than used without argument to condemn a material in itself.

The passages about ornaments obtained from the sea and worn by people (An-Nahl 16:14; Fatir 35:12) are relevant, though a masculine plural grammatical form alone does not settle a sex-specific rule. Likewise, the descriptions of Pharaoh’s arrogance in Az-Zukhruf 43:51, Az-Zukhruf 43:52, Az-Zukhruf 43:53, Az-Zukhruf 43:54 concern self-exaltation, not an automatic prohibition of a metal for half of humanity. If a report is used to establish the restriction, its attribution, wording, and normative scope must be addressed. The burden of justification cannot be replaced by either the popularity of the restriction or a superficial comparison with heavenly clothing.

60. Does the Qur’an need hadith to supply stoning as the penalty for married adulterers? #

Forqan’s study examines the connected passages in An-Nur 24:1, An-Nur 24:2, An-Nur 24:3, An-Nur 24:4, An-Nur 24:5, An-Nur 24:6, An-Nur 24:7, An-Nur 24:8, An-Nur 24:9 and An-Nisa 4:25, including the proof required for accusations, the protection of life, and the stated punishment. Adding execution or setting aside a written command through a report requires a substantial argument. An authenticity label cannot settle that relationship on its own.

The chapter introduces its prescriptions in An-Nur 24:1; the penalty in An-Nur 24:2 is one hundred lashes, without an express restriction there to unmarried offenders. The passages in An-Nur 24:6, An-Nur 24:7, An-Nur 24:8, An-Nur 24:9 address a husband’s accusation against his wife and reciprocal oaths, with An-Nur 24:8 discussing the averting of punishment. In An-Nisa 4:25, half the punishment is specified in particular circumstances; death is not a divisible penalty. A defender of stoning may appeal to legal distinctions concerning marriage and free or enslaved status. Those distinctions must be argued in relation to the text rather than merely assumed. Claims about a removed stoning verse also raise questions about the preservation of revelation (Al-Hijr 15:9). The dedicated study examines these steps in detail; the seriousness of taking life makes their justification indispensable.

61. Can the punishment for theft be understood without hadith? #

The relevant text is present in Al-Ma'idah 5:38, Al-Ma'idah 5:39. Interpreting the instruction concerning thieves’ hands requires attention to the words for cutting and hand, the punitive context, deterrence, and the following passage about repentance and reform. A physical or figurative interpretation should not be declared certain without that investigation. Forqan’s dedicated study examines a reading centered on cutting off access to theft alongside the bodily reading.

A figurative interpretation cannot be established merely by finding a figurative use of a root elsewhere. The verb family can describe severing bonds (Al-Baqarah 2:27; Muhammad 47:22), and “hand” can concern power or control. Yet Yusuf 12:31 describes physical cuts to hands, and Al-Ma'idah 5:33 uses cutting hands and feet in a punitive setting. The particular grammar and context of Al-Ma'idah 5:38 must therefore carry the argument. The distinctions between theft and violent aggression, and the place of deterrence and reform, also matter. Acknowledging these challenges is necessary for testing the interpretation on the text itself; disagreement among interpreters does not give an external report an automatic right to override the passage.

62. Does the absence of a Qur’anic death penalty for leaving a religion make a reported penalty necessary? #

An absent punishment is not permission to create one in God’s name. The Qur’an discusses repeated changes between belief and disbelief (An-Nisa 4:137) and rejects compulsion in religion (Al-Baqarah 2:256). Changing belief must be distinguished from an independently established offense such as murder or violent attack. Forqan’s study connects the Messenger’s duty of delivery with each person’s responsibility for belief.

The command to seize and kill in An-Nisa 4:89 is often raised here. It must be read with An-Nisa 4:88, An-Nisa 4:89, An-Nisa 4:90, An-Nisa 4:91, where hostile relations, warfare, agreements, withdrawal from fighting, and peace are central. The exceptions for those joining a treaty community or refraining from war are important: if a change of belief alone were the issue, why would peaceful withdrawal and nonparticipation in combat matter? A universal death penalty for changing religion cannot simply be extracted from that setting while Al-Baqarah 2:256 and An-Nisa 4:137 are left aside. The historical report and any claimed generalization still need their own justification.

63. Without reports about intercession, is hope of salvation lost? #

The Qur’an makes shafa‘ah (intercession on another’s behalf) dependent on God’s permission and judgment (Al-Baqarah 2:255; Ta-Ha 20:109). That does not itself instruct people to find a necessary intermediary who can negotiate salvation for them. The account of God’s nearness and response to prayer in Al-Baqarah 2:186 also matters. Permission for intercession and a command to seek particular intercessors are different claims.

The requests for Jacob’s prayer in Yusuf 12:97 and the Messenger’s prayer for forgiveness in An-Nisa 4:64 should not be omitted. They support asking a living person for prayer; they do not make that person the owner of forgiveness or a necessary recipient of worship. The criticism of self-appointed intercessors in Yunus 10:18 and the statement that all intercession belongs to God in Az-Zumar 39:44 set further boundaries. Asking another person to pray for us does not automatically justify invoking absent or deceased people as possessors of independent power to remove harm. That extension requires a separate argument.

64. Is someone miserly or disrespectful for not adding a fixed blessing every time Muhammad’s name appears? #

The command concerning prayer and peace upon the Prophet appears in Al-Ahzab 33:56. It does not expressly say that every written occurrence of his name must be followed by a particular formula as a condition of respect or faith. The Qur’an itself names prophets respectfully without appending the same sentence after every name. The command and additional conventions must be distinguished.

Salawat (prayers of blessing or expressions of support, with the precise Qur’anic sense examined in the linked study) should not be reduced without argument to a mandatory writing convention. If the judgment “miserly” is based on a particular report, that report’s authority as a measure of someone’s character needs to be established. The Qur’anic command in Al-Ahzab 33:56 must be respected, but respect does not by itself prove that one prescribed phrase after each occurrence of a name is the decisive test. The fuller study examines the meaning of the command and the reporting tradition used to extend it.

65. Do we need hadith to supply names, numbers, and details of the unseen that the Qur’an leaves unstated? #

The Qur’an reports some things about the unseen and leaves others unstated. Filling a narrative gap does not make the added detail certain religious knowledge. The conventional name given to the angel of death, the number of the people of the cave, accounts of future events, and elaborations about named figures are examples where a report’s source and status require attention.

Some additions are discussed under Isra’iliyyat (reports associated with earlier Jewish and Christian narrative traditions that entered Muslim interpretation). The label itself does not decide the truth of every item. The relevant question is what the evidence establishes. Recognizing the boundary of our knowledge is not a defect in revelation, and an intriguing story should not be made a binding belief merely to complete the account.

66. Is salvation restricted to members of a particular hadith-based school? #

The Qur’an does not make the name of a community or its approved book list a sufficient guarantee of salvation. Passages such as Al-Baqarah 2:62, Al-Ma'idah 5:69, and An-Nisa 4:123, An-Nisa 4:124 address faith, good conduct, responsibility, and judgment. Acceptance or rejection of a historical collection cannot simply replace God’s judgment of a person’s belief and actions. This does not make truth or accountability irrelevant; it challenges a guarantee based on affiliation alone.

The statements in Al-Imran 3:19 and Al-Imran 3:85 about Islam must also be addressed. Islam (submission to God in the usage examined here) should not automatically be reduced to the inherited label of a particular later school. Abraham is described as submitting to God in Al-Imran 3:67, while An-Nisa 4:123, An-Nisa 4:124 rejects wishful claims of privilege. Likewise, din (a term whose uses include religious commitment, obligation, and judgment) needs contextual study. Rejecting sectarian entitlement does not reject submission to God or the reality of judgment. The studies of religious belonging and salvation develop that argument further.

67. Does criticizing hadith authority mean Muslim history and scholarship have no value? #

No. Questioning a collection’s status as revelation does not eliminate its potential historical, literary, or social value. Reports can help investigate what people believed and practiced. Linguistic scholarship can help explain words. Practical teaching can pass on a form of worship. Each can be assessed according to the kind of knowledge it offers.

The additional claim requiring justification is that such humanly transmitted information establishes God’s certain command for everyone. Recognizing that distinction makes critical use of the material possible without either treating the entire heritage as infallible or dismissing it as worthless.

Concluding argument

The questions in this study can be addressed without treating every later convention as a requirement that the Qur’an must reproduce. The Qur’an is the criterion for a divine command; the Messenger conveys God’s message. Learning a practice from a community, a fact from history, or a meaning from language does not automatically elevate those sources to revelation.

Prayer has purposes and practical components in the text, while communal instruction transmits familiar ways of performing it. Giving has responsibilities and identified recipients, although no universal 2.5 percent table appears in the verses. Pilgrimage includes the House, movement around it, Safa and Marwah, restrictions, and offerings; an established movement or count still needs justification before it becomes a divine condition of validity. Fasting has stated temporal boundaries and concessions. In legal and doctrinal questions, a historical account cannot displace a Qur’anic command merely by being called a report of the Prophet.

This approach preserves a place for human learning while asking that it be described honestly. Where someone claims an additional duty, prohibition, or universally applicable punishment on the basis of a report, the questions are: What establishes divine authorization? What establishes attribution? Who was addressed, and what was the instruction’s scope? These questions concern selected reports as well as claims about whole collections. They are not answered merely by noting that a collection contains errors, nor by appealing to its reputation.

The argument therefore rests on three separate inquiries: authority to legislate and the need for divine permission (Ash-Shura 42:21; Yunus 10:59; An-Nahl 16:116); the historical evidence linking a report to its alleged source; and the character of the instruction, even when its transmission is credible. Was it a permanent command, a situational decision, an explanation, or a practical example? Respect for the Messenger, useful social organization, and scholarly expertise do not make those inquiries unnecessary. They give us reasons to pursue them carefully.
