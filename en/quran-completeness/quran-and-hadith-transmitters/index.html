The claim and its central confusion

One common argument among defenders of Hadith-centered religion is this: “The same people who transmitted the Qur’an to us also transmitted Hadith to us; therefore, if you question Hadith, you have weakened the Qur’an as well.”

On the surface, this sounds simple and persuasive. In reality, it rests on a major confusion: confusing two completely different kinds of transmission. The Qur’an reached us through a public, living, written, devotional, and communal transmission. Hadith, however, mostly reached us through individual reports, limited chains, human memories, transmission by meaning, disagreements among transmitters, and later compilations.

So the issue is not whether human beings had a role in both paths. Yes, human beings had a role in both. The issue is that the structure of Qur’anic transmission and the structure of Hadith transmission are not the same. Sharing the word “transmission” does not mean sharing the same authority, certainty, or religious rank.

1. The Qur’an presents itself as preserved and final criterion

Before any historical discussion, the Qur’an makes a clear claim within itself about its own status:

إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

We ourselves sent down the Reminder, and We ourselves are its guardian.

Al-Hijr 15:9

This divine guarantee is about the Qur’an. It is not about Bukhari, Muslim, Tirmidhi, Al-Kafi, or any other Hadith collection. No Hadith book receives the seal of divine preservation in the Qur’an. Therefore, from the Qur’an’s own point of view, the preserved text and final religious criterion is the revealed message, not later reports narrated about the Prophet.

The Qur’an also presents itself through internal criteria: challenge, absence of contradiction, clarity of guidance, and judgment over disputes. The Qur’an says that if this Book had been from other than The God, they would have found much contradiction in it:

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ ۚ وَلَوْ كَانَ مِنْ عِندِ غَيْرِ اللَّهِ لَوَجَدُوا فِيهِ اخْتِلَافًا كَثِيرًا

Do they not reflect upon the Qur’an? If it had been from other than The God, they would have found in it much contradiction.

An-Nisa 4:82

This criterion is very important. A text from The God must be coherent, firm, and free of contradiction in its principles. Hadith collections do not occupy this position. In Hadith, we find many disagreements, conflicts between reports, conflicts with clear Qur’anic principles, and disagreement among schools over accepting or rejecting reports. This alone shows that Hadith cannot be placed on the same level as the Qur’an.

2. The Qur’an came through public tawatur and living communal presence

The Qur’an was not merely a historical report transmitted by one or two individuals. During the Prophet’s life, the Qur’an was recited, memorized, written, recited in prayer, heard in society, and recognized as the central text of the religion.

From the beginning, the Qur’an was the text of worship, teaching, judgment, and the identity of the believing community. Muslims carried it with them in prayer, gathering, instruction, memorization, and writing. Such a text could not remain hidden like an individual report in the memory of one narrator.

The tawatur of the Qur’an means the transmission of a text from a community to the next community, not the transmission of one sentence from one individual to another. No verse of the Qur’an depends on a narrow private chain. The Qur’an was the public text of the ummah, not the private memory of a few narrators.

This difference is the root of the answer to the argument. The transmitter of the Qur’an, in the true sense, was the whole believing community and the living tradition of recitation and writing. The transmitter of a Hadith, however, is usually a specific chain of individuals, each of whom must be examined for memory, truthfulness, school affiliation, chronology, meeting, verbal precision, and possibility of error.

3. Hadith is structurally in the category of probable report

A large portion of Hadith, even according to Hadith scholars, belongs to the category of solitary report. That means that in one or more layers of the chain, transmission narrows down to one or a few people. Such a report, even if its narrators are truthful, is not on the same epistemic level as the Qur’an.

A truthful narrator can still forget. He may understand the meaning but change the wording. He may mix the Prophet’s speech with his own understanding. He may not know part of the context. He may report something tied to a specific circumstance as if it were a permanent ruling. These are not accusations against the narrator; they are natural limits of human transmission.

For this reason, Hadith science itself had to develop rules such as narrator criticism, chain continuity, precision, moral reliability, irregularity, hidden defects, abrogation, conflict among reports, and preference between reports. The existence of these rules shows that Hadith was not self-evidently certain and Qur’an-like from the beginning. If Hadith were preserved, public, and certain like the Qur’an, such a complex system for rescuing, filtering, and preferring reports would not have been necessary.

4. Hadith sources themselves contain tension over writing Hadith

One important point is that within the narrative sources themselves, there are reports about the Prophet forbidding the writing of anything from him except the Qur’an. In Sahih Muslim, Abu Sa’id al-Khudri is reported to have narrated that the Prophet said not to write anything from him except the Qur’an, and whoever had written anything other than the Qur’an should erase it.

Alongside this, there are also reports that some individuals were allowed to write certain statements. This disagreement itself shows that Hadith did not possess a textual status equal to the Qur’an from the beginning. The Qur’an had to be written, memorized, and established. Other statements of the Prophet, even if useful and wise, were not treated as identical with the revealed text.

This point is very important: the Prophet was entrusted with delivering the Qur’an. The Qur’an is the speech of The God. But not every daily statement, situational judgment, social decision, personal answer, or historical explanation from the Prophet is itself the Qur’an. Respecting the Prophet means preserving this boundary, not placing every report attributed to him on the same level as revelation.

5. Final Hadith compilation is later than Qur’anic stabilization

The Qur’an was known, recited, memorized, and written during the Prophet’s life, and then stabilized as an official mushaf in the first generation of the Islamic community. The wide and final compilation of the famous Hadith books, especially the Sunni Sahih collections, came several generations later.

This historical gap matters. During that period, there were civil wars, political conflicts, sectarian competition, the formation of Umayyad and Abbasid power, legal disagreements, and many motives for fabricating or strengthening reports. Hadith scholars themselves acknowledged widespread Hadith fabrication. If widespread fabrication did not exist, the science of men, authentication, and weakening of reports would not have been so necessary.

So the real question is this: how can a text that was public, written, devotional, and central to the community from the beginning be placed on the same level as reports collected centuries later through memory, chains, disagreement, transmission by meaning, and biographical filtering?

These two paths are not the same.

6. Early Qur’an manuscripts witness to an early written Qur’anic tradition

Today, for the basic reliability of the Qur’an, we do not rely only on the trustworthiness of one or a few narrators. Early Qur’anic manuscripts, such as the Birmingham fragments, the Tubingen manuscript, and the early Sana’a materials, show that the written Qur’anic tradition formed very early and that the Qur’anic text had an objective and material presence in the Islamic community from the beginning.

Of course, care is required in this discussion. Some manuscripts attributed to Uthman or Ali are not historically certain in that attribution and should not be carelessly registered under their names. Likewise, in the case of Sana’a, the erased lower text contains differences, even though the upper text is very close to the common Qur’anic tradition. Therefore, the correct argument is not that no manuscript discussion exists. The correct argument is that early evidence confirms the early and public presence of the Qur’anic text, while Hadith does not have the same written, public, and contemporaneous status.

7. The “same transmitters” argument is false at its foundation

When someone says, “The same people who transmitted the Qur’an also transmitted Hadith,” we must ask: what does “the same people” mean?

If it means that both were transmitted within Muslim society, this is far too general and proves nothing. History, poetry, law, reports of battles, and tribal stories were also transmitted within Muslim society. Are all of them equal to the Qur’an?

If it means that the exact same individuals transmitted the exact same kind of text by the exact same method, the claim is not true. The Qur’an was transmitted through public tawatur, communal recitation, wide memorization, early writing, and devotional presence. Hadith was mostly transmitted through individual chains, limited reports, chain disagreements, textual differences, and later compilation.

This is where the fallacy lies: from a surface-level similarity in “transmission,” it draws the conclusion of equality in “authority.” This conclusion is not consistent with reason, history, or the standards of the Islamic sciences themselves.

8. The Qur’an comes before every religious statement as the measure

The Qur’an repeatedly warns the human being against following conjecture, assumption, blind imitation, and speech without knowledge. The Qur’an asks:

فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَهُ يُؤْمِنُونَ

Then in what hadith after it will they believe?

Al-Mursalat 77:50

And it also says:

تِلْكَ آيَاتُ اللَّهِ نَتْلُوهَا عَلَيْكَ بِالْحَقِّ فَبِأَيِّ حَدِيثٍ بَعْدَ اللَّهِ وَآيَاتِهِ يُؤْمِنُونَ

These are the signs of The God that We recite to you in truth. Then in what hadith after The God and His signs will they believe?

Al-Jathiyah 45:6

These verses clarify a great principle: religious faith is built upon the signs of The God, not upon probable reports later placed beside the signs. Every statement, every narration, every fatwa, every historical custom, and every religious inheritance must be measured before the Qur’an. The Qur’an is the criterion; Hadith is not the criterion by which the Qur’an is understood, limited, or set aside.

9. The comparison with the Gospels exposes a double standard

If someone says that a time gap, oral transmission, and compilation generations later are enough to produce certain authority, then he must explain why he does not accept the same logic for the Christian Gospels.

Many scholars date the writing of the Gospels several decades after Jesus. If transmission by a religious community and later compilation are enough to create certainty, then the defender of Hadith must accept that Christians can use the same logic to declare their texts certain. But it is clear that Qur’an-centered Muslims do not accept such a criterion. Our criterion is preserved, clear, and overseeing revelation, not every text that a religious community later made sacred.

So one cannot create an easy standard for oneself and a difficult standard for others. Truth is not recognized through double standards.

Conclusion

Trust in the Qur’an does not depend on absolute trust in Hadith. The Qur’an and Hadith did not reach us through the same path, do not have the same status, do not have the same degree of certainty, and do not play the same role in religion.

The Qur’an is the speech of The God, the final criterion, the preserved text, and the furqan between truth and falsehood. Hadith, at best, is a historical report attributed to the Prophet or to the earliest generations. It may contain wisdom, history, explanation, or religious experience, but it cannot become equal to the Qur’an or govern over the Qur’an.

Therefore, rejecting the equalization of Hadith with the Qur’an is not rejecting the Prophet. It is defending the Prophet’s place, because the Prophet was entrusted with calling people to the Book of The God, not with leaving behind a body of probable reports to replace the certain text of revelation.

The Qur’an was carried by the whole ummah through worship, writing, memorization, and public recitation. Hadith was transmitted by chains of individuals within the conditions of memory, disagreement, politics, sectarian identity, and later compilation.

So the claim, “If you do not accept Hadith, you cannot accept the Qur’an either,” is false at its foundation. It takes the Qur’an hostage to Hadith, while the Qur’an is free, independent, clear, and standing upon truth.

Any statement that agrees with the Qur’an is worthy of attention to the extent of that agreement; and any statement that conflicts with the Qur’an, whatever name or chain it carries, cannot be placed before the signs of The God.
