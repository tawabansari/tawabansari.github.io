Introduction

We grew up with the name "Zakat (Charity)," yet perhaps we have spent too little time examining it away from the noise of a few religious books and within the mirror of the Qur'an itself. In the common imagination, for many people, Zakat (Charity) means money, nisab, a fixed percentage, an annual payment, or a financial duty that must be discharged once a year and handed over to a ruler or an institution. In the middle of this, the truth of Zakat (Charity) is lost. In this way, a deeply inward and human act gives way to a dry formula: a religious tax whose identity card is religious, but in which the pulse and spirit of the Qur'an no longer beats.

The issue is not the denial of order, accounting, trustworthiness, and just mechanisms for distributing help. Clearly, without such discipline, helping the poor, the orphan, and the stranded cannot be done properly. The Qur'an itself did not leave the channels of sadaqat vague, and it recognizes workers who serve this path. But the center of the discussion lies elsewhere: did Zakat (Charity) come in the Qur'an to build levers of wealth and power, or to purify the soul and wealth of the human being and repair social bonds? Is Zakat (Charity) meant to open a path toward dignity and honor for the poor, or to become a means of extortion and domination over people's backs?

The Qur'an repeatedly places Zakat (Charity) beside salat. This pairing is not accidental. Salat brings the human being, before God, into remembrance, humility, and moral orientation; Zakat (Charity) shows what effect that faith has toward other human beings. A person who performs salat but remains indifferent to hunger, debt, deprivation, displacement, and the breaking of the backs of the poor has not yet understood the social meaning of faith. Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an is not merely a matter of giving money; it is a mirror of the truthfulness of faith, a means of purifying wealth, a field for the growth of the soul, and a living step toward reconnection with society.

But in the history of Muslims, this truth underwent transformation. Zakat (Charity), whose wisdom was to open a path for the purification of the soul and the support of society, became in many periods a source of government revenue, a display of power, a certificate of obedience before the ruler, and a criterion by which people's religiosity was judged. Formal jurisprudence and ruling politics took hold of that Qur'anic truth which was tied to growth, dignity, and justice; politics and jurisprudence joined hands so that what the Qur'an had linked to dignity and justice became limited to numerical calculations, bureaucracy, and pressure upon people. The bitterness reached its peak at turning points in history when, if someone did not hand over Zakat (Charity) to a political center, the matter was not treated merely as a financial or political offense; rather, he was called an apostate and outside the religion so that his suppression could be justified.

From here, the discussion of Zakat (Charity) reaches one of the great knots of Islamic history. After the Prophet's death, the young Muslim community faced a crisis of transfer of power, the absence of a clear political mechanism, tribal disagreements, and the question of obedience to Medina. In that atmosphere, wars took place that later became known as the Ridda wars. The dominant narrative presents those wars as a single, unified war against apostates. But history, when read carefully, does not give us such a simple picture. Among the opponents of Medina were groups that claimed prophethood or had moved away from the new religious order; yet there were also groups that did not deny Islam, salat, or their faith, but refused to pay Zakat (Charity) to the political center of Medina, or regarded their financial and political covenant as having ended after the Prophet's death.

These very historical reports place us before unavoidable questions: were all those groups truly apostates? Is withholding payment of Zakat (Charity) to a government equal to leaving the foundation of religion? Does the Qur'an permit us to answer a political and financial disagreement with the weapon of takfir and apostasy? And was it not precisely from this point that the flood of takfir against opponents, compulsion in religion, and war in the name of protecting faith began to flow through Muslim history?

The aim of this treatise is not to issue final judgments about the inner motives and personal intentions of historical actors. Knowledge of the intentions and inner states of Abu Bakr, Umar, the Companions, the tribes, commanders, and opponents belongs to God's knowledge. The subject of this study is not psychological evaluation or judgment over people's consciences; rather, it is the objective explanation of decisions, logical foundations, and structural consequences of these events under Qur'anic criteria. It is assumed that some of these decisions may have been made under emergency conditions and with the aim of preventing anarchy and the collapse of social order, and that the managers of the time may have seen themselves facing a full-scale crisis. But even if one grants the hypothesis of good intentions, the fundamental analytical question remains: is it legitimate to transform a Qur'anic obligation and act of worship into a tool of government power, military confrontation, and religious takfir? Does protecting political order and governmental interests justify separating Zakat (Charity) from its original function, namely individual purification and social repair, and turning it into an instrument for proving submission and obedience to power?

In this treatise, Zakat (Charity) will be examined through several paths. First, we return to the Qur'an to see what place this word has in the revealed text and how it relates to salat, faith, taqwa, sadaqah, infaq, birr, qist, and covenant. Then we examine the verses about sadaqah, its recipients, the workers over it, and the command "take from their wealth a sadaqah," so that it becomes clear how the Qur'an causes Zakat (Charity) to flow in society and whether it places it intrinsically in the hands of government or presents it as a faith-based and social responsibility that can have different forms of execution. After that, we enter history and examine Saqifah, the crisis of authority, the Ridda wars, the centralization of power, and the formation of the logic of political takfir.

Next, we compare juristic and historical Zakat (Charity) with Qur'anic Zakat (Charity). The important question is to what extent fixed numbers, juristic nisabs, and government structures arise from the Qur'an, and to what extent they are the product of history, report, state, and post-Qur'anic jurisprudence. The relation between Zakat (Charity) and modern taxation must also be measured. Today's states need a tax system for education, health care, security, infrastructure, and public welfare. But this reality must not cause Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) to dissolve into state taxation. Taxation is an instrument of public administration; Zakat (Charity) is an ethical, faith-based, and social covenant. The two may approach one another in some aims, but they are not the same.

Another part of this treatise is devoted to diagnosing contemporary distortions in the use of Zakat (Charity). When Zakat (Charity) is separated from its concrete aims and authentic Qur'anic criteria, the position of the poor is transformed from that of a dignified rights-holder into the victim of a system of compulsory extraction under the cover of religious duties. Authorities and movements that collect financial resources from lower classes, whether farmers, workers, women without income, weak villagers, or crisis-stricken societies, and then spend them on military conflict, internal suppression, propaganda projects, the reproduction of hypocrisy, or the strengthening of power, have preserved only the title of Zakat (Charity) while emptying it of its foundational meanings. This process is neither individual purification nor a supportive social dynamic; it is the seizure of public wealth under religious titles. It does not resolve social challenges; it imposes structural pressure. In the end, it does not purify wealth, but pollutes religious statements with the interests of political power.

The goal of this treatise is a methodical rereading of the Qur'an away from shallow and formal readings. This rereading means weighing written inheritance, juristic traditions, governmental approaches, and historical claims on the scale of revelation. If Zakat (Charity) in the logic of the Qur'an was legislated for aims such as purification of the soul, distributive justice, human dignity, removal of deprivation, and breaking accumulation, then any mechanism that reduces it to an instrument for crushing the poor, concentrating government authority, doctrinal takfir, or reproducing violence has deviated from the Qur'an's original aims, even if it is attributed to religion.

This treatise is not written to defend one school or attack another. It speaks of a structural reality. The question is how a divine concept, when placed inside the circle of power relations and history, can lose its original function; and how people who want to guard religion sometimes reproduce the very structures that the Qur'an came to reform. If Zakat (Charity) is explained on the basis of the Qur'an itself, it is a mechanism for the purification of the human being and the repair of society. But if it is transformed within political and sectarian interests, it can become one of the most effective tools for domination in the name of God.

For this reason, the title of this study has been chosen as follows: "Zakat (Charity) and Its Historical Distortion: From Qur'anic Tazkiyah to Religious Taxation, Takfir, and Political Authority." This title marks the path of the discussion. We begin by explaining the growth-giving and purifying meaning of Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an, then anatomize the process by which, across history, it became a government tax, an instrument of takfir, and a pillar of political power. In the end, we attempt to suggest a way to rebuild the Qur'anic understanding of Zakat (Charity) in the modern world: an understanding in which wealth is purified, the needy gain dignity, society is repaired, and religion is freed from being made into an instrument of power and compulsion.

Part Two: Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an; Purity through Growth and the Flow of Good

To enter this discussion, it is necessary to recognize Zakat (Charity) through the Qur'anic text itself, not through definitions transmitted to us by historical settings, juristic traditions, governmental structures, or religious habits. The root of many theoretical distortions begins here: a word is taken from the sacred text, then concepts that formed centuries later in the atmosphere of official jurisprudence and power relations are imposed upon it, and finally that transformed concept is presented to society in the name of religion. Zakat (Charity) has not been spared from this harmful process.

In the Qur'an, Zakat (Charity) is not merely a title for taking and giving money. In its linguistic genealogy, the word is tied to purity, growth, healthy increase, structural reform, and fruitfulness. In the language of the Qur'an, purity and growth are two sides of one reality. What is polluted does not reach healthy growth, and whatever grows in the right direction moves away from corruption and stagnation. On this basis, Zakat (Charity) should not be understood as reducing or cutting away a portion of wealth. In the Qur'anic view, Zakat (Charity) is purification of the soul through giving, growth through generosity, and the repair of society by breaking the monopoly of wealth.

Zakat (Charity) means tazkiyah and purity. When the Qur'an speaks of the tazkiyah of the self, the final aim is not the payment of money; the aim is purifying the human soul from miserliness, self-centeredness, arrogance, and inner corruption. God is Rich and has no need of our money in order to feed the hungry or make the poor wealthy. The One God, if He willed, could Himself make the poor rich, feed the hungry, and satisfy the thirsty. The Qur'anic statement "qad aflaha man zakkaha" clearly shows that success is tied to the process of tazkiyah. The human being must bring the self out of a raw, material, closed condition and place it on the path of growth. The same behavioral spirit flows into financial Zakat (Charity). A person's wealth is not entirely separate from his being; wealth is tied to his fears, greed, sense of security, and accumulation of power. A human being is tested through wealth just as he is tested through life, social position, and knowledge.

The Qur'an says:

قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَن زَكَّاهَا

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَن دَسَّاهَا

Truly successful is the one who purifies and causes it to grow; and truly failed is the one who buries it in pollution.

Ash-Shams 91:9-10

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَأَوْلَادُكُمْ فِتْنَةٌ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ عِندَهُ أَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

And know that your wealth and your children are a trial, and that with God there is a great reward.

Al-Anfal 8:28

Supporting references: At-Taghabun 64:15, Al Imran 3:186, Al-Fajr 89:15-20.

Thus the Qur'an's purpose in presenting Zakat (Charity) is not merely the physical transfer of a portion of property from one hand to another. The primary aim is to reform the human relationship with ownership. Wealth, if it remains in its proper instrumental place, is a source of good. But if it settles deep inside the soul and becomes the measure of honor and superiority, it becomes a veil over truth, and Zakat (Charity) breaks that veil. This process reminds the human being that his ownership is not absolute and unconditional; rather, it is a trust in which the rights of others are also defined.

The Qur'an says:

وَفِي أَمْوَالِهِمْ حَقٌّ لِّلسَّائِلِ وَالْمَحْرُومِ

And in their wealth there is a right for the one who asks and the deprived.

Adh-Dhariyat 51:19

وَالَّذِينَ فِي أَمْوَالِهِمْ حَقٌّ مَّعْلُومٌ

لِّلسَّائِلِ وَالْمَحْرُومِ

And those in whose wealth there is a known right for the one who asks and the deprived.

Al-Ma'arij 70:24-25

In these verses, the needy person is not a passive object of pity. He is a bearer of right. Zakat (Charity), therefore, is not the favor of the wealthy over the poor; it is the recognition of a right that already exists within wealth. This point is a foundation for separating Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) from mere charity in the modern sentimental sense. The deprived person is not a decorative figure through whom the wealthy person polishes his image. The deprived person is a rights-holder, and the owner of wealth is being tested in whether he recognizes that right or conceals it behind the language of ownership.

Zakat (Charity) is also tied directly to the problem of accumulated wealth, hoarding, and the confinement of property in the hands of a limited group. Wealth that is merely stored and does not flow through the veins of society may increase outwardly, but ethically and socially it becomes stagnant and corrupt. Such possessions may make the figures in the owner's account heavier, but they make the structure of society ill. Zakat (Charity) is a way of bringing wealth out of stagnation and returning it to the cycle of life. In this rereading, Zakat (Charity) is not decrease; it is restoration to health. The apparent reduction of wealth, when it occurs in the path of truth and justice, leads to growth of the self, strengthening of public trust, preservation of the dignity of the needy, and health in the structure of society.

The Qur'an says:

أَلْهَاكُمُ التَّكَاثُرُ

حَتَّىٰ زُرْتُمُ الْمَقَابِرَ

Rivalry in increase distracted you, until you visited the graves.

At-Takathur 102:1-2

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ الْأَحْبَارِ وَالرُّهْبَانِ لَيَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ النَّاسِ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَيَصُدُّونَ عَن سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۗ وَالَّذِينَ يَكْنِزُونَ الذَّهَبَ وَالْفِضَّةَ وَلَا يُنفِقُونَهَا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَبَشِّرْهُم بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

O you who have believed, many of the religious scholars and monks consume people's wealth falsely and turn away from the path of God; and those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the path of God, give them news of a painful punishment.

At-Tawbah 9:34

مَّا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْقُرَىٰ فَلِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنكُمْ

Whatever God restored to His Messenger from the people of the towns belongs to God, the Messenger, relatives, orphans, the poor, and the stranded traveler, so that it may not circulate only among the wealthy among you.

Al-Hashr 59:7

The vulnerable classes of society, including the poor, the destitute, debtors, stranded travelers, and people under pressure, are not outside the social responsibility of believers. Faith that remains only at the level of language and individual ritual has not yet reached the stage of visible truthfulness. Zakat (Charity) moves faith from the individual sphere into the social arena. A person who claims faith while his wealth has no relation to the pains of society has not passed one of the clearest tests of faith.

The Qur'an says:

لَّيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا ۖ وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ ۗ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ

Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward east or west. Rather, righteousness is one who believes in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; gives wealth, despite love for it, to relatives, orphans, the poor, the stranded traveler, those who ask, and for freeing those in bondage; establishes salat and gives Zakat (Charity); fulfills covenants when covenanting; and is patient in hardship, distress, and time of battle. Those are the ones who have been truthful, and those are the mindful.

Al-Baqarah 2:177

This is why the Qur'an continually places Zakat (Charity) beside salat. Salat is the human being's bond with the Source of existence, while Zakat (Charity) shows what effect that worship leaves on the human being's conduct toward creation. If someone performs rites but his heart remains closed toward the challenges of society, his worship has not yet borne the fruit of responsibility. The Qur'an does not allow religiosity to be limited to outward rites. The same gaze that calls the human being to salat commands him toward giving, purification of the self, and breaking the wall of miserliness.

The Qur'an says:

وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَارْكَعُوا مَعَ الرَّاكِعِينَ

Establish salat, give Zakat (Charity), and bow with those who bow.

Al-Baqarah 2:43

وَمَا أُمِرُوا إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ حُنَفَاءَ وَيُقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَيُؤْتُوا الزَّكَاةَ ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ الْقَيِّمَةِ

They were not commanded except to worship God, making the religion sincere for Him as hunafa, to establish salat and give Zakat (Charity); and that is the upright religion.

Al-Bayyinah 98:5

Supporting references: Al-Baqarah 2:83, Al-Baqarah 2:110, An-Nisa 4:77, Al-Hajj 22:41, An-Nur 24:56, Al-Muzzammil 73:20.

Within this framework, Zakat (Charity) is not an isolated instruction. It is part of a structured whole that the Qur'an presents for cultivating the human being and society. Concepts such as faith, salat, Zakat (Charity), taqwa, sadaqah, infaq, fulfillment of covenant, support of the deprived, and prevention of wealth circulating only in the hands of a small minority all belong to one system. Separating them from one another produces an incomplete understanding of religion. Zakat (Charity) without taqwa becomes a dry transaction; Zakat (Charity) without justice becomes a display of power; Zakat (Charity) without preserving dignity becomes humiliation of the needy; Zakat (Charity) without freedom becomes governmental compulsion.

The Qur'an says:

الَّذِينَ إِن مَّكَّنَّاهُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ أَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَوُا الزَّكَاةَ وَأَمَرُوا بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَنَهَوْا عَنِ الْمُنكَرِ ۗ وَلِلَّهِ عَاقِبَةُ الْأُمُورِ

Those who, if We give them position in the land, establish salat, give Zakat (Charity), command what is recognized as good, and forbid what is rejected as wrong; and to God belongs the outcome of matters.

Al-Hajj 22:41

The Qur'anic logic of Zakat (Charity) is designed so that it simultaneously purifies the individual and repairs society. It frees the individual from servitude to wealth and saves society from indifference, class fracture, and the forgetting of the deprived. These two fields cannot be separated. An unjust structure produces wounded human beings, and irresponsible human beings sustain unjust structures. Zakat (Charity) is a bridge between these two realms.

Therefore, reducing Zakat (Charity) to a fixed rate or an annual administrative payment is a loss of its Qur'anic function. Discipline and numerical calculations can help at the stage of implementation, but they never replace the original aim. If financial giving is done with condescension, display, fear of authority, or merely religious habit, it is far from the tazkiyah intended by the Qur'an. Likewise, if a structure under the name of Zakat (Charity) takes people's wealth but spends it not on removing deprivation but on expanding power, military conflicts, institutional propaganda, or strengthening itself, the mere use of the title Zakat (Charity) creates no Qur'anic legitimacy.

The Qur'an says:

خُذْ مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ صَدَقَةً تُطَهِّرُهُمْ وَتُزَكِّيهِم بِهَا وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ إِنَّ صَلَاتَكَ سَكَنٌ لَّهُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Take from their wealth a sadaqah by which you purify them and cause them to grow, and pray over them; indeed, your prayer is tranquility for them; and God is Hearing, Knowing.

At-Tawbah 9:103

The addressee of Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an is the soul and thought of the human being. Zakat (Charity) concerns the redefinition of the human being's view of wealth and the place of the lower classes in society. If the mechanism for carrying out Zakat (Charity) is such that the dignity of the needy is damaged, the wealthy merely feel that they have discharged a duty, and ruling power uses it as a lever of domination, its path has separated from Qur'anic authenticity.

One of the foundational criteria of Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) is its companionship with the preservation of dignity. The Qur'an explicitly warns that sadaqah and infaq are ruined by reminders of favor and injury. This statement shows that, according to the Qur'an, merely delivering wealth is not enough; the manner and process of delivery also matter. If financial support is joined with breaking people's personalities, it loses its purifying function. The aim is at once the purification of the giver and the protection of the honor of the receiver.

The Qur'an says:

قَوْلٌ مَّعْرُوفٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِّن صَدَقَةٍ يَتْبَعُهَا أَذًى ۗ وَاللَّهُ غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ

Recognized good speech and forgiveness are better than a sadaqah followed by injury; and God is Rich, Forbearing.

Al-Baqarah 2:263

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُم بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ كَالَّذِي يُنفِقُ مَالَهُ رِئَاءَ النَّاسِ وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ

O you who have believed, do not invalidate your sadaqat with reminders of favor and harm, like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in God and the Last Day.

Al-Baqarah 2:264

Here the foundational difference between the Qur'an and many historical approaches becomes clear. In many periods, Zakat (Charity) was turned from a living mechanism between faith and justice into a bureaucratic financial file, and the questions revolved only around the amount of wealth, items subject to Zakat (Charity), authorities of collection, and systematic punishment of those who refused to pay. Such questions may be necessary in an executive structure, but when an administrative approach replaces the ethical concern, the spirit of Zakat (Charity) disappears. The Qur'an's essential question is: has wealth been purified? Has the self been freed from miserliness? Has the right of the deprived been delivered, and has society moved closer to justice?

Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) calls the human being to a fundamental change in outlook: to understand that within his wealth lies a trial; that the confinement of wealth weighs down the self; and that the proper flow of wealth creates the field of purification and growth. Thus, in its deep meaning, Zakat (Charity) is a process of liberation: freeing wealth from monopoly, freeing the self from miserliness, freeing the needy from isolation, and freeing society from structural cruelty.

The Qur'an says:

وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

Whoever is protected from the tight miserliness of his own self, they are the successful.

Al-Hashr 59:9

Also refer to: At-Taghabun 64:16.

This methodical understanding prepares us for historical critique. When the aim of Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an becomes clear, we reread history with a more precise scale. Wherever the use of Zakat (Charity) has led to the expansion of justice, preservation of dignity, and support of the deprived, it is closer to the Qur'an's aim. Wherever it has become an instrument of treasury, takfir of opponents, pressure on weak classes, or political imposition, we must state clearly that only the name of Zakat (Charity) has been preserved while its truth has been harmed.

Thus the first principle of this study is as follows: before Zakat (Charity) is a merely financial system, it is an institution of tazkiyah; and before it is a governmental instrument, it is an element in the realm of faith, ethics, and social responsibility. Any executive mechanism, whether individual, civil, or governmental, has legitimacy only when it serves these aims: purity, growth, dignity, justice, and the delivery of the rights of the deprived classes of society.

Part Three: Zakat (Charity), Sadaqah, and Infaq; Three Approaches to Purifying Wealth and Repairing Society

To understand Zakat (Charity) accurately, this concept must be analyzed inside a system beside two other key words: sadaqah and infaq. Much theoretical confusion arises from the fact that these three concepts are either treated as completely synonymous, or separated from one another so sharply that their structural connection in the Qur'an is lost. The Qur'an does not use these words beside one another without reason. Each has its own semantic field, yet all move in one direction: taking the human being out of self-centeredness, causing good to flow in society, and repairing the human relationship with wealth.

As stated earlier, Zakat (Charity) is tied to purity and growth, and its central emphasis is tazkiyah: purifying wealth and the self from the pollution of miserliness, monopoly, and social indifference. Zakat (Charity) proves that faith is not merely inward-looking; it must leave a visible effect in the lives of others, and a person's wealth cannot be separated from the pain of society.

The word sadaqah comes from the root of sidq, truthfulness. It shows that financial giving is a testimony to a person's truthfulness in claiming faith. A person can claim faith with words, but the truthfulness of that claim is revealed when he gives up wealth, time, ability, and personal comfort. Sadaqah is not merely the movement of money; it is a test of truthfulness. For this reason, sadaqah joined with reminders of favor, harm, or humiliation contradicts its own structural root. The truth of sadaqah is not only in its amount, but in its motive, manner, and consequences.

The Qur'an presents sadaqah in relation to repentance, return, and inner reform:

أَلَمْ يَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ يَقْبَلُ التَّوْبَةَ عَنْ عِبَادِهِ وَيَأْخُذُ الصَّدَقَاتِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ

Do they not know that God accepts repentance from His servants and takes the sadaqat, and that God is the Accepting of Repentance, the Merciful?

At-Tawbah 9:104

This verse shows that sadaqah is not only a financial act; it takes meaning in the atmosphere of return, reform, and the human being's relationship with God. In the same direction, the Qur'an speaks of men and women who give sadaqah and describes this act as a good loan to God:

إِنَّ الْمُصَّدِّقِينَ وَالْمُصَدِّقَاتِ وَأَقْرَضُوا اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَاعَفُ لَهُمْ وَلَهُمْ أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ

Indeed, the men who give sadaqah and the women who give sadaqah, and those who lend God a good loan, it will be multiplied for them, and for them is a noble reward.

Al-Hadid 57:18

Infaq comes from a root related to spending and bringing wealth out. In the Qur'an it has a broader field: causing possessions to flow in the path of good. These possessions are not necessarily money. They include wealth, knowledge, skill, time, social influence, experience, bodily ability, good speech, emotional support, teaching, and giving refuge to others. Through infaq, the Qur'an brings the human being out of the fortress of self-centeredness.

The Qur'an says:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ مَاذَا يُنفِقُونَ ۖ قُلِ الْعَفْوَ

They ask you what they should spend. Say: the surplus beyond need.

Al-Baqarah 2:219

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنبُلَةٍ مِّائَةُ حَبَّةٍ ۗ وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَن يَشَاءُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

The likeness of those who spend their wealth in the path of God is like a seed that grows seven ears, in each ear a hundred grains; and God multiplies for whom He wills; and God is Vast, Knowing.

Al-Baqarah 2:261

These three concepts complete one another: Zakat (Charity) emphasizes purification and growth; sadaqah emphasizes the truthfulness of faith; and infaq emphasizes the flow of blessings in the path of God. With this systemic view, Zakat (Charity) can no longer be reduced to a dry administrative payment. Zakat (Charity) is the minimum of responsibility, not the end of the path of good. The Qur'an reveals this truth in the verse of birr, explaining that righteousness is not merely attachment to outward directions of ritual, but the joining of faith with giving wealth out of love to relatives, orphans, the poor, and stranded travelers, alongside establishing salat and giving Zakat (Charity). This shows that Zakat (Charity) belongs within a larger geometry of birr, truthfulness, and taqwa.

لَّيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا ۖ وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ ۗ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ

Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward east or west; rather, righteousness is one who believes in God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets, gives wealth despite love for it to relatives, orphans, the poor, the stranded traveler, those who ask, and for freeing those in bondage; establishes salat and gives Zakat (Charity); fulfills covenants when covenanting; and is patient in hardship, distress, and time of battle. Those are the truthful, and those are the mindful.

Al-Baqarah 2:177

The Qur'an enters the language of right and speaks of the right of relatives, the poor, and the deprived, emphasizing that the issue is not merely the personal generosity of the wealthy. The needy person is a holder of right, and society must not be arranged so that the wealthy see themselves as absolute owners and the poor as recipients of allowances. Once something becomes a right, it no longer depends on the wealthy person's taste or self-display; it must be delivered.

وَآتِ ذَا الْقُرْبَىٰ حَقَّهُ وَالْمِسْكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ لِّلَّذِينَ يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَ اللَّهِ ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

Give the relative his right, and the poor and the stranded traveler. That is better for those who seek the Face of God, and they are the successful.

Ar-Rum 30:38

وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ

And give its right on the day of its harvest.

Al-An'am 6:141

This point is an important basis for critiquing purely calculative and number-obsessed approaches to Zakat (Charity). If someone imagines that by paying a fixed amount all his social and ethical responsibilities have fallen away, he has not understood the logic of the Qur'an. The Qur'an speaks of a broad system that takes meaning through the real needs of human beings and the conditions of society, not merely through a fixed numerical formula.

The Qur'an does not want all social good to be stopped inside one formal and administrative form. Sometimes what is needed is direct help to relatives, rescuing a debtor, or supporting a stranded traveler. If this broad field is reduced to an official transfer to governmental or religious institutions, much of the Qur'an's functional spirit disappears.

يَسْأَلُونَكَ مَاذَا يُنفِقُونَ ۖ قُلْ مَا أَنفَقْتُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍ فَلِلْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۗ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ

They ask you what they should spend. Say: whatever good you spend is for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and the stranded traveler; and whatever good you do, God knows it.

Al-Baqarah 2:215

Of course, this does not mean denying institution-building, discipline, or collective work. Sometimes identifying real need and managing resources well requires trustworthy, transparent, and accountable organization, and the Qur'an speaks of workers in this matter. But such work is a responsibility and a trust, not a governmental privilege. The collecting institution does not own Zakat (Charity), and any handling of people's wealth is legitimate only when the wealth is spent precisely in the paths defined by the Qur'an.

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا وَالْمُؤَلَّفَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَالْغَارِمِينَ وَفِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۖ فَرِيضَةً مِّنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

The sadaqat are only for the poor, the destitute, the workers over them, those whose hearts are to be brought near, freeing those in bondage, debtors, in the path of God, and the stranded traveler: an obligation from God. God is Knowing, Wise.

At-Tawbah 9:60

In this context, the verse "take from their wealth a sadaqah by which you purify them and cause them to grow" is highly important. In this command, the aim of taking wealth is not described as building a treasury or accumulating power for government; rather, its purpose is explicitly the purification and educational growth of the believers themselves. Even in the station of taking wealth, the verse's approach is educational and faith-based: to cut the human being's dependence on wealth, not to make the ruler owner over people's will and property.

خُذْ مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ صَدَقَةً تُطَهِّرُهُمْ وَتُزَكِّيهِم بِهَا وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ إِنَّ صَلَاتَكَ سَكَنٌ لَّهُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Take from their wealth a sadaqah by which you purify them and cause them to grow, and pray over them. Indeed, your prayer is tranquility for them; and God is Hearing, Knowing.

At-Tawbah 9:103

This statement also shows that the concepts of Zakat (Charity) and sadaqah in the Qur'an are not locked into closed and separate administrative files. The word sadaqah appears, but its function is purification and tazkiyah. The Qur'an looks to the aim of the act. If later Zakat (Charity) and sadaqah were turned into two entirely independent administrative files, one must return to the semantic dynamism of the Qur'an, which sometimes uses the word sadaqah for an act with the function of Zakat (Charity) and, through infaq, opens a broader field than fixed numbers.

Distortion begins when the collecting apparatus becomes so large and self-centered that the needy are pushed to the margins, or when the title Zakat (Charity) is used to fund military, propaganda, and power structures. This is not the implementation of Zakat (Charity); it is the exploitation of religion's name to strengthen an apparatus that has no relation to tazkiyah. The recipients named in the Qur'an do not leave distribution to the ruler's desire, and political power cannot arbitrarily call every expenditure "in the path of God."

On this basis, we must distinguish between the institution of Zakat (Charity) and the tax system. Taxation in modern society is an administrative necessity based on citizenship law for infrastructure, public services, health, and education. But Zakat (Charity) takes meaning from faith, tazkiyah, and ethical responsibility. Identifying the two opens the way to functional distortion: if Zakat (Charity) is completely dissolved into taxation, the government sees itself as its absolute owner, and any refusal to pay the state can be treated as departure from religion. This is the sensitive point that must be examined in history: the transformation of an act of worship into an index of political obedience.

Infaq and sadaqah carry the same structural warnings. If a person helps the needy with humiliation, or an institution distributes wealth while damaging people's honor, or a government takes wealth through coercion and threat, such a process is not tazkiyah and it is not infaq in the path of God.

الَّذِينَ يُنفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ لَا يُتْبِعُونَ مَا أَنفَقُوا مَنًّا وَلَا أَذًى ۙ لَّهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

Those who spend their wealth in the path of God and then do not follow what they spent with reminders of favor or harm, their reward is with their Lord; no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve.

Al-Baqarah 2:262

Qur'anic infaq also does not allow us to measure the social value of people only by money. A woman raising righteous children, a teacher transmitting knowledge, a doctor or young person dedicating time and skill to serving society: all are present in the field of value-creation and infaq. Limiting Zakat (Charity) to money deprives a large part of society of participation in faith.

This is especially important regarding lower classes. In distorted systems, even the needy are sometimes pressured by compulsory religious payments, whereas in the logic of the Qur'an, vulnerable groups are the center of distribution and expenditure, not a source for structural pressure.

In conclusion, Zakat (Charity), sadaqah, and infaq are not three labels for one dry financial administration. They are three windows onto one truth: faith must become visible in wealth, conduct, and social relationships. Zakat (Charity) purifies the self, sadaqah shows the truthfulness of faith, and infaq takes possession out of monopoly. All three are tied to human dignity, justice, sincerity, and social responsibility.

Therefore, throughout this treatise, whenever we speak of Zakat (Charity), we analyze it within this wide Qur'anic field. We do not reduce it to an administrative tax, we do not detach it from ethical responsibility, and we do not allow its name to become a cover for accumulated power, takfir, and governmental compulsion. Zakat (Charity) is Qur'anic when it is joined with truthfulness, breathes through infaq, honors the needy, and frees the human being from servitude to wealth.

Part Four: Zakat (Charity) beside Salat; The Link between Worship and Social Responsibility

One of the most repeated structures in the Qur'an is the pairing of salat and Zakat (Charity). This pairing is not merely stylistic. The Qur'an joins these two so that religion does not collapse into individual ritual without justice, and social giving does not collapse into political management without sincerity. Salat directs the human being toward God; Zakat (Charity) tests whether that direction toward God has softened the hand of the human being toward creation.

The Qur'an commands:

وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَارْكَعُوا مَعَ الرَّاكِعِينَ

Establish salat, give Zakat (Charity), and bow with those who bow.

Al-Baqarah 2:43

وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ ۚ وَمَا تُقَدِّمُوا لِأَنفُسِكُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍ تَجِدُوهُ عِندَ اللَّهِ

Establish salat and give Zakat (Charity); whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with God.

Al-Baqarah 2:110

The pairing shows that the Qur'an does not accept a split between worship and social responsibility. If salat is a movement toward God, Zakat (Charity) is the visible social consequence of that movement. A person cannot stand before God and then remain unmoved before hunger, debt, homelessness, humiliation, and deprivation among people. Salat without Zakat (Charity) can become ritual isolation; Zakat (Charity) without salat can become a tool of arrogance, propaganda, or political management.

The Qur'an also says:

وَمَا أُمِرُوا إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ حُنَفَاءَ وَيُقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَيُؤْتُوا الزَّكَاةَ ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ الْقَيِّمَةِ

They were not commanded except to worship God, making religion sincere for Him as hunafa, to establish salat and give Zakat (Charity); and that is the upright religion.

Al-Bayyinah 98:5

This verse places four pillars beside one another: worship of God, sincerity of religion for Him, being hanif, establishing salat, and giving Zakat (Charity). Zakat (Charity) takes meaning inside this atmosphere, not inside compulsion, display, power-seeking, or political loyalty to an apparatus. When religion is sincere for God, Zakat (Charity) too must be for tazkiyah and justice. If Zakat (Charity) becomes a means to strengthen a ruling class, clergy, sect, party, or state, it is no longer compatible with sincerity in religion.

The link between salat and Zakat (Charity) prevents a major danger: separating worship from justice. If salat is separated from Zakat (Charity), religion can become individual worship with no effect in society. If Zakat (Charity) is separated from salat, financial aid can become pride, power, propaganda, or political administration. By placing the two together, the Qur'an protects the human being from both deviations. Salat turns the heart toward God, and Zakat (Charity) opens the hand toward creation.

This pairing also shows that Zakat (Charity) cannot become a soulless payment. Just as salat without presence of heart, humility, and remembrance of God is emptied of its spirit, Zakat (Charity) without truthfulness, sincerity, dignity, and justice is emptied of its truth. Salat is not merely movement, and Zakat (Charity) is not merely transfer of wealth. Salat must restrain the human being from indecency and wrong, and Zakat (Charity) must free him from miserliness, cruelty, and servitude to wealth.

A very important point must be noticed here: the Qur'an never makes salat an instrument of political obedience to a ruler. No one has the right to say that because salat is a pillar of religion, whoever does not perform salat according to state instruction is an enemy of the government and must be suppressed. The same logic must be observed regarding Zakat (Charity). If Zakat (Charity) is worship, it must not be treated as a political instrument. If Zakat (Charity) is a sign of faith, it must not be turned into a sign of loyalty to power.

This point becomes essential in the historical discussion of the Ridda wars. If some groups did not deny the foundation of faith, salat, or their belonging to religion, but disagreed over paying Zakat (Charity) to the political center of Medina, can they be placed in the same category as those who left the religion or claimed prophethood? The pairing of salat and Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an shows that both are important in the structure of faith; but that importance must not become an excuse to interpret every financial or political disagreement as apostasy. The Qur'an clarifies the criterion, but history sometimes turned the criterion into an instrument of power.

Zakat (Charity) beside salat means that faith must face both God and people. A prostration that remains indifferent to the pain of society has not yet reached its Qur'anic completion. A payment separated from remembrance of God, sincerity, and human dignity, even if named Zakat (Charity), is in danger of becoming a tax of power. The Qur'an places the two together to protect religion from two falls: the fall into worship without justice, and the fall into justice-performance without sincerity.

Therefore, in this treatise, when we speak of Zakat (Charity), we understand it beside salat: beside sincerity, being hanif, worship of God, repair of society, support of the deprived, fulfillment of covenant, and ethical responsibility. Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) is neither a payment separated from faith nor a tax separated from human dignity. Zakat (Charity) is the continuation of salat in the field of society; the same faith that bends toward God in salat must soften before the pain of creation in Zakat (Charity).

Part Five: Has the Qur'an Made Zakat (Charity) a Government Tax?

After examining Zakat (Charity) beside concepts such as salat, sadaqah, infaq, right, birr, and tazkiyah, we must now approach one of the most sensitive questions of this treatise: has the Qur'an made Zakat (Charity) intrinsically a government tax? Was Zakat (Charity), from the beginning, wealth that had to be handed to the center of power? Or does the Qur'an present Zakat (Charity) as a faith-based and social responsibility that can have order, workers, and executive mechanisms, while its ownership belongs neither to the state, the ruler, the caliphate, nor any political apparatus?

This question must not be treated simplistically. On one side, the Qur'an commands believers directly to give Zakat (Charity). On the other side, it speaks of the Prophet taking sadaqah and of workers over it. Therefore, one cannot say that Zakat (Charity) is purely individual action without structure; but one also cannot claim that the Qur'an turned it into a state tax. The truth lies between these two views: Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an is a divine and social right that must reach its true owners. Society may create order and workers to deliver that right, but this order must not become the ownership of power over the wealth of people and the deprived.

First, we must look at the important verse in Surat at-Tawbah:

خُذْ مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ صَدَقَةً تُطَهِّرُهُمْ وَتُزَكِّيهِم بِهَا وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ إِنَّ صَلَاتَكَ سَكَنٌ لَّهُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Take from their wealth a sadaqah by which you purify them and cause them to grow, and pray over them; indeed, your prayer is tranquility for them; and God is Hearing, Knowing.

At-Tawbah 9:103

In this verse, the command to take is clear. The Prophet is commanded to take a sadaqah from people's wealth. Therefore, the principle of organization, receiving, managing, and directing wealth toward the proper path cannot be denied. The Prophet did not merely invite people to good intentions; in his society, wealth too was drawn out of monopoly and entered the path of social repair. Our critique, then, is not directed at the principle of order and collection. It is directed at how later history turned this order into ownership of the ruler, the treasury of power, and an instrument of political obedience.

The verse itself states the purpose of this taking: "so that you may purify them and cause them to grow." The aim is purification and growth of human beings. Taking wealth here is for cutting human dependence on material possession, purifying the soul, and returning wealth to the path of right; not so that the ruler may dominate people's wealth and will. If someone sees only the command to take but sets aside the aim of purification, growth, and the Prophet's tranquil prayer, he has separated the verse from its spirit.

The Qur'an says regarding the mission of the Prophet that he recites God's verses, purifies people, and teaches them the Book and wisdom. Thus the Prophet's taking of sadaqah stands in continuation of his educational and purifying mission. The Prophet had workers to bring this order into society, but the direction of this work must be understood under the light of the Qur'an. The worker of Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'anic view is not appointed to transfer wealth from power to power; he is appointed to transfer right to the owner of right. If wealth is taken from those with means, its destination is the deprived, debtors, the stranded, the freeing of human beings, and the legitimate paths defined by the Qur'an, not unbounded accumulation at the center of power.

The following verse also shows that the truth of sadaqah ultimately faces God, not the state:

أَلَمْ يَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ يَقْبَلُ التَّوْبَةَ عَنْ عِبَادِهِ وَيَأْخُذُ الصَّدَقَاتِ

Do they not know that God accepts repentance from His servants and takes the sadaqat?

At-Tawbah 9:104

This verse comes after the command to take and opens the horizon further. The Prophet takes, but the Qur'an says that God takes the sadaqat. Therefore no earthly intermediary, whether the Prophet in the station of messengerhood or a worker in the station of execution, owns the truth of sadaqah. If the Prophet, as Messenger, takes wealth for the purification of souls, how can a ruler after him consider it the property of his political treasury?

The verse of the recipients of sadaqat also reveals this limitation:

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا وَالْمُؤَلَّفَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَالْغَارِمِينَ وَفِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۖ فَرِيضَةً مِّنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

The sadaqat are only for the poor, the destitute, the workers over them, those whose hearts are to be brought near, freeing those in bondage, debtors, in the path of God, and the stranded traveler: an obligation from God. God is Knowing, Wise.

At-Tawbah 9:60

This verse is one of the strongest proofs against the political confiscation of Zakat (Charity). The Qur'an does not say that sadaqat belong to the ruler's treasury. It does not say they are for the expansion of monarchy, wars of power, propaganda apparatuses, institutions of repression, or the luxury of the ruling class. Rather, it names their recipients clearly. The poor, the destitute, debtors, stranded travelers, and freeing those in bondage are destinations of sadaqat. Even the workers who collect them appear in the same list and beside the poor and destitute, not above them.

This point is extremely important: the workers of Zakat (Charity) are not its owners. They have a right to compensation for work, not ownership over the whole wealth. If the worker truly labors in this path, he may receive from the wealth according to legitimate and necessary work; but all sadaqat do not become the property of his apparatus. The verse legitimizes the worker's share, but it does not make government the absolute heir of the wealth of the deprived.

In the Prophet's society, workers could be public officials, and this should not be denied. But that fact does not prove the desired conclusion of later political jurisprudence. The existence of a public worker does not mean state ownership of Zakat (Charity). There is a difference between agency for delivering a right and rule over wealth in the name of religion. The Qur'an accepts the first and limits the second. The worker in the verse is a servant of the path of Zakat (Charity), not its center and destination.

From here we can say that the legitimacy of taking wealth depends on the correctness of spending it. If an institution takes wealth but does not deliver it to Qur'anic recipients, the mere act of taking has no legitimacy. If a government receives wealth in the name of Zakat (Charity) but spends it on power struggles, repression, sectarian propaganda, political luxury, or strengthening its domination, this is not Qur'anic Zakat (Charity), even if it is called Zakat (Charity). The Qur'an has determined the destination, and no ruler has the right to replace that destination with his own desire.

The command "give" must also be read carefully. The Qur'an repeatedly says: establish salat and give Zakat (Charity). This is a direct command to believers, but it does not mean Zakat (Charity) is scattered, disorganized, and entirely individual. This address can contain a collective and social responsibility. The believing community, as an ethical body, is charged with delivering the right to its people. If a trustworthy and righteous structure exists, payment can be organized through it. But if the structure deviates, swallows the right, marginalizes the poor, or turns Zakat (Charity) into an instrument of power, the original duty does not fall away from the believer; he must still deliver the right to its people.

In this meaning, the Qur'an says:

وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ ۚ وَمَا تُقَدِّمُوا لِأَنفُسِكُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍ تَجِدُوهُ عِندَ اللَّهِ

Establish salat and give Zakat (Charity); whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with God.

Al-Baqarah 2:110

Zakat (Charity) in this verse is part of the good that the human being sends ahead. Therefore, if someone pays only under government compulsion but does not know where his wealth goes and whether it reaches the real needy, the Qur'anic question remains. The Qur'an asks the believer for responsibility, not mere administrative submission.

Another verse places this command beside obedience to the Messenger and hope for mercy:

وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

Establish salat, give Zakat (Charity), and obey the Messenger, so that you may receive mercy.

An-Nur 24:56

Here too, obedience to the Messenger stands beside salat and Zakat (Charity), not obedience to every power that later rules in the name of the Messenger. Obedience to the Messenger is obedience to the divine message and the clear path of revelation. Every later structure must be measured by that message, not place itself in the Messenger's position.

The verse of empowerment in Surat al-Hajj must also be read with this precision:

الَّذِينَ إِن مَّكَّنَّاهُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ أَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَوُا الزَّكَاةَ وَأَمَرُوا بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَنَهَوْا عَنِ الْمُنكَرِ ۗ وَلِلَّهِ عَاقِبَةُ الْأُمُورِ

Those who, if We give them position in the land, establish salat, give Zakat (Charity), command what is recognized as good, and forbid what is rejected as wrong; and to God belongs the outcome of matters.

Al-Hajj 22:41

This verse speaks of righteous power, but it does not make power the owner of values. If a community gains position and capability in the land, it must establish salat, give Zakat (Charity), bring ma'ruf to life, and forbid munkar. But ma'ruf and munkar are not the property of government. A ruler cannot name whatever he wants ma'ruf and call every critic of himself munkar. Ma'ruf must be recognized through revelation, sound reason, justice, dignity, and human conscience. Munkar must truly be wrong, not a label for silencing critics.

The same logic applies to Zakat (Charity). Having position and power in the land means building conditions in which wealth leaves monopoly, the poor are not forgotten, the right of the deprived is delivered, miserliness and accumulation are broken, and the path of sadaqah and infaq is made easier. Power must serve the delivery of right, not make Zakat (Charity) a tribute for the survival of authority.

If someone concludes from this verse that government owns Zakat (Charity), he must accept the same logic regarding salat, because salat and Zakat (Charity) appear together. But it is clear that government does not own people's salat. Government can provide security, education, freedom of worship, and conditions for collective salat; but it cannot turn salat into an instrument of political loyalty. Zakat (Charity) is the same. A righteous government can organize the path of delivering right, but it has no right to detach Zakat (Charity) from its destination and turn it into a sign of obedience to itself.

The verse about Jesus in Surat Maryam clarifies the matter from the root:

وَجَعَلَنِي مُبَارَكًا أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُ وَأَوْصَانِي بِالصَّلَاةِ وَالزَّكَاةِ مَا دُمْتُ حَيًّا

He has made me blessed wherever I am and has enjoined upon me salat and Zakat (Charity) as long as I live.

Maryam 19:31

Jesus had no state, no political treasury, no army, no tax apparatus, and no governmental authority. He lived under the powers of his time. Yet the Qur'an says through his tongue that as long as he lives he has been enjoined to salat and Zakat (Charity). This verse alone shows that Zakat (Charity) cannot be intrinsically dependent on the state. Zakat (Charity) has meaning in the life of a prophet who has no state. Therefore, before Zakat (Charity) has a governmental form, it has a faith-based, ethical, and purifying root.

About Ishmael the Qur'an says:

وَكَانَ يَأْمُرُ أَهْلَهُ بِالصَّلَاةِ وَالزَّكَاةِ وَكَانَ عِندَ رَبِّهِ مَرْضِيًّا

He used to command his family to salat and Zakat (Charity), and he was pleasing to his Lord.

Maryam 19:55

Here Zakat (Charity) enters the sphere of family, upbringing, and the pleasure of the Lord. Ishmael commands his family to salat and Zakat (Charity), not to pay tax to a state. This verse shows the field of Zakat (Charity) in faith-life, not in administrative apparatuses.

Regarding other prophets, the Qur'an says:

وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَئِمَّةً يَهْدُونَ بِأَمْرِنَا وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْهِمْ فِعْلَ الْخَيْرَاتِ وَإِقَامَ الصَّلَاةِ وَإِيتَاءَ الزَّكَاةِ ۖ وَكَانُوا لَنَا عَابِدِينَ

We made them leaders guiding by Our command, and We revealed to them the doing of good deeds, establishing salat, and giving Zakat (Charity); and they were worshippers of Us.

Al-Anbiya 21:73

In this verse, Zakat (Charity) stands beside good deeds and worship. Zakat (Charity) is part of the prophetic path of repair: a path that returns wealth, faith, family, society, and justice toward God.

Now the boundary of the discussion can be clarified. The Qur'an is not opposed to organizing Zakat (Charity); it is not opposed to workers over sadaqat; it is not opposed to the Prophet taking; and it is not opposed to social order. But the Qur'an has not made Zakat (Charity) the property of government. This is the difference that historical jurisprudence and politics often conceal.

If righteous government enters this field, it is only the trustee of the right of the deprived. Its task is to bring wealth out of miserliness, accumulation, and monopoly, and deliver it to Qur'anic recipients. It may create structure, accounting, workers, and order for this task; but it has no right to treat Zakat (Charity) as its free political budget. It has no right to imagine itself owner of the wealth. It has no right to sacrifice Qur'anic spending to the interest of power. It has no right to call every critic or opponent of payment to its apparatus an apostate or enemy of religion.

The difference between Zakat (Charity) and taxation becomes clear here. Taxation in modern society is taken on the basis of citizenship law and the public needs of the state; it is necessary for education, health, security, infrastructure, roads, and administration, and that discussion has its own place. But Zakat (Charity) takes meaning from faith, purification of the self, the right of the deprived, dignity, truthfulness, and ethical responsibility. Taxation is an instrument of public administration, while Zakat (Charity) is a faith-based and social covenant. The two may approach one another in some social effects, but they are not the same.

If Zakat (Charity) dissolves completely into taxation, two great dangers arise: first, the state imagines itself owner of worship and people's wealth; second, nonpayment to the state is interpreted not as an administrative, political, or legal disagreement, but as departure from religion. This is the point that must be examined carefully and without fear in history. An act of worship that should purify the human being may become an instrument of political obedience; and a person who questions the legitimacy, use, or justice of the ruling apparatus may be silenced with religious accusations.

The principle of no compulsion in religion must also be kept in view:

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ قَد تَّبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ

There is no compulsion in religion; the path of maturity has become clear from misguidance.

Al-Baqarah 2:256

If Zakat (Charity) is a purifying act of worship, its spirit is destroyed by religious compulsion. Society can have financial law, public order, and supportive institutions; but turning an act of worship into a tool of religious coercion is not compatible with the spirit of the Qur'an. Religion becomes clear through growth, not fear. Zakat (Charity) must bring the human being and society toward growth, not pull wealth from people under the shadow of political threat.

Thus the answer of this section is as follows: the Qur'an recognizes Zakat (Charity) as a social responsibility with order, not as a government tax in the sense of state ownership over people's wealth. The Qur'an knows the taking of wealth, but it sets its purpose as purifying the human being and delivering right. The Qur'an knows workers, but it makes them servants of the path, not owners of the wealth. The Qur'an connects the empowerment of the righteous with Zakat (Charity), but it wants power to serve Zakat (Charity), not Zakat (Charity) to serve power.

Therefore, any reading that turns Zakat (Charity) into state taxation, government budget, an instrument of political obedience, or a criterion for takfir of opponents must bring a clear proof from the Qur'an. What is clearly obtained from the Qur'an is this: Zakat (Charity) must purify the soul and wealth, deliver the right of the deprived to them, free society from miserliness and accumulation, and any institution or government that enters this path must be a trustee of the Qur'an's aim, not its owner.

Part Six: The Recipients of Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an; The Right of the Deprived, Not the Free Budget of Power

After clarifying that the Qur'an does not bring Zakat (Charity) into the ownership of the state or ruler, we must now turn to the next question: for whom and in what paths is Zakat (Charity) spent? This question is the practical heart of the discussion, because any system that collects Zakat (Charity) but diverts its spending away from the Qur'anic path carries only the name of Zakat (Charity) while transforming its truth.

In Surat at-Tawbah, the Qur'an introduces the recipients of sadaqat with explicit restriction:

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا وَالْمُؤَلَّفَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَالْغَارِمِينَ وَفِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۖ فَرِيضَةً مِّنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

The sadaqat are only for the poor, the destitute, the workers over them, those whose hearts are to be brought near, freeing those in bondage, debtors, in the path of God, and the stranded traveler: an obligation from God. God is Knowing, Wise.

At-Tawbah 9:60

The verse begins with a particle of restriction: only. That means the path of expenditure is locked and limited. This boundary is vital. The Qur'an does not allow sadaqat to become floating, directionless wealth without owners in the hands of power. Zakat (Charity) and sadaqat are not the free budget of a treasury, to be spent however a ruler, caliph, administrative apparatus, or religious institution wishes. The verse defines the directions precisely, and through this definition it binds the hand of power.

This point must be clear from the beginning: Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an is not merely an optional ethical recommendation or a voluntary charity left to personal taste. When the Qur'an speaks of a "right" in wealth, the matter enters the field of binding social responsibility. The deprived person has a right in the wealth of the wealthy; and if that right is not delivered, a righteous society needs a just mechanism to recover it and deliver it to its true owners. But the great challenge is this: whatever the executive mechanism may be, it does not become the owner of Zakat (Charity). The main difference lies precisely at this narrow boundary: Zakat (Charity) can and must have enforcement, but enforcement exists to protect the right of the deprived, not to strengthen the authority of rulers.

Therefore, the issue must not be simplified by saying that Zakat (Charity) is left only to individual conscience. If the wealthy person deliberately blocks the right of the deprived and society has no way to recover it, the meaning of right is lost. But on the other side, if this enforcement falls into the hands of an institution that sees itself as owner of religion and people's lives, Zakat (Charity) becomes an instrument of religious coercion and political domination. The Qur'anic path lies between these two falls: the right has enforcement, but the institution that enforces must be limited, accountable, trustworthy, and bound to the specific recipients of the Qur'an.

In reality, a public institution for carrying out Zakat (Charity) may be unavoidable, because taking right from those who refuse cannot be done without order, judgment, and executive force. But the existence of such an institution does not mean state ownership of the wealth of Zakat (Charity). Just as a judge in a financial dispute does not become the owner of the disputed property, the executive institution of Zakat (Charity) does not become the owner of the wealth of the deprived. Its duty is only to deliver the right. If that institution leaves the path of trust and imagines itself as the destination of wealth, it is no longer an institution of Zakat (Charity); it is an apparatus of confiscation in the name of religion.

The Qur'an insists on this rights-based view elsewhere: in their wealth is a known right for the one who asks and the deprived. When the deprived person is the owner of right, the ruler cannot imagine himself as the absolute owner of its disposal.

Each category in At-Tawbah 9:60 must be read carefully. The poor are those whose lives are under the pressure of need. The masakin are those whose situation has been brought to stillness, weakness, and social immobility. The workers over it are administrators and agents whose service is necessary for delivery, but who must remain within their limited share. Those whose hearts are brought near refer to a social and reconciliatory dimension; this cannot be turned into the purchase of loyalty for rulers. Freeing those in bondage shows that Zakat (Charity) has a liberation function, not merely a feeding function. Debtors are present because debt can crush human dignity. The path of God must be read under the Qur'an's own moral criteria, not expanded arbitrarily to include every war, party, propaganda campaign, or state project. The stranded traveler is the person cut off from normal support and in need of restoration to safety.

Thus the verse draws a map of human need and social repair. Its concern is not the appetite of government, but the wounds of society. It begins with poverty and destitution, includes administrative workers only as part of service, moves toward reconciliation, liberation, debt relief, the path of God, and the stranded traveler. This order itself teaches us that Zakat (Charity) is aimed at restoring human beings, not expanding institutions of power.

This also means that Zakat (Charity) has a protected destination. The poor and the deprived are not a decorative category placed beside the state; they are the reason the institution exists. If the first and most visible effect of a Zakat (Charity) system is the strengthening of offices, salaries, guards, commanders, party structures, or ideological messaging, then the institution has inverted the verse. The Qur'an begins from human need; power begins from institutional survival. These two directions must not be confused.

The phrase \"workers over it\" must also be held inside the verse, not pulled above it. Workers are recognized because any serious distribution system needs labor: identification, receiving, accounting, delivery, protection from fraud, and follow-up. But the worker's share is proof of limited service, not proof of ownership. If the worker becomes a ruling class, the category has been inflated beyond its Qur'anic function. The worker is a bridge; when the bridge becomes the city, the path has been lost.

The categories also reveal that Zakat (Charity) is not only emergency food relief. It includes debt, bondage, being stranded, social reconciliation, and the path of God. This shows that the Qur'an sees poverty as more than lack of cash. A human being may be trapped by debt, captivity, migration, loss of support, social fracture, or the breakdown of communal solidarity. Zakat (Charity) must therefore repair conditions, not merely distribute small sums while leaving structures of harm intact.

When governments or armed groups take Zakat (Charity) and spend it on weapons, palaces, propaganda, prisons, political loyalty, or the luxurious life of officials, they have not merely committed administrative error. They have changed the direction of a Qur'anic trust. The name Zakat (Charity) does not sanctify such spending. If the Qur'an has restricted the paths, no ruler can remove the restriction with political necessity.

Even the phrase "in the path of God" must not become a blank check. The path of God in the Qur'an is tied to justice, liberation, defense of the oppressed, sincerity, and the removal of oppression. It cannot be stretched to cover every governmental project, factional war, sectarian militia, or institutional advertisement. If "in the path of God" becomes a word that power can fill however it wants, then the restrictive word "only" at the beginning of the verse loses its function. The Qur'an does not open a loophole only to close nothing.

Therefore, the recipients of Zakat (Charity) show that Zakat (Charity) is a right of the vulnerable, not a free budget of power. It may have workers, but the worker is not the owner. It may have structure, but the structure is not the destination. It may have enforcement, but enforcement is for rights, not domination. The poor, the debtor, the one in bondage, and the stranded traveler stand at the center; the state, if present, stands at the margin as trustee.

Part Seven: Zakat (Charity) and Taxation; Similarities, Differences, and the Boundaries of Distortion

Zakat (Charity) and taxation can resemble one another in outward form. Both may involve taking wealth from people and spending it for social needs. Both may require order, accounting, fairness, and accountability. Both may be connected to public welfare. Yet similarity in form does not mean identity in essence. The Qur'an presents Zakat (Charity) as a matter of tazkiyah, right, faith, and social responsibility. Modern taxation is a legal instrument for public administration. Confusing the two opens a dangerous door.

A just tax system can be necessary. Roads, schools, hospitals, sanitation, security, civil administration, disaster response, and public services require collective funding. A society that rejects every form of taxation may leave the weak without infrastructure and make public life impossible. Therefore the critique of turning Zakat (Charity) into a state tax is not a critique of every tax. The issue is not whether society needs public revenue. The issue is whether the Qur'anic institution of Zakat (Charity) may be absorbed into the unrestricted budget of government.

Taxation is usually based on citizenship, law, and the needs of administration. Zakat (Charity) is based on faith, tazkiyah, the right of the deprived, and purification of the human relation to wealth. Tax can be spent on broad state functions, but Zakat (Charity) has Qur'anic recipients. Tax can be debated as public policy, but Zakat (Charity) is a trust tied to divine purpose. Tax may fund infrastructure, while Zakat (Charity) is directed toward the vulnerable and toward social repair as named by the Qur'an.

If Zakat (Charity) is dissolved into tax, two distortions occur. First, the government treats all collected wealth as its own budget, and the Qur'anic recipients lose their protected status. Second, payment to the government becomes identified with religious obedience. At that point, refusal to pay a particular state may be treated as refusal of Zakat (Charity) itself, and refusal of Zakat (Charity) may be treated as apostasy. This is the historical road that must be examined carefully.

The Qur'an does not forbid public organization. It also does not leave the right of the poor to the whims of the wealthy. But it does not give the ruler a free hand to absorb Zakat (Charity) into his general budget. The difference between trustee and owner must remain clear. The state may be an administrator; it is not the owner. The worker may be compensated; he is not the destination. The poor have a right; they are not recipients of the ruler's favor.

Taxation can be just or unjust. A secular state that collects taxes but spends them on corruption, war, class privilege, or oppression is morally blameworthy. A religious state that collects money under sacred language and spends it on the same things is even more dangerous, because it sanctifies injustice. The problem is not only financial; it is theological. When a state uses God's name to extract wealth and then spends it against God's moral aims, it has polluted religion with power.

For this reason, saying that taxation is necessary does not answer the question of Zakat (Charity). Public administration may need revenue, but the need of public administration does not erase the Qur'anic destination of Zakat (Charity). A hospital, road, or school may be a legitimate public need, and a just tax may support it. But Zakat (Charity) has its own moral architecture. If the state wants to fund all public services, it should speak honestly in the language of taxation. It should not take a Qur'anic trust whose recipients are named and then dissolve it into the broad appetite of administration.

Nor should the distinction be used to weaken social responsibility. Someone may say: since Zakat (Charity) is not the same as tax, I will pay neither. That is not the Qur'anic path. The Qur'an makes the right of the deprived real. The point is not to escape responsibility, but to protect responsibility from capture. Zakat (Charity) must not be swallowed by taxation; taxation must not become an excuse to neglect Zakat (Charity); and both must be measured by justice, transparency, and the actual condition of people.

Therefore, the boundary is this: Zakat (Charity) may cooperate with public systems, but it must not disappear inside them. If a modern state wants to manage Zakat (Charity), it must keep its account separate, its recipients clear, its spending transparent, and its institution accountable. If it cannot do that, then calling public taxation Zakat (Charity) becomes a distortion. Zakat (Charity) must remain tied to tazkiyah, dignity, rights, and the Qur'anic map of recipients.

Part Eight: After the Prophet's Death; From a Guided Community to a Crisis of Political Authority

After the Prophet's death, the Muslim community entered a difficult historical moment. The Prophet had been not only the carrier of revelation but also the central moral, social, and political point of unity for the community. His departure created a question that the Qur'an had not turned into a detailed constitutional procedure: how would political authority be transferred? Who would represent the center? How would tribes relate to Medina? What would become of covenants made in the Prophet's lifetime?

The situation was not simple. Some groups claimed prophethood and challenged the new religious order. Some tribes may have sought to leave the community entirely. Some resisted the political authority of Medina. Some may have continued to recognize Islam or salat but refused to send Zakat (Charity) to the new center. Later memory gathered many of these different phenomena under one title: ridda, apostasy. But historical reality appears more layered.

This distinction matters because the Qur'an does not teach careless naming. A false prophet, a tribe breaking a security covenant, a group refusing a tax collector, a community doubting the legitimacy of a new political center, and a person denying the foundation of faith are not the same phenomenon. They may occur at the same time and even overlap in a crisis, but overlap does not erase moral distinction. A Qur'anic reading must ask what exactly was denied, what exactly was broken, and what exactly was being defended.

The absence of the Prophet also changed the meaning of personal loyalty. During his life, giving to the Messenger was tied to revelation, trust, purification, and the living presence of the one commanded to deliver God's message. After his death, the political center could claim continuity, but it could not simply become identical to the Prophet's station. The question of continuity needed moral and Qur'anic argument; it could not be solved merely by inheriting the language of obedience.

The question is not whether Medina faced a genuine crisis. It did. A new community surrounded by tribal politics, fragile alliances, economic pressure, and competing claims of authority could easily have collapsed. The question is how that crisis was named, and what consequences followed from that naming. If every political refusal becomes apostasy, then religion becomes the language of state survival. If every refusal to send wealth to the center becomes refusal of Zakat (Charity), then Zakat (Charity) becomes a sign of political obedience.

This is where the Qur'anic criteria become necessary. The Qur'an distinguishes faith, hypocrisy, rebellion, covenant, justice, aggression, and wrongdoing. It does not permit us to collapse every disagreement into one theological label. To call someone an apostate is not a small administrative description. It is a religious judgment with enormous consequences. If political conflict is given that title too easily, takfir becomes a weapon of authority.

In this discussion, we are not claiming to know the hidden intentions of Abu Bakr, Umar, the Companions, or the tribes. Nor are we denying that some opponents may truly have broken with the religion or followed false prophets. The concern is structural: what happens when a political center uses the language of apostasy to define a complex crisis, including financial and tribal refusal? What happens when Zakat (Charity), whose Qur'anic aim is purification and the right of the deprived, becomes an index of obedience to the center?

The community after the Prophet needed order. But order and revelation are not identical. A political center may need to preserve unity, but preserving unity does not automatically give it the right to label every refusal as disbelief. The Qur'an teaches responsibility, covenant, justice, and resistance to oppression; it does not teach that the state inherits the Prophet's spiritual station in such a way that disagreement with the state becomes disagreement with God.

The crisis after the Prophet therefore must be read carefully, not as a simple tale of believers versus apostates, but as a mixture of faith, politics, tribe, finance, security, and authority. This does not absolve every opponent. It also does not sanctify every decision of the center. It places the matter under Qur'anic judgment rather than under inherited slogans.

Part Nine: Zakat (Charity) in the First Caliphate; From the Trust of the Deprived to a Sign of Obedience to the Center

In the first caliphate, Zakat (Charity) stood at a sensitive crossroads. On one side, it was a Qur'anic duty tied to faith, purification, the right of the deprived, and social repair. On the other side, in the political reality after the Prophet, payment to Medina became tied to recognition of the new center. The danger was that a trust belonging to the deprived could become a sign of obedience to political authority.

The famous reports about Abu Bakr's insistence on fighting those who separated salat and Zakat (Charity) must be read in this atmosphere. If someone denied the Qur'anic duty of Zakat (Charity) itself, then his position contradicted the Qur'an. But if someone accepted Islam and salat while disputing the obligation to send wealth to the new political center, the matter is different. The question becomes: was he denying the right of the deprived, or refusing the authority of Medina? These are not the same.

The difference is not a small technicality. Denying the right of the deprived attacks a Qur'anic moral foundation. Refusing a particular channel of payment may be a political, tribal, administrative, or trust-based dispute. A person may ask: who now has the right to collect? Where will the wealth go? Does the old covenant continue in the same form? Is the collector trustworthy? These questions can be wrong, selfish, or dangerous in a crisis, but they are not automatically disbelief. The Qur'an does not allow us to replace analysis with a slogan.

It is also possible that some refusals were mixed: part greed, part tribal autonomy, part distrust, part political resistance. Human history is rarely clean. But mixed motives are precisely why takfir is dangerous. When power uses one label for a mixed crisis, it can punish every layer with the most extreme religious category. The Qur'anic method requires distinction, testimony, justice, and restraint.

In the crisis of the first caliphate, these boundaries became severely blurred. Zakat (Charity), sadaqah, tax, pledge of allegiance, obedience, apostasy, rebellion, and preservation of public order became mixed. When these concepts are not distinguished, power can use the sanctity of religion to stabilize itself. This is the danger that repeated itself throughout later Muslim history: opposition to the ruler is presented as opposition to religion; refusal to pay the treasury is called refusal of Zakat (Charity); and questioning the legitimacy of spending is treated as rebellion against Islam.

The Qur'an, however, defines the destination of Zakat (Charity). If wealth is taken in the name of Zakat (Charity), it must go to the paths God named. The political center may organize that delivery, but it cannot make itself the destination. If the center becomes the measure, then Zakat (Charity) is transformed: no longer primarily the right of the poor, but a sign of submission to central authority.

This transformation may begin under emergency conditions. A leader may fear collapse. He may believe that if tribes stop sending wealth, the community will fragment. Such fears may be historically understandable. But understanding the fear does not remove the analytical question. Does emergency allow the language of religion to be used in a way that turns financial-political disagreement into apostasy? Does preserving the center justify making the center the measure of religion?

The first caliphate thus became a foundational moment. It shaped later imagination. If the refusal to send Zakat (Charity) to the center can be treated as apostasy, then future rulers inherit a powerful precedent. They can claim that obedience to their financial authority is obedience to religion. They can present resistance to unjust collection or spending as rebellion against God. The path from trust to authority begins here.

Part Ten: The Ridda Wars; Religious Apostasy or a Multi-Layered Crisis of a New State?

The wars known as the Ridda wars are among the most decisive events in early Islamic history. The common narrative describes them as wars against apostasy. But the historical field was not one-dimensional. It included false prophets, tribes leaving the Medinan order, political refusals, financial disputes, tribal autonomy, and possibly groups who maintained aspects of Islam while rejecting the authority of the new center. Placing all of this under one label hides the complexity of the crisis.

Some groups certainly moved into explicit religious opposition. Claims of prophethood, rejection of the Prophet's message, or open construction of rival religious authority cannot be reduced to tax disputes. But other cases appear to have involved the question of Zakat (Charity), allegiance, and whether obligations made during the Prophet's lifetime continued in the same form after his death. Here the issue becomes political and legal, not simply doctrinal apostasy.

Therefore the title \"Ridda wars\" itself should be handled carefully. It may describe part of the crisis, but if used as a blanket title for all opponents, it becomes an interpretation before the evidence is examined. The name guides the moral imagination of later generations. Once the entire event is called apostasy, the reader is trained to see the sword as theological necessity rather than as a mixture of political, security, financial, and religious decisions.

This is why a Qur'an-centered reading does not rush to romanticize the tribes or demonize Medina. Some tribal movements may have been unjust, opportunistic, violent, or religiously deviant. Medina may have had real reasons to fear collapse. But the existence of danger does not remove the need for moral categories. Emergency can explain severity; it cannot automatically sanctify every label used under pressure.

The danger in inherited memory is that it often absorbs every layer into the word ridda. Once that happens, the sword of takfir becomes easier to use. Instead of asking who denied God, who followed a false prophet, who broke a covenant, who refused unjust centralization, who disputed spending, and who sought tribal independence, all are placed under one theological label. This makes history easier to narrate, but less truthful to the complexity of events.

The Qur'an itself teaches careful judgment. It does not allow believers to rush into declaring people outside faith without clarity. It distinguishes aggression from disagreement, hypocrisy from weakness, covenant-breaking from ignorance, and denial of truth from political conflict. If this Qur'anic care is lost, religion becomes a tool of rapid classification.

The Ridda wars must therefore be read as a multi-layered crisis of a new state. They were about religion, but not only religion. They were about politics, but not only politics. They were about security, tribe, economy, prophecy claims, allegiance, and the authority of Medina. To recognize this complexity is not to deny the seriousness of the crisis; it is to prevent later power from using a simplified memory as a weapon against every opponent.

When Zakat (Charity) is pulled into such a crisis, its meaning is endangered. Instead of being the right of the deprived and a means of tazkiyah, it can become a test of loyalty. Instead of asking whether the poor received their right, the system asks whether the center received its payment. This shift is subtle but enormous. It changes the moral center of Zakat (Charity) from the deprived to the ruler.

Part Eleven: From Financial Refusal to Takfir; How Political Disagreement Took the Color of Apostasy

The movement from financial refusal to takfir is one of the most dangerous transformations in religious history. A person may fail in a financial duty, dispute the recipient institution, distrust the collector, question the political center, or refuse to pay an unjust apparatus. These are different states. If all of them are interpreted as apostasy, then religious language becomes a weapon for solving political and financial conflict.

The Qur'an does not deny that refusing the right of the deprived is a serious moral and religious offense. Hoarding, refusing infaq, consuming people's wealth falsely, and neglecting the poor are all condemned. But condemnation of injustice is not the same as declaring every financial dispute apostasy. The Qur'an's severity toward greed and hoarding must not be turned into a blank permission for rulers to excommunicate opponents.

Here the difference between sin, crime, rebellion, and disbelief must be preserved. A person may sin by withholding wealth. A group may commit a political crime by breaking a covenant. A tribe may rebel against an agreed order. A claimant may fall into disbelief by inventing prophethood or rejecting revelation. These are not one category. When they are fused, the ruler gains the ability to treat all resistance as disbelief and all disagreement as war against God.

The Qur'an repeatedly holds people responsible for their deeds, but it also warns against injustice in judgment. To name someone outside faith is a grave matter because it changes the treatment of life, property, honor, and belonging. If that naming becomes a tool of fiscal policy, religion has been placed under the command of the treasury.

In the early crisis, political authority needed unity. But when unity is protected through theological labeling, later generations inherit a dangerous tool. A ruler can say: whoever refuses my treasury refuses Zakat (Charity); whoever refuses Zakat (Charity) refuses Islam; therefore whoever refuses me is an apostate. This chain is not Qur'anic. It is a political chain using religious words.

The Qur'an centers the right of the deprived. If someone refuses that right and consumes wealth unjustly, he is blameworthy. But if someone asks whether the collector is trustworthy, whether the spending is Qur'anic, whether the political center is legitimate, or whether the wealth will reach its rightful recipients, these questions are not disbelief. In fact, such questions may arise from the very meaning of amanah.

Takfir closes the door of accountability. Once the critic is called apostate, his question no longer needs an answer. His blood, wealth, and dignity become vulnerable. This is why the transformation of political disagreement into apostasy is so dangerous. It changes the field from argument to elimination.

Qur'anic religion should do the opposite. It should clarify rights, protect the deprived, restrain power, demand justice, and leave final judgment of hearts to God. It should not allow rulers to hide behind sacred language when they are asked where the wealth went. The right to ask about Zakat (Charity) is not rebellion against religion; it is defense of the trust.

Part Twelve: Caliphate, Centralization, and Zakat (Charity); From Limited Trust to Accumulated Authority

As political authority centralized, Zakat (Charity) too was drawn toward the center. The logic of centralization is always strong: the center says that without unified collection there will be fragmentation, without a treasury there will be weakness, without obedience there will be disorder. Some of this may be true in administrative terms. But the danger begins when administrative necessity becomes religious ownership.

In the Qur'an, Zakat (Charity) is a trust with defined aims. It must purify the giver, protect the receiver's dignity, and deliver rights to the named paths. When a central authority collects it, that authority must remain a trustee. But centralization tends to accumulate power. The more wealth is gathered at the center, the more the center becomes capable of controlling people, rewarding loyalty, punishing refusal, and defining religious obedience.

This is the institutional problem. A trust may begin with a limited and sincere function, yet over time the office that manages the trust may gain interests of its own. It needs staff, buildings, guards, records, influence, and protection. Then the institution may begin to defend itself even when the original beneficiaries are no longer being served. Zakat (Charity) is especially vulnerable to this because its language is sacred. If an institution can claim that questioning it is questioning Zakat (Charity), then accountability becomes religiously dangerous.

The Qur'an's insistence on defined recipients is a safeguard against this. It prevents the institution from becoming the goal. The more centralized the system, the more necessary transparency becomes. Otherwise, the center gradually replaces the poor in the moral imagination of the community. People begin to ask whether the center has been paid, not whether the deprived have been restored.

Zakat (Charity) must not dissolve into an unbounded bayt al-mal. If bayt al-mal means a treasury of different trusts, each trust must retain its specific use. But if bayt al-mal means the ruler's free treasury, then Zakat (Charity) is swallowed. In such a condition, the poor person is no longer a rights-holder; he becomes a recipient of the power's ration. The worker is no longer the trustee of the poor; he becomes an employee of the state.

This change is subtle but deep. In the Qur'an, the poor person is the owner of right. In the system of power, the poor person receives assistance from the state. In the Qur'an, the wealthy person must understand that his wealth is a trust. In the system of power, the wealthy person learns only that he must pay the treasury. In the Qur'an, Zakat (Charity) repairs the human relation to God, wealth, and the deprived. In the system of power, Zakat (Charity) can become a tax receipt.

If the caliphate sees itself only as the manager of a trust, it must remain limited. But if it sees itself as owner of the trust, corruption begins at that moment. This corruption may begin with the intention of preserving order, but gradually becomes structure. Structure is stronger than personal intention. A caliph may have good intention, but once a mechanism is created that makes payment to the center the measure of religion, later rulers can use it for any aim.

The Qur'an warns against accumulation and circulation of wealth among the rich:

كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنكُمْ

So that it may not circulate only among the wealthy among you.

Al-Hashr 59:7

This principle is not only about wealthy individuals; it also concerns structures of power. If wealth is collected from people and circulates among the ruling class, commanders, officials, families, and dependent institutions, the verse has been violated, even if the government bears an Islamic name.

Our critique, then, is not against financial order. Society needs order. The critique is against the swallowing of trust by the structure of power. Zakat (Charity) must preserve its path: from the possessor to the holder of right; from monopoly to distribution; from miserliness to tazkiyah; from power to the dignity of the deprived. Wherever this path is reversed, Zakat (Charity) has been distorted.

From this angle, the Ridda wars were not only the beginning of a military conflict; they were the beginning of an institutional problem. After them, the question remained forever: is the Islamic ruler a limited worker of Zakat (Charity), or its political owner? The answer of power usually favored the ruler. The answer of the Qur'an favors the deprived.

Part Thirteen: From Caliphate to Monarchy; How Zakat (Charity) Was Swallowed by the Logic of the State

With the passage of time, the first caliphate turned into larger political structures. The state expanded, conquests increased, diwans were formed, administrative and military classes emerged, and power moved from the simplicity of Medina toward the complex apparatuses of empire. In such an atmosphere, the old danger became more intense: religious concepts were swallowed by the logic of the state.

In the Qur'an, Zakat (Charity) had appeared beside salat, faith, infaq, the right of the deprived, and tazkiyah. But in large governmental structures, Zakat (Charity) became one element in the financial order. The larger the state became, the more resources it needed. The more resources it needed, the more the class of officials and administrators grew. The more this class grew, the greater the danger that wealth would move away from its Qur'anic destination.

Monarchy, even when it names itself caliphate, has its own logic. Monarchy needs loyalty, taxation, army, propaganda, religious legitimacy, and control. If religion is placed in the service of monarchy, Zakat (Charity) too leaves its path. Then people no longer know whether the wealth they give in the name of God truly reaches the poor or enters the apparatus of power.

This transformation is not always announced openly. No ruler says, \"I am replacing the right of the poor with the needs of my throne.\" The language remains religious: justice, order, unity, defense, public interest, obedience, protection of Islam. But beneath the language, the direction of wealth changes. The poor become one item among many state concerns, while the survival of power becomes the hidden priority. Qur'anic vocabulary remains on the surface; political appetite moves underneath.

Once this happens, even acts of charity can become part of domination. The ruler distributes from what was already the right of the vulnerable, then presents himself as generous. The deprived person receives a portion of his own right through the hand of power and is expected to feel gratitude toward the very structure that controls him. This is the opposite of Qur'anic dignity.

The Qur'an warns against such danger by repeatedly critiquing religious classes whose religion has been distorted:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ الْأَحْبَارِ وَالرُّهْبَانِ لَيَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ النَّاسِ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَيَصُدُّونَ عَن سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

O you who have believed, many of the religious scholars and monks consume people's wealth falsely and turn away from the path of God.

At-Tawbah 9:34

This verse is not only a critique of historical Jews and Christians; it reveals a law of religious corruption. Whenever a religious class forms that takes people's wealth in the name of God and blocks the path of God for the sake of power and its own interests, the same pattern has repeated. If Muslims imagine that this verse is only about others and can never apply to themselves, they have committed precisely the error the Qur'an critiques.

In religious monarchy, Zakat (Charity) can be distorted in three ways. First, it becomes a source of income for power. Second, it becomes an instrument for legitimizing the ruler. Third, it becomes a means of social control: whoever pays is obedient, and whoever asks questions is accused.

In such a condition, the poor person too is transformed from a rights-holder into a propaganda object. The ruler helps the poor, but not in order to deliver their right; rather, to display his own justice. Sadaqah leaves the path of tazkiyah and becomes a performance of power. The Qur'an rejects such performance. Infaq accompanied by reminders, injury, display, and domination loses its soul.

لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُم بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ

Do not invalidate your sadaqat with reminders of favor and harm.

Al-Baqarah 2:264

If an individual invalidates his sadaqah with condescension and injury, a government too can invalidate the spirit of Zakat (Charity) through political condescension, humiliation of the poor, propaganda of power, and performative spending. Zakat (Charity) must not become a means of humiliating the deprived. The poor person is a holder of right, not an emotional debtor to the ruler.

The institutional transformation from early caliphate to monarchy was not only a change of names; it was also a change in financial logic. When diwan, kharaj, jizyah, fay', booty, Zakat (Charity), and various taxes were placed together inside the apparatus of power, the danger was that all would be translated into one language: the language of the treasury. In the language of the treasury, the primary question is not what Qur'anic destination each type of wealth has; the question is how many resources the state possesses and how it can use them for its survival.

Here Zakat (Charity), if not protected, is swallowed by the logic of monarchy. Outwardly the name Zakat (Charity) remains; the jurist speaks of nisab, the preacher mentions its virtue, the ruler speaks of his justice. But in practice, the right of the deprived becomes part of the performance of power. The poor person, instead of being a rights-holder, becomes a witness to the ruler's generosity.

From caliphate to monarchy, the most important transformation was that religion gradually became the language of legitimizing power. The jurist, judge, preacher, diwan, army, and treasury entered one network. Of course, within this history there were righteous people, courageous scholars, and just rulers. But our discussion is not about individuals. It is about structure. The structure of power, even with good individuals, consumes religion for its own benefit if left without restraint.

Therefore Zakat (Charity) in religious monarchy is no longer merely a financial ruling; it is an index for understanding the relation between religion and state. If Zakat (Charity) empowers the poor, it is closer to the Qur'an. If it empowers the ruler, it is distorted. If it frees the deprived, it is on the path of the Qur'an. If it makes people more submissive and silences questioners, it has left the path of the Qur'an.

Part Fourteen: Juristic Zakat (Charity); From Administrative Order to Numerical Freezing

Historical jurisprudence tried to make Zakat (Charity) executable. This effort should not be rejected wholesale. Society without criteria, nisab, calculation, timing, and order cannot have a stable financial system. Jurists dealt with the real world: livestock, wheat, dates, gold, silver, trade, agriculture, and people's needs. They wanted to clarify obligations and prevent disorder.

But the problem began when administrative order took the place of the spirit of the Qur'an. What should have been a tool for understanding and execution became a final and sacred form of ruling. Numbers, nisabs, and lists of wealth became so fixed that many imagined the Qur'an itself had revealed those tables. Yet in the matter of Zakat (Charity), the Qur'an clarifies the aim, spirit, direction, right, and recipients more than it gives numerical tables.

The most famous example is the rate of 2.5 percent. This number is so deeply established in the minds of many Muslims that it is as if a direct verse exists about it. But the Qur'an does not state such a number as a general rate of Zakat (Charity). The number belongs to juristic and report-based tradition, not to a direct Qur'anic text. The problem is not that jurisprudence created numbers for implementation; the problem is that the number became the ceiling of justice.

When a tool becomes sacred, it stops serving the aim. A number can help people calculate. It can prevent confusion and arbitrary demand. It can make administration possible. But if the number becomes the whole meaning of Zakat (Charity), then the living question of need disappears. A society may drown in debt, hunger, unemployment, displacement, and structural poverty, while the wealthy person says: I have paid the percentage, therefore the matter is over. The Qur'an does not teach such moral closure.

The same problem applies to lists of Zakat (Charity)able property. Lists are useful in their context, but the Qur'an's concern is not the museum of ancient property types. The Qur'an is concerned with wealth, hoarding, circulation among the rich, the right of the deprived, and purification of the self. If wealth changes its form while responsibility remains tied only to old forms, the wealthy learn how to hide from God behind the history of jurisprudence.

In some juristic systems, Zakat (Charity) was defined through closed lists of traditional forms of wealth, as if the financial duty of religion dealt only with a few known goods of the old economy: livestock, grain, gold, silver, and the like. Even in jurisprudence that includes trade and cash, the danger remains: if historical lists take the place of Qur'anic principle, the real capital of the age disappears from view.

This is the crisis of nisab in frozen jurisprudence. If an exhausted farmer in Sistan or Helmand struggles to cultivate land and his small crop becomes subject to tithe or Zakat (Charity), while the owner of a large exchange business, apartment towers, commercial networks, or complex financial assets escapes through narrow definitions, the spirit of the Qur'an has been reversed. The Qur'an did not come to take from the visible weak and leave the structurally hidden powerful untouched.

Today the value of a large technology company, financial network, real-estate holding, or investment fund may exceed the wealth of thousands of farmers and herders. If Zakat (Charity) jurisprudence still speaks only the language of camel, sheep, wheat, and coin and cannot see modern capital, it has buried Qur'anic justice inside a historical form. The Qur'an deals with the truth of wealth, not only with wealth's old clothing.

When the number becomes the ceiling, the human being considers himself finished after paying the minimum. A wealthy person with billions in complex assets may calm his conscience through a limited calculation, while society remains under the pressure of poverty, debt, illness, and injustice. The Qur'an does not invite the human being to such minimalism. The Qur'an says:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ مَاذَا يُنفِقُونَ ۖ قُلِ الْعَفْوَ

They ask you what they should spend. Say: the surplus beyond need.

Al-Baqarah 2:219

Al-'afw has a dynamic horizon. The human being must measure what is surplus, what is genuine need, and what is only hoarding, fear, accumulation, and attachment. Numerical jurisprudence, if it kills this dynamism, weakens the spirit of infaq.

Classical jurisprudence formed in a world of herding, agriculture, and simple trade. That world knew camel, cow, sheep, dates, wheat, gold, and silver. But today's economy is not limited to these. Today wealth hides in factories, shares, banks, insurance, financial markets, urban real estate, multinational companies, intellectual property, data, digital assets, industrial monopolies, and networks of capital. If Zakat (Charity) jurisprudence cannot see this reality, the poorest traditional producer comes under obligation while the largest accumulations of capital escape Qur'anic responsibility.

This is inversion. The Qur'an wants wealth not to circulate only among the rich. Frozen jurisprudence may help preserve the circulation of wealth among the rich, because instead of asking about the real concentration of wealth, it looks only at old lists of property. If wealth appears in a form known by old jurisprudence, it has Zakat (Charity); if it hides in a modern and complex form, it leaves the field. This is not Qur'anic justice; it is captivity inside the historical form of jurisprudence.

Of course, the solution is not for everyone to say whatever they want in the name of the spirit of the Qur'an. That too is dangerous. We must distinguish between the text of the Qur'an, the aims of the Qur'an, and the executive tools. The Qur'anic text clarifies recipients, the right of the deprived, tazkiyah, infaq, qist, and the prevention of concentrated wealth. The executive tool must be measured by time, economy, social structure, and people's real capacity. Tools can change; the aim must not change.

Therefore, critiquing Zakat (Charity) jurisprudence is not a critique of jurisprudence as such; it is a critique of frozen jurisprudence. If jurisprudence is alive, it must return to the Qur'an and ask: does today's Zakat (Charity) system truly support the poor? Does it free the debtor? Does it make great capital responsible? Does it bring wealth out of monopoly? Or does it pressure only traditional forms of wealth while leaving modern capital free?

Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) cannot be imprisoned in lifeless numbers. Numbers are necessary, but not sufficient. Calculation is necessary, but the destination is more important. Order is necessary, but if order works in favor of power and wealth, it is no longer Qur'anic.

Part Fifteen: Zakat (Charity) in the Contemporary World; From Religious Emirate to Ideological Taxation

In the contemporary world, the old distortions of Zakat (Charity) have not disappeared; they have taken new forms. Religious states, ideological emirates, armed groups, sectarian organizations, and institutions claiming sacred authority may collect wealth in the name of Zakat (Charity), shari'ah, Islamic order, or support for religion. But the decisive question remains Qur'anic: where does the wealth go, who benefits from it, and does it purify the giver and strengthen the right of the deprived, or does it strengthen the apparatus of power?

In some modern contexts, the poorest people are pressured in the name of Zakat (Charity) while the powerful remain beyond accountability. Farmers, laborers, villagers, widows, people without stable income, and crisis-stricken communities may be forced to pay, while the collected wealth is spent on armed conflict, internal control, propaganda, offices, salaries, intelligence networks, prisons, and the consolidation of rule. This is not Zakat (Charity); it is ideological taxation under a religious name.

The cruelty of this distortion is that the people who should be protected by Zakat (Charity) become sources from which power extracts. The Qur'an places the vulnerable at the destination of the flow; distorted authority places them at the beginning of extraction. The poor farmer, the exhausted laborer, the woman without income, the displaced family, and the village under pressure become payers to the machine that claims to protect religion. This reverses the current of the Qur'an.

Such systems often preserve religious vocabulary with great seriousness. They may speak of shari'ah, emirate, obedience, jihad, and obligation. But vocabulary cannot replace destination. If the destination is the strengthening of coercive power rather than the repair of human life, the vocabulary has become a mask. A Qur'anic test must ask not what the institution calls the money, but what the money becomes after collection.

The tragedy becomes greater when refusal to pay such structures is treated as disobedience to religion, opposition to Islam, or rebellion against an emirate. Then the old mechanism of the Ridda wars is reproduced in contemporary form: theological language is used to solve the budgetary crisis of war. Instead of asking whether the institution is trustworthy, whether the poor receive their right, and whether the spending is Qur'anic, the questioner is accused.

In such a structure, Zakat (Charity) is no longer the right of the deprived; it becomes part of the economy of domination. The poor may receive a small package of help, but the whole system is not built to free them; it is built to preserve power. When wealth taken in the name of God becomes fear, prison, war, and suppression, its religious name has no value.

Here we must be careful that our critique does not become an excuse to deny religious giving and charity. Many believers give Zakat (Charity) and sadaqah with pure intention. Many small and sincere institutions truly help the poor. Our critique is not directed at the principle of help. It is directed at structures that turn Zakat (Charity) into a source of authority, legitimacy, social control, and religious propaganda.

In the modern world, another danger exists: performative charity. Wealthy people, states, and religious institutions can use a small part of their wealth to build an image of benevolence, while the same economic structure from which they benefit produces poverty. Such Zakat (Charity) or sadaqah, if not joined with structural repair, may be only a painkiller, not a cure.

The Qur'an does not ask only for individual feeding; it asks that the relation between wealth, power, and the human being be repaired. If an economic system makes millions poor and then gives them food packages from a small portion of that same wealth, this is not Qur'anic justice. Zakat (Charity) must treat the wound, not only cover the face of the wound.

On the other hand, the modern state too, even if it takes taxes in secular language, is not morally defensible if it produces injustice, corruption, war, and discrimination. Therefore our critique is not directed only at religious governments. Every structure that takes wealth from people and spends it in the service of the ruling class, war, repression, or concentration of wealth is far from the spirit of qist. The difference is that religious government carries a greater danger, because it sanctifies its oppression with the name of God.

The contemporary criterion is therefore the same Qur'anic criterion: does the wealth reach its people? Does the poor person gain dignity? Is the debtor freed? Is the human being in bondage released? Does wealth leave monopoly? Is the collecting apparatus accountable? Is the questioner safe? Does the wealth return to human life instead of war and propaganda?

If the answer is no, the name Zakat (Charity) changes nothing. Zakat (Charity) does not become Qur'anic by the name Zakat (Charity); it becomes Qur'anic by aim, trust, qist, transparency, and tazkiyah.

Part Sixteen: Rebuilding Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) Today; From Frozen Jurisprudence to Living Justice

Now we must move beyond critique and ask: if Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) is not the free tax of the state, not disorganized voluntary charity, not a frozen juristic table, and not an instrument of religious power, how should it be understood and implemented today?

The first principle is that Zakat (Charity) must return to the Qur'an. Returning to the Qur'an means returning to aims: tazkiyah, growth, the right of the deprived, removal of poverty, liberation of human beings, help for the debtor, support for the stranded, repair of social bonds, and prevention of concentrated wealth. Any system that ignores these aims, however religious its appearance, has moved away from Zakat (Charity).

The second principle is distinguishing Zakat (Charity) from public taxation. A state may take just taxes, but it must not call all its taxes Zakat (Charity). Zakat (Charity) has Qur'anic expenditure. Public taxation may be for education, health, roads, security, infrastructure, and administration. The two may support one another, but they are not identical. If the state wants to collect Zakat (Charity), its account must be separate, its spending clear, and its aim aligned with the Qur'an.

The third principle is complete transparency. Every institution that collects Zakat (Charity) must report: how much was collected? From where did it come? How much was spent on administration? How much reached the poor, the destitute, debtors, the freeing of human beings, and stranded travelers? Who made decisions? Who supervised? If Zakat (Charity) is a trust, secrecy is betrayal.

Transparency is not a modern luxury added from outside religion; it is a natural consequence of amanah. A trust that cannot be inspected is already in danger. If the collector says, \"trust us because we speak in the name of religion,\" he has misunderstood trust. The greater the sacred language, the greater the duty of clarity. The wealth was not given for the collector's dignity; it was given for the right of the deprived and for the purification of the giver.

The fourth principle is locality alongside broad solidarity. Zakat (Charity) must first see nearby pains: family, relatives, neighborhood, city, and surrounding society. But it must not become closed tribalism. The stranded traveler, debtor, migrant, refugee, captive, and distant distressed person are also present in the Qur'anic field. Therefore a Zakat (Charity) system must have local roots and human horizons.

The fifth principle is accountability of the executive institution. The workers over Zakat (Charity) have a right to compensation, but they must not become a fat class. Administrative cost must be limited, clear, and proportionate. If most of the wealth is spent on offices, salaries, advertising, buildings, power, and management, Zakat (Charity) has been swallowed. The worker must see himself as a servant of the poor, not owner of the wealth.

The sixth principle is dynamism in identifying wealth. Zakat (Charity) must not remain limited to livestock and traditional crops. Wherever real wealth accumulates, Qur'anic responsibility must be seen. Industrial capital, shares, trade, large real estate, financial profits, complex assets, and monopolistic resources cannot hide behind the silence of old jurisprudence. The Qur'an deals with the reality of wealth, not merely its historical form.

The seventh principle is avoiding takfir. If someone denies Zakat (Charity) and rejects the right of the deprived, his statement is contrary to the Qur'an. But every disagreement over method of payment, amount, receiving institution, or place of spending is not apostasy. We must distinguish denial of the deprived person's right from critique of the collecting structure. A Qur'anic society must not use the language of takfir to solve financial disagreement.

The eighth principle is connecting Zakat (Charity) with economic repair. Zakat (Charity) must not be only a painkiller for poverty. It must be joined with struggle against riba, economic gambling, hoarding, corruption, monopoly, waste, discrimination, and concentration of wealth. If the economic structure produces poverty every day and Zakat (Charity) only calms a little pain, the Qur'an's aim has not been fulfilled.

The ninth principle is the dignity of the receiver. The poor person must not be humiliated. The needy must not become an instrument of propaganda. Photos, videos, display, reminders of favor, humiliation, and psychological control kill the spirit of Zakat (Charity). The Qur'an says not to invalidate sadaqat with reminders and harm. This principle applies to individuals and institutions alike.

The tenth principle is freedom to question. People have the right to ask about Zakat (Charity). Asking about its spending is not irreligion; it is the essence of trustworthiness. If an institution takes money in the name of religion but fears accounting, it deserves suspicion. The light of the Qur'an does not fear transparency.

Based on these principles, Zakat (Charity) today can become a living mechanism of justice: not a religious state tax, not scattered charity without effect, not an instrument for legitimizing the ruler, but an accountable, transparent, local, and human network for delivering right to the rights-holder and breaking the monopoly of wealth.

Such a network can include families, neighborhoods, trustworthy civil institutions, transparent public bodies, and specialized programs for debt relief, food security, housing, migration, medical need, and liberation from exploitative dependency. The form may vary by time and place. What cannot vary is the aim: wealth must move from hoarding toward life, from domination toward dignity, and from fear toward trust.

Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) must purify the wealthy and empower the deprived. If only the wealthy person feels lighter but the deprived person remains under pressure, Zakat (Charity) is incomplete. If the poor person receives a package of help but the structure of poverty remains, Zakat (Charity) has not reached its aim. If the Zakat (Charity) institution becomes stronger but the poor person does not become freer, Zakat (Charity) has been distorted.

Rebuilding Zakat (Charity) means returning it to its original place: a divine trust in human wealth, for the purification of the soul and the repair of society.

Part Seventeen: Conclusion; Returning Zakat (Charity) from Religious Taxation to Qur'anic Tazkiyah

This treatise began with a simple question: what is Zakat (Charity) in the Qur'an? But the answer took us into many fields: language, root, tazkiyah, sadaqah, infaq, salat, Qur'anic recipients, taxation, caliphate, the Ridda wars, jurisprudence, monarchy, and the contemporary world. This breadth shows that Zakat (Charity) is not a small financial ruling; it is one of the central knots in the relation between religion, wealth, power, and justice.

In the Qur'an, Zakat (Charity) is first of all tied to purity and growth. Zakat (Charity) brings wealth out of monopoly and miserliness and frees the human being from bondage to wealth. But this purity is not only inward and individual. Zakat (Charity) must affect society: support the poor, lift the destitute from immobility, free the debtor, not abandon the stranded traveler, move the person in bondage toward freedom, and bring wealth out of closed circulation among the wealthy.

The Qur'an determines the destination of Zakat (Charity) and sadaqat. That destination is not the free treasury of the state. The poor, the destitute, workers over it, those whose hearts are to be brought near, freeing those in bondage, debtors, the path of God, and the stranded traveler are specific paths. The state, if it enters this field, is not owner; it is trustee. The worker has a right to compensation, but he has no right to become the destination.

Zakat (Charity) has obligation, but its obligation is for the benefit of the deprived, not the benefit of rulers. If the wealthy person consumes the right of the deprived, society must have a way to deliver the right to its owner. But this enforcement must not become the sanctification of power. The important difference is precisely here: Zakat (Charity) is rights-based, but it is not state-owned. It has enforcement, but its destination is fixed. It has order, but it must not serve domination.

History after the Prophet showed how this point could become a place of slipping. In the crisis of the first caliphate, Zakat (Charity) moved from the right of the deprived toward a sign of obedience to the center. The Ridda wars were not merely wars against doctrinal apostasy; they were a mixture of doctrinal, political, tribal, financial, and security crises. When all of these were placed under the single title apostasy, the way opened for political takfir.

To see the path of distortion, the historical movement can be summarized as follows:

Stage 1: The Qur'anic text: Zakat (Charity) equals the right of the deprived plus purification of the self; the destination is fixed in favor of the lower classes.

Stage 2: Saqifah and the Ridda wars: Zakat (Charity) becomes an index of loyalty to the center of Medina; refusal to send it to the center moves near the accusation of political apostasy.

Stage 3: The era of diwan and administrative state: Zakat (Charity) becomes one source beside kharaj, fay', jizyah, and booty; the danger is dissolution into the logic of the treasury.

Stage 4: Empire and religious monarchy: Zakat (Charity) becomes an instrument of legitimacy, display of justice, and social control; the poor person changes from a rights-holder into the receiver of the favor of power.

Stage 5: Frozen jurisprudence: Zakat (Charity) becomes numerical tables and traditional forms of wealth; complex modern capital disappears from view.

Stage 6: The contemporary age: Zakat (Charity) becomes ideological taxation in the hands of armed groups or emirates; theological language is used to fund the machinery of war and domination.

This critique does not mean denying the difficulty of Medina's situation. Medina truly faced the danger of collapse. But political necessity has no right to confiscate the language of religion. If the matter is preservation of a center of power, it should be examined in the language of politics and security, not by calling every refusal to pay the center departure from religion. This joining of political necessity with religious takfir became one of the most dangerous inheritances in Islamic history.

After that, caliphates and monarchies repeatedly used religion to stabilize power. Zakat (Charity), kharaj, jizyah, booty, fay', and taxes mixed inside the state structure. The poor person who was a rights-holder in the Qur'an sometimes became a receiver of the ration of power. The worker who should have been a servant of the path of Zakat (Charity) sometimes became part of a fat administrative or religious class. And asking about the spending of wealth was sometimes presented as opposition to religion.

Historical jurisprudence, despite its efforts at order, sometimes imprisoned Zakat (Charity) inside fixed numbers and nisabs. The problem is not calculation; the problem is when calculation replaces the destination. The Qur'an does not want dry numbers; it wants living justice. If a number is implemented but poverty, debt, monopoly of wealth, and humiliation of the deprived continue, the purpose of Zakat (Charity) has not been realized.

In the contemporary world, the same distortions have taken new forms. Religious states, ideological emirates, armed groups, and sectarian institutions can take wealth from people in the name of Zakat (Charity) and shari'ah. But if the result is suppression, war, propaganda, ruling class, and silencing of people, this is not Zakat (Charity). A religious name does not purify injustice.

Returning to the Qur'an means returning to clear criteria: the right of the deprived, trust, qist, transparency, no compulsion, fulfillment of covenant, rejection of political takfir, and prevention of concentrated wealth. Zakat (Charity) must purify the human being, not fatten power. It must see the poor as rights-holder, not as display. It must free the wealthy from miserliness, not only from guilt. It must bring society closer to justice, not make the state more sacred.

If Zakat (Charity) becomes religious taxation, it loses its spirit. If it is reduced to scattered and ineffective charity, it loses its social power. If it is imprisoned in frozen tables, it loses its Qur'anic dynamism. If it becomes an instrument of takfir, it falls from tazkiyah into corruption.

Qur'anic Zakat (Charity) is another path: the path of purity and justice, right and trust, freeing the human being from miserliness and poverty, breaking the monopoly of wealth, and connecting worship with social responsibility.

So the end of this treatise returns to the same principle with which it began: Zakat (Charity) comes from the root of tazkiyah. Every reading that makes the human being and society purer, more just, freer, and more trustworthy is closer to the Qur'an. And every reading that turns Zakat (Charity) into an instrument of power, fear, takfir, accumulation, and domination, even if it bears the name of religion, is far from the truth of the Qur'an.
