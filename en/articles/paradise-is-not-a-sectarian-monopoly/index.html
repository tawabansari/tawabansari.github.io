Articles and Reflections

Paradise Is Not the Monopoly of One Sect or One Religious Law

Introduction

One of the most dangerous changes a religious tradition can undergo begins when guidance gives way to identity. Instead of asking where we stand before The God, how we respond to truth, and what our actions amount to, we gradually take comfort in belonging to the right community: surely salvation will be ours in the end. A name, a religious lineage, a sect, or an inherited religious law can then take the place of faith, conduct, and accountability.

The claim is not always as blunt as “Whatever we do, paradise belongs to us.” It can take a more sophisticated and reassuring form. A person may sin, suffer punishment, and even spend time in the Fire; yet membership in the right group guarantees the outcome. Eventually that person will emerge and enter paradise. Someone outside the group, meanwhile, may spend a lifetime practicing honesty, justice, and kindness, but without the approved name and religious law, no such guarantee is available.

Within this framework, conduct still appears to matter. Yet religious identity becomes an insurance policy for the final outcome. Sin may affect the length or severity of punishment, while membership in one’s own community settles the ultimate destination in advance.

The Qur’an does not criticize this failing only in other people. The same text that calls Jewish and Christian claims to exclusive possession of paradise “wishful hopes” tells its own audience: “Neither your wishful hopes nor those of the People of the Book.” Muslims cannot read these verses against others and then reproduce the same conviction under a different name.

This article asks:

Has The God guaranteed final salvation through membership in a sect or adherence to a particular religious law, or does He hold each person responsible for faith, conduct, and their own response to truth?

Asking this does not mean that salvation has no criteria. The Qur’an speaks of faith, righteous action, doing good, and reverent vigilance; it sets against them wrongdoing, kufr, associating partners with The God, and sin. The purpose here is not to remove those criteria. It is to challenge the assumption that a community’s name, or affiliation with a particular religious law, carries an independent privilege before The God’s judgment.

The title deliberately avoids saying that paradise is not the monopoly of “one dīn.” In contemporary speech, “religion” commonly means a historical religious system such as Islam, Christianity, or Judaism. That usage does not cover the same range of meanings as dīn in the Qur’an. Sharīʿah concerns a prescribed way of practice; islām, in Qur’anic usage, speaks of surrender to The God before we treat it as the historical name of a community. These distinctions are examined in Dīn, Islām, and Sharīʿah in the Qur’an.

The question here is narrower, and more unsettling:

Who owns paradise?

“Paradise Is Ours Alone”: Where Is Your Proof?

The Qur’an records an especially explicit claim to exclusive salvation:

«وَقَالُوا لَنْ يَدْخُلَ الْجَنَّةَ إِلَّا مَنْ كَانَ هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَىٰ تِلْكَ أَمَانِيُّهُمْ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ»

2:111

They said: “No one will enter paradise unless they are Jewish or Christian.” Those are their wishful hopes. Say: “Bring your proof, if you are truthful.” (Al-Baqarah 2:111)

The Qur’an calls this claim amānī: hopes people want to be true. But it does more than tell them they are mistaken. It makes a clear demand: “هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ”—bring your proof. If The God has granted a community exclusive assurance of paradise, where is the evidence?

The following verse matters even more. If the problem were merely that Jews and Christians had chosen the wrong group name, the answer would be simple: “No, paradise belongs only to Muslims.” Yet that is not what the Qur’an says:

«بَلَىٰ مَنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَلَهُ أَجْرُهُ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ»

2:112

Rather, whoever surrenders their face to The God while doing good will have their reward with their Lord. No fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve. (Al-Baqarah 2:112)

There is a telling absence in this answer. One community’s name is not replaced by another’s; the answer is surrender and doing good. If we read the verse and conclude, “Exactly: paradise does not belong to Jews and Christians; it belongs to us Muslims,” we have changed the names without understanding the verse’s reasoning.

“The Fire Will Touch Us for Only a Few Days”: Where Is Your Covenant?

The same failing appears a few verses earlier:

«وَقَالُوا لَنْ تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ إِلَّا أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَةً»

2:80

They said: “The Fire will touch us for only a limited number of days.” (Al-Baqarah 2:80)

The Qur’an does not ask how many centuries old this belief is, how many scholars have transmitted it, or how many books endorse it. It asks one question:

«قُلْ أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا فَلَنْ يُخْلِفَ اللَّهُ عَهْدَهُ أَمْ تَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ»

2:80

Say: “Have you obtained a covenant from The God, such that The God will never break His covenant? Or do you attribute to The God what you do not know?” (Al-Baqarah 2:80)

Where is your covenant with The God? The question belongs to no particular Jewish, Christian, or Muslim community. Anyone issuing a categorical guarantee about people’s ultimate fate must be able to answer it.

Nor do the following verses make communal identity the answer:

«بَلَىٰ مَنْ كَسَبَ سَيِّئَةً وَأَحَاطَتْ بِهِ خَطِيئَتُهُ فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ»

2:81

Rather, whoever earns evil and is encompassed by their wrongdoing—those are the people of the Fire, remaining there. (Al-Baqarah 2:81)

«وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ»

2:82

Those who have faith and do righteous deeds are the people of paradise, remaining there. (Al-Baqarah 2:82)

Verse 81 does not say that every minor lapse consigns a person to everlasting fire. It speaks of wrongdoing that encompasses a person. Yet the answer to “a few days in the Fire” still offers no communal insurance policy.

Al-Imran returns to the same belief:

«ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا لَنْ تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ إِلَّا أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ وَغَرَّهُمْ فِي دِينِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ»

3:24

That is because they said: “The Fire will touch us for only a limited number of days.” What they used to invent deceived them in their dīn. (Al-Imran 3:24)

This warning goes deeper than the claim of temporary punishment itself: a religious belief can deceive people within their own religion. Sometimes the most dangerous belief is the one that assures us that, in the end, The God will make a special exception for us because of who we are.

We Recognized Their Failing and Overlooked Our Own

At this point, the Qur’an’s criticism comes directly back to the Muslim community.

Across much of the Muslim theological inheritance, it has become an established belief that a sinful monotheist, although possibly entering Hell for their sins, will not remain there forever. Sunni, Imami, and other schools do not formulate the belief identically. Each gives its own account of intercession, faith, allegiance, major sin, and the conditions of release.

But two claims must be distinguished. If “monotheist” means merely someone who bears the Muslim name, has pronounced the testimony of faith, or belongs to a religious community, the Qur’an itself does not permit us to equate a name with faith. If the term means a person of genuine monotheism and faith, the central question remains: Which verse guarantees that every such sinner will certainly leave the Fire?

If the answer is, “They may be punished, but they will definitely emerge in the end,” Al-Baqarah 2:80 confronts us again: “أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا؟” Where is your covenant with The God?

A painful inconsistency emerges. When another community offers this kind of guarantee, we call it distortion, superiority, or groundless wishful thinking. When we encounter our own version, we call it sound doctrine, monotheism, intercession, or divine grace. We see other people’s faults while remaining blind to our own. The reports and theological positions behind this double standard are examined in Blind to Our Own Faults: Double Standards in Salvation.

Even a verse frequently invoked to support hope for the forgiveness of sins other than associating partners with The God gives no universal guarantee:

«إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَغْفِرُ أَنْ يُشْرَكَ بِهِ وَيَغْفِرُ مَا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ»

4:48

The God does not forgive the association of partners with Him, but forgives what is less than that for whomever He wills. (An-Nisa 4:48; also 4:116)

The verse does not say that every monotheist will eventually be forgiven. It says “لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ”—for whomever He wills. The God’s will cannot be converted into a sect’s guarantee.

This concerns a person who comes to judgment in that condition, not someone who has repented and turned back. The Qur’an leaves the way back open:

«قُلْ يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ ... وَأَنِيبُوا إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَسْلِمُوا لَهُ»

39:53 · 39:54

Say: “My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of The God’s mercy…” And turn back to your Lord and surrender to Him. (Az-Zumar 39:53–54)

Repentance is one thing; constructing an assurance of salvation out of identity is another.

The Qur’an also gives an exceptionally grave warning about intentional murder:

«وَمَنْ يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاؤُهُ جَهَنَّمُ خَالِدًا فِيهَا»

4:93

Whoever deliberately kills a believer, their recompense is Hell, remaining there. (An-Nisa 4:93)

At the very least, these verses prevent us from closing the question with the simple formula that monotheism has already guaranteed every sinner’s ultimate outcome.

Intercession matters here for the same reason. The Qur’an does not make it independent of The God’s permission: “مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ”—who can intercede with Him except by His permission? (Al-Baqarah 2:255) The possibility of intercession is not a guarantee of intercession. Intercession itself deserves a separate study. For the present argument, it is enough to recognize that The God’s authority cannot be turned into sectarian insurance.

“Neither Your Wishful Hopes nor Those of the People of the Book”

If we still imagine that this criticism applies only to others, An-Nisa 4:123 closes that escape:

«لَيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ وَلَا أَمَانِيِّ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مَنْ يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا يُجْزَ بِهِ وَلَا يَجِدْ لَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا»

4:123

It is neither according to your wishful hopes nor those of the People of the Book. Whoever does evil will be recompensed for it and will find no protector or helper apart from The God. (An-Nisa 4:123)

We should not hurry past the opening words: “لَيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ”—not according to your wishful hopes. The Qur’an does not allow its own readers to take shelter behind the Book and fire its verses only at other people.

The following verse says:

«وَمَنْ يَعْمَلْ مِنَ الصَّالِحَاتِ مِنْ ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنْثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَأُولَٰئِكَ يَدْخُلُونَ الْجَنَّةَ وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًا»

4:124

Whoever does righteous deeds, male or female, while having faith—those will enter paradise and will not be wronged in the slightest. (An-Nisa 4:124)

Again, no sectarian name appears. The criteria are faith and righteous action. Verse 125 then returns to the same criterion found in Al-Baqarah 2:112:

«وَمَنْ أَحْسَنُ دِينًا مِمَّنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ وَاتَّبَعَ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا»

4:125

Who is better in dīn than someone who surrenders their face to The God while doing good and follows Abraham’s way, turning single-mindedly toward Him? (An-Nisa 4:125)

The same two criteria return: surrender and doing good. For a fuller examination of faith and the Qur’an’s description of a believer, see What Is Faith in the Qur’an, and How Was Its Meaning Distorted?.

“We Are The God’s Beloved”

The Qur’an records another claim to religious privilege:

«وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ نَحْنُ أَبْنَاءُ اللَّهِ وَأَحِبَّاؤُهُ»

5:18

The Jews and Christians said: “We are The God’s children and His beloved.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:18)

The answer challenges the premise itself:

«قُلْ فَلِمَ يُعَذِّبُكُمْ بِذُنُوبِكُمْ بَلْ أَنْتُمْ بَشَرٌ مِمَّنْ خَلَقَ»

5:18

Say: “Why, then, does He punish you for your sins? Rather, you are human beings among those He has created.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:18)

The Qur’an does not replace one privileged community with another. It breaks the claim to inherited privilege itself. If Muslims reconstruct that claim in new language, imagining that affiliation with Muhammad’s community secures their final outcome independently of other criteria, the illness has not changed. Only the patient’s name has.

What, Then, Was Muhammad’s Mission For?

A serious objection now arises. If Jews and Christians cannot be excluded from salvation simply because of their communal names, what was the point of Muhammad’s coming? Why a further mission?

If salvation depends on joining a new community, the question becomes a forced choice: either everyone must change their religious identity, or Muhammad’s mission served no purpose. But the Qur’an does not describe the prophets’ mission in those terms:

«شَرَعَ لَكُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا وَصَّىٰ بِهِ نُوحًا وَالَّذِي أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ وَمَا وَصَّيْنَا بِهِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ أَنْ أَقِيمُوا الدِّينَ وَلَا تَتَفَرَّقُوا فِيهِ»

42:13

He prescribed for you of dīn what He enjoined upon Noah, what We have revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses, and Jesus: establish dīn and do not divide within it. (Ash-Shura 42:13)

Muhammad introduces neither a new deity nor a new principle of devotion. He stands within the same succession. His invitation to the People of the Book likewise begins with serving The God:

«قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ كَلِمَةٍ سَوَاءٍ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ أَلَّا نَعْبُدَ إِلَّا اللَّهَ»

3:64

Say: “People of the Book, come to a common word between us and you: that we serve none but The God.” (Al-Imran 3:64)

The first invitation is to serve The God, not to exchange one communal name for another.

In the Qur’an’s account, Jesus did not come merely to preserve everything handed down to him. He confirmed the Torah, yet said:

«وَلِأُحِلَّ لَكُمْ بَعْضَ الَّذِي حُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ»

3:50

“And to make lawful for you some of what had been forbidden to you.” (Al-Imran 3:50)

The last prophet is likewise described as one who:

«وَيَضَعُ عَنْهُمْ إِصْرَهُمْ وَالْأَغْلَالَ الَّتِي كَانَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ»

7:157

Lifts from them their burden and the shackles that were upon them. (Al-A‘raf 7:157)

This is the picture of a prophet removing shackles, not manufacturing hundreds of new ones and fastening each around people’s necks in The God’s name. Muhammad did not come to open a competing religious enterprise beside synagogue and church, take the keys to paradise from them, and hand those keys to his own followers. If prophetic missions merely transfer ownership of the monopoly, the same failing survives under a new owner.

Muhammad’s mission renews Abraham’s call: return to The God, break with the association of partners, practice justice, accept responsibility, and surrender to truth.

Can the People of the Book Affirm the Qur’an and Remain People of the Book?

If Muhammad’s mission calls people to truth rather than merely a change of identity, the next question follows naturally: how does the Qur’an speak of People of the Book who affirm this revelation?

The Qur’an itself says:

«الَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ هُمْ بِهِ يُؤْمِنُونَ ۝ وَإِذَا يُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ قَالُوا آمَنَّا بِهِ إِنَّهُ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا مِنْ قَبْلِهِ مُسْلِمِينَ»

28:52 · 28:53

Those to whom We gave the Book before it have faith in it. When it is recited to them, they say: “We have faith in it. It is the truth from our Lord. We were already surrendered before it.” (Al-Qasas 28:52–53)

Here, People of the Book affirm the Qur’an without saying that they have become surrendered for the first time. They say: “إِنَّا كُنَّا مِنْ قَبْلِهِ مُسْلِمِينَ”—we were already surrendered before it.

Al-Imran offers another direct example:

«وَإِنَّ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ لَمَنْ يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكُمْ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْهِمْ خَاشِعِينَ لِلَّهِ»

3:199

Among the People of the Book are those who have faith in The God, in what has been sent down to you, and in what was sent down to them, humbling themselves before The God. (Al-Imran 3:199)

The Qur’an still calls them “مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ”—among the People of the Book, even as it explicitly affirms their faith in the revelation sent down to the Qur’an’s audience. “People of the Book” and muslim, a person surrendered to The God, are therefore not necessarily mutually exclusive identities in Qur’anic language.

Al-Ma’idah also says:

«وَلْيَحْكُمْ أَهْلُ الْإِنْجِيلِ بِمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ فِيهِ»

5:47

Let the people of the Gospel judge by what The God has sent down in it. (Al-Ma’idah 5:47)

Elsewhere it says:

«قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لَسْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ حَتَّىٰ تُقِيمُوا التَّوْرَاةَ وَالْإِنْجِيلَ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكُمْ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ»

5:68

Say: “People of the Book, you stand on nothing until you uphold the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been sent down to you from your Lord.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:68)

These verses do not permit the easy claim that the arrival of the Qur’an makes the designation “People of the Book,” and every possible continuation of their way, meaningless. Nor does this automatically endorse every rule and tradition that developed over centuries within their communities. Just as the Muslim inheritance must be measured against revelation, every Jewish or Christian rule and tradition must be assessed against The God’s guidance in its own right. Retaining a community’s historical name is not the same as denying truth; neither does this argument endorse that community’s entire historical inheritance.

Different Prescribed Ways, and Judgment That Still Belongs to The God

After discussing the Torah, the Gospel, and the Qur’an, Al-Ma’idah 5:48 says:

«وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَمُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ»

5:48

We have sent down the Book to you in truth, confirming the Scripture before it and exercising oversight over it.

The same verse continues:

«لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ فِي مَا آتَاكُمْ فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ»

5:48

For each of you We have appointed a prescribed way and a path. Had The God willed, He would have made you one community; but He wills to test you through what He has given you. So strive to outdo one another in good deeds. (Al-Ma’idah 5:48)

The end of the verse makes clear where judgment belongs:

«إِلَى اللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ جَمِيعًا فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ»

5:48

To The God you will all return. He will then inform you about that in which you differed. (Al-Ma’idah 5:48)

The sequence is clear: prescribed ways differ; in this world, people are to strive to outdo one another in good deeds; final judgment concerning their differences belongs to The God. Al-Hajj recognizes a corresponding difference in practice:

«لِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ فَلَا يُنَازِعُنَّكَ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَادْعُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ»

22:67

For every community We have appointed rites that they observe. So let them not dispute with you about this matter, and call to your Lord. (Al-Hajj 22:67)

Rites may differ; the invitation remains an invitation to the Lord. The distinctions between dīn, islām, and sharīʿah are explored in Dīn, Islām, and Sharīʿah in the Qur’an. The claim that all earlier religious laws were abrogated is examined separately in Naskh in the Qur’an.

The People of the Book Are Not All Alike

Where religious language places all Jews or all Christians under a single verdict, the Qur’an has a very simple statement:

«لَيْسُوا سَوَاءً»

3:113

They are not all alike. (Al-Imran 3:113)

It then describes a group among the People of the Book:

«مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ أُمَّةٌ قَائِمَةٌ يَتْلُونَ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ آنَاءَ اللَّيْلِ وَهُمْ يَسْجُدُونَ ۝ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَيَأْمُرُونَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَيَنْهَوْنَ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ وَيُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْخَيْرَاتِ وَأُولَٰئِكَ مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ»

3:113 · 3:114

Among the People of the Book is an upright community. They recite The God’s verses through the hours of the night and prostrate. They have faith in The God and the Last Day, enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and hasten to do good. They are among the righteous. (Al-Imran 3:113–114)

It continues:

«وَمَا يَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَلَنْ يُكْفَرُوهُ»

3:115

Whatever good they do will never be denied them. (Al-Imran 3:115)

Al-Baqarah also says:

«إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالنَّصَارَىٰ وَالصَّابِئِينَ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا فَلَهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ»

2:62

Those who have believed, the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians—whoever has faith in The God and the Last Day and acts righteously will have their reward with their Lord. (Al-Baqarah 2:62)

The same criterion recurs in Al-Ma’idah 5:69. These verses do not say that every Jew, Christian, or Sabian is saved simply by virtue of a name. They give the criteria as faith in The God, the Last Day, and righteous action. Just as those names do not guarantee salvation, neither does the name Muslim. A broader examination of faith appears in What Is Faith in the Qur’an, and How Was Its Meaning Distorted?.

Not Every Person of the Book Is a Kāfir

In Muslim religious usage, kāfir sometimes functions as another name for every non-Muslim. The Qur’an, however, does not speak so indiscriminately:

«إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ وَالْمُشْرِكِينَ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ»

98:6

Those who have committed kufr among the People of the Book, and those who associate partners with The God, are in the fire of Hell. (Al-Bayyinah 98:6)

The verse does not say, “The People of the Book are in the Fire.” It says “الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ”—those among the People of the Book who have committed kufr. It even names those People of the Book and those who associate partners under two distinct designations. Kufr is not an ethnic or religious identity on a registration form.

One of its clearest expressions in the Qur’an is juḥūd: denial of what one inwardly knows to be true.

«وَجَحَدُوا بِهَا وَاسْتَيْقَنَتْهَا أَنْفُسُهُمْ ظُلْمًا وَعُلُوًّا»

27:14

They denied them, although inwardly certain of them, out of wrongdoing and arrogance. (An-Naml 27:14)

The problem here is not ignorance. They have recognized the truth and stood against it. Yet not every Qur’anic use of kufr can be confined to this one form; the root and its uses have a wider range. For a detailed discussion, see Who Is a Kāfir, and What Is Kufr?.

Accepting Some Messengers and Knowingly Rejecting Others

Surrender to The God does not mean selecting truths to suit sectarian preferences. The Qur’an says:

«إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَكْفُرُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِ وَيُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُفَرِّقُوا بَيْنَ اللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِ وَيَقُولُونَ نُؤْمِنُ بِبَعْضٍ وَنَكْفُرُ بِبَعْضٍ ... أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْكَافِرُونَ حَقًّا»

4:150 · 4:151

Those who reject The God and His messengers, who seek to separate The God from His messengers and say, “We believe in some and reject others”… they are truly kāfirs. (An-Nisa 4:150–151)

If someone truly knows that Muhammad is The God’s messenger and then rejects him solely to protect sectarian standing, power, family, or personal interests, this is no longer a difference in religious law. That person has set themselves against a truth they have recognized.

But this is precisely where the harder question begins: When can we say that Muhammad’s truthfulness has actually become clear to a particular person?

Does What Reaches People as “Islam” Actually Convey Muhammad’s Message?

This question deserves more than a few reassuring sentences.

Many theological systems reduce the matter to something simple. If someone has heard Muhammad’s name, knows that a book called the Qur’an exists, and is familiar with a community called Muslims, “the message of Islam has reached them.” But has it?

Hearing Muhammad’s name is not the same as knowing his mission. Hearing the word Islam is not the same as knowing the surrender described by the Qur’an. Seeing Muslims’ conduct is not the same as seeing the Qur’an’s message. Reading a work of jurisprudence or a collection of hadith, or encountering a Muslim government and society, does not in itself amount to encountering Muhammad’s mission.

The Qur’an connects the sending of messengers with responsibility:

«وَمَا كُنَّا مُعَذِّبِينَ حَتَّىٰ نَبْعَثَ رَسُولًا»

17:15

We would not punish until We had sent a messenger. (Al-Isra 17:15)

Of the messengers it says:

«رُسُلًا مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنْذِرِينَ لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَى اللَّهِ حُجَّةٌ بَعْدَ الرُّسُلِ»

4:165

Messengers bringing good news and warnings, so that people would have no argument against The God after the messengers. (An-Nisa 4:165)

The issue, then, is not simply that a prophet existed in history. The case must actually reach the person.

Imagine someone born into a Christian or Jewish family. What is presented to them as Islam is not the Qur’an free of accretions and Muhammad’s clear invitation, but a collection of sectarian laws, conflicting reports, religious wars, and rules devised centuries later in The God’s name.

They are told that Islam requires religious coercion, while the Qur’an says:

«لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ»

2:256

There is no compulsion in dīn. (Al-Baqarah 2:256)

They are shown a system in which human beings boldly declare things lawful or forbidden on The God’s behalf, while the Qur’an asks:

«قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ مِنْ رِزْقٍ فَجَعَلْتُمْ مِنْهُ حَرَامًا وَحَلَالًا قُلْ آللَّهُ أَذِنَ لَكُمْ أَمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ تَفْتَرُونَ»

10:59

Say: “Have you considered the provision The God sent down for you, some of which you then made forbidden and some lawful?” Say: “Has The God authorized you, or are you inventing lies about The God?” (Yunus 10:59)

And:

«وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ»

16:116

Do not say, through the falsehood your tongues describe, “This is lawful and this is forbidden,” thereby inventing lies about The God. (An-Nahl 16:116)

The prophet whom the Qur’an describes as lifting burdens and shackles has, in parts of the Muslim historical inheritance, become a symbol of endless restrictions. In his name, people have declared others unbelievers, killed opponents, criminalized questioning, restricted women’s education and participation, forbidden what The God did not forbid, and made obligatory what He did not make obligatory.

Then someone outside this community is expected to look at all of it and say: “This is The God’s ultimate truth. I must leave my own religious way and join this.”

Why?

If what we have presented as Islam is, in some respects, harsher, more violent, more closed, and more entangled with human pronouncements than the religious law we ask someone to leave, how does rejecting that portrayal automatically amount to rejecting Muhammad? If we have buried his message beneath thick layers of reports, jurisprudence, politics, sectarianism, prejudice, and habit, what justice is there in condemning someone for refusing the distorted picture we presented?

A person may reject something called Islam that the Qur’an itself rejects. Rejecting religious coercion is not rejecting the Qur’an. Rejecting unauthorized declarations of the lawful and forbidden is not rejecting the Qur’an. Rejecting oppression in The God’s name is not rejecting The God. And rejecting a superstition attributed to Muhammad does not necessarily mean rejecting Muhammad.

At this point the article’s painful question returns:

If what we have conveyed as Muhammad’s mission has, in some respects, become more corrupted than what we ask people to abandon, which side has the monopoly on paradise?

This question does not concern only Christians and Jews. Many people who call themselves Muslims have never allowed the Qur’an to speak beyond the framework of their inherited sect and religious law. For them, the Qur’an does not judge the inheritance; the inheritance judges the Qur’an. A verse is accepted insofar as it fits the system already in place. When it does not, reports, school doctrine, consensus, or interpretation are called upon to contain it.

Merely hearing the name Islam, then, does not establish that the case has been made to a person. Neither does seeing a Muslim society or reading a report or a book of jurisprudence. Even rejecting something that people call Islam does not, by itself, prove that someone has recognized the Qur’an’s truth and consciously rejected it.

We cannot discover from a person’s religious identity what truth has become apparent to them, how much, or with what clarity. Final judgment belongs to The God alone:

«إِلَى اللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ جَمِيعًا»

5:48

To The God you will all return. (Al-Ma’idah 5:48)

He knows what has reached a person, what has become clear to them, what has remained hidden, and how they have responded to the truth they recognized.

What About “Whoever Seeks a Dīn Other Than Islām”?

Al-Imran 3:85 is among the principal verses used to confine salvation to the historical Muslim community: “وَمَنْ يَبْتَغِ غَيْرَ الْإِسْلَامِ دِينًا فَلَنْ يُقْبَلَ مِنْهُ”—whoever seeks a dīn other than islām, it will not be accepted from them. But the sentence does not occur in isolation. The meaning of islām must be read within the connection the Qur’an has just established.

Two verses earlier, we read:

«أَفَغَيْرَ دِينِ اللَّهِ يَبْغُونَ وَلَهُ أَسْلَمَ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ طَوْعًا وَكَرْهًا»

3:83

Do they seek a dīn other than The God’s, when everyone in the heavens and the earth has surrendered to Him, willingly or unwillingly? (Al-Imran 3:83)

This use of aslama—“surrendered”—plainly extends beyond the name of a historical community. The inevitable submission of all existence to The God’s sovereignty is, of course, different from a human being’s willing, faithful surrender. Yet the verse still prevents us from assuming at the outset that the word means membership in a religious institution.

The next verse names Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Jesus, and other prophets, then says:

«لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْهُمْ وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ»

3:84

We make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we are surrendered. (Al-Imran 3:84)

Verse 85 then says that whoever seeks a dīn other than islām will not have it accepted. Within this sequence, islām does not appear without explanation. Immediately before it comes “وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ”—to Him we are surrendered. The passage thus concerns seeking a way outside surrender to The God and His guidance, rather than simply remaining outside a historical name.

The same meaning appears near the beginning of the surah. After “إِنَّ الدِّينَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ الْإِسْلَامُ”—dīn with The God is islām—the Qur’an says: “أَسْلَمْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّهِ ... أَأَسْلَمْتُمْ فَإِنْ أَسْلَمُوا فَقَدِ اهْتَدَوْا”—I have surrendered my face to The God… Have you also surrendered? If they surrender, they have found guidance. (Al-Imran 3:20) The verbal noun islām is explained through the verb aslama itself, and the object of that surrender is clear: The God.

For a detailed examination of dīn, islām, and sharīʿah, see Dīn, Islām, and Sharīʿah in the Qur’an.

Abraham’s Way, Not a Claim to His Identity

After dismissing communal wishful hopes, An-Nisa 4:125 returns the reader to Abraham’s way: “وَاتَّبَعَ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا”—and followed Abraham’s way, turning single-mindedly toward The God. Al-Imran describes that same Abraham as follows:

«مَا كَانَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَٰكِنْ كَانَ حَنِيفًا مُسْلِمًا»

3:67

Abraham was neither Jewish nor Christian. Rather, he was single-mindedly devoted and surrendered. (Al-Imran 3:67)

Every community may try to register Abraham as one of its own, but the Qur’an sets aside precisely that appropriation. Abraham was neither Jewish nor Christian. Nor can his being muslim mean membership in a historical community that arose centuries later. He is muslim because he has surrendered himself to The God.

In the Qur’an, Abraham is more than a name several traditions share. He confronted his people’s inheritance, questioned what others held sacred, refused blind imitation, and consciously turned toward the Creator of the heavens and the earth. Following millat Ibrāhīm—Abraham’s way—therefore does not mean inheriting his identity on paper. It means placing truth above names, habits, and communal inheritance, and surrendering to it when we recognize it.

That is what we should inherit from Abraham: his way, rather than a name later generations gave themselves.

Conclusion

This study has challenged the belief that a community’s name, sectarian affiliation, or adherence to a particular religious law can by itself guarantee salvation. Being Jewish, being Christian, or being called Muslim does not alone determine anyone’s fate. Just as belonging to the People of the Book does not automatically make someone a kāfir, the title Muslim does not guarantee faith, goodness, or surrender in its bearer. Each person faces the truth that has reached them, what they have understood of it, and how they have responded. Final judgment of that situation belongs to The God, not to sects or religious institutions.

On this reading, Muhammad’s mission does not establish a new class of exclusive owners of paradise. Continuing the call of Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, he invites people to serve The God, accept responsibility, practice justice, and surrender to truth. Hearing the name Islam, or encountering what Muslims have constructed under that name throughout history, therefore does not necessarily mean knowing the Qur’an’s message. Nor can rejection of a corrupted portrayal of Islam be treated, without evidence, as conscious rejection of The God and His messenger.

The Qur’an recognizes differences between prescribed ways and rites. It does not treat the People of the Book as a uniform group; it even describes people among them who have faith in the revelation sent down to Muhammad. At the same time, kufr and knowing denial cannot be reduced to identity labels. Conscious rejection of truth is different from ignorance, never receiving the message, or rejecting a distorted portrayal of it. Ultimately, assessing those differences belongs to The God, who knows what has actually reached each person.

The conclusion is simple: paradise is no sect’s property, and religious law is no insurance policy for salvation. No scholar, hadith specialist, jurist, priest, rabbi, or religious institution can issue its community a guarantee that The God has not issued. If anyone still claims that their group will eventually be saved by virtue of its identity, the Qur’an’s two questions remain: Where is your covenant with The God? What is your proof?
