<h2 id="intro">Introduction</h2>

For many readers, riba (the financial increase prohibited by the Qur’an) has become almost interchangeable with interest: a lender gives money, a borrower returns more money, and the extra amount is called unlawful. That definition is not worthless. It names a real danger. But it is too small for the world in which we now live, and it is also too small for the moral horizon of the Qur'an.

The Qur'an does not speak about riba as a narrow bookkeeping problem. It speaks about it near charity, mercy, debt, delay, hardship, justice, and the refusal to consume another person's life through financial power. Its question is not only, “Was there a percentage?” It is also, “What kind of increase is this? Who carries the risk? Who is protected from loss? Whose need becomes another person's growth? Does the transaction circulate life, or does it extract life?”

This essay begins before coins, banks, and modern finance to help identify present-day cases, while taking the Qur’anic meaning from the Qur’an itself rather than making it depend on an economic theory. Money has changed. Debt has changed. The power to create money has changed. Inflation has changed the meaning of “returning the same amount.” A nominal equality can hide a real loss, and a formal sale can hide a loan. When the form changes but the harm remains, the Qur'anic question must remain alive.

The purpose here is not to condemn every modern instrument, nor to declare every bank account or mortgage to be the same moral act. The purpose is to clear the fog. A society cannot reform what it cannot name. If riba is reduced to one old formula, it becomes easy to escape its spirit while preserving its vocabulary. If riba is treated as a living Qur'anic warning, then the discussion becomes wider, more honest, and more difficult.

<h2 id="chapter-1">Chapter One: Value Before Abstract Money</h2>

Before money became paper, digits, bank balances, or tradable debt, human beings still exchanged value. A farmer had grain. A carpenter had skill. A shepherd had milk or wool. A potter had bowls. A healer had knowledge. One simple form of exchange was barter: goods exchanged for goods, or work for goods. The farmer could offer wheat for tools; the shepherd could offer livestock for clothing. In these examples, exchange arose from need and capacity: one person had what another person needed, and the other person had something useful in return.

The first difficulty was the double coincidence of wants. The person who needed wheat might have shoes, but the farmer might not need shoes at that moment. The exchange could fail even though both parties had something valuable. This is one reason communities moved toward commonly accepted goods: grain, salt, livestock, cloth, metals, and other objects that could store or represent value better than a single personal need.

Even before coinage, people had to ask practical questions. Can this good be divided? Can it be stored? Does it rot? Is it widely accepted? Can it be transported? Can it be measured honestly? A good that answers these questions well begins to behave like money.

But in that early world value was still close to life. A thing was valuable because it fed people, clothed them, protected them, carried labor, or preserved trust. Even when people used cattle, grain, or metal as a medium of exchange, the distance between money and human work was smaller. The danger of abstraction was present, but it had not yet reached its modern scale.

Debt also existed before banks. A neighbor could borrow seed, food, tools, or labor. But such debt was usually embedded in relationship. The lender saw the borrower. The village saw both. Shame, mercy, reputation, kinship, and future cooperation shaped the exchange. A debt could still become oppressive, but the social world made pure extraction harder to hide.

This matters because riba is not merely about numbers. It is about the moral shape of increase. When value is close to labor and need, injustice is more visible. When value becomes abstract, transferable, multiplied, securitized, and distant from the human being who carries the burden, injustice can become polite.

<h2 id="chapter-2">Chapter Two: Gold, Silver, and Durable Value</h2>

In many civilizations, gold and silver became important media of exchange and stores of value, although other societies used other goods and monetary instruments. They became powerful forms of money because they answered many of the old practical problems. They were durable, portable, divisible, recognizable, and difficult to produce. A coin did not rot like grain. It did not need pasture like livestock. It could pass through many hands without losing its physical form.

This helped trade. It allowed merchants to travel, governments to tax, soldiers to be paid, and markets to compare prices. It also created a new kind of social imagination. Wealth could now be held in a compact form. It could be hidden, hoarded, inherited, transported, and lent. Value became more movable, but also more detachable from the work and need that first produced it.

Metal money has an honest quality: it cannot be created by writing numbers in a ledger. A person who lends a coin gives up the use of that coin for a time. The lender's loss of liquidity is real. The borrower receives a definite thing and is expected to return a definite thing. This is one reason classical jurists discussed riba in terms that fit a world of measurable commodities and metallic money.

At-Tawbah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/009-at-tawbah/#ayah-034">9:34</a> and At-Tawbah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/009-at-tawbah/#ayah-035">9:35</a> condemn hoarding gold and silver without spending, while Al-Humazah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/104-al-humazah/#ayah-002">104:2</a> speaks of accumulating and counting wealth. These references support criticism of hoarding; they do not automatically define every act of saving as riba.

But even metal money can become a tool of domination. A wealthy person may hoard coins while the poor need food. A merchant may use scarcity to demand humiliating terms. A lender may treat another person's emergency as a business opportunity. The Qur'an does not oppose trade; it opposes devouring human beings through financial advantage.

The shift from useful goods to precious metals also created a temptation: wealth could grow without visible work. A person could sit near money while others carried risk. This is not yet modern banking, but it is already a moral doorway. When the owner of wealth can demand increase while being shielded from loss, the question of riba begins to press.

<h2 id="chapter-3">Chapter Three: The Economic World of the Qur'an's First Audience</h2>

The Qur'an was first recited among people who understood trade, debt, delay, partnership, caravan risk, trust, and loss. Mecca was not an isolated village. It was connected to routes, contracts, seasonal markets, and merchant households. People knew the difference between a sale and a loan. They knew what it meant to delay payment. They knew how need could place one party under the power of another.

Yet their world did not contain central banks, digital balances, fractional reserve systems, securitized mortgages, global reserve currencies, or high-speed markets. Their money was not created as bank credit in the modern sense. Debt, trade, changing prices, and financial obligations still existed. Metallic money did not make inflation impossible. A creditor could oppress a debtor, but the financial architecture did not multiply debt through the same mechanisms we now take for granted.

This is why we must be careful. We should not pretend that the old world was simple or pure. It was not. But we should also not pretend that modern finance is only a larger version of the old marketplace. It is different in kind as well as scale.

Today, central banks issue base money, while commercial banks can create new deposit money when granting loans. This money need not have direct gold or silver backing, and creating it does not simultaneously produce new real wealth.

The Qur'an's guidance entered a world where debt could trap the weak. A debt could be extended, then increased. A desperate person could become less free with each delay. The lender's wealth could grow precisely because the borrower was unable to pay. That is an important form of economic injustice. Yet hardship is not stated as a condition defining riba; it is a circumstance that gives rise to a particular duty toward the debtor.

The Qur'an's answer is not merely technical. It commands writing debts with clarity in <a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-282">Al-Baqarah 2:282</a>. It calls for time and relief when the debtor is in difficulty in <a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-280">Al-Baqarah 2:280</a>. It places riba in contrast with charity, mercy, and justice. It teaches that wealth is not purified by finding clever ways to make the vulnerable pay more.

<h2 id="chapter-4">Chapter Four: Banking and Credit Money</h2>

Modern banking changes the discussion because modern banks do not merely lend existing coins stored in a chest. In a credit-money system, new purchasing power can be created through lending. A bank extends a loan, a deposit appears, and the borrower enters the economy with money that did not previously circulate in that form. The debt and the money arrive together.

Consider a simplified historical example. An institution holds the equivalent of 100 units of gold but issues receipts or monetary promises worth 300 units. It has issued 200 units beyond its existing metal backing. If it lends this additional credit, it can demand interest without a corresponding quantity of new gold or real wealth having been produced. This illustrates issuance beyond metallic backing; modern commercial banking also has a different mechanism.

New credit money is not the same as newly produced gold, goods, services, or real wealth. A bank can provide fresh credit as a loan and receive interest. The important questions are who benefits from that credit and who bears its risks and costs.

Suppose a bank creates 100 units of credit money and requires repayment of 110. The ten units of interest do not necessarily require a new loan of ten units: existing money circulates and can reach the borrower through income and transactions. The central questions remain: what does the bank gain from creating credit, what does the borrower pay, and can credit expansion impose debt pressure and reduced purchasing power on society?

If credit and debt expand beyond the real capacity to produce and repay, households, companies, and governments may become trapped by debt pressure. New borrowing can then be used to repay old borrowing and deepen dependence. This is a real danger, not the inevitable consequence of every loan and every interest payment.

Growth in money and credit can be one cause of inflation when it is not matched by a corresponding increase in the supply of goods and services.

Inflation complicates the matter further. If money loses purchasing power, returning the same nominal amount may not be returning the same real value. A loan of 100 units today may not equal 100 units years later if those units buy far less. In such a world, both lender and borrower can be wronged depending on the structure of the contract, the source of money creation, and the distribution of inflation's effects.

This is why the question of riba cannot be answered by nominal arithmetic alone. “More money came back” may be unjust, but “the same number came back” may also hide injustice. The Qur'anic principle, “do not wrong and do not be wronged,” from <a href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-279">Al-Baqarah 2:279</a>, demands a real analysis, not only a formal one. The verse alone does not prescribe a definitive method of calculating or compensating for inflation.

The Qur’anic commands concerning honest weights and measures—Al-An‘am <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/006-al-anam/#ayah-152">6:152</a>, Al-Isra <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/017-al-isra/#ayah-035">17:35</a>, Al-Mutaffifin <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/083-al-mutaffifin/#ayah-001">83:1</a>, Al-Mutaffifin <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/083-al-mutaffifin/#ayah-002">83:2</a>, and Al-Mutaffifin <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/083-al-mutaffifin/#ayah-003">83:3</a>—provide a relevant ethical comparison when examining money as a measure of value.

Debt money also changes social power. Those closest to money creation can buy assets before prices fully adjust. Those whose wages lag behind prices carry the loss later. A society may call this normal economics, but the moral question remains: who receives fresh purchasing power, who pays through diminished purchasing power, and who is told that the suffering is natural?

<h2 id="chapter-5">Chapter Five: Ownership, Risk, and Speculation</h2>

The Qur'an permits trade and condemns riba. That distinction is not superficial. Trade involves exposure to reality. A seller may misjudge demand. A partner may lose capital. A farmer may lose a crop. A merchant may carry goods and fail to sell them. In a productive partnership, profit and loss depend on the activity’s outcome, and each party’s responsibilities should be clear. This does not establish that every legitimate return requires sharing production risk: the kind of relationship, the value provided, and both parties’ rights also matter.

The lender can also face nonpayment or loss of purchasing power. Some contracts nevertheless allow the lender to transfer much of the risk and economic pressure to the borrower. That distribution deserves examination. A fixed return alone does not prove riba, and a partnership is not automatically fair.

This does not mean every investment is pure or every fixed contract is corrupt. It means the moral distinction must be real. A share in a productive business is different from a debt claim. A partnership is different from a disguised loan. A lease, sale, wage, equity stake, and charity each has its own moral structure. The names matter less than the substance.

Speculation is another doorway. Buying and selling can serve real allocation, price discovery, and liquidity. But markets can also become arenas where participants do not care about the underlying good, service, or human consequence. They care only about the next price movement. When wealth is extracted from volatility itself, the market begins to resemble <a href="/quran-terminology/en/roots/y-s-r/">maysir (gambling or gain through wagering)</a>: not because every uncertainty is gambling, but because risk becomes detached from service and turned into a game of transfer.

The Arabic root y-s-r concerns ease and opening a way. This study also examines how a benefit is obtained and what it does to the parties; the root alone does not establish that every easy gain is maysir. Examples include lottery-like financial wagers, political manipulation of prices for private benefit, and markets in tokens or claims where participants chiefly seek to profit from other participants’ losses. These examples preserve the distinction between productive risk, which can create goods and services, and wager-like risk aimed only at transferring wealth through price movements.

Modern finance often blurs ownership, debt, insurance, speculation, and leverage. A product may look like investment while behaving like a wager. It may look like trade while functioning like a loan. It may look like risk-sharing while placing the real loss on the weaker side. The Qur'anic task is to look through the costume.

<h2 id="chapter-6">Chapter Six: Riba in the Qur'an</h2>

To understand riba (the financial increase prohibited by the Qur’an), we must begin with the Qur’an itself. Monetary and economic history help us examine present-day cases, but the Qur’anic meaning does not depend on the truth of any economic theory.

Money and credit have changed, and creating credit money is not the same as simultaneously producing real wealth. The critical issue arises when those with financial power use privileged access to credit to impose costs and losses on others, conceal contractual terms, or exploit human need. Such practices deserve direct criticism. But credit creation alone does not prove that every bank is deceptive or engaged in theft. Each arrangement must be examined: what value does it provide, who benefits, and who bears its risks and costs? Contemporary markets can create value, but unjust arrangements can also transfer the fruits of others’ work to those who hold power.

The Qur’an contrasts riba both with trade in Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-275">2:275</a> and with charity and zakat (giving associated with purification and growth) in Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-276">2:276</a> and Ar-Rum <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/030-ar-rum/#ayah-039">30:39</a>. Both contrasts are essential. The question is not merely how much profit is made, but how it is obtained and whether people’s rights are respected.

In the first direction, someone gives wealth so that it will increase for them within other people’s wealth; the Qur’an says that this increase does not grow with God. In the second, someone gives zakat seeking God’s approval, and the Qur’an speaks of multiplication. The distinction draws attention to the difference between a numerical increase in assets and the growth of their benefits for people and society.

The question is therefore not simply whether money has increased, but at whose expense. Wealth that should circulate can instead collect at the top, like blood clotting in one place rather than sustaining the body. Zakat is compared here to movement that keeps the economic body active, while riba obstructs that circulation.

The preceding verses repeatedly address giving, charity, sound intention, and the dignity of people in need (Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-261">2:261</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-262">2:262</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-263">2:263</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-264">2:264</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-265">2:265</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-266">2:266</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-267">2:267</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-268">2:268</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-269">2:269</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-270">2:270</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-271">2:271</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-272">2:272</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-273">2:273</a>, Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-274">2:274</a>). The verse distinguishes trade from riba: trading profit is not inherently riba. The root r-b-w (increase or rising) is distinct from r-b-h (profit or gain). Qiyam (rising or standing) and takhabbut (confusion and loss of balance) describe a striking contrast. An-Nisa <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/#ayah-005">4:5</a> also describes wealth as a means of sustaining life. Without sufficient evidence, this image should not be turned into a psychiatric diagnosis or confined exclusively to worldly life or the hereafter. The verse leaves room for correction when someone receives admonition and stops, entrusting that person’s affair to God, while warning against returning to riba.

The reported claim is “Trade is just like riba.” Its direction must be preserved: it makes trade resemble riba, rather than saying that riba resembles trade.

The Qur’an reports this comparison without specifying exactly why they made it. They may have viewed both as ways to increase wealth, or sought to erase the distinction between them. These are possibilities for investigation, not explanations explicitly stated in the verse.

Not every financial increase is riba. The Qur’an permits trade and forbids riba. Understanding the distinction requires examining the other verses concerning riba, debt, principal, injustice, and charity.

In an honest sale, both parties exchange things of value with consent and respect for each other’s rights. In financial relationships we must ask what value is provided, where the return comes from, who carries the risk, and whether one person exploits another’s need or incapacity. A specified return alone is not sufficient to establish riba.

Aal ‘Imran <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/003-al-imran/#ayah-130">3:130</a> prohibits multiplying riba, and An-Nisa <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/004-an-nisa/#ayah-161">4:161</a> recalls taking riba despite a prior prohibition. Riba cannot therefore be defined solely by a particular percentage, nor can every financial increase be called riba without examination.

Taken together, these verses connect riba with financial increase, the rights of debtors and creditors, principal, and the rejection of injustice. Exploiting need, unfairly transferring risk, and concentrating wealth must also be assessed against Qur’anic principles; but not every oppressive practice is automatically the specific offense of riba. The Qur’an does not oppose wealth itself. It protects justice and balance.

The question today is therefore not only what riba looked like in the past, but which contemporary relationships reproduce it and which involve other forms of economic injustice.

<h2 id="chapter-7">Chapter Seven: The Social Symptoms of Riba</h2>

The social effects of financial arrangements matter, but establishing injustice and establishing that a relationship specifically constitutes riba are distinct questions. A society shaped by riba does not merely contain interest-bearing contracts. It begins to normalize debt as the path to survival. Housing requires debt. Education requires debt. Medical care requires debt. Business entry requires debt. Governments operate through debt. Families inherit debt. The future is sold before it arrives.

A report in Sahih al-Bukhari describes Bilal bringing good dates to the Prophet after exchanging two measures of poorer dates for one measure of better dates. The report attributes to the Prophet a rejection of the exchange as riba and an instruction to sell the poorer dates first, then use the proceeds to buy better dates. It proposes replacing a direct exchange with two separate transactions. Without knowing the prices, however, we cannot claim that their economic outcome must be identical. The central question is whether changing a contract’s form, without changing an unjust relationship’s substance, can make it just.

Changing contract names or invoking reports cannot conceal the effects on people’s lives. Criticism should address what a financial arrangement actually does, rather than assume that a different name has removed its injustice.

In such a society, productive work often becomes secondary to financial positioning. Asset owners rise because prices rise. Wage earners fall behind because wages move slowly. Young families must borrow more to enter the same life their parents entered with less. The poor pay fees for being poor. The rich receive returns for already having access.

This is not always caused by one evil person. That is part of the difficulty. Riba can become institutional. People inside the system may be kind, polite, and personally generous while the structure itself transfers loss downward and gain upward. A bank employee may be friendly. A borrower may sign willingly. The harm may still be real.

Another symptom is the moral reversal of time. In a healthy economy, the future is built by work, learning, cultivation, saving, trust, and shared risk. In a debt-dominated economy, the future is colonized by payment schedules. Life becomes organized around servicing past money. Human beings become predictable cash flows.

Where one party uses its power to shift most risk and loss onto a weaker party, the arrangement may conflict with Qur’anic justice and the prohibition of wrongdoing. That criticism must be made without defining every guaranteed return as riba.

The Qur'an does not ask us to hate commerce. It asks us to prevent commerce from devouring persons. The test is not whether a transaction has modern paperwork or religious vocabulary. The test is whether it circulates provision with justice or converts vulnerability into yield.

Where one party’s profit rests on pressure, incapacity, and losses imposed on others, the relationship must be assessed against Qur’anic justice. Identifying oppression is one task; establishing that a relationship constitutes riba is another.

<h2 id="chapter-8">Chapter Eight: Why the Classical Definition Is Not Enough Today</h2>

Classical definitions of riba were developed in a world very different from ours. They responded to real transactions, real coins, real commodities, and real abuses. Modern credit banking and the present monetary system did not exist in that setting, and monetary supply depended heavily on metals and physical constraints. Inflation and changes in purchasing power nevertheless occurred in earlier societies too; their monetary mechanisms and scale differed from today’s. They deserve careful study. Respect is not fossilization. A definition that was precise for one monetary environment may become incomplete in another.

If money itself is created through debt, if inflation silently changes value, if loans are packaged and sold, if contracts are engineered to imitate trade while guaranteeing debt-like returns, and if the poorest people face the highest effective cost of money, then the old question must be asked again with greater care.

Some modern “Islamic” structures avoid the word interest but preserve the economic substance of interest. A sale is inserted where a loan would have been. A commodity changes hands for a moment. A markup replaces a percentage. The documents look different, but the cash-flow logic may remain the same. This does not mean every Islamic finance product is false. It means form alone is not enough.

The Qur’an’s account of the coastal town in Al-A‘raf <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/007-al-araf/#ayah-163">7:163</a> concerns its Sabbath test and disobedience. The details of setting nets before the Sabbath and collecting the fish afterward are not stated in the verse itself. To criticize contractual evasion, we must show that changing the form has left an unjust relationship unchanged. Details absent from the verse should not be presented as Qur’anic text.

Gold and silver, because of scarcity and natural limits on supply, sometimes maintained relatively stable purchasing power, especially compared with currencies whose supply can grow rapidly. But metallic money’s prices and purchasing power were never permanently fixed.

The reverse is also true. In an inflationary economy, some payments that look like increase may be attempts to preserve real value rather than exploit need. A person who lends in depreciating money can be wronged if only the original number is returned after years of price erosion. A household that borrows to buy a home in a system where currency loses value and rents rise may not be morally identical to a predatory lender profiting from desperation.

This is why the Qur'anic phrase “do not wrong and do not be wronged” is so powerful. It prevents lazy condemnation and lazy permission at the same time. It asks for substance, consequences, risk, coercion, inflation, alternatives, and power. The verse by itself does not specify a definitive method for calculating or compensating for inflation.

Neither a numerical amount nor an outcome is sufficient on its own to identify riba. The wording and context of the Qur’anic verses, the nature of the financial relationship, both parties’ rights, and its actual consequences must be examined together. Neither a contract’s name nor its interest rate alone establishes economic justice.

Severe inflation, especially when driven by imbalanced expansion of money and credit, can become a major means of hidden wealth transfer and economic injustice. Some of its effects may resemble those of riba-based relationships: one group loses purchasing power while another benefits. Inflation itself, however, is not the Qur’anic definition of riba.

The Qur’an connects riba with financial increase, principal, debt, and the rejection of injustice. Its principles of economic justice extend beyond this particular subject. Other structures that exploit vulnerability must also be criticized without calling all of them riba without evidence. Not every profit is riba, not every loss is injustice, not every debt is oppression, and not every uncertainty is gambling.

<h2 id="chapter-9">Chapter Nine: A Final Synthesis Without Issuing Judgment</h2>

In every age, economic relationships must be assessed through justice, the rights of both parties, and avoidance of exploiting human need. An arrangement that imposes risk and loss on vulnerable people may be oppressive despite its legal form. Calling it riba also requires establishing its connection with the Qur’anic concept.

The Qur’an contrasts riba with charity and protects the creditor’s principal while rejecting wrongdoing toward either side. Its moral criticism remains forceful, but it should not be expanded into an unsupported definition encompassing every economic injustice.

The practical path forward is not simple. We live inside systems that no single household can redesign. A person may need a bank account. A family may face rent, inflation, medical bills, and housing prices. A small business may need financing to survive. A government may operate in debt markets whether citizens like it or not. Moral clarity should not become cruelty toward people trapped in structures they did not create.

At the same time, difficulty should not become surrender. Muslims and Qur'an-centered communities can still ask better questions. Does this contract share risk honestly? Does it hide a loan inside a sale? Does it profit from distress? Does it preserve real value or exploit weakness? Does it create productive capacity or merely transfer wealth through leverage? Does it make society more merciful or more extractive?

Communities can create cooperative funds, lend without exploitation, and make clear local agreements grounded in trust. Personal loans also require attention to the difference between nominal amounts and real purchasing power so that both parties’ rights are respected. Al-Baqarah <a class="verse-citation" href="/quran-reflection/en/002-al-baqarah/#ayah-279">2:279</a> alone does not determine a definitive method of calculating or compensating for inflation.

A healthier economy would move toward real partnership, transparent debt, relief for hardship, limits on predatory lending, protection from inflationary theft, productive investment, and forms of ownership that connect gain to responsibility. It would not pretend that money is morally neutral. Money is a social promise. If that promise is corrupted, the weak pay first.
