Part One: A Reflection on Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs

Abraham Maslow, the well-known twentieth-century psychologist, sought to explain human needs as a hierarchy. Its most familiar representation is a pyramid: basic needs occupy the base, while each higher level introduces more subtle dimensions of human and spiritual life. His central insight was that a mind preoccupied with survival, fear, insecurity, and basic deprivation struggles to reach toward self-respect, meaning, ethics, creativity, and self-actualization. Put plainly, someone who is hungry, sleep-deprived, physically unsafe, or constantly anxious about making ends meet will usually have little mental and emotional energy left for wisdom, art, selfless action, spirituality, or inner growth.

At the base of the pyramid are physiological needs: food, water, sleep, breathing, rest, and, in many accounts, sexual desire. These needs are directly connected to bodily survival and the nervous system’s most basic sense of ease. When this foundation is disrupted, the mind remains on alert. A person who has gone several nights without sleep, is hungry or ill, or has become physically exhausted has little patience for deep reasoning, a diminished capacity for moral restraint, and difficulty making balanced decisions. Many instances of moral or emotional breakdown therefore arise from severe bodily deprivation before they can be explained simply as failures of character. Human development begins here: the body must find enough relief for the mind to emerge from survival mode.

The second level is safety. Once bodily needs are reasonably stable, a person needs an environment that does not feel persistently threatening. Safety means more than the absence of war and violence. It includes economic, family, emotional, and legal security, as well as some confidence that the future is predictable. A child growing up amid arguments and threats, a worker who fears dismissal every day, a woman afraid of humiliation or assault at home, and a man who does not know how he will feed his family tomorrow are all trapped at this level. Under such conditions, the mind turns toward self-defense rather than development. Movement beyond this stage begins when a person gradually senses that the world has not entirely fallen apart, that tomorrow is not wholly dark, and that at least some part of life can be relied upon and brought into order.

Next comes the need for love, belonging, and connection. The question is no longer simply whether one can stay alive, but whether one is accepted, loved, seen, and included. Human beings are individuals, yet they are also profoundly relational. Someone cut off from family, friends, community, a spouse, children, or a wider human circle may have food and security and still feel an inner emptiness. Many experiences of depression, meaninglessness, and fractured identity take shape here. At this stage, people want to know whether they matter to anyone, whether they are heard, and whether they have a place in someone else’s heart. Growth occurs as isolation, rejection, and emotional abandonment give way to healthy bonds, mutual trust, and relationships that preserve human dignity.

The fourth level concerns self-esteem and dignity. Once people can sustain their bodies, escape serious danger, and experience some degree of love and belonging, they want to feel that they have worth. This has two dimensions: the respect they have for themselves and the respect they receive from others. They seek more than survival or acceptance. They want to know whether they are capable and useful, whether they can influence their own lives, whether their voices carry weight, and whether others truly respect them or merely tolerate them. Humiliation at this level may leave a person physically alive but inwardly broken. By contrast, taking responsibility, learning a skill, developing an independent identity, and escaping chronic humiliation allow a person to grow into a stronger sense of worth.

Maslow called the fifth level self-actualization. Here the questions change. Beyond asking how to survive, remain safe, be loved, or earn respect, a person begins to ask: What was I created for? What am I truly capable of? Which possibilities within me remain unrealized? What would it mean to become the best person I can be? This stage involves developing one’s abilities, creating, finding meaning, cultivating inward honesty, and living in harmony with the truth of one’s own being. An artist devoted to the work, a teacher who teaches from a sense of calling, a physician who sees medicine as more than a job, or anyone who moves beyond consumption and competition in search of meaning participates in this development. Self-actualization, however, should not be confused with public success or fame. An entirely unknown person may possess a depth of integrity, insight, and inner coherence that far exceeds that of many celebrated figures.

Later, some psychologists, including Maslow himself in his final years, described a further stage: transcendence, or moving beyond the self. At this level, people turn beyond self-centered concerns toward goodness, truth, sacrifice, service, worship, beauty, and a connection with something greater than the ego. The central question shifts from “What do I want?” to “What is right, what is good, and what responsibility do I bear before the truth?” Many great women and men throughout history have lived at this level, giving their lives, time, wealth, and comfort to a truth larger than themselves. This is the highest horizon of human development: the point at which a person becomes a bearer of meaning rather than merely a consumer seeking to satisfy needs.

How, then, does a person move from one level to another? The ascent is neither sudden nor complete, and it does not always follow a straight line. Usually, a measure of stability at one level creates the capacity to attend to the next. As the body settles, the mind gains room to think. As persistent fear subsides, the heart opens to relationship. As relationships take root, the person has space to develop. As self-respect heals, energy once spent tending old wounds becomes available for moving beyond oneself and considering one’s calling. Each stage therefore requires both a degree of security in the preceding stage and education, awareness, experience, and choice. Improved external conditions alone do not produce growth. People must also develop inwardly and acquire the capacity to sustain life at a higher level.

None of this makes the pyramid a rigid or absolute law. Human beings are complex. Some attain extraordinary moral heights in extreme poverty; others enjoy every material advantage yet remain absorbed in their most basic appetites. Maslow’s hierarchy describes a broad pattern, not an iron rule. It helps explain why humiliation, insecurity, hunger, instability, and the constant stimulation of instinct make it harder for most people to pursue justice, wisdom, and transcendence. It also helps explain why corrupt powers seeking to control a society attack the base of the pyramid: they keep people afraid, impoverished, preoccupied with sexual desire, or absorbed in entertainments that appeal to instinct. A person continually occupied with hunger, fear, and stimulation has less room to pursue truth, freedom, dignity, and awareness.

Part Two: Satan’s Strategy—Confinement to Basic Needs

This brings us to the central concern. If Satan seeks to cripple a person or a society, he does not always need to lead them into elaborate corruption or philosophical unbelief. Imprisoning them in the first two levels is enough. A society consumed day and night by desire, survival, fear, humiliation, insecurity, and nervous turmoil will struggle to cultivate justice, selflessness, reflection, ethical conduct, and spiritual growth. The struggle therefore takes place in the structure of everyday life as well as in belief. Anyone who confines people to the base of the pyramid effectively blocks their ascent. Anyone who helps provide food, safety, dignity, love, understanding, and a horizon of meaning helps them move from one level toward another.

Sexual intimacy is a basic, natural need in mature human life. The body has processes that require regulation and release; just as it must eliminate waste, its sexual drive also needs a healthy and legitimate channel for expression and balance. Ignoring that drive or suppressing it excessively often produces psychological tension, distorted behavior, and disruption of individual and social equilibrium. Poverty and financial hardship already obstruct marriage and a stable life. In many societies, particularly Muslim societies, legitimate marriage is made still harder by burdensome customs, unrealistic expectations, and sometimes religious reports or regional traditions. These obstacles place a natural need under sustained pressure. People remain caught at the lower level of bodily needs: instead of finding peace and growth through a healthy, responsible relationship, they become trapped in pressure, postponement, and sometimes harmful behavior.

Meeting this need in a healthy and responsible way, like bringing other bodily needs into balance, can steady the body and mind and reduce persistent tension. Freedom from recurring pressure and the mental preoccupation caused by deprivation or constant stimulation releases cognitive and emotional energy. It allows a person to move beyond survival and immediate impulses. Concentration, patience, and emotional steadiness improve; decisions become more considered; and there is room to think about meaning, ethics, responsibility, creativity, and deeper relationships. Balanced fulfillment of this need is therefore not the final destination. It provides a foundation for further growth, freeing people from constant preoccupation with basic demands so that they can attend to the wider possibilities of life.

Part Three: What Qur’anic Marriage Is—and Is Not

In Western societies, forming a relationship is comparatively straightforward, and young people consequently have less need to organize their intimate lives around secrecy. They choose partners according to their personal values, talk to one another, and let their families know about the relationship.

Two features distinguish Qur’anic marriage from this modern Western model. The first is commitment, or mithaq: a covenant. Beginning a relationship in the Western model does not necessarily entail a commitment to a shared future. In Islamic nikah, the marriage bond rests from the outset on a long-term covenant to establish a family. The second is mahr, the marriage gift owed to the woman. It serves as a tangible pledge of responsibility, for which the Western model has no exact equivalent.

Restored to its Qur’anic foundations, Islamic marriage is neither confinement within oppressive custom nor a relationship without commitment. It is a simple, transparent, responsible bond centered on human dignity. It guides people from the turmoil of instinct toward peace, commitment, growth, and sakinah—a settled inner tranquility.

1. Intellectual and Physical Maturity

A person must have reached both physical and intellectual maturity. During adolescence, parents have a duty to protect their children from harm and exploitation while their judgment is still developing and powerful instincts can overwhelm it. Yet protection is not meant to become permanent control. With maturity comes a stage at which agency, responsibility, and the right to make decisions must gradually pass to the individual. The Qur’an describes this transition with precision and realism:

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُم مِّنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا أَن يَكْبَرُوا ۚ وَمَن كَانَ غَنِيًّا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ وَمَن كَانَ فَقِيرًا فَلْيَأْكُلْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۚ فَإِذَا دَفَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فَأَشْهِدُوا عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ حَسِيبًا

Test the orphans until they reach marriageable age. Then, if you discern sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them. Do not consume it wastefully or hastily before they grow up. Let a guardian who is wealthy refrain, and let one who is poor take only what is reasonable. When you hand over their property, have witnesses present. The God is sufficient as a reckoner.

An-Nisa 4:6

This verse establishes two distinct and essential criteria: reaching marriageable maturity and demonstrating rushd, sound judgment. Biological development alone is insufficient; a person must also possess a measure of understanding, balance, and the ability to manage life. Even where the issue is material property, the Qur’an does not authorize its transfer without evidence of sound judgment. Intellectual maturity is therefore all the more essential in a matter as sensitive as entering a relationship and building a life together.

Once both conditions are met, continued control and the denial of agency lose their justification and obstruct the natural course of human development. The boundary between parental responsibility and individual independence becomes clear: protection and education at the beginning, followed by trust and the transfer of responsibility. A society that fails to maintain this balance either releases young people before they are ready or keeps them constrained after they are ready. Both failures can create the conditions for harmful behavior and crisis.

2. Transparency Instead of Secrecy

Islam opposes secret relationships pursued solely for sexual gratification without responsibility. A relationship should be clear and open, within a framework that protects the dignity and emotional security of both people. Their families should know of it, so that the bond does not remain hidden and unsupported. Parents, in turn, bear responsibility for keeping legitimate marriage accessible. Unnecessary restrictions, excessive demands, and exhausting customs can close that path. When the permissible route becomes unnecessarily difficult, people are pushed toward secrecy.

الْيَوْمَ أُحِلَّ لَكُمُ الطَّيِّبَاتُ ۖ وَطَعَامُ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ حِلٌّ لَّكُمْ وَطَعَامُكُمْ حِلٌّ لَّهُمْ ۖ وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ مِن قَبْلِكُمْ إِذَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ وَلَا مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ ۗ وَمَن يَكْفُرْ بِالْإِيمَانِ فَقَدْ حَبِطَ عَمَلُهُ وَهُوَ فِي الْآخِرَةِ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

Today, wholesome things have been made lawful for you. The food of those given the Scripture is lawful for you, and your food is lawful for them. Also lawful are chaste women among the believers and chaste women among those given the Scripture before you, when you give them their marriage dues, yourselves seeking chastity, neither engaging in illicit sexual relations nor taking secret lovers. Whoever rejects faith has rendered his work fruitless and will be among the losers in the Hereafter.

Al-Ma'idah 5:5

The verse establishes a precise and balanced framework. It places sexual intimacy within ihsan, understood here as a protected, responsible relationship grounded in commitment. It explicitly rejects two departures from that framework: musafahah, unrestrained, openly illicit sexual relations, and taking akhdan, secret lovers.

The Qur’an does more here than prohibit an act. It outlines a healthy social structure: paying the marriage gift, acknowledging the relationship openly, and bringing it out of secrecy. The issue extends beyond individual sin to the abandonment of transparency and responsibility. Wherever burdensome customs or unjustified social pressure block this clear framework, society effectively drives people toward the very alternatives the Qur’an rejects: open sexual irresponsibility or concealed relationships. Responsibility therefore belongs to families and society as well as to the individual.

3. Exclusivity and Fidelity

A fundamental condition of a legitimate relationship is that, when the covenant is made, neither party is already bound to someone else. The new bond must rest on honesty and exclusivity. Betrayal, deception, and overlapping relationships destroy personal trust and weaken society’s moral foundations. The Qur’an states the principle clearly and firmly:

وَالْمُحْصَنَاتُ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ إِلَّا مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ ۖ كِتَابَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ ۚ وَأُحِلَّ لَكُم مَّا وَرَاءَ ذَٰلِكُمْ أَن تَبْتَغُوا بِأَمْوَالِكُم مُّحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ

And married women are forbidden to you, except those whom your right hands possess. This is The God’s decree for you. Beyond these, it is lawful for you to seek marriage through your wealth, seeking chastity rather than illicit sexual relations.

An-Nisa 4:24

The expression al-muhsanat min al-nisa’—women protected within a marriage bond—clearly refers here to women already enclosed within a covenant and commitment. The Qur’an explicitly forbids a relationship with them. Entering a relationship with someone already committed to another directly violates the moral and social order. This concerns the preservation of trust throughout society, not merely an isolated personal act. Once that boundary is disregarded, covenants lose their meaning and emotional and family security begin to collapse. Fidelity and exclusivity are therefore more than moral recommendations. They are foundational Qur’anic principles that protect human relationships from disintegration.

وَلَا تَعْزِمُوا عُقْدَةَ النِّكَاحِ حَتَّىٰ يَبْلُغَ الْكِتَابُ أَجَلَهُ

Do not resolve to conclude the marriage bond until the prescribed term has reached its end.

Al-Baqarah 2:235

This verse brings the principle into sharper focus. Even during the transition out of a previous relationship, such as after divorce or a spouse’s death, the Qur’an does not permit a new marriage commitment until the ‘iddah, the prescribed waiting period, has fully ended. Even uncertainty about the previous commitment must be cleared before a new bond begins. Within Qur’anic reasoning, a human relationship is more than a physical connection: it is a covenant with its own duration, sanctity, and clearly defined boundaries. While obligations from the previous relationship remain in force, entering a new one is prohibited.

If such care is required even during this transitional period, entering a relationship with someone still actively committed to another is all the more unacceptable. The Qur’an is defending transparency, preventing overlapping relationships, and safeguarding human trust. Any simultaneous or conflicting commitments undermine the honesty and security on which a relationship depends.

4. Economic Maturity, Commitment, and Mahr

Agreeing to mahr, the woman’s marriage gift, is a sign of a man’s economic maturity. By accepting this obligation, he declares that he can support a household. In this understanding, mahr gives material weight to his decision, discourages impulsive decisions to separate, and creates a measure of balance in the relationship’s power and material security for the woman.

Mahr should therefore not become a display of status, an instrument of class pressure, or a burdensome transaction. It should express seriousness, ability, and responsibility. If it becomes so excessive that marriage is difficult or impossible, it has departed from its purpose. If it becomes so empty that it carries no meaningful responsibility, it also loses the spirit of commitment. Qur’anic balance requires considering the woman’s rights, the man’s responsibilities, and the practical possibility of building a healthy family together.

5. Repair, Separation, and the Possibility of a Healthier Relationship

If a relationship becomes toxic and reaches an impasse despite every precaution, The God does not insist that suffering continue. Efforts at reconciliation and arbitration between the families come first. If repair proves impossible, divorce is available as a last resort, governed by justice, so that both people may continue their development along another path.

This is an essential part of Qur’anic reasoning. The family is sacred, but endless suffering is not. The marriage covenant deserves respect, but when it becomes a source of oppression, humiliation, injury, and psychological breakdown, the Qur’an leaves open a path toward repair and, if necessary, a just separation. Qur’anic marriage therefore opposes both the secret, irresponsible beginning of a relationship and the merciless continuation of a poisonous one.

Conclusion

Ultimately, Qur’anic marriage offers a way out of confinement at the first level of the hierarchy of needs. When marriage becomes difficult, customs grow burdensome, expectations lose contact with ordinary life, or mahr and ceremony become an unbearable load, people remain trapped at the base of the pyramid: preoccupied with desire, delay, insecurity, and sometimes harmful behavior.

When marriage returns to its five simple foundations—maturity and sound judgment, transparency, fidelity, economic responsibility, and the possibility of repair or fair separation—it serves the purpose described in the Qur’an. It becomes a foundation that releases people from constant preoccupation with elementary needs and opens a path toward peace, dignity, and ultimately a fuller awareness and sakinah.

Qur’anic marriage neither suppresses sexual instinct nor releases it from responsibility. It transforms a biological need into a responsible bond, and that bond into tranquility, dignity, and spiritual growth.
