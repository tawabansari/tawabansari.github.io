Cutting the Thief’s Hand in the Qur’an: Amputation or Cutting Off Access to Theft?

Introduction

One of the Qur’an’s most difficult and contested passages concerning punishment is Al-Ma’idah 5:38:

وَالسَّارِقُ وَالسَّارِقَةُ فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا نَكَالًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

As for the male thief and the female thief, cut off their aydi (“hands”), as recompense for what they have earned, a deterrent from The God. And The God is Mighty, Wise.

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

Throughout history, this verse has most often been understood as requiring physical amputation. That understanding became established in systems of jurisprudence and was also placed in the hands of governments that were sometimes far removed from economic justice. The poor and powerless were punished in the name of religious law, while greater thieves—those with power, wealth, and influence—escaped justice.

But does the Qur’an actually command that every thief’s physical hand be severed from the body? Or does the verse carry a more precise, deeper, and wiser meaning?

By reading the Qur’an through the Qur’an itself, this essay argues that fa-qta‘u aydiyahuma, conventionally rendered “cut off their hands,” does not necessarily mean severing a bodily limb. In Qur’anic usage, qat‘, cutting or severing, is not always physical, and yad, hand, does not always refer to the palm or bodily organ. Read alongside other passages, the more defensible meaning is that the thief’s reach into theft must be cut off: the power, tools, access, influence, and opportunity through which illicit gain becomes possible must be removed.

1. The Root q-t-‘: Breaking a Connection

The Arabic root q-t-‘ has a broad range of meanings in the Qur’an. At its core is the breaking of a connection or the ending of something’s continuation. Such a break, however, need not be physical.

The Qur’an says:

وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَا أَمَرَ اللَّهُ بِهِ أَن يُوصَلَ

And they sever what The God has commanded to be joined.

Al-Baqarah 2:27

Here, severing does not mean cutting a physical body. It means breaking bonds that The God intends to remain intact: the bonds of covenant, kinship, rights, justice, and reconciliation.

Elsewhere, it says:

فَقُطِعَ دَابِرُ الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا

So the last remnant of the people who committed injustice was cut off.

Al-An'am 6:45

Here, qat‘ al-dabir means bringing an oppressive course of events to an end, cutting off its continuation. It does not mean amputating a limb.

In Surah An-Naml, the Queen of Sheba says:

مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا

I would not settle any matter.

An-Naml 27:32

Here, “cutting” a matter means reaching a final decision and ending a state of uncertainty.

And in Surah Muhammad:

وَتُقَطِّعُوا أَرْحَامَكُمْ

And sever your ties of kinship.

Muhammad 47:22

Once again, cutting means breaking human and social relationships, rather than severing a bodily organ.

The Qur’anic meaning of cutting is therefore not confined to the physical. It can involve breaking a bond, ending a continuation, cutting off a route, or settling a situation. In the verse about theft, we must likewise ask what is to be cut off: a bodily limb, or the access and power that make theft possible?

2. Yad: Hand, Power, Control, and Action

The word yad, “hand,” also extends beyond the physical organ in Qur’anic usage. It can refer to human power, ability, action, control, authority, and achievement.

The Qur’an repeatedly uses expressions such as:

ذَٰلِكَ بِمَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيكُمْ

That is because of what your hands have sent ahead.

Al-Imran 3:182

Clearly, the reference is not limited to acts performed by fingers and palms. Aydi, the plural of yad, stands here for people’s deeds, choices, and what they have brought about.

Elsewhere, it says:

وَلَا تُلْقُوا بِأَيْدِيكُمْ إِلَى التَّهْلُكَةِ

And do not, by your own hands, cast yourselves into destruction.

Al-Baqarah 2:195

Here too, “by your own hands” means through your own actions, decisions, and conduct, rather than simply through the physical movement of a hand.

So when the Qur’an says of thieves:

فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا

Then cut off their aydi (“hands”).

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

We can ask whether this must mean severing a physical organ. Could it instead mean ending the thief’s power to take possession, the capacity for illicit gain, the tools of theft, and access to opportunities for stealing?

This possibility gains weight from the phrase that immediately follows:

جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا

As recompense for what they have earned.

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

The verse describes the offense in terms of kasb, earning or acquisition. The subject therefore extends beyond a bodily hand to the act itself, the acquisition of property, the criminal proceeds, and the route through which other people’s wealth was taken. Recompense should accordingly address that same power and means of illicit acquisition.

3. Al-Ma’idah 5:33 and 5:38: Warfare and Theft

One of the most important passages for understanding the verse on theft appears a few verses earlier in the same surah. Concerning those who wage war against The God and His Messenger and spread corruption in the land, the Qur’an says:

إِنَّمَا جَزَاءُ الَّذِينَ يُحَارِبُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا أَن يُقَتَّلُوا أَوْ يُصَلَّبُوا أَوْ تُقَطَّعَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَأَرْجُلُهُم مِّنْ خِلَافٍ أَوْ يُنفَوْا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ

The recompense of those who wage war against The God and His Messenger and strive to spread corruption in the land is that they be killed, or crucified, or have their hands and feet cut off on opposite sides, or be banished from the land.

Al-Ma'idah 5:33

This passage cannot be set aside. Within the same surah, when the Qur’an describes an exceptionally severe bodily punishment, its wording is more specific: hands and feet, on opposite sides, alongside killing, crucifixion, and banishment. The context is also different from ordinary theft. It concerns muharabah—waging war—armed insecurity, widespread corruption, and an assault on the social order.

The wording in the verse on theft is quite different:

فَاقْطَعُوا أَيْدِيَهُمَا

Then cut off their aydi (“hands”).

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

There is no mention of feet, no min khilaf (“on opposite sides”), no accompanying reference to killing or crucifixion, and no setting of warfare or wartime devastation. The verse concerns theft, and its expression is shorter and different.

That difference matters. If 5:38 intended the same kind of severe bodily amputation, why did it not repeat the structure of 5:33? Why does it not specify where the hand should be cut? Why does it omit the feet and the phrase “on opposite sides”? Why is the command not placed in the context of waging war?

These differences caution against immediately equating “cutting the hands” in the theft verse with amputating a bodily organ. The command may concern cutting off the thief’s control and capacity to steal: ending the reach into other people’s property and removing the opportunity to repeat the offense.

4. Joseph’s Story: Supporting Evidence, Not the Main Foundation

In the story of Joseph, the Qur’an says:

وَقَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ

And they cut their hands.

Yusuf 12:31

Later, it says:

مَا بَالُ النِّسْوَةِ اللَّاتِي قَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ

What was the matter with the women who cut their hands?

Yusuf 12:50

Overcome by astonishment and momentarily inattentive, the women cut or wounded their hands. Their hands were not detached from their bodies.

A grammatical distinction matters here. The verb in this passage, qatta‘na, is Form II, traditionally called taf‘il. In the theft verse, fa-qta‘u comes from the basic Form I verb. The Joseph passage should therefore not be made the central pillar of the argument.

It nevertheless establishes an important point: in Qur’anic language, a combination of “cutting” and “hands” does not invariably mean completely severing hands from the body. Context, verb form, the subject of the ruling, and surrounding indications must determine the meaning.

The story of Joseph is thus supporting evidence rather than decisive proof. It helps prevent us from assigning only one possible meaning to the expression “cutting the hands.”

5. “Recompense for What They Have Earned”: Illicit Acquisition

In the verse about theft, the Qur’an does not simply say to cut a hand because property was taken. It says:

جَزَاءً بِمَا كَسَبَا

Recompense for what they have earned.

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

In the Qur’an, kasb commonly refers to human action and the result acquired through one’s own will and effort. Here, theft is described as a form of acquisition: the thief has obtained something wrongfully, taken possession of property, and used the power to take and control it to violate another person’s rights.

The recompense must therefore address that illicit acquisition. If a thief takes people’s property through influence, power, a network, a privileged position, access, tools, office, the market, an administrative institution, commerce, or any other means, the Qur’anic command is to cut off that power to appropriate it.

“Cutting the hands” means severing the thief’s route to theft. If an office made the theft possible, the offender should be removed from that office. If a financial network enabled it, the network should be shut down. If access to other people’s assets facilitated it, that access should be revoked. If particular tools or capabilities enabled the crime, those means should be taken away. If theft arose within an oppressive structure of poverty and corruption, that structure must be reformed.

This interpretation fits the verse more closely because it connects recompense to ma kasaba—“what they have earned”—rather than only to a physical part of the body.

6. “A Deterrent from The God”: Deterrence Guided by Wisdom

The verse also states the purpose of the ruling:

نَكَالًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ

A deterrent from The God.

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

Nakal is something that prevents an offense from recurring, serves as a lesson, and closes the way to continued oppression. Its purpose is not blind revenge, a spectacle of violence, or the destruction of a human being. It aims to restrain corruption and prevent repeated violations of other people’s property.

Understanding “cutting the hands” as removing access, power, and the tools of theft gives deterrence a more precise meaning. Society must cut off the offender’s route to crime so effectively that the offender cannot readily repeat it and others do not regard theft as an easy way to acquire wealth.

Such deterrence can take several forms: returning property, compensating losses, disqualification from office or financial access, supervision, legal restrictions, dismantling a theft network, and reforming the structures that make stealing possible or profitable.

On this reading, the Qur’anic ruling is not directed only at the petty thief. It bears even more heavily on those who steal on a large scale: people who exploit public funds, political power, markets, the law, poverty, and social injustice to acquire wealth unlawfully.

7. The Next Verse: Repentance and Reform

Immediately after the verse on theft, the Qur’an says:

فَمَن تَابَ مِن بَعْدِ ظُلْمِهِ وَأَصْلَحَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and makes amends, The God turns toward him in forgiveness. Surely The God is Forgiving, Merciful.

Al-Ma'idah 5:39

This verse is crucial. Immediately after stating the ruling, the Qur’an opens the path of repentance and reform. The ruling’s purpose is therefore to close the route to corruption while opening the way back.

One may object that a person can repent even after bodily punishment. But the central question remains: which reading better follows the direction of the passage? One that deprives a person of the ability to rebuild a healthy life, work, make restitution, and return to society? Or one that cuts off the person’s involvement in crime while leaving open a path to reform and return?

The next verse’s emphasis on repentance and making amends indicates that the ruling must be compatible with restoration and reform. The verse on theft should therefore be understood in light of the verse that follows, rather than in isolation from it.

8. Questions Raised by the Amputation Reading

If fa-qta‘u aydiyahuma is taken to mean the physical amputation of a hand, several fundamental questions arise:

What amount of theft warrants this punishment?

At what point should the hand be severed?

With what instrument, and by which authority?

Is stealing out of hunger equivalent to stealing out of greed?

Does large-scale embezzlement carry the same ruling as taking a small item?

If the economic system is itself oppressive, is that system entitled to cut off a poor person’s hand?

If rulers and other powerful figures are themselves responsible for the greatest thefts, how is the verse’s ruling to be applied?

The Qur’an does not provide these details in the verse. Traditional jurisprudence has sought answers through transmitted reports and additional conditions: nisab, a minimum threshold of value; hirz, the requirement that property have been kept in a protected place; the point of amputation; and other qualifications. These details, however, do not appear in the verse itself.

That silence matters. Given the social and penal consequences of the ruling, the absence of specifications concerning amount, point of amputation, instruments, and conditions of enforcement demands closer attention to the meanings of qat‘ and yad. Perhaps the difficulty lies not in the Qur’an but in a reading that immediately reduces “cutting the hands” to severing a bodily organ.

9. The God Is Mighty and Wise

The verse ends with two divine attributes:

وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

And The God is Mighty, Wise.

Al-Ma'idah 5:38

‘Aziz, Mighty, describes invincible power. Yet divine power is not accompanied by injustice or disproportion. Hakim, Wise, means that His ruling rests on wisdom, proportion, purpose, justice, and knowledge.

The God’s ruling therefore cannot be understood in a way that makes it a tool for oppressive rulers to amputate the hands of the poor while protecting those responsible for far greater thefts. A wise ruling must address the roots of the offense, remove the capacity to repeat it, protect people’s property, establish social justice, and leave the possibility of reform open.

The God is Mighty: He does not need to prove His authority by cutting human bodies. And He is Wise: His ruling must therefore accord with justice, deterrence, proportion, and the possibility of repentance.

Conclusion

Read together, these passages establish several points:

First: The root q-t-‘ does not always mean physical cutting in the Qur’an. It can mean breaking a bond, ending a continuation, traversing a route, or bringing a situation to a decision.

Second: Yad does not always denote a bodily organ. It can refer to a person’s action, power, control, authority, tools, and acquisitions.

Third: The passage on waging war in the same surah demonstrates a more specified structure when describing severe bodily punishment: hands and feet, on opposite sides, in the context of warfare, alongside killing and crucifixion. The theft verse does not have that structure.

Fourth: Joseph’s story shows that combining “cutting” with “hands” does not necessarily entail the complete severance of a limb. Because the verb forms differ, however, this passage supplies supporting evidence rather than the argument’s main foundation.

Fifth: Jaza’an bi-ma kasaba—“recompense for what they have earned”—directs attention to criminal acquisition, illicit proceeds, and the means of wrongful appropriation.

Sixth: Nakalan min Allah—“a deterrent from The God”—identifies deterrence as the ruling’s purpose, rather than revenge or the destruction of a person.

Seventh: The following verse opens the way to repentance and reform. The ruling must therefore be understood in a manner compatible with the possibility of making amends and returning to society.

On this basis, the stronger and more Qur’an-grounded reading of fa-qta‘u aydiyahuma is to cut off the thief’s reach into theft: to remove the control, access, tools, influence, power, and opportunity through which illicit gain is obtained. Properly understood, the ruling addresses more than petty theft. Above all, it confronts those who take people’s wealth through power, office, networks, the law, markets, or structural oppression.

The Qur’an is a book of justice, not an instrument of violence. Its ruling on theft must be understood in the light of that justice, wisdom, mercy, repentance, and reform.
