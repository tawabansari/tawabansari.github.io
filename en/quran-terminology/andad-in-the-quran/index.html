Andad (Rivals Set Up Alongside God) in the Qur’an: How Do We Give Created Things Divine Standing?

Introduction

The Qur’an uses andad (rivals or counterparts that people set up alongside God) to describe a problem that deserves attention in its own right. Discussions of religion more often focus on shirk (assigning partners to God in worship or divine authority) or kufr (rejection or concealment of truth, or ingratitude, depending on context). The more specific term is sometimes passed over with a brief translation such as “idols” or “equals of God.” Those expressions convey part of the meaning. Do they capture everything the Qur’an is asking us to examine?

Are these rivals necessarily statues made of stone or wood? Can a person acknowledge one Creator while granting something else a competing place in belief, worship, or conduct? Could attachment to a loved one, allegiance to a leader, or acceptance of an authority develop into such a relationship? And how does setting up a rival relate to assigning a partner to God?

This study asks what the term means, in which circumstances the Qur’an uses it, and what its different occurrences have in common.

Our method begins with the Qur’an itself. We examine every occurrence of the word from the Arabic root N D D, reading each in its immediate setting. We then compare these passages and consider other Qur’anic expressions that help clarify their meaning. On that basis, we explore the connections with worship, love, obedience, human authority, rejection of truth, and the attribution of partners to God.

Finally, we consider the logical and philosophical implications for contemporary life. The aim is to understand what the warning asks of us today without turning every human attachment or mistake into an accusation of idolatry.

1. What Does the Word Mean?

Andad (rivals or counterparts) is the plural of nidd (a counterpart or rival placed alongside or against another). Both belong to the Arabic root N D D. In the uses examined here, the word involves a position of parity, competition, or opposition.

It is close in meaning to mithl (a likeness or counterpart), but simple resemblance does not exhaust its force. Two people can resemble one another in a particular respect without competing for the same position. To treat one as a rival or equal of the other introduces a further question about the standing each is given.

Other Arabic words from N D D can refer to fleeing or moving away. That does not justify importing those senses into the noun wherever it occurs. In the Qur’anic passages at issue, the surrounding argument supports the meaning of a counterpart or rival.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an, each time in the same plural noun: Al-Baqarah 2:22 and Al-Baqarah 2:165, Ibrahim 14:30, Saba 34:33, Az-Zumar 39:8, and Fussilat 41:9.

In five of these passages, the noun accompanies verbs meaning to make, place, or assign. In Al-Baqarah 2:165, it accompanies a verb meaning to take or adopt.

The distinction is crucial: these passages do not say that God has actual equals. They say that human beings set up or adopt rivals for Him.

A person can place someone on a throne without giving that individual the qualities, legitimacy, or power required to be a king. The status people assign is not necessarily a description of what its recipient truly is.

Likewise, an object of devotion does not have to possess divine attributes for a person to treat it as though it did. The question is what position that person has given it in belief and practice.

We still need to establish which kinds of position are involved. The six Qur’anic settings provide the starting point.

2. The Six Qur’anic Occurrences

2.1. Al-Baqarah 2:22: Recognizing the Creator While Setting Up Rivals

Al-Baqarah 2:21 calls people to worship the Lord who created them and those who came before them. Al-Baqarah 2:22 then speaks of the earth, the sky, rainfall, and the fruits provided as sustenance. It concludes by warning people not to set up rivals for God while they know.

The sequence connects the recognition of the Creator and provider with worship and the refusal to give Him partners. Earth, rain, and sustenance belong to an order that human beings did not create. How could something produced within that order, or dependent upon it, occupy the position of its Creator and Lord?

The closing words raise a further question: why does the warning add “while you know”?

Other passages, including Al-Ankabut 29:61, Al-Ankabut 29:62, Al-Ankabut 29:63, Luqman 31:25, and Az-Zukhruf 43:87, describe people who, when asked who created the heavens and the earth, acknowledge God as Creator.

Acknowledging a Creator, therefore, does not necessarily mean a person has ceased assigning partners to Him. Someone may recognize God as the source of creation while also granting another being a share in worship or in powers and authority that belong to God.

The first occurrence brings together recognition of the Creator, worship, and the rejection of partners. A statement about who created the world is not the whole question; what follows from that recognition also matters.

2.2. Al-Baqarah 2:165: Giving a Rival a Place in Our Love

Al-Baqarah 2:163 declares the oneness of the deity worthy of worship. Al-Baqarah 2:164 points to signs in the creation of the heavens and the earth, the alternation of night and day, winds, clouds, and other features of the natural world.

Al-Baqarah 2:165 then describes people who adopt rivals besides God and love them as God is loved:

Al-Baqarah 2:165

“They love them with a love like that given to God.”

Love adds another dimension to the problem. It is more than the name of a feeling. What we love draws our attention, shapes our priorities, and may lead us to seek its approval or arrange our conduct around its demands.

Yet loving others is not, by itself, assigning partners to God. The Qur’an calls for kindness toward parents, respect for the rights of relatives, and justice toward other people. Al-Baqarah 2:165 does not establish that intense love for a child, spouse, friend, or homeland is sufficient to make that person or place a divine rival.

The issue is giving love for something other than God the position occupied by devotion to God in worship and servitude.

The verse contrasts this with the believers’ stronger love for God. Human loves are not abolished; their place in relation to ultimate devotion and loyalty is clarified.

The end of the verse also locates all power with God. Attachment can lead people to imagine that a person or thing has more power or standing than it really possesses. Whatever power a created being has remains limited and dependent.

Al-Baqarah 2:166 and Al-Baqarah 2:167 continue the discussion. Those who were followed disown their followers when they see the punishment. Their followers wish they could return and disown those leaders in turn.

The continuation connects love and attachment with following others and relying on them as authorities. It does not, by itself, establish that every rival mentioned in Al-Baqarah 2:165 was a human person. It does establish the importance of the relationship between leaders and followers to this sequence of verses.

In this setting, the question is not confined to the name someone gives an object of worship. It also concerns love, dependence, allegiance, and perceptions of power.

2.3. Ibrahim 14:30: Rivals and Departure from God’s Path

Ibrahim 14:28, Ibrahim 14:29, and Ibrahim 14:30 describe people who exchange God’s favor for rejection, lead their people toward ruin, and set up rivals for God. Here the concept is connected with being led away from God’s path.

Setting up a rival can therefore reach beyond an individual belief and affect other people’s lives. Someone who grants divine standing to a being may invite others to recognize that standing too.

The invitation may operate through persuasion and encouragement, or through social influence, fear, partisanship, and power.

This does not warrant saying that every person caught in the practice consciously intends to mislead others. A person can be mistaken and spread that mistake without recognizing it as such. Intent and the consequences of an action must be distinguished.

What this passage makes clear is the connection between setting up rivals, departure from God’s path, and the spread of misguidance.

2.4. Saba 34:33: The Role of Powerful Leaders

Saba 34:31, Saba 34:32, and Saba 34:33 portray an exchange at judgment between people who had been kept weak and those who had behaved arrogantly from positions of influence.

The former blame the powerful for leading them astray. The latter reject the accusation. The followers then point to the leaders’ persistent scheming: they had commanded people to reject God and set up rivals for Him.

This is one of the clearest connections between the practice and social power. Its wording nevertheless requires care.

The passage does not explicitly identify the leaders themselves as the rivals. It says that they commanded people to reject God and assign Him rivals. Their role in promoting the belief and the identity of its objects are related questions, but they are not identical.

Two points follow. First, such beliefs can be spread through leaders’ influence, propaganda, and commands. Second, followers remain responsible. Blaming leaders does not automatically erase their own responsibility for choosing whom to follow.

The passage warns that social power and human authority, accepted without being tested against truth, can draw people into misguidance and the attribution of partners to God.

2.5. Az-Zumar 39:8: Forgetting God After Receiving Relief

Az-Zumar 39:8 describes a person who calls upon the Lord and turns back to Him when suffering. After receiving a favor, the person forgets the distress that prompted the appeal and sets up rivals for God.

This is a recognizable pattern in human life. Danger and helplessness can expose the limits of the powers on which we normally rely. In such a moment, a person may turn to God and become aware of dependence upon Him.

Once the crisis passes, however, comfort and new opportunities may so absorb the same person that the earlier recognition fades.

Al-Ankabut 29:65 describes a related situation: people call upon God sincerely when facing danger at sea, but after being brought safely to land they again assign partners to Him. Together, these passages show how unstable a person’s awareness of dependence can be.

Az-Zumar 39:8 does not explicitly say that wealth, health, or the favor received is itself the rival. It describes someone who forgets after receiving relief and then turns to setting up rivals. That sequence should not be replaced by an assumption that the benefit itself was worshipped.

Enjoying a blessing or benefiting from the resources of life is not automatically idolatry. The warning is that a truth recognized in need can be abandoned when circumstances change.

2.6. Fussilat 41:9: Can a Creature Be the Creator’s Equal?

Fussilat 41:9 asks how people can reject the One who created the earth and assign Him rivals, when He is the Lord of all worlds. The following verses continue the discussion of the creation of the earth and the heavens.

The setting is close to that of Al-Baqarah 2:22. Both passages direct attention to creation and then question the act of giving the Creator counterparts.

The central question is straightforward. How could something created and dependent upon the order of creation stand alongside its Creator in the capacity of creating and sustaining it?

People may attribute divine power to a natural phenomenon, a living being, or a human personality. But attributing that power does not change what the recipient actually is.

Once again, we must distinguish the reality of a being from the status human beings assign to it.

3. What the Six Passages Show

Read together, the six occurrences reveal several connected concerns. Al-Baqarah 2:22 and Fussilat 41:9 address creation and lordship. Al-Baqarah 2:165 brings love and attachment into view. Ibrahim 14:30 and Saba 34:33 connect rivals with misguidance and the influence of leaders. Az-Zumar 39:8 describes forgetting God and returning to such practices after receiving a favor.

In every case, people give God rivals; the Qur’an does not recognize those rivals as genuinely possessing divine standing.

Ash-Shuara 26:97, Ash-Shuara 26:98, and Ash-Shuara 26:99 help clarify the distinction. People who had gone astray acknowledge that they placed their objects of worship on a level with the Lord of all worlds:

Ash-Shu'ara 26:98

“When we equated you with the Lord of all worlds.”

The verb nusawwikum (we equate you or place you on an equal footing) makes the relationship explicit. It describes the assigning of parity that is central to the discussion of rivals.

The speakers do not say that their objects of worship truly possessed the power and standing of the Lord of all worlds. They admit that they themselves treated them as His equals.

Other passages deny that God has a real likeness, equal, or counterpart: Ash-Shura 42:11, Al-Ikhlas 112:4, and Maryam 19:65. An-Nahl 16:20 and An-Nahl 16:21 also remind people that the beings invoked besides God are themselves created and cannot create.

The distinction is fundamental: God has no actual rival; human beings assign something the position of a rival in their beliefs or worship.

Does this make every likeness or comparison between two things an instance of the problem? No. These passages concern parity in positions connected with a person’s relationship to God, worship of Him, and the attributes and rights that belong uniquely to Him.

To understand the concept more precisely, we therefore need to examine those positions in the Qur’an itself.

4. What Positions Does the Qur’an Reserve for God?

The Qur’an does not explain divine oneness only by saying, “Do not assign partners.” It repeatedly asks what those alleged partners can actually do and what authority they possess.

Ar-Rad 13:16, Luqman 31:11, and Fatir 35:40 raise the question of creation: have the supposed partners created anything? Yunus 10:34 and Ar-Rum 30:40 address creation and provision. Yunus 10:18 and Al-Furqan 25:55 question the attribution of benefit, harm, and intercession to objects worshipped besides God.

Yusuf 12:40 and Ash-Shura 42:21 concern judgment and the authority to prescribe religion. Al-Baqarah 2:165 places love for rivals in relation to love for God.

These passages do not provide a closed checklist covering every divine right. They do identify important fields: creation and lordship; worship and invocation; independent divine power and authority; religious legislation; and the ultimate claims of love and devotion.

Understanding them requires distinguishing God from the natural means through which events occur. A doctor can genuinely contribute to healing through medical knowledge. That does not make the doctor the Creator. Seeking another person’s help, advice, or expertise does not by itself make that person an object of worship.

Similarly, love for other people, ordinary fear of danger, and trust in a competent person are not identical with worship or ultimate reliance upon God.

The presence of love, fear, obedience, or a request for help is therefore not enough to establish that a rival has been set up. We must ask what kind of power, standing, or authority is being attributed to the other party.

Assigned Status and the Need for Evidence

In Yusuf 12:39 and Yusuf 12:40, Joseph asks his fellow prisoners whether separate lords are better than the One God who prevails over all. He then describes the objects worshipped besides God as names that people and their ancestors have assigned, without God having sent down any sultan (authorizing proof or warrant) for them.

An-Najm 53:23 likewise speaks of names that people and their ancestors have devised without divine warrant.

Naming, imitation, and inherited belief can thus give something a standing for which there is no adequate evidence. The relevant question is not how familiar or socially accepted that status has become. It is whether the status is true and what justifies assigning it.

Anyone claiming that a being possesses divine power, a right to worship, or an authority belonging to God must support the claim. Popularity, antiquity, the number of adherents, and the respect of a community do not, by themselves, establish its truth.

5. How Does Setting Up Rivals Relate to Assigning Partners to God?

Shirk (assigning a partner to God) belongs to the Arabic root SH R K, whose meanings involve sharing and partnership. The word for a rival emphasizes parity or competing standing; the language of partnership emphasizes giving another a share.

The terms are not linguistically identical, but they meet in questions of worship and lordship. To grant something the position of God’s counterpart is to give it a share in a standing that belongs to Him.

One point is especially important: assigning partners to God does not necessarily mean believing in two creators who are equal in every respect.

Az-Zumar 39:3 describes people who take protectors besides God and justify worshipping them by saying that they bring them nearer to Him. They need not be claiming that those protectors are greater than God or created the world. They may acknowledge God’s superiority and still worship other beings as a means of approaching Him.

The Qur’an does not endorse that justification. Yunus 10:18 likewise describes people worshipping things that cannot harm or benefit them and calling those things their intercessors with God.

It is therefore unnecessary to believe that another being equals God in every attribute for the question of partnership to arise. Giving another being a place in worship that belongs to God is itself at issue.

Setting up rivals is one of the Qur’an’s ways of describing these attributed counterparts and partners. They are not real peers of God; their supposed status is a human attribution.

Is Every Intermediary a Partner to God?

An intermediary in human life is not automatically an object of worship. Teachers communicate knowledge, doctors contribute to healing, and judges resolve disputes. The Qur’an does not reject assistance, teaching, adjudication, or human responsibility.

Even when discussing intercession, Al-Baqarah 2:255 speaks of God’s permission. But intercession dependent upon that permission is different from worshipping a being other than God in the hope of drawing nearer to Him.

Permission does not mean that independent divine power has been transferred to another. Nor does the existence of permitted intercession establish that God commands people to worship someone else or invent intermediary deities.

The boundary is essential: being a means of assistance is not the same as becoming an object of worship. This distinction is explored further in Intercession in the Qur’an.

6. The Connection with Rejection and Ingratitude

In four of the six occurrences, the language of kufr (rejection or concealment of truth, or ingratitude, according to context) appears in the verse itself or its immediate surroundings.

In Ibrahim 14:28, Ibrahim 14:29, and Ibrahim 14:30, setting up rivals is connected with responding to God’s favor through rejection. In Saba 34:33, leaders command their followers to reject God and assign Him rivals. In Az-Zumar 39:8, the person who forgets after receiving a favor and sets up rivals is warned about that rejection. Fussilat 41:9 places rejection of the Creator of the earth alongside the act of assigning Him counterparts.

The association is substantial, but rejection and partnership are not interchangeable terms. Depending on its setting, the language of rejection can concern denying, concealing truth, refusing signs, or failing to acknowledge a blessing. Partnership concerns giving another a share alongside God. Setting up rivals concerns granting something the standing of His counterpart.

All three may occur in a single pattern of conduct without losing their distinct meanings.

A person might recognize God as provider, then forget that recognition after receiving a favor and turn to invented objects of worship. In that situation, ingratitude and the attribution of rivals can form part of the same course of action.

The wording “while you know” in Al-Baqarah 2:22 is relevant here too. Someone can possess a measure of knowledge about the Creator and act contrary to it.

A verbal acknowledgment of God does not, by itself, rule out assigning Him partners. Equally, every human error is not automatically evidence that a rival has been set up.

For a fuller examination of rejection, concealment, ignorance, and ingratitude, see Who Is a Disbeliever, and What Does Disbelief Mean?.

7. Can Religious Leaders Be Given Divine Standing?

Does the practice concern only statues and nonhuman beings, or can people themselves be elevated into positions that belong uniquely to God?

Al-Imran 3:64, in its invitation to a common commitment, rejects people taking one another as lords besides God. At-Tawbah 9:31 describes people taking their religious scholars and monks as lords besides Him.

Al-Imran 3:79 and Al-Imran 3:80 make a related point even about a person whom God has given scripture, judgment, and prophethood: such a person should not call others to become his servants instead of God’s.

A human being’s religious distinction, however great, does not confer divinity. Respecting prophets and scholars is different from turning them into objects of worship.

Ash-Shura 42:21 raises the question of partners who prescribe religion for people without God’s permission. Al-Anam 6:121, in its particular context of food rules and the arguments of opponents, warns that obedience to those drawing people into opposition to God’s command can lead to partnership with Him.

These passages connect the issue with obedience and religious authority. They do not establish that every act of obedience to a person, or every disagreement over religious interpretation, is an instance of it.

The Qur’an also commands that trusts be delivered to those entitled to them and that judgment be just in An-Nisa 4:58. An-Nisa 4:59 speaks of obeying God, the Messenger, and those entrusted with authority, while directing disputes back to God and the Messenger. Ash-Shura 42:38 identifies consultation as a characteristic of believers.

Leadership, judgment, consultation, and the organization of collective life are therefore not rejected in principle. The question is what kind of authority and standing is granted to a human source.

A scholar may investigate the Qur’an and support a conclusion with evidence. A judge may adjudicate justly. A community may adopt rules to manage its affairs.

A different issue arises when a person or institution claims independent divine authority, or declares something religiously obligatory or forbidden without evidence and permission from God.

At-Tawbah 9:37 gives an example involving the manipulation of sacred months: people move the period of prohibition to make what is lawful and unlawful fit their own wishes. An-Nahl 16:116 forbids attributing declarations of lawful and unlawful to God on the basis of unsupported assertions.

Power, tradition, and religious office do not entitle anyone to prescribe in God’s name what He has not prescribed.

Learning and human responsibility exceed their proper limits when they are turned into an independent, sacred authority that cannot be tested against God’s judgment.

Yet establishing that an opinion conflicts with the Qur’an also requires evidence and investigation. No researcher or school may simply declare its own interpretation to be God’s certain judgment and, on that basis, deny others the right to question or examine it.

The same discipline must apply to every claim of religious authority, including our own.

8. Can Desire Become an Object of Worship?

Al-Furqan 25:43 and Al-Jathiyah 45:23 describe a person who takes hawa (personal desire or inclination) as an ilah (a deity or object of worship).

These verses do not use the word for rivals examined in this study. They must not be counted as additional occurrences of N D D. Conceptually, however, they show that the Qur’an’s discussion of taking something as a deity is not confined to making a statue or worshipping an external being.

A person may elevate preferences and impulses until truth itself is judged by them. In Sad 38:26, David is warned against following desire because it can lead him away from God’s path. There is a conceptual connection here with departure from that path in Ibrahim 14:30.

Consider someone who hates another person. Evidence of that person’s innocence emerges, but the accuser refuses to accept it because he wants the person punished. Personal desire has been placed above a just assessment of the evidence.

Does every such wrong, by itself, amount to setting up a divine rival? That conclusion does not follow. The verses about making desire a deity and the verses about rivals differ in wording and immediate setting.

What they share is a warning about turning away from divine guidance and giving something else priority over it. Their conceptual relationship should be explored without pretending that the two expressions are identical in every respect.

9. Rivals in Contemporary Life: Has the Danger Disappeared?

Many people today do not worship statues made of stone or wood. Does that mean the danger of assigning rivals to God has disappeared? An answer requires more care than a quick analogy.

The direct occurrences in the Qur’an concern creation, worship, love, misguidance, leaders, and forgetting God. They provide grounds for examining contemporary life.

Yet a new example should not immediately be declared a definite instance of the Qur’anic concept. We first need to establish what standing is being given to the person, object, or idea, and how that standing relates to a right belonging uniquely to God.

9.1. Love of Family: When Affection Becomes an Excuse for Injustice

Love for a spouse, children, and family is among the most natural and valuable human attachments. The Qur’an neither rejects it nor asks people to become indifferent to those close to them.

Imagine a father determined to provide his children with an extravagant home, extensive opportunities, and a life of comfort. His lawful income cannot satisfy all these ambitions, so he abuses his public position, takes bribes, or deprives others of their rights.

When challenged, he replies, “I am doing this for my children’s happiness.” Love of family has become a justification for injustice.

Why should one family’s wishes be fulfilled by depriving another family of what it is entitled to? Does affection for those close to us authorize harm to others?

An-Nisa 4:135 calls on believers to stand firmly for justice even when truthful testimony is against themselves, their parents, or close relatives. Al-Maidah 5:8 warns that hostility toward a group must not lead people away from justice.

Love and hatred must not be allowed to rewrite the standard of justice.

Suppose, however, that the father does more than commit a wrong. He treats the wishes of the person he loves as an absolute claim, consciously placing them above God’s command and other people’s rights. Love has then been assigned a position beyond its proper human place.

The issue is no longer simply that he loves his family. It is which claim he recognizes as higher when loyalty to God and the wishes of a loved one conflict. At that point, the danger of assigning a rival through love and allegiance can be examined in light of Al-Baqarah 2:165.

Even so, committing a sin for the sake of family does not, by itself, establish partnership with God. Moral weakness must be distinguished from knowingly giving a loved one the standing of an ultimate authority.

The example extends beyond spouses and children. Someone might give false testimony to protect a brother, hide the truth to preserve the reputation of an ethnic community, or defend a favored group’s evident wrongdoing.

In each case, the question is whether affection and loyalty bring a person closer to respecting what is right, or become instruments for setting it aside.

9.2. Religious Personalities: When Respect Becomes Unquestionable Authority

People can learn from scholars, honor teachers, and follow a capable leader. None of these relationships is inherently worship.

Now suppose a group decides that its leader’s judgments must never be questioned. Even clear evidence from the Qur’an against one of those judgments is dismissed or reinterpreted simply to protect the leader’s position.

The scholar has ceased to be treated as a knowledgeable human being whose claims can be examined. He has been made an authority immune to challenge.

The problem is not merely whether a particular judgment happens to be right or wrong. The more basic question is what supplies the final standard: evidence and truth, or a person’s identity and social standing?

When a human opinion is independently invested with the status of certain divine judgment, the danger of assigning a rival through religious authority arises.

Trusting a scholar, consulting an expert, following a sound argument, or even choosing the wrong intellectual authority does not automatically establish that this has happened. The standing attributed to the person remains decisive.

9.3. Political and Social Power: When a Human Command Overrides Truth

Az-Zukhruf 43:54 describes Pharaoh treating his people with contempt and their obedience to him. Hud 11:97 speaks of people following Pharaoh’s command even though it did not lead rightly.

Social power can therefore draw people into obedience to a course inconsistent with truth and justice.

A modern employee may know that a superior’s instruction will violate people’s rights and still carry it out to protect a position or its privileges. Someone else may defend a favored political leader so fiercely that no evidence of lying, corruption, or oppression is allowed to count against that leader.

These actions can reveal partisanship, fear, self-interest, or moral corruption. But applying the Qur’anic concept of rivals requires a further inquiry: has the person given that authority a position beyond legitimate human power?

Obeying a just law, cooperating socially, and respecting legitimate authority do not conflict with devotion to God. The danger arises when power is made sacred and beyond criticism, so that truth and justice must give way before it. Law and government are needed to organize collective life, but no human office becomes exempt from responsibility and justice simply because it holds power.

9.4. Wealth: When a Means of Living Becomes the Standard of Value

Wealth can support a family, assist other people, and contribute to a community’s flourishing.

Consider a business owner who places personal profit above every other consideration. He knowingly sells defective goods, violates workers’ rights, and harms others to increase his income.

Asked why, he replies, “In business, profit is all that matters. Whatever earns more is right for me.” Wealth has shifted from a means of meeting needs to a standard for deciding what is morally right.

Al-Qasas 28:78 describes Qarun, a wealthy figure in the Qur’anic account, attributing his riches to knowledge he possesses. Saba 34:35 describes people treating abundant wealth and children as grounds for superiority and immunity from punishment.

These passages warn that wealth can foster false perceptions of independence, power, and personal worth.

Being wealthy or wanting comfort is not, by itself, assigning a partner to God. The danger concerns the final standing attributed to wealth: does it become an authority before which no right or responsibility is allowed to remain binding?

9.5. Science: When a Method Becomes an Exclusive Judge of All Reality

The natural and empirical sciences are important means of understanding the world. Observation, experimentation, measurement, and the assessment of evidence enable people to discover how many natural phenomena work and to revise their knowledge.

The Qur’an also calls people to observe and reflect on the heavens and the earth, as in Yunus 10:101. Within its field, empirical science has genuine authority. Scientific results should be evaluated by sound scientific methods, not personal preference or religious prejudice.

Every method, however, is suited to particular kinds of questions. A set of scales can measure weight. Those same scales cannot, on their own, determine whether a philosophical argument is valid or an ethical judgment is just.

The limitation does not make the scales worthless. It means the instrument must fit the question. A corresponding distinction matters when considering the reach of the natural sciences.

To say that God’s existence has not been measured by the ordinary instruments of empirical science is to make a statement about a kind of evidence and the reach of a method. To conclude that anything not measurable by that method necessarily does not exist is to make a further claim.

The second claim is not simply a laboratory finding. It is a philosophical judgment about the scope of reality. There is a logical gap between “this method cannot establish the existence of something” and “that thing therefore does not exist.” Invoking the authority of science does not, by itself, bridge the gap.

Al-Jathiyah 45:24 describes people who restrict life to the present world and attribute their destruction to the passage of time, without knowledge supporting their claim. The verse is not a discussion of modern empirical science. It does, however, underscore the need for knowledge and grounds when making claims about existence.

Al-Isra 17:36 likewise forbids following what one has no knowledge of. A religious claim needs grounds; a philosophical claim about all reality does not become exempt from that requirement merely by being presented in the name of science.

We should distinguish empirical science from exclusive scientism (the philosophical claim that empirical science alone can determine every kind of truth). The first is a method for investigating natural phenomena. The second makes a claim about the limits of all knowledge.

Could this kind of absolutizing be relevant to the Qur’anic discussion of rivals? If someone makes a human method or theory the final, unquestionable judge of every truth, there is a philosophical comparison to be made with assigning an authority a standing beyond its actual limits.

The comparison alone does not prove that the person has assigned a rival to God. That further conclusion would require showing that, in the person’s beliefs and conduct, the method or theory occupies a position the Qur’an reserves for God.

Nor should everyone who does not believe in God be described as a follower of exclusive scientism. Some arrive at their position through philosophical arguments or a different assessment of evidence. Their views must be addressed through the reasons they actually give.

Questioning, scientific doubt, and criticism of arguments are not themselves the creation of divine rivals. The problem considered here is the exemption of a limited instrument or theory from scrutiny, together with an attribution of authority beyond its actual capacity and field.

10. Distinguishing Sin, Error, and the Attribution of Divine Standing

The concept examined here can involve worship, love, power, and authority. Used without boundaries, however, it could turn every mistake or sin into an accusation of assigning partners to God. The passages we have examined do not support that conclusion.

Suppose two people take bribes. The first acts out of greed or moral weakness. He knows he has done wrong, may regret it, and may try to repair the harm.

The second not only takes a bribe but claims that his wishes or his family’s advantage are so supreme that even a clear divine command should not stand in their way.

Both acts violate justice and other people’s rights. Yet the standing the two people give their desires is not necessarily the same.

In the first case, wrongdoing and moral weakness are evident. In the second, another question arises: has the person assigned a competing authority to personal desire or advantage in relation to God’s command?

Even then, conscious disobedience, denial of a command, and a claim to independent authority against God must be distinguished. Every act of disobedience does not, by itself, demonstrate belief in a divine counterpart.

The same boundary matters in love and obedience. A person can trust a leader and be mistaken without making that leader a deity. Someone may obey a wrongful command out of fear without attributing divine standing to the person who issued it.

An observed action alone is therefore insufficient. Its relationship to belief, worship, authority, and the position assigned to something other than God must be examined.

Three considerations are especially important in light of the passages discussed.

First is the kind of standing assigned. Has a being or authority been given a share in worship, lordship, or an authority belonging uniquely to God?

Second is the evidence and legitimacy of that standing. Is there a warrant for attributing it, or has it been constructed through conjecture, imitation, and naming? Yusuf 12:40 and An-Najm 53:23 bring this question into focus.

Third is the authority’s relationship to truth and guidance. Are its claims and position open to examination, or has it been made so absolute that even a clear divine command and its supporting evidence must be set aside before it?

These considerations help distinguish legitimate human authority from the invention of divine rivals. None should become an instrument for hastily declaring people unbelievers.

Understanding a person’s intention, knowledge, circumstances, and responsibility requires more than observing a single outward act. Before this study becomes a means of judging other people, it should prompt us to examine our own beliefs and loyalties.

11. The Philosophical Problem: Giving Something More Standing Than It Has

A common thread runs through the preceding examples. People can take something from its limited and appropriate place and assign it a position exceeding what it actually is.

Knowledge serves understanding, yet a method can be made into an authority that dismisses every question beyond its own reach. Law organizes collective life and protects rights, yet it can be treated as so sacred that even manifest injustice no longer permits criticism.

Leadership serves guidance and administration, yet it can develop into a claim to unlimited domination. Wealth meets the needs of life, yet it can become the final measure of human worth. Love supports connection, care, and kindness, yet it can be severed from justice and used to excuse harm.

In each example, a means, relationship, or human value may have been carried beyond its proper limits. Does every such distortion constitute the Qur’anic practice of assigning rivals to God? Not necessarily.

What the Qur’an condemns under this expression is specifically setting up counterparts for God. A contemporary practice can be examined within that concept only when its relationship to God’s unique standing has been established.

This distinction guards against two errors. One is restricting the concept to visible idols and missing other ways of assigning divine standing. The other is expanding it until every attachment, mistake, exercise of power, or human theory is automatically treated as a rival.

The sound approach is to establish the Qur’anic meaning and then test each proposed example against it.

Az-Zumar 39:29 presents the contrast between a man subject to quarrelsome joint owners and a man belonging to one owner. Yusuf 12:39 asks about separate lords in contrast with the One God. These images illuminate divided allegiances and conflicting commands.

If someone has established several independent and final authorities, what standard will settle the matter when their demands conflict?

At this level, tawhid (affirming God’s oneness and exclusive right to worship) means more than acknowledging a single Creator. It calls people to recognize boundaries: between God and creatures, worship and respect, divine authority and human discretion, and a means of assistance and an object of worship.

Conclusion: What Have We Placed in God’s Position?

The six occurrences examined in this study describe rivals that human beings assign to God. Al-Baqarah 2:22 and Fussilat 41:9 place that act against the uniqueness of the Creator. Al-Baqarah 2:165 addresses love for rivals in relation to love for God. Ibrahim 14:30 and Saba 34:33 connect it with misguidance and leaders’ influence. Az-Zumar 39:8 describes a return to it after a person receives a favor and forgets God.

Other passages, including Ash-Shuara 26:98, Yusuf 12:40, Al-Imran 3:64, and Ash-Shura 42:21, help clarify the underlying problem. People can assign something a standing and authority it does not truly possess, or give another a place in worship and religious obedience that belongs uniquely to God.

The concept cannot therefore be confined to statues of stone or wood. Such objects can be examples, but the decisive feature is not the material or outward form. It is the position granted to the object of devotion.

This also explains the connection with assigning partners to God. By treating something as His counterpart, a person grants it a share in His unique standing—even while continuing to acknowledge God verbally as Creator and supreme Lord.

At the same time, not every love, act of obedience, sin, or mistake can be called the setting up of a rival. The distinctions between respect and worship, legitimate authority and claims to divine power, moral failure and attributed divinity must be maintained.

Contemporary life continues to pose the questions raised by these verses. Love of family may become an excuse for injustice. A religious leader may be placed beyond criticism. Political power may be treated as higher than truth. A limited method of inquiry may be declared the final judge of all reality.

These examples are not, by themselves, conclusive judgments that someone has assigned partners to God. They are reasons to examine the positions we grant our authorities and loyalties.

The Qur’anic question is not only what we verbally call a deity. It is what we worship, what we invest with divine standing, and what we give priority when truth conflicts with other demands.

Loving family, respecting scholars, benefiting from science, obeying just laws, and using wealth can all be valuable in their proper place. The danger begins when something is granted a position to which it is not entitled and set against a standing that belongs uniquely to God.

These are not God’s actual equals. They are the rivals human beings construct in belief and devotion.

The One God has no equal in creation and admits no partner in the ultimate claims of worship and lordship.

Recognizing this calls us to understand things in their proper place: to enjoy blessings, love others, learn from knowledge and experience, and fulfill our social responsibilities, without enthroning any creature, power, or invented authority in a position that belongs only to the Lord of all worlds.
