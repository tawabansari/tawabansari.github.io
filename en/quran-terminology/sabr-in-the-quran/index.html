Qur’anic Concepts

Ṣabr in the Qur’an: Patience, Steadfastness, and What Makes Them Worthwhile

نسخه فارسی

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ

In the name of The God, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

Introduction

Ṣabr is a Qur’anic concept commonly translated as “patience.” In everyday religious advice, it is often understood chiefly as putting up with hardship. Someone who is ill, bereaved, poor, or facing a crisis is told to be patient. In that setting, patience tends to mean enduring pain, not complaining, remaining calm, and waiting.

That understanding is not unrelated to the Qur’an. The Qur’an speaks of ṣabr in the face of fear, hunger, loss of property and life, and other difficulties. But do these circumstances define the concept in full, or do they represent only part of its range?

If ṣabr means only enduring hardship, why does the Qur’an place it alongside truth, mindfulness of God, good action, certainty, trust in God, and compassion? Why are prophets commanded to exercise it while calling people to truth, pursuing reform, and confronting falsehood? Why does it appear in accounts of battle, worship, social relationships, and resistance to life’s desires and attractions?

A further question is whether every kind of persistence or resistance is valuable in the Qur’an’s assessment. A person committed to a mistaken belief or an oppressive practice may also withstand opposition. Is the resistance itself a virtue, or does its value depend on what the person is resisting for?

To answer, we must begin with the word and its uses. We can then examine its contexts, the concepts with which it appears, and its relationship to distress, despair, obstinate persistence, uprightness, firmness, and resolve. These related ideas should not be treated as interchangeable.

This study reads the Qur’an through the Qur’an itself. Linguistic evidence can illuminate the word’s structure, but its uses across the Qur’an remain the final criterion for understanding the Qur’anic concept. Whenever a verse is quoted in Arabic, the complete verse and its English meaning are provided. Elsewhere, references alone keep the discussion moving. Two concepts occurring together in a verse should not, for that reason alone, be understood as identical.

The study does not claim to establish a final, unchanging definition for every occurrence of ṣabr. By comparing passages, it seeks to clarify a shared core of meaning, differences of context, and the grounds on which patience or steadfastness acquires moral value.

Part I: The language and range of ṣabr

1. The root ṣ-b-r and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ṣ-b-r is associated with holding, restraining, controlling, enduring, and remaining steadfast. This range already suggests that ṣabr need not mean suffering. A person may endure an external hardship, restrain a desire, or continue a task that has become difficult.

Words derived from this root occur 103 times across 93 verses in 45 surahs, or chapters. Each individual word derived from the root counts as an occurrence. If two such words appear in one verse, both are counted.

The occurrences can be grouped by grammatical form as follows:

Grammatical forms of ṣ-b-r
Grammatical formOccurrencesExplanation

Verb, Form III1Ṣābirū: persevere in the face of others’ persistence
Verb, Form VIII3Iṣṭabir: remain steadfast or persist

Active participle, masculine and feminine22Ṣābir, ṣābirūn, ṣābirāt, and ṣābirah: a person or people described as patient or steadfast

Total103Across 93 verses

These forms do different grammatical work. A verb may describe or command an act of endurance; an active participle describes a person or group as patient or steadfast; an intensive form emphasizes the prominence of that quality. Yet grammatical form alone, without the verse’s context, cannot establish a definite moral judgment.

In Al-Kahf 18:69, for example, Moses promises that he will be ṣābir—patient. The rest of the account shows how difficult that promise is to keep. Even an active participle therefore does not, by itself, establish that the patience described has actually been achieved.

2. What is ṣabr directed toward?

To identify a shared core of meaning, we need to ask what the word relates to within a sentence. Sometimes the Qur’an names the object of patience directly; sometimes it specifies its direction or purpose.

In Al-Baqarah 2:61, the Children of Israel say that they will not endure one kind of food. The immediate subject is not hunger, illness, or disaster, but an unwillingness to continue in a particular condition.

In Al-Kahf 18:28, the command concerns holding oneself in the company of a particular group. In Luqman 31:17, patience relates to what befalls a person. In Al-Muddaththir 74:7 and Al-Tur 52:48, its direction is toward God or His judgment.

Together, these uses suggest a common core: ṣabr concerns the capacity to hold oneself, exercise restraint, or remain steadfast against something that presses one to change course, abandon a decision, or follow another attraction.

That description does not yet tell us whether the course being maintained is right or wrong.

3. Is every kind of persistence praiseworthy?

Passages in which opponents of devotion to God alone use this root help answer the question.

Sad 38:6

وَانطَلَقَ الْمَلَأُ مِنْهُمْ أَنِ امْشُوا وَاصْبِرُوا عَلَىٰ آلِهَتِكُمْ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ يُرَادُ

Their leaders went off, saying, “Go on, and stand firm by your gods. This is surely something intended.”

The leaders opposing the messenger’s call tell their followers to persist in allegiance to their gods. The same imperative used elsewhere for commendable steadfastness is being used here to preserve a mistaken belief.

A few verses later in the same surah, the command to be steadfast is addressed to the messenger himself.

Sad 38:17

اصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَاذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا دَاوُودَ ذَا الْأَيْدِ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

Bear patiently what they say, and remember Our servant David, endowed with strength; he was ever turning back to Us.

In the first passage, the opponents call for loyalty to their gods. In the second, the messenger is told to endure their words. The word is shared; the positions and commitments are not.

Al-Furqan provides another especially clear example.

Al-Furqan 25:42

إِن كَادَ لَيُضِلُّنَا عَنْ آلِهَتِنَا لَوْلَا أَن صَبَرْنَا عَلَيْهَا وَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ حِينَ يَرَوْنَ الْعَذَابَ مَنْ أَضَلُّ سَبِيلًا

“He almost led us away from our gods, had we not stood firm by them.” But when they see the punishment, they will know who has strayed further from the way.

The opponents speak proudly of their persistence. They believe that they have resisted the messenger’s invitation and protected their previous commitments.

The same surah, in Al-Furqan 25:75, speaks of a reward for those who persevered. One root can thus occur in the speech of different groups and describe conduct with different moral values.

Al-Tur 52:16 supplies a further example.

At-Tur 52:16

اصْلَوْهَا فَاصْبِرُوا أَوْ لَا تَصْبِرُوا سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْكُمْ إِنَّمَا تُجْزَوْنَ مَا كُنتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

Enter it and burn. Whether you endure or do not endure makes no difference to you. You are repaid only for what you used to do.

In this context, endurance does not change the outcome for those addressed. Al-Baqarah 2:175 and Fussilat 41:24 also use the root in settings that do not praise it.

We therefore need to distinguish two levels: ṣabr in its broad linguistic sense, as the capacity to endure or persist, and commendable ṣabr, which the Qur’an values within a framework of guidance, mindfulness of God, and right action.

These are analytical labels used in this study, not two separate words found in the Qur’an.

Persistence is not automatically a moral achievement. A person may remain faithful to truth, but may also persist in prejudice, wrongful interests, or a false belief.

Part II: Why do human beings need ṣabr?

4. Responding to hardship and prosperity

The Qur’an portrays human beings as encountering difficulty, attraction, fear, hope, blessing, and deprivation.

Al-Balad 90:4 speaks of human beings created amid toil and hardship. Al-Inshiqaq 84:6 connects human life with strenuous effort and movement toward the Lord. Al-Anbiya 21:35 speaks of testing through both bad and good circumstances.

Our need for patience does not arise only from external difficulties. The Qur’an also examines our inward responses.

Al-Ma'arij 70:19

إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا

The human being was indeed created anxious and restless—

Al-Ma'arij 70:20

إِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا

distressed when adversity touches him,

Al-Ma'arij 70:21

وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا

and withholding when good comes his way—

Al-Ma'arij 70:22

إِلَّا الْمُصَلِّينَ

except those who establish salat, prayerful worship.

These verses identify two responses: distress in adversity and withholding in prosperity. The surah then describes a range of qualities belonging to those excepted from this pattern.

The issue extends beyond enduring disaster. Deprivation and abundance can both unsettle a person’s balance.

Hud 11:9, 11:10, 11:11 presents the same two sides of the test. Someone who loses mercy previously enjoyed may become despairing and ungrateful; someone who receives blessings after hardship may become boastful and self-satisfied. Those who persevere and do good are then excepted.

Taken together, these passages show ṣabr as more than a response to pain. It is part of the capacity to maintain a sound direction through changing circumstances.

The pairing of ṣabbār and shakūr makes this connection clearer. All four occurrences of ṣabbār, an intensive form emphasizing patience or steadfastness, accompany shakūr, “deeply grateful”: 14:5, 31:31, 34:19, and 42:33. This pairing brings steadfastness and gratitude together in responding to God’s signs and life’s changes. It does not, by itself, assign patience exclusively to hardship and gratitude exclusively to ease. Both can help preserve a sound direction in different circumstances. For a fuller account of gratitude and its relationship to recognizing and using blessings, see Shukr in the Qur’an.

5. Patience in adversity: a genuine but partial meaning

Fear, hunger, loss, and hardship are among the clearest settings in which patience is needed.

Al-Baqarah 2:155

وَلَنَبْلُوَنَّكُم بِشَيْءٍ مِّنَ الْخَوْفِ وَالْجُوعِ وَنَقْصٍ مِّنَ الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَنفُسِ وَالثَّمَرَاتِ وَبَشِّرِ الصَّابِرِينَ

We will certainly test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and produce. Give good news to those who remain steadfast—

Al-Baqarah 2:156

الَّذِينَ إِذَا أَصَابَتْهُم مُّصِيبَةٌ قَالُوا إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّا إِلَيْهِ رَاجِعُونَ

those who, when a calamity befalls them, say, “We belong to The God, and to Him we are returning.”

Here patience is directly related to calamity. Yet the patient are not identified simply by their ability to bear pain. Their response recalls their belonging to God and their return to Him.

Al-Baqarah 2:45, 2:46 also connects awareness of meeting the Lord and returning to Him with humility. The response in Al-Baqarah 2:156 can therefore be understood within the Qur’an’s wider account of life and the human return.

Al-Baqarah 2:177 is particularly important to this subject.

Al-Baqarah 2:177

لَّيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ

Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward east or west. Rather, righteousness is in one who believes in The God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; gives wealth, despite loving it, to relatives, orphans, those in need, stranded travelers, those who ask, and for freeing people from bondage; and establishes salat and gives zakat. It is in those who fulfill their covenant when they make one, and those who persevere in deprivation, suffering, and the time of battle. These are the ones who have been true, and these are the ones mindful of God.

The verse names three settings for steadfastness: hardship and deprivation, suffering and injury, and confrontation or battle. It places this quality alongside faith, giving wealth, keeping covenants, salat, and zakat—the practice of purification and growth discussed in its own study.

Endurance in adversity is thus an authentic part of Qur’anic ṣabr, but not its entirety. A person can remain steadfast through difficulty while continuing to meet responsibilities toward others.

6. Beautiful patience: may a patient person grieve or complain?

Jacob’s story offers one of the clearest ways to approach this question.

Yusuf 12:18

وَجَاءُوا عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

They brought his shirt stained with false blood. He said, “Rather, your own selves have made a matter seem acceptable to you. So, beautiful patience! It is The God whose help is sought against what you describe.”

Jacob does not accept his sons’ account without question. He identifies what he sees behind their conduct, then speaks of beautiful patience and God’s help.

Later, following the episode involving Joseph’s other brother, he uses the same expression again.

Yusuf 12:83

قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَن يَأْتِيَنِي بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

He said, “Rather, your own selves have made a matter seem acceptable to you. So, beautiful patience! Perhaps The God will bring them all back to me. He is indeed the All-Knowing, the Wise.”

Beautiful patience is joined here with hope. Jacob still regards his sons’ return as possible. In Yusuf 12:87, he sends them to search and warns them not to despair of relief from God.

The Qur’an does not conceal Jacob’s profound grief. Yusuf 12:84 describes his sorrow and its effect on his eyes.

Yusuf 12:86

قَالَ إِنَّمَا أَشْكُو بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي إِلَى اللَّهِ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

He said, “I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to The God, and I know from The God what you do not know.”

Jacob’s beautiful patience coexists with grief, suffering, a request for divine help, and continuing hope. His story does not establish patience as the absence of sorrow or the concealment of emotion.

Job provides a similar example.

Al-Anbiya 21:83

وَأَيُّوبَ إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الضُّرُّ وَأَنتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ

And remember Job, when he called to his Lord: “Suffering has touched me, and You are the most merciful of those who show mercy.”

Sad 38:44 describes Job as patient. Yet he voices his suffering and turns to God for relief.

Al-Ma‘arij 70:5 also speaks of beautiful patience. The adjective jamīl, “beautiful,” accompanies other actions, including gracious pardon in Al-Hijr 15:85 and gracious withdrawal in Al-Muzzammil 73:10. These uses suggest that the quality of an action matters, not merely whether it is performed. They do not, without further examination, establish one identical definition of “beautiful” in every context.

Another important distinction is between expressing sorrow and reacting in distress in ways that pull a person away from their commitments. Jacob grieves, but does not abandon hope or the search. That difference matters for understanding patience.

Nor does Jacob’s taking his complaint to God establish a ban on expressing suffering to other people. Al-Nisa 4:148 makes an exception for someone who has been wronged in its discussion of speaking openly of evil. In Al-Mujadilah 58:1, a woman argues with the messenger about her husband and complains to God; God affirms that He hears their exchange.

These passages do not yield an absolute prohibition on complaint. Its subject, manner, and consequences for conduct must be considered.

7. Distress, despair, weakness, and obstinacy: distinctions that matter

Some verses contrast jazaʿ—agitated distress or loss of composure—with ṣabr. In Ibrahim 14:21, people facing the consequences of their actions in the hereafter say that distress and endurance are equally futile for them. Al-Ma‘arij 70:19, 70:20, 70:21 likewise identifies distress as a human response to adversity.

From one perspective, jazaʿ stands against the ability to endure and regulate a response. But it should not be extended to every instance of crying, grieving, or asking for help. The accounts of Jacob and Job do not support such a generalization.

Despair also has an important relationship with patience. In Yusuf 12:87, Jacob warns his sons against despair after having spoken of beautiful patience. The concepts are closely related in his story, but are neither synonyms nor exact opposites in every setting. Someone may lose hope of a particular outcome while continuing faithfully in another responsibility. Despair must therefore be examined in relation to its object.

In Ali ‘Imran 3:146, the words wahn, ḍaʿf, and istikānah concern faltering, weakness, and submission under pressure: people suffer in God’s way without giving way to these responses. Al-Anfal 8:46 discusses disputes and their consequences—loss of resolve and collective strength.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an speaks of obstinate persistence, defiance, arrogance, and following desire. Examples include Ali ‘Imran 3:135, Al-Jathiyah 45:8, Nuh 71:7, and Al-Mulk 67:21.

These descriptions do not always indicate simple ignorance. A person may knowingly persist in wrongdoing. Al-Naml 27:14 even describes denial despite inward certainty.

The difference between commendable steadfastness and persistence in falsehood cannot therefore be reduced to being informed or uninformed. More important are the truth of the commitment, its direction and motivation, and the conduct it produces.

For a closer examination of denial, kufr, and their differing uses across contexts, see Kāfir in the Qur’an. Lack of knowledge or disagreement should not automatically be equated with knowing denial.

Part III: Ṣabr in responsibility and action

8. Ṣabr and truth: knowing is not the same as remaining faithful

Surah Al-‘Asr places ṣabr alongside truth and good action.

Al-'Asr 103:1

وَالْعَصْرِ

By time,

Al-'Asr 103:2

إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ

the human being is indeed in loss,

Al-'Asr 103:3

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْحَقِّ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ

except those who believe and do good deeds, and urge one another to truth and urge one another to steadfastness.

Truth and steadfastness are distinct commitments placed together in this surah. The distinction matters: recognizing truth does not necessarily give someone the ability to remain faithful to it when circumstances become difficult.

A person may know that a course of action is right yet abandon it under the influence of fear, self-interest, social pressure, or desire. In that situation, ṣabr is the practical ability to remain faithful to what has been recognized as right.

This reading is consistent with Yunus 10:109 and Al-Rum 30:60.

Yunus 10:109

وَاتَّبِعْ مَا يُوحَىٰ إِلَيْكَ وَاصْبِرْ حَتَّىٰ يَحْكُمَ اللَّهُ وَهُوَ خَيْرُ الْحَاكِمِينَ

Follow what is revealed to you, and persevere until The God judges. He is the best of judges.

Following revelation and perseverance appear together. Patience does not replace following; it accompanies it.

Ar-Rum 30:60

فَاصْبِرْ إِنَّ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ حَقٌّ وَلَا يَسْتَخِفَّنَّكَ الَّذِينَ لَا يُوقِنُونَ

So remain steadfast. The promise of The God is true. Do not let those who lack certainty unsettle you.

Here steadfastness is related both to the truth of God’s promise and to resisting the destabilizing influence of opponents.

Ṣabr does not itself determine what is true. Within a life of guidance, however, it enables a person to continue along the path of truth. In this sense, it is a practical virtue connecting understanding, decision, and conduct.

The relationship between patience, certainty, and the responsibility to guide is also explicit in 32:24. The Qur’an speaks of leaders among the Children of Israel who guided by God’s command when they had persevered and held certainty in His signs. Here patience accompanies certainty and guidance directed by God. Mere persistence or possession of followers therefore cannot establish a leader’s truthfulness. This relationship is explored further in Imamah in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Position and the Question of Leadership.

9. Ṣabr and compassion: persevering in the difficult work of doing good

Surah Al-Balad describes a steep path, including freeing someone from bondage and feeding those in need. It then places faith, steadfastness, and compassion together.

Al-Balad 90:17

ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْمَرْحَمَةِ

Then to be among those who believe, urge one another to steadfastness, and urge one another to compassion.

This combination separates steadfastness from indifference to others’ suffering. Within the surah’s context, people are called not only to bear their own difficulties but also to take the demanding path of doing good and responding to others’ needs.

Al-Insan 76:8, 76:9, 76:10, 76:11, 76:12 likewise places feeding the needy, the orphan, and the captive—without expecting repayment from them—within a sequence that culminates in a reward for perseverance.

In Al-Baqarah 2:177, giving away wealth despite loving it also appears alongside steadfastness and other qualities of the righteous.

These passages suggest that ṣabr can take the form of doing good, bearing its costs, and sustaining a social responsibility.

Such steadfastness is not an instruction to the deprived to accept their suffering silently. It includes our responsibility toward them.

10. Ṣabr and reform: standing firm under wrongful pressure

In Luqman’s advice to his son, patience accompanies a responsibility to promote what is right.

Luqman 31:17

يَا بُنَيَّ أَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ وَأْمُرْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَانْهَ عَنِ الْمُنكَرِ وَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا أَصَابَكَ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عَزْمِ الْأُمُورِ

My dear son, establish salat, enjoin what is recognized as good, discourage what is wrong, and bear patiently what befalls you. These are matters requiring firm resolve.

Patience here accompanies action and reform. Fulfilling a responsibility may bring difficulty, but the existence of difficulty should not itself be a reason to abandon that responsibility.

Ali ‘Imran 3:186 describes steadfastness and mindfulness in the face of extensive abuse as matters of firm resolve. Al-Shura 42:43 uses the same expression in connection with patience and forgiveness. Across these three passages, ṣabr accompanies different actions as an expression of settled commitment.

In Al-Insan 76:24, patience is even joined with refusing a wrongful demand.

Al-Insan 76:24

فَاصْبِرْ لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ وَلَا تُطِعْ مِنْهُمْ آثِمًا أَوْ كَفُورًا

So persevere for your Lord’s judgment, and do not obey any sinner or deeply ungrateful person among them.

Patience does not mean yielding to people’s pressure here. Steadfastness in relation to the Lord’s judgment accompanies refusal to obey those described as sinful or ungrateful.

Sometimes, then, ṣabr appears in refusing to do what others pressure a person to do.

11. Ṣabr under threat and in confrontation

In the account of the magicians who come to faith after witnessing Moses’ signs, Pharaoh threatens them with punishment. They respond:

Al-A'raf 7:126

وَمَا تَنقِمُ مِنَّا إِلَّا أَنْ آمَنَّا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّنَا لَمَّا جَاءَتْنَا رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَتَوَفَّنَا مُسْلِمِينَ

You resent us only because we believed in our Lord’s signs when they came to us. Our Lord, pour steadfastness upon us and cause us to die surrendered to You.

Ṣabr here is not silent acceptance of Pharaoh’s command. It is the ability to hold their position under his threat.

The plea for steadfastness to be poured upon people also appears in Al-Baqarah 2:250.

Al-Baqarah 2:250

وَلَمَّا بَرَزُوا لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِ قَالُوا رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَانصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

When they came out to face Goliath and his troops, they said, “Our Lord, pour steadfastness upon us, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the people who reject the truth.”

In both passages, people seek God’s help in becoming steadfast. The Qur’an does not present ṣabr simply as a resource independent of a person’s relationship with God. People are commanded to persevere and may also ask God for the strength to do so.

Ali ‘Imran 3:146 praises people who suffer in God’s way without faltering or submitting to pressure. Al-Anfal 8:65, 8:66 also discusses steadfastness in relation to the capacity to resist. Yet verse 66 recognizes human weakness and a corresponding lightening of the obligation.

Al-Imran 3:200

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اصْبِرُوا وَصَابِرُوا وَرَابِطُوا وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

You who believe, persevere, persevere against others’ resistance, remain ready and firm, and be mindful of The God, so that you may prosper.

The commands iṣbirū and ṣābirū occur together here. The second belongs to the Arabic pattern known as Form III and can convey perseverance against another side’s perseverance, or striving to outlast it. The grammatical pattern alone, however, cannot establish one of these possibilities as the sole certain meaning.

What the context makes clear is the connection between steadfastness, confrontation, readiness, and mindfulness of God. Persistence in a conflict is accompanied by an ethical standard.

12. Ṣabr and justice: seeking redress or forgiving?

One of this study’s central questions is whether patience prevents someone who has been wronged from defending themselves or seeking justice.

The answer requires reading Al-Shura 42:36, 42:37, 42:38, 42:39, 42:40, 42:41, 42:42, 42:43 together, rather than isolating its final verse.

Al-Shura 42:36, 42:37, 42:38 describes believers through trust in God, avoidance of sin, forgiveness when angry, the establishment of salat, consultation, and giving. Another quality follows.

Ash-Shura 42:39

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا أَصَابَهُمُ الْبَغْيُ هُمْ يَنتَصِرُونَ

And those who, when oppression befalls them, defend themselves.

Self-defense against oppression is included among the qualities of this same group of believers. That is crucial to the passage.

Ash-Shura 42:40

وَجَزَاءُ سَيِّئَةٍ سَيِّئَةٌ مِّثْلُهَا فَمَنْ عَفَا وَأَصْلَحَ فَأَجْرُهُ عَلَى اللَّهِ إِنَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الظَّالِمِينَ

The recompense for a wrong is an equivalent wrong. But whoever pardons and puts matters right has their reward with The God. He does not love those who do wrong.

Ash-Shura 42:41

وَلَمَنِ انتَصَرَ بَعْدَ ظُلْمِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَا عَلَيْهِم مِّن سَبِيلٍ

As for those who defend themselves after being wronged, there is no cause to proceed against them.

Ash-Shura 42:42

إِنَّمَا السَّبِيلُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ يَظْلِمُونَ النَّاسَ وَيَبْغُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

The cause lies only against those who wrong people and transgress unjustly in the land. For them there is a painful punishment.

Ash-Shura 42:43

وَلَمَن صَبَرَ وَغَفَرَ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ لَمِنْ عَزْمِ الْأُمُورِ

But whoever is patient and forgives—this is indeed a matter of firm resolve.

Taken together, these verses present two responses: defending oneself and seeking redress against oppression, and patience accompanied by forgiveness.

In this setting, patience and forgiveness can be an alternative to retaliation. The Qur’an does not prohibit proportionate defense, while also valuing pardon joined with putting matters right.

Al-Nahl 16:126 makes a similar distinction.

An-Nahl 16:126

وَإِنْ عَاقَبْتُمْ فَعَاقِبُوا بِمِثْلِ مَا عُوقِبْتُم بِهِ وَلَئِن صَبَرْتُمْ لَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لِّلصَّابِرِينَ

If you retaliate, respond only to the extent of what was inflicted on you. But if you are patient, that is certainly better for those who exercise patience.

In this particular setting, patience is contrasted with a reciprocal response. We therefore cannot say that ṣabr always means taking action and defending oneself at the same time.

Nor does this particular use establish non-retaliation as its only meaning throughout the Qur’an. In passages concerning migration, calling people to truth, striving, and enjoining good, ṣabr accompanies continued action.

Two uses must be distinguished: steadfastness while fulfilling a responsibility or resisting pressure, and restraint from a response to which one is entitled because one chooses forgiveness instead.

The Qur’an does not even impose an absolute ban on voicing a complaint when someone has been wronged.

An-Nisa 4:148

لَّا يُحِبُّ اللَّهُ الْجَهْرَ بِالسُّوءِ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ إِلَّا مَن ظُلِمَ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ سَمِيعًا عَلِيمًا

The God does not love the public expression of evil speech, except by one who has been wronged. The God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

Al-Mujadilah 58:1 likewise describes God hearing a woman who protests and complains about her marital situation.

These passages make clear that patience cannot be turned into a means of silencing the wronged, denying their right to seek justice, or preserving an oppressive situation.

Defense is not without limits either. The Qur’an calls for a proportionate response and rejects transgression. Patience and justice belong within a shared ethical framework, rather than standing in absolute opposition.

13. Ṣabr in anger and immediate reaction

In human relationships, patience sometimes means restraining a response that might otherwise arise from anger or hostility.

Fussilat 41:34

وَلَا تَسْتَوِي الْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا السَّيِّئَةُ ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُ عَدَاوَةٌ كَأَنَّهُ وَلِيٌّ حَمِيمٌ

Good and evil are not equal. Respond with what is better, and the person between whom and you there was hostility may become as though a close, devoted friend.

Fussilat 41:35

وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

None receive this except those who persevere; none receive it except one endowed with a great share of good.

Here patience is connected to choosing a better response to someone else’s wrongdoing. Anger can prompt an immediate harsh reply, but a person can hold themselves back and choose a more considered course.

Ali ‘Imran 3:134 offers a related description of the righteous as those who restrain anger and forgive people. Al-Shura 42:37 also speaks of forgiveness in anger.

Restraint in anger need not mean an inability to respond. Sometimes a person has the power to retaliate but forgoes it for a better purpose.

Al-Muzzammil 73:10 places patience alongside gracious withdrawal. In such a setting, ṣabr need not mean prolonging a dispute at any cost. It can accompany leaving a quarrel whose continuation serves no good.

14. Ṣabr and migration: remaining steadfast does not always mean staying put

Surah Al-Nahl connects steadfastness with migration after persecution.

An-Nahl 16:41

وَالَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا فِي اللَّهِ مِن بَعْدِ مَا ظُلِمُوا لَنُبَوِّئَنَّهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةً وَلَأَجْرُ الْآخِرَةِ أَكْبَرُ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ

Those who migrated for The God after being wronged—We will certainly settle them well in this world. Yet the reward of the hereafter is greater, if only they knew—

An-Nahl 16:42

الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ

those who persevered and place their trust in their Lord.

The steadfast people in the second verse are the same people whose migration is described in the first.

This connection matters. These people changed their living conditions to preserve their faith and their way of life. Their patience did not consist in remaining under the same oppression.

Migration, striving, and patience also appear together in 16:110. In 29:56, 29:57, 29:58, 29:59, the spaciousness of God’s earth, faith, action, and patience are connected with trust in Him.

This does not mean that every departure is an act of patience, or that staying always contradicts it. In 14:12, 14:13, 14:14, the messengers remain steadfast despite threats and abuse. Sometimes steadfastness requires staying and bearing pressure; sometimes it requires leaving an environment in which living rightly has become impossible.

Patience is therefore not tied to a place. It concerns maintaining the right direction through difficult decisions.

Part Four: Patience amid Attractions, Desires, and the Limits of Knowledge

15. Resisting the Attractions of Worldly Life

An important use of ṣabr occurs in chapter 18, al-Kahf.

Al-Kahf 18:28

وَاصْبِرْ نَفْسَكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُم بِالْغَدَاةِ وَالْعَشِيِّ يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُ وَلَا تَعْدُ عَيْنَاكَ عَنْهُمْ تُرِيدُ زِينَةَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَلَا تُطِعْ مَنْ أَغْفَلْنَا قَلْبَهُ عَن ذِكْرِنَا وَاتَّبَعَ هَوَاهُ وَكَانَ أَمْرُهُ فُرُطًا

Keep yourself with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His face. Do not let your eyes pass beyond them, desiring the adornment of worldly life. And do not obey someone whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follows his own desire, and whose conduct exceeds all bounds.

Here ṣabr means keeping oneself in the company of certain people. The competing attraction is also identified: the adornment of worldly life and the pursuit of personal desire.

Similarly, 6:52 forbids driving away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His face. The parallel shows that this is more than an inward feeling: it also concerns the choice of companions and judgments based on social standing.

The account of Korah presents this test from another angle. In 28:79, people see his wealth and display and wish they could enjoy the same position. In the next verse, those who have knowledge respond.

Al-Qasas 28:80

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ وَيْلَكُمْ ثَوَابُ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ لِّمَنْ آمَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا وَلَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الصَّابِرُونَ

Those who had been given knowledge said: Woe to you! The reward of The God is better for whoever believes and does what is right. But none receive this except those who are patient.

This verse brings knowledge and patience together in the face of wealth and worldly display. A person may know that God’s reward is better, yet lose their direction when confronted with conspicuous wealth and power.

Patience is not only resistance to what hurts us. Sometimes it is resistance to what attracts us.

16. Perseverance in Worship

In 2:153, patience and salat are presented as two means of seeking help.

Al-Baqarah 2:153

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

You who believe, seek help through patience and salat. The God is indeed with those who are patient.

In 19:65 and 20:132, the form iṣṭabir appears in connection with worship and salat. It belongs to the Arabic verb pattern conventionally called Form VIII. In these contexts, it can convey persistence involving sustained effort.

Two consecutive verses in chapter 20 make the connection particularly clear.

Ta-Ha 20:131

وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ إِلَىٰ مَا مَتَّعْنَا بِهِ أَزْوَاجًا مِّنْهُمْ زَهْرَةَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ

Do not stretch your eyes toward what We have allowed some groups of them to enjoy—the blossom of worldly life—through which We test them. Your Lord’s provision is better and more lasting.

Ta-Ha 20:132

وَأْمُرْ أَهْلَكَ بِالصَّلَاةِ وَاصْطَبِرْ عَلَيْهَا لَا نَسْأَلُكَ رِزْقًا نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكَ وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ

Enjoin salat upon your household and persevere in it. We do not ask you for provision; We provide for you. And the final outcome belongs to God-consciousness.

The first verse addresses the attraction of worldly display; the second commands perseverance in salat. Their proximity suggests that perseverance in worship can be a practical response to desires and attractions that draw people away from their responsibilities.

Patience and salat nevertheless remain distinct concepts. Patience can sustain worship, but worship itself cannot be reduced to patience.

For a separate examination of salat and its Qur’anic uses, see Salat in the Qur’an.

In 54:27, iṣṭabir appears alongside watching and waiting. Patience can therefore involve waiting, but the word is not simply a synonym for waiting in every context. The Qur’an also uses other words for waiting, watching, and taking time.

17. Patience, Knowledge, and the Limits of Human Understanding

In the account of Moses accompanying a servant to whom God has given special knowledge, patience concerns the ability to bear what is not yet understood.

Al-Kahf 18:67

قَالَ إِنَّكَ لَن تَسْتَطِيعَ مَعِيَ صَبْرًا

He said: You will not be able to remain patient with me.

Al-Kahf 18:68

وَكَيْفَ تَصْبِرُ عَلَىٰ مَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ خُبْرًا

And how could you remain patient with something of which you have no comprehensive knowledge?

Al-Kahf 18:69

قَالَ سَتَجِدُنِي إِن شَاءَ اللَّهُ صَابِرًا وَلَا أَعْصِي لَكَ أَمْرًا

He said: If The God wills, you will find me patient, and I will not disobey any command of yours.

Moses promises patience, but then encounters events whose reasons he does not know and asks questions about them.

In 18:70, his companion sets a condition for their continued journey: Moses must not question the events before they are explained to him. At the end of the account, in 18:78, 18:79, 18:80, 18:81, 18:82, the actions are explained and their significance disclosed.

These verses show how insufficient knowledge of an event’s reasons and consequences can make patience difficult. They also invite reflection on withholding hasty judgment and recognizing the limits of human knowledge.

But this account must not be turned into a general permission to suspend questioning, criticism, or moral judgment. It concerns a particular situation, and the Qur’an itself ultimately supplies the explanation. Moses’ promise of obedience is also made within that specific companionship and its stated condition.

The central point is that knowledge and patience can reinforce one another without being identical. Understanding a subject does not automatically ensure the ability to persevere through its difficulties.

18. Related Concepts: Uprightness, Firmness, Resolve, and Readiness

The Qur’anic concepts of istiqāmah, thabāt, ʿazm, and ribāṭ—uprightness, firmness, resolve, and sustained readiness—are related to ṣabr, but are not interchangeable with it in every context.

In 11:112, 41:30, and 42:15, istiqāmah concerns remaining upright and steady on a course. In 8:45 and 14:27, thabāt concerns standing firm and being made firm. In 3:159, 20:115, and 46:35, ʿazm concerns decision and strength of will.

Firmness and patience appear in consecutive verses in chapter 8.

Al-Anfal 8:45

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا لَقِيتُمْ فِئَةً فَاثْبُتُوا وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

You who believe, when you encounter a force, stand firm and remember The God often, so that you may prosper.

Al-Anfal 8:46

وَأَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا فَتَفْشَلُوا وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ وَاصْبِرُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

Obey The God and His messenger, and do not quarrel with one another, lest you lose heart and your strength depart. Be patient: The God is indeed with those who are patient.

These verses place firmness in confrontation and patience within a broader set of commands. Patience accompanies avoiding quarrels and preserving collective strength.

Their proximity suggests an overlap between firmness and patience. Yet the use of different words in the two commands also invites examination of their differing emphases.

In 46:35, patience is associated with messengers of firm resolve. This does not establish that resolve and patience are exactly the same. Resolve may concern the firmness of a decision, while patience concerns maintaining that decision under pressure and difficulty.

This distinction is an analytical proposal based on context, not an absolute rule that fully separates every use of these words.

19. Does Patience Conflict with Submission?

A study of patience must distinguish submission to God from submission to wrongful power.

In the account of Abraham’s son, patience accompanies acceptance of a command that the Qur’anic narrative relates to God.

As-Saffat 37:102

فَلَمَّا بَلَغَ مَعَهُ السَّعْيَ قَالَ يَا بُنَيَّ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ فِي الْمَنَامِ أَنِّي أَذْبَحُكَ فَانظُرْ مَاذَا تَرَىٰ قَالَ يَا أَبَتِ افْعَلْ مَا تُؤْمَرُ سَتَجِدُنِي إِن شَاءَ اللَّهُ مِنَ الصَّابِرِينَ

When he had reached the age of working alongside him, he said: My dear son, I see in a dream that I am sacrificing you. Consider, then, what you think. He said: My dear father, do what you are commanded. If The God wills, you will find me among those who are patient.

As-Saffat 37:103

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

Then, when they had both submitted and he had laid him down upon his forehead…

These two verses place patience and submission within the same account. Patience therefore cannot always be understood as the opposite of submission.

In 7:126, too, those who stand against Pharaoh ask God for patience and ask to die in submission to Him.

Yet in 76:24, patience accompanies refusing obedience to the sinful and ungrateful.

The decisive issue is what submission or resistance is directed toward. Submitting to God’s command is not the same as yielding to an oppressor’s pressure.

To understand patience, we must therefore keep asking what a person remains committed to, and what they restrain themselves from doing.

Part Five: Patience and Its Outcomes

20. Does Patience Always Bring Worldly Victory?

The Qur’an speaks of a great reward for those who are patient. In 2:157, they receive mercy and guidance. The reward for patience also appears in 13:24, 16:96, 25:75, and 76:12.

These promises should not be taken to mean that every patient person must attain comfort, wealth, or worldly victory on their preferred timetable.

In 3:140, 3:141, 3:142, believers experience injury, defeat, and testing; patience is discussed within those very circumstances. Likewise, in 2:155, 2:156, 2:157, the patient are people who experience calamity, not people protected from every calamity.

Some accounts connect patience with deliverance and victory: 7:137 concerning the Children of Israel and 6:34 concerning earlier messengers are examples. But these cases cannot be made into a promise that every patient person, in every situation, will obtain their desired worldly result.

In 40:77, the command to be patient accompanies God’s true promise, while the verse allows for the possibility that the Prophet will not live to see everything promised to his opponents.

In 39:10, the patient are promised their reward without measure.

Praiseworthy patience thus has a value in the Qur’an that is not confined to immediate, visible worldly results.

This does not make the results of action unimportant. People remain responsible for striving, reform, justice, avoiding wrongdoing, and fulfilling their duties. Patience does not guarantee the desired result at the expected time. It enables fidelity to the right course even while the outcome remains uncertain or difficulties persist.

Final Analysis: What Patience Is—and What It Is Not

The verses suggest that, in its broad linguistic sense, ṣabr is the capacity to endure, hold oneself steady, and persist. That capacity can operate in different circumstances and can even serve a wrongful purpose.

Praiseworthy Qur’anic patience, however, connects this capacity with truth, God-consciousness, trust, good action, compassion, certainty, and responsibility.

On one side, people must remain committed to what it would be wrong to abandon: fidelity to truth, continuing responsibility, keeping commitments, worship, and doing good.

On the other, they must restrain themselves from what it would be wrong to follow: temptation, destructive anger, oppression, self-serving desire, and disproportionate retaliation.

These two directions do not conflict. Continuing on the right course can require refusing a wrong one. Restraint from a harmful response can itself make fidelity to one’s values possible.

Patience therefore cannot be identified by one outward behavior. Silence may express patience in one situation and fear or indifference in another. Speaking out and protesting may sometimes arise from anger and haste, and sometimes from responsibility and fidelity to truth.

Remaining in a situation is not always patience; leaving it is not always impatience. Sometimes people must stay and resist. Sometimes preserving their values and responsibilities requires migration or a change of course.

Four questions can guide our assessment:

  1. What is the person persisting in?
  2. What pressure or attraction are they resisting?
  3. What motivates and sustains their persistence?
  4. What conduct and consequences does that persistence produce?

These questions help distinguish informed patience from persistence in falsehood, enduring calamity from accepting oppression, and considered restraint from inaction.

Conclusion

Patience in the Qur’an extends beyond enduring illness, poverty, calamity, and life’s difficulties. These are part of its field of application, but not the whole of it.

The root and the contexts of the verses connect ṣabr with holding oneself steady, exercising restraint, and persisting. This capacity alone, however, does not distinguish truth from falsehood: the Qur’an uses the same root for persistence in wrongful belief.

Within Qur’anic guidance, patience becomes valuable when it keeps a person on the right course and restrains attractions and reactions that would lead to abandoning truth or doing wrong.

The following definition is therefore proposed as the conclusion of this study:

Praiseworthy patience in the Qur’an is the capacity to hold oneself steady and exercise self-restraint under pressure and attraction: to remain on the right course, refrain from wrongful reactions, and, where appropriate, choose forgiveness even when one has a right to respond in kind.

This capacity can appear in bearing calamity, remaining faithful to truth, controlling anger, forgiving, resisting oppression, leaving a harmful environment, or persevering in worship and good action.

Patience in this sense is neither a synonym for suffering, nor a general command to remain silent and submit, nor permission to insist on every belief and desire.

Its value must be understood through the truth of the course a person pursues and the conduct that their steadfastness produces in life.
