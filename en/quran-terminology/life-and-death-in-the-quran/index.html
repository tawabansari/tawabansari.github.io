Qur’anic Concepts

Life and Death in the Qur’an: Does Bodily Death End Human Existence?

A Qur’anic and philosophical study of the self, death, awareness, and life with God

نسخه فارسی

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ

In the name of The God, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

Introduction: Is Life Only What We Can Observe?

We recognize life through familiar signs: breathing, growth, movement, and responses to the world around us. When the body’s vital functions cease irreversibly, biological life has ended. But does that also mean the person has ceased to exist?

Our experience of life is largely an experience of living bodies. It is understandable, then, that we tend to associate life with organs and their functions. Yet familiarity with one form of life does not establish that no other form is possible. The question is how far the evidence about bodily life allows us to go.

The Qur’an speaks of jinn (beings ordinarily hidden from human perception), angels, the self, sleep, death, resurrection, and life with God. In 2:154, it describes people killed in God’s way as alive, while acknowledging that their life lies beyond our perception.

وَلَا تَقُولُوا۟ لِمَن يُقْتَلُ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ أَمْوَٰتٌۢ ۚ بَلْ أَحْيَآءٌ وَلَـٰكِن لَّا تَشْعُرُونَ

Do not say of those killed in the way of The God that they are dead. Rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive it. 2:154

The verse acknowledges that these people have been killed, yet calls them alive. For this group, at least, it distinguishes the end of bodily life from the end of life altogether.

What does that distinction establish, and what does it leave unexplained? Answering requires us to examine the Qur’an’s language of life and death, its account of the self in sleep and death, and the experiences it attributes to those who have died. Further questions arise about existence before earthly life and about beings whose nature differs from our own. This study approaches those questions through the Qur’an, then considers what philosophical reflection can contribute. Its concern is both the evidence for life beyond the body and the limits of what that evidence tells us. The central question is whether bodily death ends a person’s entire existence. A related question—whether awareness could precede earthly life—will be considered separately, because it rests on a different and less conclusive argument.

Part One: Method and an Important Distinction

1. Reading the Qur’an: Language, Context, and Philosophical Reflection

The inquiry begins with the Qur’an’s uses of life, death, the self, provision, and witnessing. Each passage is read in context and compared with others, including those that might challenge the proposed interpretation. Arabic terms are examined where their meaning affects the argument. A word’s range of meaning matters, but a dictionary definition cannot establish a complete account of reality. Nor does the same word necessarily describe the same kind of experience wherever it appears. The Qur’an, for example, speaks of bodily life, life associated with guidance, and life with God. These uses need to be understood in their own contexts before drawing connections between them.

Three kinds of claim will be distinguished throughout: what the Qur’an explicitly states, what can reasonably be inferred from several passages together, and what philosophy offers as a possible explanation. The report that those killed in God’s way are alive belongs to the first category. An account of how they perceive or what that life consists of requires further interpretation. Keeping these levels separate allows the argument to develop without making its conclusions more certain than its evidence.

The natural sciences have a different but important role. Biology investigates living bodies; it does not, by itself, settle every question about unseen reality. Conversely, an interpretation of scripture must not be presented as an experimental finding. The purpose is to examine what each source of knowledge contributes, without asking one to do the work of another.

2. Witnesses and Those Killed in God’s Way: Why the Distinction Matters

A distinction about language is needed before considering those killed in God’s way. In contemporary religious usage, shahīd (a witness; often translated as “martyr”) commonly refers to someone killed in God’s way. That familiar meaning can obscure the broader Qur’anic language of witnessing.

The root sh-h-d concerns presence, observation, knowledge, and testimony. God is described as a witness, and messengers bear witness concerning their communities. Jesus speaks of his witnessing in 5:117. In 2:143, believers are called to bear witness concerning humanity; 2:282 concerns witnesses to transactions. In none of these examples does the word primarily mean being killed.

In 3:140, shuhadāʾ (the plural of shahīd) appears in the context of warfare and testing. That context is relevant to its interpretation, but it does not establish that everyone killed in war has the same standing before God. Verses 2:154 and 3:169 use a different expression: “those killed in God’s way.” They identify the group directly, without using shahīd as a synonym for someone who has been killed.

Bearing witness and being killed must therefore be distinguished. A person may bear witness to truth through understanding, honesty, justice, and steadfastness, sometimes at the cost of their life. Being killed alone, however, does not establish the character of their actions or their standing before God.

The distinction is explored in Who Is a Shahīd in the Qur’an?. Here it helps identify precisely whose life the verses describe.

Part Two: Biological Life and the Wider Meaning of Living

3. What Does Life Mean in Biology?

Biology recognizes known terrestrial life through a combination of features: cellular organization, energy use, metabolism, internal regulation, growth, and responses to the environment. Heredity, reproduction, and evolution across generations also characterize living systems. Human bodies belong to this biological world. The brain, heart, nervous system, circulation, and respiration operate through physical and chemical processes. Medicine and biology investigate those processes and the irreversible loss of the vital functions on which bodily life depends.

Whether every possible form of life must have the same characteristics is a broader question. Even astrobiology—the study of life’s origins and possibilities in the universe—uses working definitions rather than a universally settled definition of life. One describes life as a chemical system that sustains itself and can undergo Darwinian evolution. That definition helps researchers investigate known life and search for life elsewhere; it does not purport to settle every question about existence or consciousness.

For the working definition used in astrobiology, see NASA’s explanation of life and its requirements.

If something lacks familiar cellular organization, we have not established that it is alive in the biological sense. Whether it could be alive in some other sense remains a separate question requiring its own evidence. The distinction becomes especially important when considering what happens to a person at death.

4. Does the End of Bodily Life Establish the End of the Person?

The end of the body’s vital activity establishes that its biological life has ended. A further claim—that the person no longer exists in any sense—concerns more than bodily functioning. These claims should be assessed separately. “The body is no longer alive” describes a biological condition. “Nothing of the person remains” makes a claim about the whole person. Moving from the first statement to the second requires an additional argument.

Bodily death alone does not establish that the person has ceased to exist altogether. This distinction does not prove that consciousness survives death. It identifies the question that remains to be answered. In a Qur’anic inquiry, that answer must be sought in the relevant passages.

Verses 2:154 and 3:169 are central because they affirm life for people who have been killed in God’s way. The case for their continuing life rests on that affirmation, not merely on the inability of bodily observation to rule it out.

None of this makes the body unimportant. We experience the world, act, and fulfill our earthly responsibilities through it. The issue is whether bodily existence exhausts what a person is.

5. Does the Qur’an Confine Life to the Body?

Ḥayāh (life) and related words are not used only for biological activity in the Qur’an. In 8:24, people who are already physically alive are called to respond to an invitation that gives them life. In 6:122, being given life is connected with receiving light and walking among people. Both passages concern guidance and awakening. Their use of “life” extends beyond the beginning of a heartbeat, breathing, or growth.

The Qur’an also states in 29:64:

وَمَا هَـٰذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا لَهْوٌ وَلَعِبٌ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ

This worldly life is nothing but diversion and play. The home of the hereafter is truly life, if only they knew. 29:64

The verse places earthly life within a wider account of life and its fulfillment. These passages do not make biological life, spiritual awakening, and life in the hereafter identical. Each has its own context. In particular, a metaphorical use of “life” for guidance does not by itself prove survival after death. What the passages establish here is the breadth of the Qur’an’s language. Evidence about life after death must still come from passages addressing that subject.

For a broader examination of this vocabulary, see The Qur’anic Root Ḥ-Y-Y: Life and Bringing to Life.

Part Three: The Self in Sleep and Death

6. The Self in Sleep and Death: Reading 39:42

The relationship between sleep, death, and the self comes into focus in 39:42:

ٱللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى ٱلْأَنفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا وَٱلَّتِى لَمْ تَمُتْ فِى مَنَامِهَا ۖ فَيُمْسِكُ ٱلَّتِى قَضَىٰ عَلَيْهَا ٱلْمَوْتَ وَيُرْسِلُ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰٓ إِلَىٰٓ أَجَلٍۢ مُّسَمًّى ۚ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَـَٔايَـٰتٍۢ لِّقَوْمٍۢ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

The God takes the selves at the time of their death, and those that have not died during their sleep. He holds those for whom He has decreed death and sends the others back until an appointed time. In this are signs for people who reflect. 39:42

The verse uses nafs (the self or person, often translated as “soul”; plural anfus). That term identifies what is taken without, by itself, defining its nature or its relationship to the body. It also uses tawaffī (taking in full) for both sleep and death. The term therefore cannot simply be treated as another word for dying. The difference lies in what follows: one self is held, while the other is sent back until an appointed time. The account describes more than the condition of bodily organs, but it does not explain how this taking occurs.

Verse 32:11 likewise connects being taken at death with returning to the Lord. Together, these passages invite reflection on the relationship between the self and bodily life, without supplying a complete theory of that relationship.

The distinction matters particularly when considering consciousness. The Qur’anic account of the self being taken is not, by itself, scientific proof that consciousness operates independently of the brain. The body remains alive during sleep, and the brain remains active. Dreams therefore do not demonstrate conscious experience in the absence of brain activity. Nor does 39:42 offer an empirical explanation of how the self and body interact.

Its shared language for sleep and death provides material for interpretation, rather than an experimental account of the soul. The clear distinction in the verse is between being held and being returned. How those conditions are experienced is not explained.

7. Is There Awareness after Death?

In 3:169 and 3:170, the Qur’an attributes life, provision, joy, and gladness to those killed in God’s way. These descriptions concern the people themselves, rather than merely the memories they leave in survivors’ minds. For this group, the descriptions provide strong grounds for speaking of awareness and experience with their Lord.

Can that same quality or degree of awareness be extended to everyone after death? Such a generalization would require examining passages about other groups and the different stages following death.

In 23:99, a person asks to return when death arrives. In 23:100, the request is refused, and a barzakh (a barrier or interval separating stages) is described as lasting until the day people are raised. The passage presents death as the end of the opportunity to return and amend the life already lived.

In 36:52, those being raised speak of a resting place. The expression invites comparison with sleep, but it does not establish that everyone experiences the whole interval between death and resurrection in the same way. The question must therefore be examined through the passages concerning each group and stage. Affirming that awareness can continue after bodily death does not yet tell us how continuous, extensive, or similar to earthly awareness it might be.

8. The Boundary between Death and Resurrection

The barzakh (a separating barrier or interval) mentioned in 23:100 lasts until resurrection. In this context, it marks the boundary beyond which a person cannot return to earthly life. Those whom death has reached cannot recover the opportunities they have lost simply by asking to return.

The word alone does not describe the bodies, time, or experiences that might belong to this interval. Its role in the passage is to establish a boundary between earthly life and resurrection. Philosophical reflection may explore that interval, but any further details must be recognized as interpretation rather than attributed directly to the verse.

Part Four: Life with the Lord and the Limits of Human Perception

9. Alive with Their Lord: Reading 3:169

Verse 3:169 states:

وَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ قُتِلُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ أَمْوَٰتًۢا ۚ بَلْ أَحْيَآءٌ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ يُرْزَقُونَ

Do not think of those killed in the way of The God as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision. 3:169

This develops the affirmation in 2:154: the people described have been killed, yet are alive with their Lord. Their death is acknowledged rather than denied. The life affirmed here therefore cannot simply mean that their earthly bodies have continued functioning as before.

“With their Lord” describes their relationship with God. It does not specify a physical location or explain how that relationship is experienced. The passage gives grounds for affirming their life while leaving its nature largely unexplained.

10. Provision with the Lord: Must It Mean Bodily Food?

The verse describes them as receiving rizq (provision or what God grants). In Qur’anic usage, provision is not confined to food, money, or livelihood; it can describe what a creature receives from God more broadly. Verse 3:169 affirms that these people receive provision, but does not specify its form. It therefore does not settle whether that provision involves bodily nourishment.

The next verse, 3:170, describes their joy in God’s favor and their gladness concerning those who have not yet joined them. These descriptions connect what they receive with divine generosity and with their own experience of it. They do not explain how that provision is received. We should preserve that distinction between the gift affirmed and the manner left unspecified.

For a fuller examination of this vocabulary, see The Qur’anic Root R-Z-Q: Provision and What Is Granted.

11. Why This Life Is More Than Being Remembered

One possible reading is that those killed in God’s way remain alive only through their names, memories, or influence. That reading struggles to account for the passages as a whole.

Verse 2:154 describes a life its hearers do not perceive. A person’s memory and influence, by contrast, can be visible in the lives of those who remain. More significantly, 3:170 attributes joy and gladness to the people who have died themselves. Remembering someone is an experience of the survivors. The verse describes the dead as experiencing God’s favor and gladness concerning others.

The stronger reading, therefore, is that the verses affirm their own life, rather than merely their continuing influence. That reading accounts for the experiences attributed to them without claiming to explain how their life is sustained.

12. Knowing without Directly Perceiving

The closing words of 2:154 draw an important distinction between being told that something is real and perceiving it directly. The verse affirms their life and then says, “you do not perceive.” Its hearers learn of that life through revelation; they do not observe it for themselves.

The verse therefore supplies knowledge of their life while also marking a limit to that knowledge. Knowing that they live is not the same as knowing what their life is like. The limit concerns perception, rather than the possibility of learning anything about them.

Within this Qur’anic argument, the absence of familiar bodily signs does not overturn the life the text affirms. Neither does that affirmation authorize a detailed philosophical description as though it were an eyewitness account. The distinction is between what the text reports and what remains unknown.

13. Does Everyone Have the Same Condition after Death?

Verses 2:154 and 3:169 concern a particular group: those killed in God’s way. The description of this group is not a complete account of everyone’s condition after death.

Nevertheless, 22:58 speaks of people who migrated in God’s way and were then killed or died otherwise, promising good provision to both groups. The promise includes both those killed and those who die otherwise; the manner of death is not its sole criterion.

Two questions remain distinct: whether life continues after death, and what different people experience or receive. Passages about death, the intervening boundary, resurrection, judgment, and human destiny address different aspects of those questions. The argument for continuing existence should not be turned into a claim that everyone has the same experience.

Part Five: A Further Question—Could Awareness Precede Birth?

Whether awareness precedes earthly life is a separate question from whether life continues after death. The evidence considered here is less conclusive than the descriptions of those alive with their Lord.

14. The Stages of Human Life in 2:28

Verse 2:28 states:

كَيْفَ تَكْفُرُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَكُنتُمْ أَمْوَٰتًا فَأَحْيَـٰكُمْ ثُمَّ يُمِيتُكُمْ ثُمَّ يُحْيِيكُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

How can you deny The God, when you were dead and He brought you to life? Then He causes you to die, then brings you to life again, and then to Him you will be returned. 2:28

The sequence moves from being dead to life, then death, renewed life, and return to God. Amwāt (the plural description “dead”) names the initial condition without explaining it in biological or psychological terms. This leaves a question for interpretation: does that initial condition mean the absence of every kind of existence or awareness, or the absence of the earthly life subsequently given? The word amwāt alone cannot decisively answer that question.

The expression kuntum (“you were”) does not resolve the question. Referring to the hearers’ earlier condition does not establish that they then had independent personal existence or conscious experience. The reflection on 2:28 treats this sequence as a basis for considering different conditions of human existence. The sequence offers a starting point for reflection; it does not supply every detail of the stages it names.

15. Two Deaths and Two Lives in 40:11

Verse 40:11 states:

قَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَآ أَمَتَّنَا ٱثْنَتَيْنِ وَأَحْيَيْتَنَا ٱثْنَتَيْنِ فَٱعْتَرَفْنَا بِذُنُوبِنَا فَهَلْ إِلَىٰ خُرُوجٍ مِّن سَبِيلٍ

They say: Our Lord, You caused us to die twice and brought us to life twice. We have now acknowledged our sins. Is there any way out? 40:11

The verse counts two deaths and two lives. Read alongside 2:28, it raises the question of how those stages relate and where earthly life belongs among them. Counting the stages does not explain their nature.

The Qur’an uses the language of death in different contexts, including bodily death and contrasts associated with guidance. Each occurrence must therefore be examined in its own setting. The study The Qur’anic Root M-W-T: Death examines that range of usage.

16. The Covenant Passage and the Possibility of Awareness before Earthly Life

One of the most important passages for exploring awareness before birth is 7:172:

وَإِذْ أَخَذَ رَبُّكَ مِنۢ بَنِىٓ ءَادَمَ مِن ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ ۛ شَهِدْنَآ ۛ أَن تَقُولُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ إِنَّا كُنَّا عَنْ هَٰذَا غَٰفِلِينَ

When your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their backs, their descendants, and made them bear witness concerning themselves: Am I not your Lord? They said: Yes, we bear witness—lest you should say on the Day of Resurrection: We were unaware of this. 7:172

The verse attributes a response and testimony to Adam’s descendants concerning God’s lordship. That makes it relevant to questions of recognition and awareness. Does it describe an experience before earthly life, or does it express humanity’s relationship with God in another way? An experience before earthly life is one interpretation to examine.

On that interpretation, people would have stood in a relationship with God and experienced some awareness before their earthly lives. The awareness proposed need not resemble familiar memory, sensation, or self-consciousness. The difficulty is establishing whether the verse itself places this testimony at a separate time before each individual’s earthly life. Its wording does not specify the timing or manner conclusively. A detailed picture of a primordial gathering—sometimes described as “the world of particles”—should not simply be assumed to be the verse’s own account.

Another possibility relates the testimony to humanity’s innate disposition and responsibility to recognize God, a question also raised by 30:30. The testimony thus warrants investigation, but does not settle the case for conscious personal existence before earthly life.

For a wider examination of covenant and commitment, see Covenant in the Qur’an.

17. Could Awareness Exist in a State Described as Dead?

The description in 2:28 raises a further difficulty for that proposal. How could awareness exist in a condition described as “dead”? The answer depends on what the description excludes.

If amwāt excludes every kind of life and awareness, then conscious experience in that condition would be contradictory. If it refers specifically to the absence of earthly biological life, some other form of awareness remains conceivable. That reading treats death in relation to a particular kind of life. It can make the proposal logically consistent, but does not supply evidence that the proposed experience occurred.

Showing that an idea is possible is not the same as showing that it is true. Verse 2:28 does not describe awareness before earthly life, while the testimony in 7:172 still requires interpretation. The proposal therefore remains a possibility rather than an established conclusion.

18. Before the Human Being Was Anything

Verse 19:67 asks:

أَوَلَا يَذْكُرُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَٰهُ مِن قَبْلُ وَلَمْ يَكُ شَيْـًٔا

Does the human being not remember that We created him before, when he was nothing? 19:67

Verse 76:1 also asks:

هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْإِنسَٰنِ حِينٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُن شَيْـًۭٔا مَّذْكُورًا

Has there not passed over the human being a period of time when he was not a thing mentioned? 76:1

Any account of existence before earthly life must also explain these two passages. A condition described as being “nothing” cannot simply be treated as an individual conscious life. The wording “a thing mentioned” in 76:1 differs from “nothing” in 19:67. That difference deserves attention, but neither expression should be reshaped merely to protect a preferred theory.

One possible response distinguishes nonexistence before creation from a further stage after creation but before earthly life. Such a sequence is conceivable, but these passages do not explicitly establish it. Nor does God’s prior knowledge of a person establish that the person was already experiencing anything. Being known and being conscious are different claims. Awareness before earthly life remains a philosophical and interpretive possibility. It should not be given the same evidential weight as the direct descriptions of life, joy, and provision after death.

Part Six: A Philosophical Model for Considering Other Forms of Life

19. Receiving Information, Processing It, and Acting

The preceding passages invite a philosophical question: can we think about life without assuming that every living being must have human organs? One approach is to consider capacities rather than begin with a particular bodily structure.

The first is reception: receiving information from one’s surroundings. Human beings rely on sensory and nervous systems. A broader model asks whether reception could occur through other means, without claiming that such means have been demonstrated. The second is processing: distinguishing what is received, relating it to other information, and responding to its significance. The third is action: responding to that information, with choice where a capacity for choice exists.

These capacities provide a way to ask questions about possible forms of life. They are not a sufficient test for either biological life or consciousness: a system’s ability to process information and respond does not, by itself, show that it has subjective experience. The model therefore does not classify everything as living or nonliving, and it does not establish survival after death. Its purpose is narrower: to consider whether capacities familiar in embodied life could conceivably take other forms.

20. Life Does Not Require Unlimited Freedom of Choice

Unlimited freedom of choice is not a prerequisite for life. Human choices are already constrained. We do not choose the circumstances of our birth or most features of our bodies, and our decisions depend on what we know and what we can do. Those constraints do not make us any less alive.

Different forms of life might therefore involve different degrees of choice, or action without independent choice at all. The possibility does not require attributing a human kind of will to every being. Nor does it make every responsive system conscious or alive. The relevant question is whether every form of life must involve the particular kind of awareness and choice familiar to us. The passages considered here do not impose that requirement.

21. Jinn and Angels: Knowledge and Action beyond Human Life

The Qur’an describes jinn and angels as hearing, speaking, knowing, and acting. In 72:1, a group of jinn hear the Qur’an and respond to it. Verse 72:11 describes differences among their paths and circumstances, while 72:14 identifies differing orientations among them. In 2:32, angels speak of the knowledge God has taught them. In 21:26, they are described as honored servants, and 21:27 speaks of their acting according to His command.

These descriptions extend knowledge and action beyond human beings with familiar earthly bodies. Verses 55:14 and 55:15 also describe the creation of humans and jinn in different terms. Their natures should not simply be assumed to be identical. Yet the Qur’an does not explain their biology or classify them as nonbiological in the language of modern science. The descriptions do not establish their precise composition.

They allow us to consider beings whose perception and action differ from our own, while leaving much about their nature unspecified. Verse 21:30, which connects water with life, must also be considered. Its relationship to these other passages requires interpretation; it should neither be ignored nor treated as a complete account of the structure of every being. The evidence raises questions about different forms of life without settling their physical nature.

22. Does Universal Glorification Imply Universal Awareness?

In 17:44, the Qur’an speaks of tasbīḥ (glorifying God and declaring Him free of imperfection) by the heavens, the earth, and all that is within them, while reminding human beings that they do not understand this glorification. In 24:41, it also speaks of creatures’ glorification and their salat (prayer or worshipful devotion, whose form must be understood in context).

Does this mean everything is alive or conscious in the same sense as human beings or jinn? The verses do not establish that conclusion. But they also do not establish that this glorification is merely mechanical, with no other dimension to it. Both interpretations require more than the assertion that glorification occurs.

The Qur’an affirms that glorification and acknowledges the limits of human understanding. Those limits leave its character open; they do not supply evidence for whatever account we might prefer. Verse 16:21, meanwhile, calls objects of worship invoked instead of God “dead” and “not alive.” Its distinction between life and death must be read in the context of its criticism of such worship.

These passages neither establish that all things are conscious nor offer a complete philosophical account of everything that exists. Biology can assess an object’s status by biological criteria. Any further claim about other dimensions of its existence requires a separate argument. The model proposed here keeps those questions distinct rather than claiming to answer them all.

23. The Unseen and the Witnessed: Observation Does Not Exhaust Reality

The Qur’an describes God as knowing ghayb (what is unseen or hidden from perception) and shahādah (what is witnessed or manifest), as in 6:73 and 59:22. The expression concerns the scope of God’s knowledge. Speaking of an “unseen realm” can help distinguish what we perceive from what we do not, but should not imply that these words alone define two spatial worlds with the properties assigned to them in later philosophy.

In 7:27, beings are described as seeing human beings from where humans do not see them. In 56:85, a scene of dying likewise reminds those present of the limits of their sight. These passages challenge the assumption that the limits of our perception are the limits of what exists. That does not make every unseen thing real. A claim about something we cannot observe still needs evidence.

For the life of those killed in God’s way, this study relies on the Qur’an’s report. The philosophical model helps frame the question; it does not provide an independent proof of the reported life.

For the relationship between the unseen, knowledge, and guidance, see Faith in the Unseen.

Part Seven: Philosophical and Human Implications

24. What Changes If Life Is More Than Bodily Existence?

If we measure a life only by what is visible between birth and death, we may overlook the value of commitments that bring suffering rather than worldly success. This does not mean that valuing justice or compassion requires belief in life after death; the question here is how the Qur’an frames such commitments.

In 2:153, however, the Qur’an calls believers to seek help through ṣabr (patience and steadfastness under pressure) and salat. Verse 2:154 then describes life continuing despite being killed in God’s way. Verse 2:155 speaks of testing through fear, hunger, and losses of wealth and life. In 2:156, those who remain steadfast recall that they belong to God and return to Him. This sequence matters.

The Qur’an does not treat earthly life as worthless. It is here that people have opportunities to understand, choose, act, do good, and make things right. But it does not measure the value of their whole journey solely by immediate, observable results. Within that account, suffering or being killed in fidelity to truth does not exhaust the meaning of a person’s life. What others can observe of the body and its history is not the whole measure of that life.

This perspective must not become indifference to life or glorification of death. Protecting life, pursuing justice, avoiding oppression, and meeting responsibilities toward others remain important. The wider horizon gives context to earthly responsibility; it does not remove it.

25. How Far Can a Philosophical Theory Go?

The proposed model examines reception, processing, and action without assuming that every possible form of life must have a human body. It offers questions to investigate, not proof of how life with God is sustained. The proposal of awareness before earthly life is likewise open to further examination. Its possibility should not be confused with the more direct descriptions of life after death.

Interpretations should remain open to correction by closer reading, linguistic evidence, and relevant scientific findings. The aim is to follow the evidence rather than preserve a picture chosen in advance. Neither restricting all life to familiar bodies nor affirming an imagined alternative becomes justified merely through repetition.

What the Evidence Supports—and What It Leaves Open

The evidence supports different conclusions with different degrees of confidence. Biological inquiry describes the processes that sustain bodily life and the loss of those functions. It does not, on its own, answer every question about personal existence. The Qur’an uses “life” in several contexts. That range matters for interpretation, but should not be mistaken for a single account of how every kind of life works.

The strongest textual basis examined here is 2:154 together with 3:169 and 3:170. They describe people who have been killed as alive, receiving provision, and experiencing joy. Those descriptions support a reading of their own continuing life, rather than merely their memory among survivors.

Verse 39:42 adds an account of the self being taken in sleep and death. It distinguishes their outcomes without explaining how consciousness relates to the brain or what experience after death is like. Verses 2:28 and 40:11 place life and death within a sequence, but do not define every stage.

The testimony in 7:172 invites a further question about awareness before earthly life. Any proposed answer must also account for 19:67 and 76:1, rather than assume conscious personal existence throughout the period before earthly life.

The philosophical model contributes a way of considering possibilities. It neither defines life conclusively nor proves that information processing amounts to conscious experience. The distinctions are therefore essential: biological findings, scriptural claims, and philosophical possibilities have different grounds and different limits. A sound conclusion should reflect those differences in confidence.

Conclusion

Bodily death is real. The question is whether it tells us everything about the person who has died. For those killed in God’s way, the Qur’an’s answer is that they are alive with their Lord, beyond ordinary human perception. It affirms their life without giving a complete account of what that life is like.

Other questions remain open, including the character of awareness after death and the possibility of awareness before earthly life. They can be explored without treating every possibility as a fact.

The body’s death alone does not establish that the person has ceased to exist altogether. The Qur’anic passages give this study grounds to look beyond bodily death, while also setting limits on what it can claim to know.

God knows best.
