Qur’anic Concepts

Life and Death in the Qur’an: Does Bodily Death End Human Existence?

A Qur’anic and philosophical study of the self, death, awareness, and life with God

نسخه فارسی

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ

In the name of The God, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

Introduction: Is Life Only What We Can Observe?

No knowledge of Arabic is assumed. References use chapter:verse notation: 2:154 means chapter 2, verse 154. Every Arabic passage is followed by an English rendering. Root-study links explain the families of Arabic words behind key terms; the short definitions here are sufficient to follow the argument.

From our earliest years, we learn about the world through our senses. We see living things move and grow, and distinguish life from death through observable signs. A living child breathes, grows, and responds to the surroundings. When a person’s bodily life ends, the body no longer sustains its previous vital functions. This is the familiar setting in which we ordinarily speak of life and death.

But does this experience encompass everything that life could mean?

We encounter life primarily through physical, biological bodies. Almost without noticing, we may begin to define life entirely by bodily structures and the functioning of organs. We may then rule out the possibility of life wherever those structures are absent.

Such criteria have a role in studying familiar biological life. But what would justify extending them to every possible form of existence?

The question becomes especially important when reading the Qur’an. It speaks of jinn (beings ordinarily hidden from human perception), angels, the self, God’s taking of persons in sleep and death, renewed life, and life with the Lord. In 2:154, it describes a life belonging to those killed in God’s way that its hearers do not perceive.

وَلَا تَقُولُوا۟ لِمَن يُقْتَلُ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ أَمْوَٰتٌۢ ۚ بَلْ أَحْيَآءٌ وَلَـٰكِن لَّا تَشْعُرُونَ

Do not say of those killed in the way of The God that they are dead. Rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive it. 2:154

The verse does not deny that these people have been killed. It explicitly identifies them as killed, yet calls them alive. At least in this case, the end of bodily life is not presented as the end of life in every sense.

This raises further questions. Could life take forms beyond biological organization? Can we coherently conceive of human existence or awareness before earthly life? What relationship between the self, the body, and awareness remains after death? And can beings such as jinn and angels be understood solely through the biological criteria used for human bodies?

This study examines those questions. Its purpose is not to settle the nature of unseen reality through a single philosophical theory. It asks what the Qur’an says about life and death, which interpretations its passages support when read together, and where our knowledge reaches its limits.

The central question is this: Is biological life only one form of life known to us, and does bodily death necessarily mark the end of a person’s entire existence?

Part One: Method and an Important Distinction

1. Reading the Qur’an: Language, Context, and Philosophical Reflection

This inquiry begins with the Qur’an’s own language. It examines ḥayāh (life or living), mawt (death), amwāt (the plural description “dead”), and iḥyāʾ (bringing to life), alongside nafs (the self or person, often translated as “soul”), tawaffī (taking or receiving in full; used of God’s taking of persons in both sleep and death), rizq (provision or what one is granted), and shahādah (witnessing or testimony). Passages that support an initial reading are considered alongside those that might challenge it.

Linguistic evidence helps establish a word’s range of meaning. But a dictionary meaning cannot, by itself, yield a complete theory of how reality is constituted. Nor does a word’s appearance in two different contexts mean that everything described in those contexts has the same properties.

For example, the Qur’an speaks of bodily life, life associated with guidance, and life with the Lord. The shared word “life” does not establish that these three situations have identical structures or qualities.

Three levels of argument must therefore remain distinct: what the Qur’an explicitly states; what we infer by comparing its passages; and a philosophical hypothesis proposed to explain how something might be possible.

When the Qur’an reports that those killed in God’s way are alive, that report provides a starting point for this study. Explaining their mode of perception, the character of their awareness, or the constitution of their existence is a further task, requiring philosophical reflection and greater caution.

The natural sciences also have a place in this inquiry. Biology helps us understand the bodily life we know. It should not be expected to settle every question about unseen reality. Equally, a philosophical interpretation of scripture must not be presented as an empirical scientific finding.

This approach allows us to consider life beyond the body without turning a philosophical possibility into a definitive claim about all beings.

2. Witnesses and Those Killed in God’s Way: Why the Distinction Matters

Before considering the life of those killed in God’s way, we need to clarify a common ambiguity.

In contemporary religious usage, shahīd (a witness; commonly rendered “martyr” in later usage) often means someone killed in war or in God’s way. This usage is so familiar that witnessing itself can come to be understood chiefly as a particular kind of death.

In the Qur’an, however, the root sh-h-d connects with presence, observation, knowledge, and testimony. God is Himself a witness. Messengers bear witness concerning their communities. Jesus speaks of having been a witness in 5:117. In 2:143, the community of believers is appointed to bear witness concerning humanity, while 2:282 discusses witnesses to transactions.

In none of these examples does the word primarily mean being killed.

The plural shuhadāʾ does occur in the setting of warfare and testing in 3:140. This context allows a relationship between trial, faith, and witnessing. It does not establish that everyone killed in a war acquires a divinely recognized standing simply through the manner of death.

By contrast, 2:154 and 3:169 identify the people under discussion explicitly as those killed in God’s way. These verses do not define the word shahīd as “someone killed.”

Bearing witness and being killed must therefore be distinguished.

Through understanding, truthfulness, justice, and fidelity to what is right, a person may bear witness to truth in life. That fidelity may sometimes cost them their life. But the fact of being killed cannot substitute for understanding their actions or their standing before God.

The study Who Is a Shahīd in the Qur’an? examines the uses of this root in greater detail. Here the focus is life and death, rather than classifying people by how they died.

Part Two: Biological Life and the Wider Meaning of Living

3. What Does Life Mean in Biology?

Biology identifies known terrestrial life through a combination of characteristics. Living organisms generally have cellular organization, obtain and use energy, maintain metabolism and internal balance, grow, and respond to changes in their surroundings. Heredity, reproduction, and evolution across generations are also important features of this system of life.

The human body belongs to this system. The brain, heart, nervous system, circulation, breathing, and other bodily functions operate through physical, chemical, and biological processes.

The irreversible end of the body’s vital functioning marks the end of its biological life. This is a matter that medicine and biology can investigate.

Yet understanding bodily life is not the same task as understanding every possible form of life.

Even in astrobiology—the study of life’s origins and possibilities in the universe—the definition of life is not a closed question. One working definition describes life as a chemical system that sustains itself and can undergo Darwinian evolution. Such a definition is useful for studying familiar life and searching for life elsewhere. It need not settle every question about existence or awareness.

For the working definition used in astrobiology, see NASA’s explanation of life and its requirements. This is an empirical aid to searching for life; it is not used here to establish or deny unseen life.

If a being lacks the cellular structure familiar to us, we may say that biological life of the known terrestrial kind has not been established for it. That alone does not settle whether it could be alive in any other sense.

This distinction also matters when thinking about human beings.

4. Does the End of Bodily Life Establish the End of the Person?

When the body’s vital activity ends, a person’s biological life in the physical world has ended. But does that observation establish that the person has ceased to exist in every respect?

Two claims need to be separated here.

The first is that the physical, biological body is no longer alive. The second is that no other form of existence or life remains for that person.

The first concerns bodily functioning; the second concerns the entirety of human existence.

The cessation of biological activity alone does not establish the complete annihilation of the person.

This argument does not, by itself, prove continuing awareness after death either. It establishes only that observing the end of bodily life is insufficient to settle everything about human existence.

A Qur’anic inquiry into life after death must therefore turn to the relevant passages. This is why 2:154 and 3:169 are central: the Qur’an itself describes life for people whose familiar bodily lives have ended through being killed in God’s way.

The body has a real place in earthly human life. Through it we experience the world, act, and carry out responsibilities. It should not be dismissed as worthless or as merely a temporary instrument of little importance.

The point is simply that identifying the whole person with bodily structure requires justification; it cannot be assumed.

5. Does the Qur’an Confine Life to the Body?

The root ḥ-y-y, from which words for life and bringing to life are formed, is not used only for biological activity.

In 8:24, people who are already physically alive are called to respond to an invitation that gives them life. In 6:122, being given life is connected with receiving light and walking among people.

These passages concern guidance and human awakening. Life here cannot be reduced to the onset of a heartbeat, breathing, or bodily growth.

The Qur’an also states in 29:64:

وَمَا هَـٰذِهِ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا لَهْوٌ وَلَعِبٌ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ لَهِىَ ٱلْحَيَوَانُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ

This worldly life is nothing but diversion and play. The home of the hereafter is truly life, if only they knew. 29:64

This wording challenges the assumption that earthly life supplies the final measure of everything life can mean.

The different uses must nevertheless be distinguished. Biological life, life associated with guidance, and life in the hereafter occur in different settings. A shared word does not make their structures and characteristics identical.

The Qur’an thus opens a way to consider life beyond ordinary earthly experience without explaining every aspect of those further forms of life.

For a broader examination of this vocabulary, see The Qur’anic Root Ḥ-Y-Y: Life and Bringing to Life.

Part Three: Death, Sleep, and the Taking of the Self

6. The Self in Sleep and Death: Reading 39:42

A central verse for examining death and the self is 39:42:

ٱللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى ٱلْأَنفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا وَٱلَّتِى لَمْ تَمُتْ فِى مَنَامِهَا ۖ فَيُمْسِكُ ٱلَّتِى قَضَىٰ عَلَيْهَا ٱلْمَوْتَ وَيُرْسِلُ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰٓ إِلَىٰٓ أَجَلٍۢ مُّسَمًّى ۚ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَـَٔايَـٰتٍۢ لِّقَوْمٍۢ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

The God takes the selves at the time of their death, and those that have not died during their sleep. He holds those for whom He has decreed death and sends the others back until an appointed time. In this are signs for people who reflect. 39:42

The verse connects sleep and death while preserving their difference.

Both involve tawaffī, the taking of the self. Yet their outcomes differ: one is held, while the other is sent back until an appointed time.

The verse matters because it does not describe death solely in terms of bodily organs. It speaks also of the self and its being taken.

Verse 32:11 likewise describes the taking of human beings at death and their return to their Lord.

These passages provide important material for philosophical reflection on the relationship between the self and the body’s observable life, and on the human condition after earthly life ends.

But we must distinguish the Qur’an’s description of the taking of the self from a philosophical proof that awareness is entirely independent of brain activity.

During sleep, the body is alive and the brain remains active. Dreams and experiences during sleep do not, by themselves, demonstrate awareness without any brain activity.

Nor does 39:42 itself supply an empirical account of what the self is made of or how it relates to the body.

The shared language of taking in sleep and death is important for a Qur’anic inquiry into self and body. It should not be turned into scientific proof of the nature of the soul or of conscious experience wholly independent of the brain.

What the verse clearly distinguishes is the outcome for the self in these two conditions. The precise character of that process remains open to reflection.

7. Is There Awareness after Death?

In 3:169 and 3:170, the Qur’an attributes life, provision, joy, and gladness to those killed in God’s way.

These descriptions concern the people themselves, rather than merely the memories they leave in survivors’ minds.

For this group, the text therefore provides strong grounds for speaking of some form of reception and experience with their Lord.

Can that same quality or degree of awareness be extended to everyone after death?

Such a generalization would require examining passages about other groups and the different stages following death.

In 23:99, a person asks to return when death arrives. In 23:100, the request is refused, and a barzakh (a barrier or interval separating stages) is described as lasting until the day people are raised.

The end of earthly life does not leave the same opportunities for choice and correction available. A boundary separates the life already lived from what follows it.

In 36:52, people speak at resurrection of being raised from a resting place. The wording permits comparison with a sleep-like condition, but does not establish that everyone has an identical experience throughout the interval between death and resurrection.

There is thus Qur’anic evidence to consider concerning awareness after death, but each group’s condition needs to be examined in context.

The philosophical approach taken here allows that the end of bodily activity may not end every form of reception or awareness. The character and extent of any such awareness, however, must be investigated carefully through the passages themselves.

8. Barzakh: A Boundary between Stages

Verse 23:100 describes a barzakh lasting until the day people are raised.

The expression denotes a barrier or boundary between conditions.

In context, the person whom death has reached cannot simply request a return to the former life and repeat the opportunities already spent.

The word barzakh alone does not disclose a complete world: the form of bodies within it, the character of time, or every experience people may have after death.

What matters here is the interval and boundary between the end of earthly life and resurrection.

Within the study’s philosophical framework, different forms of existence and awareness during that interval may be considered. Details supplied by imagination must not be attributed to the Qur’an.

Part Four: Life with the Lord and the Limits of Human Perception

9. Life Not Confined to the Earthly Body: Reading 3:169

Verse 3:169 states:

وَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ قُتِلُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ أَمْوَٰتًۢا ۚ بَلْ أَحْيَآءٌ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ يُرْزَقُونَ

Do not think of those killed in the way of The God as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision. 3:169

This verse develops the truth already stated in 2:154.

These people really have been killed, yet the Qur’an describes them as alive with their Lord.

That life should not simply be understood as the continued functioning of their earthly bodies: the same verse identifies them as having been killed.

Nor should “with their Lord” be turned, without justification, into a physical location with familiar coordinates. The expression concerns their standing and relationship with their Lord without explaining its manner in detail.

We should avoid two extremes: denying their life because their bodily life has ended, and claiming a definitive picture of the constitution and quality of that life.

10. Provision with the Lord: Must It Mean Bodily Food?

The same verse says that they receive provision.

The Qur’anic root r-z-q is not confined to bread, water, wealth, or livelihood. It concerns what God grants and the benefit a creature receives.

Verse 3:169 does not describe digestion, eating, or drinking in the manner of biological bodies. Their provision should therefore not be confined to bodily nutrition.

Verse 3:170 also describes their joy in God’s favor and their gladness concerning those who have not yet joined them.

Their enjoyment is thus described in relation to divine favor.

This does not mean we now know the full nature of their provision. The Qur’an reports that they receive it; it does not define its kind or manner through the standards of our bodily lives.

For a fuller examination of this vocabulary, see The Qur’anic Root R-Z-Q: Provision and What Is Granted.

11. Why This Life Is More Than Being Remembered

It is sometimes suggested that the life of those killed in God’s way means only that their names, memory, and social influence endure.

That reading does not account adequately for all the details in these passages.

In 2:154, the Qur’an describes a life its hearers do not perceive. A person’s memory and continuing influence within a community, however, can be evident to those who remain.

Moreover, 3:170 attributes joy and gladness to the people who have been killed themselves.

A memory survives in other people’s minds. That memory does not itself rejoice in divine favor or experience gladness concerning those who have not yet joined it.

The life described here therefore cannot be reduced to a literary expression for a person’s enduring social influence.

That conclusion still does not provide complete knowledge of the character of this life. The Qur’an affirms it and describes some of its features, but does not explain how it is constituted.

12. “You Do Not Perceive”: Knowing That Something Is Real Is Not Observing It

The closing words of 2:154 are central to the whole inquiry.

The Qur’an says that these people are alive, but that its hearers do not perceive it.

Knowledge received through revelation should be distinguished from direct perception of a phenomenon.

The verse itself informs its hearers that those killed in God’s way are alive. It therefore does not say that there is no way to know anything about their life.

But knowing this through the Qur’an’s report is not the same as being able to observe how that life is lived.

“You do not perceive” marks a limit to ordinary human perception.

Our biological experience of earthly bodies should therefore not become a decisive standard for denying a life that the Qur’an reports.

Equally, a philosophical model should not describe unseen life in such detail that it sounds like a report of direct observation.

Knowing that a reality exists and knowing its complete nature are different achievements.

13. Does Everyone Have the Same Condition after Death?

Verses 2:154 and 3:169 concern a particular group: those killed in God’s way.

Their description should not become a complete account of everyone’s condition after death without further argument.

Nevertheless, 22:58 speaks of people who migrated in God’s way and were then killed or died otherwise, promising good provision to both groups.

Divine generosity should therefore not be confined to one particular manner of dying.

We must also distinguish the question of existence or life after death from the character of what different groups receive.

Passages about death, the intervening boundary, resurrection, judgment, and human destiny each illuminate part of that inquiry.

This study does not claim that every experience after death is identical. It examines the possibility and evidence of continuing existence and awareness beyond the body’s biological life.

Part Five: A Further Question—Could Awareness Precede Birth?

This part considers a further, distinct question. The main argument concerning life after death does not depend on proving awareness before birth. The proposals here should be read as philosophical and interpretive possibilities.

14. The Stages of Human Life in 2:28

Verse 2:28 states:

كَيْفَ تَكْفُرُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَكُنتُمْ أَمْوَٰتًا فَأَحْيَـٰكُمْ ثُمَّ يُمِيتُكُمْ ثُمَّ يُحْيِيكُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

How can you deny The God, when you were dead and He brought you to life? Then He causes you to die, then brings you to life again, and then to Him you will be returned. 2:28

The verse sets out a sequence: being dead, being brought to life, death, renewed life, and return.

Its opening description is amwāt, “dead.” It does not provide a detailed account of a biological constitution or a state of awareness.

This permits a philosophical question: must being described as dead before earthly life mean the absence of every kind of existence or reception, or might it indicate the absence of a particular kind of life?

The word amwāt alone cannot decisively answer that question.

Nor does kuntum, the grammatical expression meaning “you were,” prove that individuals possessed independent existence and actual personal awareness before birth. The verse describes a prior condition of its hearers without explaining that condition in detail.

The reflection on 2:28 treats this sequence as a basis for considering different conditions of human existence.

Such a model may be useful in philosophical inquiry, provided it does not claim more certainty than the Qur’anic wording supports.

15. Two Deaths and Two Lives in 40:11

Verse 40:11 states:

قَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَآ أَمَتَّنَا ٱثْنَتَيْنِ وَأَحْيَيْتَنَا ٱثْنَتَيْنِ فَٱعْتَرَفْنَا بِذُنُوبِنَا فَهَلْ إِلَىٰ خُرُوجٍ مِّن سَبِيلٍ

They say: Our Lord, You caused us to die twice and brought us to life twice. We have now acknowledged our sins. Is there any way out? 40:11

Here death and life are presented as stages that can be counted.

Read alongside 2:28, the verse invites consideration of how these stages relate to one another and where earthly life belongs among them.

Their number does not, however, establish that we understand the full nature of each death and each life.

Across different contexts, Qur’anic language addresses bodily death, the absence of familiar life, and uses associated with guidance and perception.

Careful study of the root m-w-t is therefore necessary. Its wider range is examined in The Qur’anic Root M-W-T: Death.

16. The Covenant Passage and the Possibility of Awareness before Earthly Life

One of the most important passages for exploring awareness before birth is 7:172:

وَإِذْ أَخَذَ رَبُّكَ مِنۢ بَنِىٓ ءَادَمَ مِن ظُهُورِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَأَشْهَدَهُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ ۛ شَهِدْنَآ ۛ أَن تَقُولُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ إِنَّا كُنَّا عَنْ هَٰذَا غَٰفِلِينَ

When your Lord took from the children of Adam, from their backs, their descendants, and made them bear witness concerning themselves: Am I not your Lord? They said: Yes, we bear witness—lest you should say on the Day of Resurrection: We were unaware of this. 7:172

In this verse, the children of Adam encounter the truth of God’s lordship, and an answer and testimony are attributed to them.

That testimony matters for this inquiry because it involves reception, recognition, and acknowledgment.

Could it indicate a degree of awareness that does not depend on a person’s ordinary experience in an earthly body?

This is an interpretive possibility worth examining.

We can conceive a philosophical model in which, before emerging as biological individuals in the physical world, human beings stood in a fundamental relationship with their Lord and experienced some form of reception or awareness. Such awareness need not be identical to familiar earthly memory, sensation, or self-consciousness.

But an essential question remains: does the verse itself locate this testimony in a separate time before each individual’s bodily birth?

It does not explain the manner or timing in a way that excludes every alternative. The later expression “the world of particles,” associated with accounts of a primordial gathering, or a complete picture of individual life before birth should therefore not be read into the text without examination.

The testimony might also relate to humanity’s innate disposition and responsibility to recognize divine lordship, a question for which 30:30 provides further material.

Awareness before earthly life can thus be considered as a possibility. More argument is needed before presenting it as an established fact about every human being.

For a wider examination of covenant and commitment, see Covenant in the Qur’an.

17. Is Being Described as Dead Incompatible with Earlier Awareness?

We can now state the difficulty more precisely.

If 2:28 describes human beings as dead before earthly life, how could awareness be possible in that same condition?

The answer depends on what “life” and “death” mean at each proposed level.

If amwāt in 2:28 is taken to mean the absence of all life, reception, and awareness in every respect, actual awareness in that condition would contradict the premise.

If, instead, it describes the absence of earthly, biological human life, another level of existence or reception remains philosophically conceivable.

On that reading, death is understood relative to a particular kind of life. Awareness at another level would not necessarily create a logical contradiction.

Nevertheless, removing a possible contradiction does not prove that the proposed awareness exists.

Verse 2:28 does not, by itself, tell us what degree of awareness human beings had before birth. Verse 7:172 also requires careful examination of the kind of testimony described and its relationship to individual awareness.

The model may therefore propose that awareness before earthly life is philosophically conceivable. It must not turn that possibility into a definitive Qur’anic conclusion.

18. Passages Describing the Human Being as Once Nothing

Verse 19:67 asks:

أَوَلَا يَذْكُرُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَٰهُ مِن قَبْلُ وَلَمْ يَكُ شَيْـًٔا

Does the human being not remember that We created him before, when he was nothing? 19:67

Verse 76:1 also asks:

هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْإِنسَٰنِ حِينٌۭ مِّنَ ٱلدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُن شَيْـًۭٔا مَّذْكُورًا

Has there not passed over the human being a period of time when he was not a thing mentioned? 76:1

Any theory of human existence and awareness before birth must account for both passages.

If a human being was at one stage “nothing,” that same stage cannot simply be described as an individual conscious life without explanation.

Verse 76:1 speaks specifically of not being “a thing mentioned.” Its difference from “nothing” in 19:67 deserves examination. Neither expression should be redefined merely to preserve a preferred theory.

One philosophical possibility is to distinguish nonexistence before creation from a possible stage after creation but before earthly birth. Such a model is logically conceivable.

But does the Qur’an specify those stages in that order and detail? The passages examined here do not explicitly supply such a complete sequence.

God’s prior knowledge of a person is a separate issue. Being known to God does not necessarily mean that the person already possessed personal experience and actual awareness at that stage.

Awareness before earthly life therefore remains a philosophical and interpretive possibility in this study. It may be investigated, but should not be assigned the same degree of confidence as the Qur’an’s more explicit statements about life after death.

Part Six: A Philosophical Model for Considering Other Forms of Life

19. Receiving Information, Processing It, and Acting

To consider life beyond biological organization, we should not assume at the outset that every living being must possess precisely the organs of a human body.

Philosophical inquiry can focus on capacities rather than beginning with a particular material composition or bodily shape.

One such capacity is reception: a being might receive information or communication from its surroundings or the domain in which it exists. In human beings, reception involves the senses and nervous system. A broader philosophical model need not assume in advance that every possible form of reception requires exactly those instruments.

A second capacity is processing: what is received can be distinguished, related, analyzed, or otherwise processed within the being’s organization.

A third is action or response. The being responds to what it has received and processed, or makes a choice where it possesses the capacity to choose.

These capacities are proposed as a way to conceive and investigate possible forms of life. Their presence alone is not being offered as sufficient proof that something is alive.

Nor is this study attempting to divide everything that exists into living and nonliving categories using these three features.

The purpose is to avoid confining the discussion from the outset to cells, carbon chemistry, and the organs of human bodies.

20. Life Does Not Require Unlimited Freedom of Choice

In this model, absolute freedom of choice is not a condition of life.

Human beings themselves do not possess unlimited freedom. We are born into circumstances we did not choose, receive bodies whose characteristics we largely did not determine, and make decisions within the limits of our knowledge, opportunities, and abilities.

These limits do not make us less than living beings.

We can therefore consider different degrees of willing and acting. A being might choose within its capacities; another might possess a constrained or implicit form of willing; another kind of action might occur without independent will or choice.

These possibilities do not make every action identical in nature, nor do they make every responsive system alive.

The question is whether life, wherever it exists, must necessarily involve the particular kind of choice and awareness familiar to human beings.

The Qur’anic passages considered here do not establish such a requirement.

21. Jinn and Angels: Reception and Action beyond Human Experience

The Qur’an attributes reception, conversation, knowledge, and action to jinn and angels.

In 72:1, a group of jinn hear the Qur’an and respond to it. Verse 72:11 describes differences among their paths and circumstances, while 72:14 identifies differing orientations among them.

In 2:32, angels speak of the knowledge God has taught them. In 21:26, they are described as honored servants, and 21:27 speaks of their acting according to His command.

Within the Qur’an’s account of reality, reception, knowledge, and action are therefore not confined to human beings with familiar earthly bodies.

In 55:14 and 55:15, human beings and jinn are described as created in different terms. We should not assume without argument that their constitutions are identical.

The Qur’an does not, however, define the biological or nonbiological constitution of jinn and angels in the terminology of the natural sciences. These passages cannot establish their precise composition.

What matters here is the possibility of conceiving beings whose organization, perception, and action differ from human experience.

We must also take seriously 21:30, which connects water and life. But without examining its context and relationship to other passages, we should not conclude that every being mentioned in the Qur’an must have an identical biological organization.

These passages call for further inquiry, rather than a hasty verdict on the substance and constitution of every being.

22. Does the Praise Offered by All Things Make Everything Alive?

In 17:44, the Qur’an speaks of tasbīḥ (glorifying God and declaring Him free of imperfection) by the heavens, the earth, and all that is within them, while reminding human beings that they do not understand this glorification.

In 24:41, it also speaks of creatures’ glorification and their salat (prayer or worshipful devotion, whose form must be understood in context).

Do these passages establish that everything is alive in precisely the sense in which human beings or jinn are alive?

That conclusion requires an argument.

Conversely, can we assert with certainty that all such glorification is merely mechanical response, without any degree of reception or inward reality?

That claim, too, goes beyond what the passages themselves establish.

The Qur’an affirms glorification while reminding us of our limited understanding. We should neither assign human consciousness to everything without evidence nor reduce its glorification to a wholly mechanical process without justification.

In 16:21, objects of worship invoked instead of God are described as dead and not alive. This usage shows that the Qur’an distinguishes life from its absence. The scope of the statement must also be understood within its criticism of false objects of worship.

This study therefore does not claim that everything is alive. Nor does it use the limited criteria of biology to pronounce a final judgment on every aspect of the existence of stones, wood, or other things before God.

Scientific criteria can describe the biological status of an object. That assessment should not, without further argument, be expanded into complete knowledge of every dimension of its existence before God.

The philosophical model proposed here is intended to keep inquiry open, rather than provide a definitive classification of everything that exists.

23. The Unseen and the Witnessed: Observation Does Not Exhaust Reality

The Qur’an describes God as knowing ghayb (what is unseen or hidden from perception) and shahādah (what is witnessed or manifest), as in 6:73 and 59:22.

The Qur’anic expression should be distinguished from later philosophical terminology. The Qur’an speaks of God’s knowledge of the unseen and the witnessed. When this study refers to an unseen realm or a domain of observable reality, it is explaining the difference between what is accessible to us and what remains hidden from our knowledge. It is not claiming that the Qur’an has thereby defined two spatial worlds with all the properties assigned to them by later philosophies.

In 7:27, beings are described as seeing human beings from where humans do not see them. In 56:85, a scene of dying likewise reminds those present of the limits of their sight.

These passages indicate that human powers of observation do not supply the final measure of everything real.

Yet inability to observe something does not, by itself, establish its existence. A claim about a hidden reality still requires evidence.

For the life of those killed in God’s way, the evidence in this inquiry is the Qur’an’s own report. The philosophical model does not replace revelation. It helps us avoid ruling out, from the outset, a reality beyond our experience of biological life.

For the relationship between the unseen, knowledge, and guidance, see Faith in the Unseen.

Part Seven: Philosophical and Human Implications

24. What Changes If Life Is More Than Bodily Existence?

If we confine life to the interval between birth and bodily death, we may also come to assess human worth solely through lifespan, physical ability, wealth, power, and visible worldly outcomes.

In 2:153, however, the Qur’an calls believers to seek help through ṣabr (patience and steadfastness under pressure) and salat. Verse 2:154 then describes life continuing despite being killed in God’s way. Verse 2:155 speaks of testing through fear, hunger, and losses of wealth and life. In 2:156, those who remain steadfast recall that they belong to God and return to Him.

This sequence matters.

The Qur’an does not treat earthly life as worthless. It is here that people have opportunities to understand, choose, act, do good, and make things right.

But it does not measure the value of their whole journey solely by immediate, observable results.

A person may suffer or lose their life in fidelity to truth. In the Qur’an’s account, however, the reality of that life is not measured only by what others have seen of the person’s body and earthly story.

This perspective must not become indifference to life or glorification of death. Protecting life, pursuing justice, avoiding oppression, and meeting responsibilities toward others remain important.

The purpose is to widen our understanding of life and its destination.

25. How Far Can a Philosophical Theory Go?

This study has proposed a model for considering different forms of life: receiving information, processing it, and acting or responding with differing degrees of will and choice.

The model makes life beyond the body’s biological organization conceivable. It does not, by itself, establish the nature of life with the Lord.

The possibility of awareness before birth likewise requires further research, despite the Qur’anic material that invites reflection on it.

If closer reading, linguistic evidence, or new scientific understanding calls for revising part of the theory, that revision should be accepted.

A Qur’an-centered inquiry should seek the meaning supported by the passages together, rather than defend a picture decided in advance.

Just as life should not be confined to carbon-based bodies without justification, every philosophical conception of life should not be treated as established truth.

Final Analysis: What Do We Know, and What Remains Unclear?

The inquiry recognizes bodily, biological life as one familiar form of living. In the physical world, it depends on physical, chemical, and biological processes and ends when the body’s vital functioning irreversibly ceases.

The Qur’an also uses the language of life in other settings, including guidance, renewed life, and life with the Lord.

In 2:154 and 3:169, it affirms the life of those killed in God’s way. In 3:170, it attributes joy and gladness to them. Taken together, these descriptions do not permit their life to be reduced to memories in survivors’ minds.

Verse 39:42 connects the taking of the self with sleep and death, but does not fully explain the nature of the self or how awareness might continue after death.

In 2:28 and 40:11, death and life belong to a sequence of stages. These passages provide material for considering different conditions of human existence.

Regarding awareness before birth, 7:172 raises an important question about the testimony of Adam’s descendants. Yet 19:67 and 76:1 prevent us from simply assuming individual conscious existence at every stage before birth.

Philosophically, forms of reception, processing, and action beyond familiar biological organization can be investigated. This model is neither a final definition of every kind of life nor a classification of all beings.

Three matters must therefore remain distinct: biological life, which can be investigated scientifically; philosophical possibilities concerning other forms of life; and what the Qur’an reports about realities beyond earthly observation.

All three matter, but none can substitute for the others.

Conclusion

We know life primarily through physical, biological bodies. A body grows, receives and responds, participates in experience, and eventually ceases its vital activity. That cessation alone does not demonstrate that the person has been annihilated in every respect.

The Qur’an describes those killed in God’s way as alive with their Lord, beyond ordinary human perception. Other passages address death, the taking of the self, renewed life, and the human return to God.

Philosophical reflection can consider further forms or levels of life that need not depend on the familiar organization of human bodies. It can also investigate awareness before birth or continuing reception after death. But philosophical possibility must not be confused with conclusive proof.

This study neither denies bodily death nor claims complete knowledge of unseen reality. It distinguishes the end of bodily life from a judgment about the entirety of human existence.

The physical, biological body can die, but its death alone does not establish the complete annihilation of the person.

What is accessible to observation is not necessarily the whole of reality. The God reports a life whose character we do not know. Inquiry can suggest ways of understanding that report, while remaining mindful of the limits of human knowledge.

God knows best.
