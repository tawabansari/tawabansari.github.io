Introduction

The Qur’an offers enduring guidance for human life. Its message is not confined to one people, country, culture, or historical period; it can be applied in different circumstances.

Its clear foundational passages, principles, limits, and rulings establish the basis of divine guidance. People cannot alter these at will or give their own customs and laws precedence over them.

These foundations can be called Qur’anic principles.

Human life, however, involves more than specified rulings. People live in different countries with different languages, cultures, economic resources, natural environments, and needs. Government, economic relationships, education, clothing, food, contracts, and many other social arrangements also change over time.

The Qur’an does not prescribe one unchanging form for every detail. In many matters, it establishes the principle and its limits while leaving appropriate implementation to human understanding, reason, experience, consultation, and circumstances.

A central term here is ma‘ruf (conduct recognized as good and right). It cannot be understood adequately without its counterpart, munkar (conduct recognized and rejected as wrong), and the other harmful actions the Qur’an prohibits.

The Qur’an commands justice, honesty, trustworthiness, generosity, and goodness. It also prohibits lies, betrayal, fahsha (grossly shameful conduct), wrongdoing, aggression, malicious tale-bearing, and corruption.

Qur’anic principles therefore include both positive commands and explicit prohibitions. Both belong to the enduring standards.

Understanding recognized good and wrong requires first understanding those principles.

This study introduces some important Qur’anic principles. It does not claim to have extracted them all. Further study of the verses and their relationships can identify many additional principles and teachings.

The aim is not a definitive enumeration of the foundations of religion. It is to establish a Qur’anic basis for understanding ma‘ruf, munkar, and fahsha, and distinguishing them from society’s changing—and sometimes wrongful—customs.

Recognized good, wrongdoing, and gross indecency in Qur’anic language

Ma‘ruf comes from the Arabic root ‘-r-f, associated with knowing and recognizing. In ethical contexts, it refers to conduct recognized as good and appropriate.

Munkar, by contrast, refers to conduct whose wrongness is recognized and rejected.

Aal Imran 3:104 states:

«وَلْتَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ أُمَّةٌ يَدْعُونَ إِلَى الْخَيْرِ وَيَأْمُرُونَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَيَنْهَوْنَ عَنِ الْمُنْكَرِ»

Al-Imran 3:104

“Let there be a community among you who invite to goodness, enjoin what is right, and forbid what is wrong.”

Al-A‘raf 7:199 also says:

«وَأْمُرْ بِالْعُرْفِ»

Al-A'raf 7:199

“And enjoin what is good.”

In this Qur’anic usage, ‘urf (recognized good conduct) is connected with acknowledged goodness. In contemporary social discussion, however, the same term often means customs, habits, and practices prevalent in a community.

These meanings should not be equated without examination. A widespread practice is not necessarily good.

An-Nahl 16:90 brings fundamental commands and prohibitions together:

«إِنَّ يَأْمُرُ بِالْعَدْلِ وَالْإِحْسَانِ وَإِيتَاءِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَيَنْهَىٰ عَنِ الْفَحْشَاءِ وَالْمُنْكَرِ وَالْبَغْيِ»

An-Nahl 16:90

“The God commands justice, doing good, and giving to relatives, and forbids gross indecency, wrongdoing, and transgression.”

This passage offers one of the clearest standards for distinguishing appropriate from wrongful conduct.

Ma‘ruf concerns goodness, what is right, and appropriate conduct.

Munkar is wrongful, blameworthy conduct that people should avoid.

Fahsha concerns grave indecency and deeply shameful conduct. Al-A‘raf 7:33 prohibits such acts, whether open or hidden; Al-Isra 17:32 describes zina (sexual intercourse outside a lawful marriage) as a fahishah (a grossly indecent act).

Baghy (transgression) concerns aggression, oppression, and encroaching on others’ boundaries and rights.

These concepts overlap in some contexts but are not interchangeable. Not every wrong action can be classified as a grave indecency without evidence, just as not everything a community dislikes can be declared forbidden by God.

Al-‘Ankabut 29:45 says of Salat (the Qur’anic practice of prayer and devotion):

«إِنَّ الصَّلَاةَ تَنْهَىٰ عَنِ الْفَحْشَاءِ وَالْمُنْكَرِ»

Al-'Ankabut 29:45

“Salat restrains gross indecency and wrongdoing.”

Qur’anic guidance therefore involves more than recognizing goodness. People must also identify and avoid wrongful and harmful conduct.

We will first examine the Qur’an’s positive and prohibitory principles, then consider how they help identify recognized good, wrongdoing, and grave indecency.

Where the Qur’an uses ma‘ruf

The term appears across different areas of life:

  • Spouses’ rights and separation: Al-Baqarah 2:228, Al-Baqarah 2:229, 2:231, Al-Baqarah 2:232; At-Talaq 65:2.
  • Financial rights and provision: Al-Baqarah 2:178, 2:180, 2:233, 2:236, 2:241.
  • Guardianship of orphans: An-Nisa 4:5, An-Nisa 4:6.
  • Relations with parents and relatives: Luqman 31:15; An-Nisa 4:8.
  • Speech and social interaction: Al-Baqarah 2:235, 2:263; Al-Ahzab 33:32.
  • Consultation and cooperation: At-Talaq 65:6.
  • Social responsibility to encourage good and discourage wrong: Aal Imran 3:104; At-Tawbah 9:71; Luqman 31:17.
  • Obedience and responsibility: An-Nur 24:53; Muhammad 47:21; Al-Mumtahanah 60:12.

This range shows that ma‘ruf is more than a family custom or a way of making a payment. It is a standard of appropriate conduct across human life.

After setting out ethical commitments for believing women, Al-Mumtahanah 60:12 says:

«وَلَا يَعْصِينَكَ فِي مَعْرُوفٍ»

Al-Mumtahanah 60:12

“And that they will not disobey you in what is right.”

Even obedience to the Prophet is expressed in terms of what is right. This cannot establish any individual’s or religious institution’s right to unlimited obedience independent of divine standards.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an specifies the substance of a ruling while connecting its implementation with recognized good conduct.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:236 states:

«عَلَى الْمُوسِعِ قَدَرُهُ وَعَلَى الْمُقْتِرِ قَدَرُهُ مَتَاعًا بِالْمَعْرُوفِ»

Al-Baqarah 2:236

“The prosperous according to their means and the person of limited means according to theirs: a provision in a fitting manner.”

The woman’s entitlement is established, while the amount is assessed according to the man’s financial capacity.

Understanding ma‘ruf therefore begins with Qur’anic principles and boundaries, followed by identifying where appropriate implementation may vary with human circumstances.

Part One: Understanding the Qur’an, revelation, and religion

1. The Qur’an’s completeness and sufficiency for religious guidance#

The Qur’an presents itself as the reference for guidance. No human compilation can add independent, binding religious rulings to it in the name of completing religion.

Example: A declaration that something is permitted or forbidden should not be treated as divine merely because it appears in a religious book, without support in the Qur’an.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:114, Al-An'am 6:115; Yusuf 12:111; An-Nahl 16:89.

2. The Qur’an as clear and illuminating#

The Qur’an describes itself as a clear, illuminating book. Understanding the divine message requires engaging with revelation, rather than assuming it is intrinsically obscure and dependent on someone else’s exclusive authority.

Example: When a religious ruling is disputed, examine the relevant verses and their contexts first.

Supporting verses: Yusuf 12:1; Al-Ma'idah 5:15; An-Nahl 16:89.

3. The authority of the foundational passages#

The Qur’an calls its muhkamat (clear, foundational passages) umm al-kitab (the foundation of the Book). They provide a basis for understanding and assessing interpretations.

Example: An uncertain reading of a verse should not override an explicit command for justice or the prohibition of compulsion in religion.

Supporting verses: Al-Imran 3:7.

4. Recognizing the place of passages open to interpretation#

The existence of mutashabihat (passages whose meaning requires careful interpretation) does not license turning possible meanings into certain doctrines. The Qur’an condemns pursuing them to foment discord.

Example: A possible explanation of the isolated letters at the opening of some chapters, or of an ambiguous image, should not become a binding rule for others.

Supporting verses: Al-Imran 3:7.

5. The Qur’an’s Arabic text#

The Qur’an was revealed in Arabic. Its vocabulary, sentence structure, and context matter for accurate understanding and translation.

Example: A translator should not insert a personal interpretive explanation as though it were a word present in the Arabic text.

Supporting verses: Yusuf 12:2; Ash-Shu'ara 26:195; Az-Zukhruf 43:3.

6. Reflecting on verses and reading them together#

The Qur’an calls people to reflect on its verses and examine their coherence and connections.

Example: Do not interpret a phrase without considering the surrounding verses and uses of the same concept throughout the Qur’an.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:82; Sad 38:29; Muhammad 47:24.

7. Revelation as the authority for religious rulings#

In religion, the final standard is what God has prescribed, not an independent power of religious leaders or institutions to legislate in His name.

Example: No legal school may place its own ruling on the same level as a divine command.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:114; Al-A'raf 7:3; Ash-Shura 42:21.

8. Not attributing human inventions to God#

The Qur’an condemns composing a statement and then presenting it as a divine ruling.

Example: An author should not publish a personal claim as God’s definitive command.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:79; Al-An'am 6:93.

9. No invented permissions or prohibitions without divine authorization#

Personal taste, cultural preference, and individual interpretation do not in themselves authorize declaring something religiously permitted or forbidden.

Example: A community’s dislike of a food does not establish that God has forbidden it to everyone.

Supporting verses: Yunus 10:59; An-Nahl 16:116; Al-Ma'idah 5:87.

10. Requiring evidence and proof#

Claims to truth require evidence. Reputation, antiquity, and numbers of followers cannot replace proof.

Example: Someone calling a custom or belief divinely obligatory should identify its Qur’anic basis.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:111; Al-Anbiya 21:24; An-Naml 27:64.

11. Not speaking without knowledge#

People should not assert as certain what they do not know, especially when attributing it to God.

Example: Do not rule without evidence on religious permissions and prohibitions, people’s ultimate fate, or their intentions.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:36; Al-A'raf 7:33; Al-Baqarah 2:169.

12. Avoiding blind imitation#

Ancestry and prestige alone do not make following earlier generations or leaders a reliable way to recognize truth.

Example: An inherited custom that violates women’s rights should not continue simply because it is inherited.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:170; Al-Ma'idah 5:104; Az-Zukhruf 43:22, Az-Zukhruf 43:23, Az-Zukhruf 43:24.

13. Popularity does not establish truth#

A belief’s many adherents, or a practice’s prevalence, does not prove it right.

Example: Widespread bribery or ethnic prejudice does not make either practice recognized good conduct.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:116; Al-Ma'idah 5:100.

14. Valuing knowledge and recognizing its limits#

The Qur’an distinguishes knowledge from ignorance and calls for learning. It also recognizes the limits of human knowledge.

Example: A researcher should examine new findings and revise an earlier interpretation instead of concealing a mistake.

Supporting verses: Az-Zumar 39:9; Ta-Ha 20:114; Al-Isra 17:36.

15. The prophets’ humanity and the limits of claims to authority#

An-Nahl 16:43 and Al-Anbiya 21:7 explain that the prophets before Muhammad were also human beings who received revelation.

On that subject, they say:

«فَاسْأَلُوا أَهْلَ الذِّكْرِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ»

An-Nahl 16:43, Al-Anbiya 21:7

“Ask the people acquainted with the reminder if you do not know.”

The question in these passages concerns the humanity of earlier prophets. It is not a universal instruction to delegate understanding of every religious ruling and life decision to a religious class.

Example: No group should invoke ahl al-dhikr (people acquainted with the reminder) to claim absolute religious authority or prevent direct examination of the Qur’an.

Supporting verses: An-Nahl 16:43; Al-Anbiya 21:7.

16. Accepting all prophets without discriminatory rejection of their missions#

The Qur’an commands acceptance of God’s prophets and rejects discriminating between them in belief.

This does not mean all prophets had identical ranks or missions: the Qur’an itself speaks of some messengers being favored above others.

Example: Muhammad’s followers should not reject the missions of Moses and Jesus because they belonged to earlier communities.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:136; Al-Baqarah 2:253; Al-Baqarah 2:285.

17. A common foundation of religion and different prescribed paths#

The Qur’an connects the prophets’ missions through divine religion while acknowledging different prescribed paths and ways among communities.

Example: Differences in communities’ rites and historical regulations should not justify denying shared principles of serving God and justice.

Supporting verses: Ash-Shura 42:13; Al-Ma'idah 5:48.

18. No sectarian monopoly on salvation#

The Qur’an challenges claims that Paradise belongs exclusively to one group, emphasizing faith, submission to God, doing good, and righteous action.

Example: No community should claim exclusive salvation merely because of its religious name.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:62; Al-Baqarah 2:111, Al-Baqarah 2:112; An-Nisa 4:123, An-Nisa 4:124.

19. Avoiding sectarian division and hostility#

Differences in interpretation should not turn religion into mutually hostile, prejudiced factions.

Example: Religious affiliation should not justify hostility or violations of other communities’ rights.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:159; Ash-Shura 42:13; Ar-Rum 30:31, Ar-Rum 30:32.

Part Two: Faith, devotion, and personal development

20. God’s oneness#

The God is one; no being is His equal.

Example: No person, institution, or power should be given independent divine standing.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:163; Al-Ikhlas 112:1, Al-Ikhlas 112:2, Al-Ikhlas 112:3, Al-Ikhlas 112:4.

21. Not associating partners with God#

Obedience, love, and loyalty to individuals or groups must not take precedence over truth and God’s command.

Example: Family prosperity or a leader’s approval should not be used to justify bribery and oppression.

Supporting verses: Luqman 31:13; Al-Baqarah 2:165; An-Nisa 4:48.

22. Faith in God and the Hereafter, joined with righteous action#

Faith is more than a religious label. It must accompany responsibility before God and appropriate conduct.

Example: Someone violating people’s rights should not rely on their sect’s name as a defense.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:62; Al-Ma'idah 5:69; An-Nisa 4:124.

23. Keeping covenants, agreements, and promises#

Keeping commitments is fundamental to Qur’anic ethics. Valid contracts, covenants, and promises should be honored: trust depends on honesty and fulfilling obligations.

The Qur’an states:

«وَأَوْفُوا بِالْعَهْدِ إِنَّ الْعَهْدَ كَانَ مَسْئُولًا»

Al-Isra 17:34

“Fulfill your commitments; you will be questioned about them.”

Example: A promise to perform work, pay money, or marry should not be used to deceive through false assurances or broken commitments. If circumstances change, explain honestly and respect rights arising from the commitment.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:34; Al-Ma'idah 5:1; An-Nahl 16:91; Maryam 19:54.

24. Sincerity#

Goodness and devotion should rest on sincere intentions, not display or a desire for fame.

Example: Help people in need to meet their needs, rather than to publicize the donor’s name.

Supporting verses: Al-Bayyinah 98:5; Al-Insan 76:8, Al-Insan 76:9.

25. Avoiding hypocrisy and double dealing#

Public professions of faith, goodness, and goodwill should not conceal deception, betrayal, and contrary conduct. The Qur’an identifies this split as a feature of hypocrisy.

Example: Someone should not publicly praise justice and devotion while secretly planning to take others’ rights.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:8, Al-Baqarah 2:9; An-Nisa 4:142; As-Saff 61:2, As-Saff 61:3.

26. Maintaining Salat#

Maintaining Salat (prayer and devotion) is a fundamental Qur’anic command, connected with remembering God and avoiding gross indecency and wrongdoing. Ritual prayer is one practical expression of it.

Example: Salat should not be confined to outward observance while daily conduct disregards others’ rights.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:43; Ta-Ha 20:14; Al-'Ankabut 29:45.

27. Giving zakat and pursuing purification#

Zakat (giving associated with purification and growth) has an important place alongside Salat and social responsibility.

Example: Wealth and ability should contribute to a cleaner, better life rather than become instruments of greed and selfishness.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:43; At-Tawbah 9:103; Ash-Shams 91:9.

28. Fasting and self-restraint#

The Qur’an connects siyam (deliberate abstention, including prescribed fasting) with attaining mindful responsibility before God. Restraint should help people govern their desires and conduct.

Example: Anger, sexual desire, greed, or improper speech should not overwhelm deliberate self-control.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:183; Maryam 19:26.

29. Pilgrimage and respect for sacred observances#

Hajj (pilgrimage to the Sacred House) is a Qur’anic command, joined with mindfulness, respect for others’ rights, and avoidance of corruption and quarrelling.

Example: Pilgrimage rites should not become instruments of discrimination, exploitation, or depriving people of safety.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:196, Al-Baqarah 2:197; Al-Imran 3:97; Al-Hajj 22:27, Al-Hajj 22:28.

30. Remembering God#

Awareness of God should remain alive in thought and conduct.

Example: When deciding about another person’s property or rights, remember responsibility before God.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:152; Ar-Ra'd 13:28; Al-Ahzab 33:41.

31. Mindful responsibility#

Taqwa (vigilant self-restraint before God) concerns guarding oneself against crossing divine boundaries.

Example: Do not violate someone’s rights even in private, when no one else knows.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:2; Al-Ma'idah 5:8; At-Taghabun 64:16.

32. Restraining personal desire#

Human wants and inclinations should not become the standard of truth and justice.

Example: A judge should not issue an unjust ruling because of personal affection or hostility.

Supporting verses: An-Nazi'at 79:40, An-Nazi'at 79:41; An-Nisa 4:135; Al-Jathiyah 45:23.

33. Avoiding gross indecency and wrongdoing#

People should avoid shameful conduct, open or hidden. The standard is the Qur’an’s commands and prohibitions, not merely society’s likes and dislikes.

Example: A wrongful practice does not become good merely because it is widespread.

Supporting verses: An-Nahl 16:90; Al-A'raf 7:33; Al-'Ankabut 29:45.

34. Persevering in what is right#

Sabr (steadfastness and self-restraint) involves more than enduring pain. It includes remaining committed to truth and governing oneself under pressure.

Example: Fear of family or society should not lead someone to conceal a truth they have recognized.

Supporting verses: Al-'Asr 103:3; Al-Imran 3:200.

35. Repentance and repair#

Turning away from error should include correcting conduct and its consequences.

Example: An employee who violates someone’s rights should acknowledge the wrong and work to restore those rights.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:160; Al-Ma'idah 5:39.

36. Gratitude#

People should recognize blessings and use them responsibly.

Example: Knowledge, wealth, and power should not be treated merely as means of superiority over others.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:152; Ibrahim 14:7.

37. Not despairing of divine mercy#

After wrongdoing, a person should not regard the way back to reform as closed.

Example: Someone who regrets harmful behavior should work to improve their life.

Supporting verses: Az-Zumar 39:53; Yusuf 12:87.

38. Ease in religion#

God did not establish religion to impose artificial and unnecessary hardship.

Example: Practices and details that the Qur’an does not require should not be imposed on everyone as religious obligations.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:185; Al-Hajj 22:78.

39. Responsibility according to capacity#

Responsibility should be assessed against a person’s actual abilities and circumstances.

Example: Do not demand payments beyond a poor person’s means.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:233; Al-Baqarah 2:286; At-Talaq 65:7.

40. Recognizing necessity#

In specified circumstances of necessity, the Qur’an allows exceptions to prohibitions within the limits of that necessity.

Example: Someone compelled to eat otherwise prohibited food to survive should not be held accountable for acting within the specified exception.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:173; Al-Ma'idah 5:3; An-Nahl 16:115.

41. Distinguishing intention, mistake, and coercion#

The Qur’an distinguishes deliberate actions from unintended errors and treats coercion as a distinct circumstance.

Example: An unintentional mistake should not automatically be equated with a deliberate offense.

Supporting verses: Al-Ahzab 33:5; An-Nahl 16:106.

42. Avoiding invented austerity and monastic restrictions#

People should not forbid themselves lawful blessings without reason or present humanly invented deprivation as divine law.

Example: Withdrawal from ordinary life should not be declared religiously obligatory without Qur’anic support.

Supporting verses: Al-Hadid 57:27; Al-Ma'idah 5:87; Al-A'raf 7:32.

43. Avoiding arrogance#

Wealth, position, knowledge, and ancestry should not lead to contempt for others.

Example: A person in authority should not humiliate others for poverty or lack of education.

Supporting verses: Luqman 31:18, Luqman 31:19; Al-Qasas 28:83.

44. Avoiding empty and harmful pursuits#

Life should not be consumed by pointless, harmful words and activities.

Example: Do not devote time to spreading rumors, carrying tales, or intruding unnecessarily into others’ lives.

Supporting verses: Al-Mu'minun 23:3; Al-Furqan 25:72.

Part Three: Freedom, dignity, and human rights

45. Freedom of belief#

People can choose to accept or reject faith. A difference of belief alone does not justify violating their rights.

Example: Someone should not lose their human rights for rejecting their family’s religious beliefs.

Supporting verses: Al-Kahf 18:29; Al-Kafirun 109:6.

46. No compulsion in religion#

Faith cannot be imposed through threats, force, imprisonment, or social pressure.

Example: No government or religious institution should force people to accept a religious belief.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:256; Yunus 10:99.

47. Human dignity#

Human beings possess dignity and should not be demeaned because of poverty, ancestry, gender, or social position.

Example: Treat a poor worker as a person with rights and dignity.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:70; Al-Hujurat 49:13.

48. Diversity in creation#

Differences of language, color, ethnicity, and nation are signs of creation, not reasons for hostility or claims of superiority.

Example: A community’s language and culture should not deprive it of education or public services.

Supporting verses: Ar-Rum 30:22; Al-Hujurat 49:13.

49. Rejecting discrimination and inherited superiority#

The Qur’an identifies mindful responsibility before God, rather than wealth, ethnicity, or ancestry, as the measure of standing with Him.

Example: Do not deny a qualified person a public position in favor of someone chosen through family or ethnic connections.

Supporting verses: Al-Hujurat 49:13; An-Nisa 4:1; Al-An'am 6:52.

50. Women’s and men’s moral responsibility#

The Qur’an addresses women and men alike in faith, good conduct, and ethical responsibility.

Example: A society should not demand honesty and fidelity from women while treating men’s betrayal as normal.

Supporting verses: Al-Ahzab 33:35; Al-Imran 3:195.

51. Individual responsibility for wrongdoing#

No person bears another person’s guilt or offense.

Example: An offender’s family, children, or ethnic community should not be punished for that individual’s crime.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:164; An-Najm 53:38.

52. The sanctity of human life#

Human life deserves protection; taking it without right is a grave violation.

Example: Religious or family hostility must not become a pretext for killing.

Supporting verses: Al-Ma'idah 5:32; Al-Isra 17:33.

53. Protecting one’s own life and health#

People should not knowingly bring about their own destruction or treat their lives as worthless.

Example: Do not carelessly expose yourself and your companions to an obvious danger.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:29; Al-Baqarah 2:195.

54. Protecting children’s lives#

Poverty, fear of the future, and a preference for sons over daughters do not justify killing a child.

Example: A family should not devalue a child’s life because of sex or financial anxiety.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:151; Al-Isra 17:31; At-Takwir 81:8, At-Takwir 81:9.

55. Sexual integrity and freedom from coercion#

People must not violate others’ sexual boundaries or use power to force sexual relations.

Example: No one should threaten or compel a woman into sex or sexual exploitation.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:32; An-Nur 24:33; An-Nisa 4:19.

56. Protecting others’ reputations#

Ridicule, slander, defamation, and spreading unsupported accusations conflict with Qur’anic ethics.

Example: After a couple separates, their families should not smear each other to justify the divorce.

Supporting verses: Al-Hujurat 49:11; An-Nur 24:4; An-Nur 24:15.

57. Avoiding backbiting#

The Qur’an prohibits backbiting and depicts it through a strongly condemnatory image. People should not discuss another person’s faults or private affairs in their absence in ways that damage their reputation or humiliate them.

Example: Colleagues should not turn an absent coworker’s personal shortcomings and family life into entertainment.

Supporting verses: Al-Hujurat 49:12.

58. Avoiding malicious tale-bearing#

The Qur’an condemns someone who circulates among people carrying malicious tales:

«هَمَّازٍ مَشَّاءٍ بِنَمِيمٍ»

Al-Qalam 68:11

“A faultfinder who goes about spreading malicious gossip.”

Passing on private remarks to provoke hostility, create division, or damage relationships is incompatible with recognized good conduct.

Example: Do not carry a wife’s words to her husband, or one family’s words to another, for the purpose of creating resentment and hostility.

Supporting verses: Al-Qalam 68:10, Al-Qalam 68:11; Al-Humazah 104:1.

59. Not spying into others’ affairs#

The Qur’an explicitly says:

«وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا»

Al-Hujurat 49:12

“And do not spy.”

People should not secretly search others’ lives to uncover faults, secrets, or private matters.

Example: Do not inspect another person’s phone, messages, correspondence, or private relationships without consent.

Supporting verses: Al-Hujurat 49:12.

60. Seeking permission before entering others’ homes#

An-Nur 24:27 instructs people to make their presence known and greet the occupants before entering another person’s home.

An-Nur 24:28 also requires returning if permission is withheld and the visitor is told to leave.

Example: Even a close relative should not enter another family’s home or private space without permission, or insist if the occupant cannot receive them.

Supporting verses: An-Nur 24:27, An-Nur 24:28; An-Nur 24:58, An-Nur 24:59.

61. Entering houses through their doors#

Al-Baqarah 2:189 states:

«وَأْتُوا الْبُيُوتَ مِنْ أَبْوَابِهَا»

Al-Baqarah 2:189

“Enter houses through their doors.”

The verse contrasts entering houses from behind with using their doors and connects this with mindfulness of God. A social lesson can also be drawn: pursue aims through clear and proper means, rather than concealed, wrongful routes that circumvent others’ rights.

Example: Use proper, transparent procedures for administrative business or asserting a right, rather than bribery and favoritism.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:189.

62. Freedom to listen and engage in respectful discussion#

People should be able to hear different views, examine evidence, and make informed choices.

Example: Questions about a religious interpretation should not be answered with threats and silencing.

Supporting verses: Az-Zumar 39:18; An-Nahl 16:125; Al-'Ankabut 29:46.

63. Not mocking other religions or insulting what others hold sacred#

The Qur’an even prohibits insulting those invoked besides The God:

«وَلَا تَسُبُّوا الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ »

Al-An'am 6:108

“Do not insult those they invoke besides The God.”

Disagreement does not authorize ridicule, abuse, or humiliation. A belief may be criticized with evidence, but defending one’s own beliefs does not justify insults and inciting hostility.

Example: Members of one religion should not mock the sacred objects, worship, and revered figures of another.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:108; Al-Hujurat 49:11; An-Nahl 16:125.

64. Protecting churches, synagogues, mosques, and other places of worship#

Al-Hajj 22:40 states:

«وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ كَثِيرًا»

Al-Hajj 22:40

“Had The God not checked some people by means of others, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which the name of The God is frequently remembered would have been demolished.”

The verse names more than mosques. Defense against aggression and destruction of places of worship should not be restricted to the worship spaces of one religion.

Example: If a group intends to destroy a church, synagogue, or mosque out of religious hostility, society should protect the worshippers and their place of worship.

Supporting verses: Al-Hajj 22:39, Al-Hajj 22:40; Al-Baqarah 2:114.

65. Migration to preserve life and dignity#

Where freedom and safety are absent, people may migrate to protect their lives and dignity. Society must also consider actual limits on their ability to travel and available routes.

Example: A family living under threats and persecution may move to a safer environment.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:97, An-Nisa 4:98, An-Nisa 4:99, An-Nisa 4:100; Al-'Ankabut 29:56.

66. Granting refuge and bringing a person to safety#

The Qur’an commands protection and safe passage for someone who requests refuge.

Example: A person from an opposing group should not be denied safety merely because of group affiliation.

Supporting verses: At-Tawbah 9:6.

67. Freeing people from bondage and slavery#

The Qur’an identifies freeing people from bondage as valuable and establishes ways of releasing those held in it.

Example: A person should not be treated as another’s tradable property without rights.

Supporting verses: Al-Balad 90:13; An-Nur 24:33; Al-Baqarah 2:177.

68. Respect, kindness, and justice toward followers of other religions#

Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 states:

«لَا يَنْهَاكُمُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَلَمْ يُخْرِجُوكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ أَنْ تَبَرُّوهُمْ وَتُقْسِطُوا إِلَيْهِمْ إِنَّ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ»

Al-Mumtahanah 60:8

“The God does not forbid you to show kindness and justice to those who have not fought you because of religion or driven you from your homes. The God loves those who act justly.”

Respect for rights does not depend on agreement in belief. People can disagree with a religion and still treat its followers fairly and justly.

Example: Religious affiliation should not produce discrimination in access to justice, work, education, or social rights.

Supporting verses: Al-Mumtahanah 60:8; Al-'Ankabut 29:46; Al-Ma'idah 5:8.

Part Four: Justice, government, trusts, and financial rights

69. Justice#

Justice must be upheld even against oneself or one’s relatives.

Example: A judge should not rule unjustly in favor of a relative.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:135; An-Nahl 16:90.

70. Judging by truth and fairness#

Government and courts should act on truth, justice, and proper consideration of people’s rights.

Example: A judgment should rest on evidence and rights rather than either party’s wealth or political influence.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:58; Sad 38:26.

71. Rejecting tyranny and the humiliation of people#

Political power should not secure obedience through humiliation, fear, and deception.

Example: A ruler who claims sole authority to determine the public interest and prevents questioning or criticism illustrates this abuse.

Supporting verses: Al-Qasas 28:4; Az-Zukhruf 43:54; Ghafir 40:29.

72. Trustworthiness and competence in leadership#

Public responsibility should not be assigned solely through ancestry, wealth, or factional loyalty.

Example: Entrust an office to a competent, trustworthy person rather than an unqualified relative.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:58; Al-Baqarah 2:124; Yusuf 12:55.

73. Consultation and participation in shared affairs#

Collective decisions should involve consultation and responsibility.

Example: Assess community views and needs before adopting a project that affects people’s lives.

Supporting verses: Ash-Shura 42:38; Al-Imran 3:159.

74. Honesty and avoidance of lies#

Speech should be truthful and sound; lies should not be used to deceive.

Example: A seller should not misrepresent a product’s quality.

Supporting verses: At-Tawbah 9:119; Al-Ahzab 33:70; Al-Hajj 22:30.

75. Not concealing truth#

People should not knowingly conceal a truth whose disclosure is necessary to protect a right.

Example: A witness should not hide what they know to protect a friend.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:42; Al-Baqarah 2:283; Al-Imran 3:187.

76. Truthful, impartial testimony#

Testimony should be given for God and grounded in truth, not advantage, kinship, or hostility.

Example: A witness should speak truthfully in court even when it harms a powerful person’s interests.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:135; Al-Ma'idah 5:8.

77. Honoring trusts#

What is entrusted should be returned to the person entitled to it.

Example: A public official should not treat public money as private property.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:58; Al-Mu'minun 23:8.

78. Avoiding betrayal#

People should not break others’ trust for personal gain.

Example: An employee should not use confidential information to blackmail people.

Supporting verses: Al-Anfal 8:27; An-Nisa 4:105, An-Nisa 4:106, An-Nisa 4:107.

79. Checking reports before acting#

Information should be examined before judgments or actions are based on it.

Example: A manager should not punish an employee on the basis of a rumor.

Supporting verses: Al-Hujurat 49:6; An-Nisa 4:83.

80. Prohibiting bribery and judicial corruption#

Money should not be used to obtain an unjust judgment or take another person’s rights.

Example: Do not change a court case’s outcome through a bribe.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:188; Al-Ma'idah 5:42.

81. Not stealing#

Wrongfully taking others’ property, including public property, violates their rights.

Example: An employee should not take government assets for personal use.

Supporting verses: Al-Ma'idah 5:38; Al-Mumtahanah 60:12.

82. Respect for others’ property rights#

No one may take another person’s property through deception, force, or abuse of power.

Example: Do not seize someone’s land or home through forged documents.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:29; Al-Baqarah 2:188.

83. Consent and fairness in transactions#

Transactions should involve genuine consent and avoid wrongful appropriation.

Example: Disclose a significant defect so that the buyer’s consent does not rest on false information.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:29; Hud 11:85.

84. Giving full measure#

Measures, weights, and the assessment of entitlements should be accurate.

Example: A seller should not deliver less than the promised quantity.

Supporting verses: Al-Mutaffifin 83:1, Al-Mutaffifin 83:2, Al-Mutaffifin 83:3; Al-Isra 17:35.

85. Clarifying and recording financial commitments#

Financial rights and obligations should, as far as possible, be clear and verifiable.

Example: Record loans and important contracts clearly to reduce opportunities for dispute.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:282, Al-Baqarah 2:283.

86. Women’s and men’s rights in what they earn#

The Qur’an speaks of a share for both women and men in what they earn.

Example: Marriage should not allow a husband or his family to take a woman’s earnings and property without right.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:32; An-Najm 53:39.

87. Employment agreements and workers’ rights#

Cooperation and employment should rest on clear agreement, trustworthiness, and respect for both parties’ rights.

Example: An employer should not unilaterally change agreed terms to a worker’s disadvantage.

Supporting verses: Al-Qasas 28:25, Al-Qasas 28:26, Al-Qasas 28:27, Al-Qasas 28:28; Al-Ma'idah 5:1.

88. Avoiding riba#

People should not exploit financial need through riba (prohibited increases imposed on debt).

Example: A debtor should not be trapped in a mechanism that continuously and unjustly increases the debt.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:275; Al-Baqarah 2:278, Al-Baqarah 2:279, Al-Baqarah 2:280.

89. Preventing public wealth from being confined to the rich#

In distributing fay’ (property coming under communal authority without battle), the Qur’an prohibits its circulation solely among the wealthy.

Example: Public resources should not be distributed only among a small influential group.

Supporting verses: Al-Hashr 59:7.

90. Proportionate justice and fair pursuit of wrongdoing#

An offense should be addressed with justice, attention to responsibility, and limits on unrestrained revenge.

Example: After a killing, the accused person’s innocent relatives should not become targets of retaliation.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:178, Al-Baqarah 2:179; Al-Isra 17:33.

91. Pardon and forgiveness#

Alongside the right to seek justice, forgiveness and reform remain possible.

Example: A person entitled to redress may freely forgive, without being forced by others to surrender their right.

Supporting verses: Ash-Shura 42:40; An-Nur 24:22.

92. Protecting orphans’ rights#

The property and rights of children without parental protection must be faithfully safeguarded.

Example: A guardian should not appropriate an orphan’s inheritance for personal use.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:2; An-Nisa 4:10; Al-An'am 6:152.

93. Giving time to a debtor in hardship#

Genuine financial inability should not become an excuse to humiliate a debtor or destroy their life.

Example: A creditor should allow a struggling debtor time and may choose to forgive part of the debt.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:280.

Part Five: Social responsibility, reform, and generosity

94. Individual responsibility toward society#

People should contribute to society’s good and improvement, rather than consider only private interests.

Example: A citizen who learns of corruption should work to address it through proper means, within their ability.

Supporting verses: Al-'Asr 103:3; Al-Imran 3:104.

95. Society’s responsibility toward individuals#

Society should not abandon deprived, oppressed, or unprotected people.

Example: Cooperate to support struggling families and children without parental care.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:177; An-Nisa 4:75.

96. Supporting people made vulnerable#

Social power and resources should also help rescue people unable to defend their own rights.

Example: Women and children living under violence and threats should not be left without support.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:75; An-Nisa 4:127.

97. Striving and standing up for what is right#

People should stand against oppression and deprivation of rights and work for justice.

Example: Take proper, proportionate steps to prevent oppression of a defenseless group.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:75; Ash-Shura 42:41.

98. No aggression, even toward enemies#

Defending rights and resisting oppression do not authorize transgression or unlimited revenge.

Example: Hostility toward a group should not justify attacks on innocent people.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:190; Al-Ma'idah 5:8.

99. Peace and reconciliation#

Where a genuine path to peace and resolution exists, hostility should not continue without reason.

Example: After resolving a dispute, two families should seek reconciliation rather than perpetuate resentment.

Supporting verses: Al-Anfal 8:61; Al-Hujurat 49:9, Al-Hujurat 49:10.

100. Cooperation in good, not wrongdoing#

Social cooperation should serve goodness and mindful responsibility, not corruption and aggression.

Example: An employee should not join a bribery or embezzlement network even if it is financially rewarding.

Supporting verses: Al-Ma'idah 5:2.

101. Encouraging right and discouraging wrong#

People should invite others toward goodness and reform rather than remain indifferent to wrongdoing. This responsibility does not authorize coercion of belief, spying, or violating rights.

Example: Criticize administrative corruption with clear evidence, rather than rumors or intrusion into private lives.

Supporting verses: Al-Imran 3:104; At-Tawbah 9:71; Al-Baqarah 2:256; Al-Hujurat 49:12.

102. Preventing corruption#

Corruption in administration, the economy, government, and society should be identified and prevented.

Example: Awarding public contracts to unqualified relatives should not be justified as public service.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:205; Al-Qasas 28:77.

103. Consistency between words and actions#

People should not call others to goodness while behaving in the opposite way.

Example: An official responsible for fighting corruption should not enjoy unjust privileges.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:44; As-Saff 61:2, As-Saff 61:3.

104. Speaking well#

Speech should be appropriate and free from humiliation and harm.

Example: Address disagreement through respectful discussion rather than insults and abuse.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:83; Al-Isra 17:53.

105. Giving from one’s resources#

People should also use what they possess to meet others’ needs and promote the common good.

Example: Someone with resources should devote part of them to supporting people in need.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:219; Al-Baqarah 2:261; Al-Hadid 57:7.

106. Giving without humiliation or reminders of favors#

Assistance should not destroy a recipient’s dignity or reputation.

Example: A donor should not publicize humiliating images of a recipient for self-promotion.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:262, Al-Baqarah 2:263, Al-Baqarah 2:264; Al-Insan 76:9.

107. Kindness and mercy#

Human relationships should be marked by compassion and mercy.

Example: Treat children, older people, and those in hardship kindly.

Supporting verses: Al-Balad 90:17; Ar-Rum 30:21.

108. Kindness to parents#

Respect and kindness toward parents are important Qur’anic commands. Obedience to them must nevertheless not lead to associating partners with God or disobeying truth.

Example: A child may reject a parent’s beliefs while continuing to treat the parent well.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:23, Al-Isra 17:24; Luqman 31:15.

109. Family ties and relatives’ rights#

Family relationships should involve kindness, support, and respect for rights.

Example: A family should not abandon a divorced daughter in the name of reputation or tradition.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:1; Al-Isra 17:26; An-Nahl 16:90.

110. Neighbors’ rights#

Kindness and social responsibility begin with one’s closest relationships.

Example: Do not undermine neighbors’ safety, peace, or rights.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:36.

111. Caring for people who are hungry or in need#

Indifference to hunger and deprivation conflicts with Qur’anic social responsibility.

Example: A community should act to secure food and basic necessities for poor families.

Supporting verses: Al-Insan 76:8; Al-Ma'un 107:1, Al-Ma'un 107:2, Al-Ma'un 107:3; Al-Balad 90:14, Al-Balad 90:15, Al-Balad 90:16.

112. Generosity beyond the minimum obligation#

People should not settle only for the minimum owed; where appropriate, they should also show generosity and grace.

Example: During separation, protect a spouse’s dignity and reputation as well as fulfilling financial rights.

Supporting verses: An-Nahl 16:90; Al-Baqarah 2:237; Ar-Rahman 55:60.

113. Not obstructing others’ good choices and reconciliation#

Interference, prejudice, and obstruction should not close off opportunities to repair relationships or make sound decisions.

Example: Relatives or administrative staff should not obstruct a mutually desired marriage through rumors or invented bureaucracy.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:224; Al-Baqarah 2:232.

Part Six: Family, marriage, and mutual rights

114. Consent in marriage#

Marriage should not rest on coercion by relatives or anyone else.

Example: A family should not force a daughter or widow to marry against her wishes.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:232; An-Nisa 4:19.

115. Marriage as a solemn commitment#

The Qur’an describes marriage as mithaqan ghalizan (a solemn, binding covenant). It is more than an outward contract or a temporary relationship pursued for personal advantage; it entails mutual rights, commitments, and responsibilities.

An-Nisa 4:21 states:

«وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا»

An-Nisa 4:21

“And they have taken from you a solemn covenant.”

Example: Both partners should enter marriage honestly, avoiding sham or deceptive arrangements and exploitation of each other’s rights.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:21; Al-Ma'idah 5:1; Ar-Rum 30:21.

116. Avoiding sexual relations outside marriage#

The Qur’an forbids approaching zina (sexual intercourse outside lawful marriage) and distinguishes marital union from sifah (sexual relations outside that covenant).

«وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا الزِّنَىٰ إِنَّهُ كَانَ فَاحِشَةً وَسَاءَ سَبِيلًا»

Al-Isra 17:32

“Do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse; it is a grossly indecent act and an evil way.”

Example: Do not pursue a secret sexual relationship outside marriage to satisfy desire, or disregard the other person’s rights through deception.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:32; An-Nisa 4:24, An-Nisa 4:25; Al-Ma'idah 5:5.

117. Not taking secret sexual partners#

An-Nisa 4:25 speaks of women not taking secret lovers, and Al-Ma’idah 5:5 uses the corresponding expression about men.

In these contexts, akhdan (secret sexual partners) concerns hidden sexual relationships contrasted with an appropriate marital bond, not every private friendship or acquaintance.

The Qur’an therefore condemns, alongside sexual relations outside marriage, secret arrangements designed to evade marital responsibilities and establish sexual relationships outside that covenant.

Example: A married person should not establish another secret sexual relationship or betray their spouse and the other party through concealment.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:25; Al-Ma'idah 5:5.

118. Respecting existing marriages and commitments before marriage#

An-Nisa 4:24 includes already-married women among those whom one may not marry. Al-Baqarah 2:235 also prohibits concluding a marriage contract before the prescribed waiting period ends.

An existing marital covenant or a period during which a new contract is prohibited cannot simply be disregarded to establish another marriage.

An engagement without a marriage contract is not itself marriage. But promises and commitments made by the partners should be governed by honesty and fidelity, without deception or harmful breaches of trust.

Example: Do not enter a secret relationship with a married person, conclude marriage with a woman during her prescribed waiting period, or dishonestly disregard valid promises during an engagement.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:24; Al-Baqarah 2:235; Al-Isra 17:34; Al-Ma'idah 5:1.

119. The marriage gift and women’s financial rights#

Mahr (the marriage gift owed to the woman) is one of her financial rights. A man cannot arbitrarily reclaim it.

Example: After separation, a husband should not demand back property given to his wife without a valid entitlement.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:4; An-Nisa 4:20, An-Nisa 4:21; Al-Baqarah 2:229.

120. Living together in a fitting manner#

Spouses should respect one another’s rights and dignity in shared life.

Example: A husband should not regard his wife solely as someone who serves and obeys while disregarding her rights.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:19; Al-Baqarah 2:228.

121. Continuing marriage appropriately or separating with generosity#

Both remaining together and separating should be conducted well.

Example: An incompatible couple should not respond to separation by attacking each other’s character and reputation.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:229; Al-Baqarah 2:231; At-Talaq 65:2.

122. Not harming a spouse or child#

Family rights should not become instruments of revenge and pressure.

Example: After divorce, neither parent should use a child to hurt the former spouse.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:231; Al-Baqarah 2:233; At-Talaq 65:6.

123. The rights of a nursing mother and child#

Divorce does not end parental responsibility toward a child.

Example: A father should provide the prescribed food and clothing for the nursing mother and meet the child’s needs according to his capacity.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:233; At-Talaq 65:6, At-Talaq 65:7.

124. Consultation and resolving family disputes#

Family disagreements should, as far as possible, be addressed through discussion, consultation, mediation, and respect for both parties’ rights.

Example: Parents should decide about nursing through mutual agreement and consultation, and seek resolution of marital disputes.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:233; An-Nisa 4:35.

125. Respecting inheritance rights#

Heirs’ rights should be honored within the boundaries prescribed by the Qur’an.

Example: A daughter should not lose her inheritance merely because of an ethnic custom.

Supporting verses: An-Nisa 4:7; An-Nisa 4:11, An-Nisa 4:12.

126. Observing prescribed waiting periods#

Where the Qur’an establishes an ‘iddah (a specified waiting period before remarriage), its limits and conditions should be respected.

Example: Do not arbitrarily abolish that period or extend it into years of deprivation in the name of religion.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:228; Al-Baqarah 2:234; At-Talaq 65:4; Al-Ahzab 33:49.

127. A widow’s choice about her future#

After the prescribed period ends, a widow may decide appropriately about her future life.

Example: Her late husband’s family should not prevent remarriage in the name of reputation or loyalty.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:234; An-Nisa 4:19.

Part Seven: Healthy living, moderation, and responsibility for the earth

128. Enjoying good and wholesome provisions#

People should benefit from clean, fitting blessings rather than turn cultural preferences into religious rulings without evidence.

Example: A food disliked in one culture should not be declared forbidden to everyone for that reason alone.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:168; Al-A'raf 7:32.

129. Cleanliness#

The Qur’an attends to cleanliness. Al-Muddaththir 74:4 says:

«وَثِيَابَكَ فَطَهِّرْ»

Al-Muddaththir 74:4

“And purify your garments.”

Clean clothing, bodies, and living environments are appropriate human practices connected with health, respect for others, and an orderly life.

Example: Keep clothes, homes, and public spaces clean; do not expose others to harm through contamination or neglect of hygiene.

Supporting verses: Al-Muddaththir 74:4; Al-A'raf 7:31; At-Tawbah 9:108.

130. Purification#

Taharah (purification) is more than everyday cleanliness in the Qur’an. It also has specific provisions concerning bodily states and preparation for worship. Al-Ma’idah 5:6 discusses washing and wiping specified parts of the body, purification from janabah (a state requiring purification after sexual activity or discharge), and tayammum (purification using clean earth) in specified circumstances.

Elsewhere, purification also concerns cleanness and freedom from defilement. These usages should not all be reduced to one narrow meaning.

Example: Follow the Qur’an’s provisions for purification associated with worship while distinguishing them from additional human ceremonies.

Supporting verses: Al-Ma'idah 5:6; Al-Baqarah 2:222; At-Tawbah 9:108.

131. Avoiding intoxicants and gambling#

People should not treat obscuring reason or earning through maysir (gambling or games of chance for gain) as a desirable way to live or make a living.

Example: Do not subject a family to harm and deprivation through addiction or gambling for income.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:219; Al-Ma'idah 5:90, Al-Ma'idah 5:91.

132. Avoiding excess and waste#

Enjoying blessings should not become overconsumption or waste of resources.

Example: Do not needlessly discard wholesome food while others face food shortages.

Supporting verses: Al-A'raf 7:31; Al-Isra 17:26, Al-Isra 17:27.

133. Avoiding corrupting luxury#

Wealth and comfort should not lead to arrogance, corruption, or resistance to social improvement.

Example: A wealthy group should not obstruct justice and economic reform to preserve its privileges.

Supporting verses: Al-Isra 17:16; Saba 34:34; Az-Zukhruf 43:23.

134. Moderation in consumption and spending#

People should avoid both miserliness and extravagance.

Example: A family should not incur crushing debt to display wealth at a wedding.

Supporting verses: Al-Furqan 25:67; Al-Isra 17:29.

135. Preventing corruption and destruction of the earth#

People should not destroy the living environment of humans and other creatures for short-term gain.

Example: A factory should not ruin a community’s water and soil by releasing pollutants.

Supporting verses: Al-A'raf 7:56; Ar-Rum 30:41.

136. Protecting crops, future generations, and life’s resources#

Human policies and activities should not destroy the resources sustaining life and future generations.

Example: Financial profit alone should not justify a project that destroys people’s farmland and water supplies.

Supporting verses: Al-Baqarah 2:205.

137. Recognizing animals’ place in creation#

The Qur’an describes animals as communities like human beings and gives a specific command not to harm Salih’s she-camel. These passages provide a basis for responsibility toward other creatures.

Example: Animals should not be harmed unnecessarily merely for entertainment.

Supporting verses: Al-An'am 6:38; Al-A'raf 7:73.

Recognizing good and wrong through Qur’anic principles

This collection shows that the Qur’an contains both positive commands and clear, fundamental prohibitions.

It commands worship of one God and forbids associating partners with Him; commands honesty and condemns lies; commands trustworthiness and forbids betrayal; commands justice and prohibits oppression and aggression; commands sexual integrity and forbids sexual relations outside marriage, promiscuity, and secret sexual partnerships.

These commands and prohibitions are complementary sides of an ethical standard.

Recognized good cannot be understood without understanding wrong. Recognizing wrong must itself rest on Qur’anic standards rather than prejudice and personal taste.

Some principles concern what people should do: justice, honesty, trustworthiness, and generosity.

Others concern what people should not do: lying, betrayal, backbiting, malicious tale-bearing, spying, aggression, corruption, and coercion.

Another group concerns how to understand and judge religious claims: the authority of clear foundational passages, reflection, demanding evidence, and avoiding claims without knowledge.

Others are specific provisions for particular situations, such as inheritance, waiting periods, marriage, and limits on transactions.

These are not all the same kind of principle or ruling. They share one essential point: human customs, tastes, and agreements cannot take precedence over a clear Qur’anic command.

At the same time, the Qur’an does not fix every detail of life in an identical form for everyone.

Justice is a Qur’anic principle, but ways of organizing courts, recording documents, and hearing complaints can change over time.

Keeping commitments is an enduring principle, but contract forms, registration procedures, and tools of proof can change with knowledge and technology.

Spouses’ rights remain, while ceremonies, gifts, household arrangements, and ways of carrying out daily responsibilities may vary by agreement and circumstances.

Cleanliness remains a principle, while tools and methods of hygiene change with knowledge and resources.

In these areas, people may develop appropriate methods through reason, experience, understanding, and consultation, provided they do not violate Qur’anic principles and boundaries.

How to identify grave indecency and wrongdoing

Al-A‘raf 7:33 states:

«قُلْ إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ رَبِّيَ الْفَوَاحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ وَالْإِثْمَ وَالْبَغْيَ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ»

Al-A'raf 7:33

“Say: My Lord has forbidden gross indecencies, whether open or hidden, sin, and unjust transgression.”

Wrongfulness is not confined to what others can see. Conduct may appear respectable while concealing deception, betrayal, or violation of another person’s rights.

Someone may publicly praise honesty while lying privately, claim family loyalty while maintaining a secret sexual relationship, or advocate justice while secretly paying to alter a court decision.

Concealment alone does not place such conduct beyond ethical judgment.

Conversely, something unfamiliar or disliked within a culture is not necessarily Qur’anic wrongdoing or grave indecency.

Clothing, food, social interaction, or a custom may be unfamiliar in a particular region. Unfamiliarity alone establishes neither divine prohibition nor moral wrongness.

Assessment requires examining the Qur’an’s commands and prohibitions: does the conduct conflict with honesty, justice, sexual integrity, trustworthiness, respect for rights, or avoidance of oppression?

No society or religious institution may label every difference as indecency or wrongdoing merely on the basis of its habits and tastes, then violate human rights in the name of combating wrong.

Opposing wrongdoing must itself respect Qur’anic principles. Lies, spying, slander, humiliation, coercion, and aggression cannot be presented as obedience to God merely because they are used to stop another wrong.

Is every custom recognized good?

No.

A prevailing custom is not, by itself, a standard of truth.

Al-An‘am 6:116 states:

«وَإِنْ تُطِعْ أَكْثَرَ مَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ يُضِلُّوكَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ »

Al-An'am 6:116

“If you obey most of those on earth, they will lead you away from the way of The God.”

The number of people following a custom does not establish its rightness.

If depriving daughters of inheritance is customary, that custom cannot cancel the share the Qur’an grants them.

A family may tell a daughter leaving to marry, “You leave this house in a wedding dress and may return only in a burial shroud”—meaning she must remain married at any cost. The saying’s popularity does not turn oppression and denial of safety into good conduct.

If demanding bribes becomes an office’s normal way of conducting business, normality does not make corruption permissible.

If a majority devalues people of another belief, it has no right to violate their freedom of faith and dignity.

The same standard applies to laws enacted by human beings.

Social laws may be necessary to organize life and may be revised as circumstances change. But a law permitting discrimination, oppression, coercion of belief, or wrongful appropriation does not become Qur’anically good merely through enactment or majority support.

Ma‘ruf is not merely what people are used to. Its goodness and appropriateness must be recognized in light of standards of right.

Recognized good, justice, and generosity

An-Nahl 16:90 states:

«إِنَّ يَأْمُرُ بِالْعَدْلِ وَالْإِحْسَانِ وَإِيتَاءِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَيَنْهَىٰ عَنِ الْفَحْشَاءِ وَالْمُنْكَرِ وَالْبَغْيِ»

An-Nahl 16:90

“The God commands justice, doing good, and giving to relatives, and forbids gross indecency, wrongdoing, and transgression.”

Justice requires honoring rights; ihsan (doing good with generosity and excellence) calls for grace as well. Appropriate social conduct must accord with these standards.

Al-Baqarah 2:236, for example, describes provision for a woman upon separation as “a provision in a fitting manner” and adds:

«حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُحْسِنِينَ»

Al-Baqarah 2:236

“A duty upon those who do good.”

Al-Baqarah 2:241 likewise connects provision for divorced women with recognized good conduct and describes it as “a duty upon those mindful of God.”

These relationships show that ma‘ruf involves more than following prevalent practice. It is also connected with rights, mindful responsibility, and generosity.

In financial matters, appropriate conduct must also reflect the parties’ actual capacity.

In Al-Baqarah 2:236, provision is assessed according to prosperity or limited means. Al-Baqarah 2:233 connects food and clothing with fitting conduct and the principle that “no soul is burdened beyond its capacity.”

The same financial amount cannot therefore be demanded of a poor person as of someone wealthy. Nor should a wealthy person conceal resources to evade another person’s rights.

Here, ma‘ruf means appropriate conduct that preserves the entitlement while taking account of actual circumstances, capacities, and needs.

Assessing good and wrong in changing circumstances

To assess whether a custom, agreement, social practice, or human law is appropriate or wrongful, several questions are essential:

1. Does the Qur’an establish a clear principle, ruling, or boundary on this subject? If so, custom cannot override it.

2. Does the practice accord with God’s oneness, justice, honesty, trustworthiness, freedom of belief, and other Qur’anic principles?

3. Does it conflict with explicit prohibitions, including lying, betrayal, backbiting, malicious tale-bearing, spying, aggression, gross indecency, and corruption?

4. Does it violate anyone’s rights or dignity, or harm another person?

5. Is it proportionate to the parties’ actual capacities, needs, and circumstances?

6. Does it rest on consent, understanding, and valid agreement, or is it imposed through coercion and deception?

7. Is it appropriate and beneficial in light of the time, place, resources, and available knowledge?

8. Does its implementation invent a new obligation or prohibition in God’s name that the Qur’an has not prescribed?

These questions distinguish enduring Qur’anic principles from changing human methods.

Al-Baqarah 2:236, for example, establishes a woman’s entitlement to provision on separation, while its amount is determined through financial capacity and fitting conduct.

Money may be appropriate in one community, while another form of provision or support may suit a different setting. What matters is respecting the woman’s entitlement and ensuring that the amount and form are fair and proportionate to both parties’ actual circumstances and capacity.

Custom can help implement a right here, but cannot erase the underlying Qur’anic command.

Conversely, normalizing secret sexual relationships, lies, betrayal, or appropriation of others’ rights does not cancel Qur’anic prohibitions.

Custom may change how matters are carried out; it cannot turn good into wrong or wrong into good.

Conclusion

Through principles, foundational passages, and divine boundaries, the Qur’an establishes the basis of human guidance without reducing life to a closed set of unchanging details.

The same book that commands worship of one God, justice, honesty, keeping commitments, trustworthiness, goodness, and generosity also prohibits lies, betrayal, gross indecency, wrongdoing, aggression, corruption, and violations of rights.

These commands and prohibitions provide standards against which customs, contracts, and social laws should be assessed.

People must not replace divine rulings with their own customs, laws, religious opinions, or desires.

Equally, they should not turn details the Qur’an leaves open into rigid, identical rules for every time, place, and culture.

Clear Qur’anic principles and boundaries are the standard; customs and human methods must be assessed against them. A custom consistent with rights, justice, and other Qur’anic principles can help organize life. A practice that conflicts with divine commands or prohibitions does not become good through age, popularity, or majority approval.

This distinction helps explain the flexibility of Qur’anic guidance in human life: it neither sacrifices divine principles to personal taste nor confines everyone’s life to a single culture or historical period.

The Qur’an sets out principles of good and wrong. People must recognize appropriate conduct in their light, avoid wrongdoing and grave indecency, and organize their ways of life accordingly.
