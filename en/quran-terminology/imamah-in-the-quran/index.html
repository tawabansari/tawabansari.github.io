Qur’an Terminology

Imamah in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Example and the Question of Leadership

نسخه فارسی

In the name of the one God, the Lord whose giving and mercy are boundless.

After Muhammad’s death, imamah became one of the major subjects of disagreement among Muslims. The term, commonly rendered as “leadership” or “the imamate,” came to encompass political succession, religious leadership, doctrinal authority, and special ranks defined differently by different schools of thought. At times it was treated as a hereditary right; at others, its legitimacy was tied to descent from a particular person or family. Before judging these interpretations, however, we need to ask how the Qur’an itself presents an imam and what it makes the basis of that figure’s standing.

What does “imam” mean in the Qur’an?

The word imam belongs to the Arabic root family ʾ-m-m. When applied to a person, it describes someone who leads and whom others follow. Yet the Qur’an does not restrict the word to religious or political leaders. An imam can be a person, a book, or a reference that people follow and use to find their way.

In Hud 11:17 and Al-Ahqaf 46:12, the Qur’an describes the Book of Moses as an imam and a mercy. Here the imam is a book that serves as a source of guidance. In Ya-Sin 36:12, the word appears in connection with recording and accounting for all things. We should therefore avoid defining imam as a religious office at the outset and then imposing that narrow definition on every Qur’anic occurrence.

Imam and ummah, a word often used for a community, belong to the same root family. That relationship does not make all their uses identical. In examining imamah, the relevant question is how a person or thing comes to serve as a leader, guide, or point of reference. Holding such a position does not yet tell us whether the path being offered is right or wrong.

We must therefore distinguish leadership from the truth of a leader’s claim. Someone may have a large following and be recognized by those followers as an imam. That fact alone establishes neither the soundness of the path nor a share in God’s covenant.

Leaders toward guidance and leaders toward error

The Qur’an speaks of imams who guide by God’s command. Of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, Al-Anbiya states:

Al-Anbiya 21:73

وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَئِمَّةً يَهْدُونَ بِأَمْرِنَا وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْهِمْ فِعْلَ الْخَيْرَاتِ وَإِقَامَ الصَّلَاةِ وَإِيتَاءَ الزَّكَاةِ وَكَانُوا لَنَا عَابِدِينَ

“We made them imams who guided by Our command, and We revealed to them the doing of good deeds, the establishment of salat, and the giving of zakat. They were devoted to Our worship.”

These imams are identified through more than their titles or standing. The Qur’an specifies the direction of their guidance and describes good deeds, salat—the prayerful devotion that is to be established—and zakat, giving associated with purification and growth, together with worship. Their leadership is connected to God’s command.

Al-Sajdah likewise describes imams raised from among the Children of Israel who guided by God’s command, noting their patience and certainty concerning His signs (32:24). Even within a community from which such leaders emerge, the verse does not make shared ancestry sufficient to place every member in the same position.

Nor is imam used only of prophets or leaders who guide rightly. In the account of Pharaoh, God speaks of a group subjected to his oppression:

Al-Qasas 28:5

وَنُرِيدُ أَنْ نَمُنَّ عَلَى الَّذِينَ اسْتُضْعِفُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَنَجْعَلَهُمْ أَئِمَّةً وَنَجْعَلَهُمُ الْوَارِثِينَ

“We wished to favor those who had been reduced to weakness in the land, to make them imams, and to make them the inheritors.”

Here imamah appears in connection with the liberation of an oppressed people. The subject is a population crushed under Pharaoh’s rule whose condition God intended to transform. It is not a family claiming inherited privilege. The verse does not, however, say that every oppressed person automatically becomes an imam. It describes God’s intention for a particular group in a particular historical event.

Strikingly, the same chapter also uses imam for leaders taking an entirely different direction:

Al-Qasas 28:41

وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَئِمَّةً يَدْعُونَ إِلَى النَّارِ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ لَا يُنْصَرُونَ

“We made them imams who called people toward the Fire, and on the Day of Resurrection they will not be helped.”

This verse concerns Pharaoh and his followers. They too are called imams, but they lead people toward the Fire rather than guidance. In Al-Tawbah 9:12, the Qur’an also speaks of leaders of rejection. The title imam does not in itself signify purity, legitimacy, or a special rank granted by God.

Even the verb jaʿala, “to make” or “to appoint,” does not by itself establish divine approval of a leader’s path. The Qur’an uses it both for leaders who guide and for leaders who call toward the Fire. Their conduct and the direction in which they lead make the difference clear.

In its description of the servants of the All-Merciful, the Qur’an records their prayer that God make them an imam for those mindful of Him (Al-Furqan 25:74). Becoming an imam is something righteous servants ask for; the passage does not present it as a position already theirs by descent from a particular family.

Read together, these passages offer a clear distinction. Being an imam is a role, but the title alone does not determine its worth. Leaders who guide do so by God’s command, with conduct tied to goodness and worship. Leaders toward error draw others onto a path that ends in the Fire. To assess an imam, we must examine the substance of the call, the leader’s conduct, and the relationship of that path to divine guidance.

Abraham’s distinctive place

Abraham holds a prominent place among those the Qur’an calls imams. He is a prophet and a leader toward guidance, but his significance extends further: later generations, including Muhammad, are called to follow his path.

The Qur’an describes Abraham as a truthful prophet (Maryam 19:41), speaks of him as God’s close friend (Al-Nisa 4:125), and offers this description in Al-Nahl:

Al-Nahl 16:120

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ كَانَ أُمَّةً قَانِتًا لِلَّهِ حَنِيفًا وَلَمْ يَكُ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

“Abraham was himself an ummah, obedient to God, a hanif, and not one of those who associated partners with Him.”

Here Abraham is described as an ummah. Although often used for a community, the word is applied here to a single person. It should not simply be equated with imam. In this setting, it presents Abraham as more than an ordinary individual within a group: he stands as an outstanding example of obedience, of being hanif—turning toward God alone—and of rejecting the attribution of partners to Him.

In Al-Baqarah 2:124, God describes Abraham’s imamah as follows:

Al-Baqarah 2:124

وَإِذِ ابْتَلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ رَبُّهُ بِكَلِمَاتٍ فَأَتَمَّهُنَّ قَالَ إِنِّي جَاعِلُكَ لِلنَّاسِ إِمَامًا قَالَ وَمِنْ ذُرِّيَّتِي قَالَ لَا يَنَالُ عَهْدِي الظَّالِمِينَ

“When Abraham’s Lord tested him with words and he fulfilled them, He said: ‘I am making you an imam for people.’ Abraham asked: ‘And from my descendants?’ He replied: ‘My covenant does not extend to wrongdoers.’”

This account connects Abraham’s imamah to being tested and fulfilling what was required of him. His trial and fulfillment come first; the announcement of his imamah follows. Al-Najm 53:37 likewise describes Abraham as one who fulfilled his commitments. These passages connect his character deeply with faithfulness and the discharge of responsibility, rather than a privilege received simply through ancestry.

The expression “an imam for people” also matters. Abraham is appointed for people, not merely for his own family or nation. That breadth should not be inferred from a single word alone. Other passages also show his path continuing to serve as a source of guidance for later generations.

When Abraham asks about his descendants, he is told that God’s covenant does not extend to wrongdoers. The answer neither excludes every descendant from imamah nor guarantees it to them all. The covenant discussed here does not belong to someone simply because they are Abraham’s child.

We should not extract more from this statement than it says. It specifies neither the names and number of future imams nor a procedure for political succession. It also gives no separate ruling covering every form of wrongdoing or the standing of someone who has repented of past conduct. Elsewhere, the Qur’an recognizes repentance and reform after wrongdoing (Al-Ma’idah 5:39). This sentence alone therefore cannot make every past mistake grounds for permanent exclusion from every kind of leadership.

What the verse clearly establishes is that even among Abraham’s descendants, the divine covenant is no unconditional hereditary privilege. If descent alone were enough, God’s answer to Abraham would have needed no such boundary.

Chosen families and inherited standing

The Qur’an does not deny the historical standing of Abraham’s family. Ali ‘Imran 3:33, 3:34 speaks of God choosing Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of Imran. Al-Nisa 4:54 also recalls the gift of scripture, wisdom, and great dominion to Abraham’s family. We cannot therefore claim that the Qur’an never describes a family or line of descent as chosen for particular responsibilities and gifts.

Yet a family being chosen in history does not automatically make every descendant worthy or a recipient of God’s covenant. The Qur’an draws this distinction itself:

Al-Hadid 57:26

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا وَإِبْرَاهِيمَ وَجَعَلْنَا فِي ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا النُّبُوَّةَ وَالْكِتَابَ فَمِنْهُمْ مُهْتَدٍ وَكَثِيرٌ مِنْهُمْ فَاسِقُونَ

“We sent Noah and Abraham and placed prophethood and scripture among their descendants. Some of them are guided, but many of them are defiantly disobedient.”

The verse places both truths side by side. Prophethood and scripture were established in the descendants of Noah and Abraham, yet those descendants are not presented as a uniformly guided group. Some followed guidance; many were disobedient.

Similarly, after recalling the blessing bestowed on Abraham and Isaac, Al-Saffat 37:113 explicitly distinguishes between descendants who do good and those who plainly wrong themselves. Blessing and shared ancestry with prophets do not erase moral differences between individuals.

One might argue that the Qur’an’s account of Abraham’s chosen family, and of prophethood and scripture continuing among its descendants, provides support for hereditary imamah. But that argument confuses two matters: God choosing people within a family, and every member of that family possessing a perpetual right to a particular rank. The Qur’an states the first; the second does not follow from it.

Even Solomon’s inheritance from David (Al-Naml 27:16) does not, by itself, establish a universal rule entitling every child or grandchild to the same position. The Qur’an describes divine gifts and responsibilities granted to particular people and families. Historical examples of this kind cannot be turned into guarantees covering every generation.

Ancestry can carry a legacy; it does not automatically carry a covenant. You may be the child of someone God chose and still take a different path. Conversely, someone with no genealogical connection to that family may follow its path and draw closer to the truth for which its members were chosen.

Who has the closest claim to Abraham?

Given Abraham’s standing, who can rightly claim to be closest to him? Is that closeness found in a family tree or in following his path?

Ali ‘Imran gives a clear answer:

Ali ‘Imran 3:68

إِنَّ أَوْلَى النَّاسِ بِإِبْرَاهِيمَ لَلَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ وَهَٰذَا النَّبِيُّ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَاللَّهُ وَلِيُّ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“Those with the closest claim to Abraham are those who followed him, this Prophet, and those who believe. God is the protector of the believers.”

This follows the statement that Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but a hanif and a muslim (Ali ‘Imran 3:67): one turned toward God alone and surrendered to Him. The question therefore extends beyond bloodline. Membership in a religious community does not, by itself, establish a closer claim to Abraham either. The Qur’an makes following him the criterion.

The instruction given to Muhammad provides the clearest example:

Al-Nahl 16:123

ثُمَّ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ أَنِ اتَّبِعْ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

“Then We revealed to you: ‘Follow the way of Abraham, a hanif.’ He was not one of those who associated partners with God.”

This command makes Abraham an example for Muhammad too, a guide to the path of undivided devotion to God. It does not suggest that Abraham’s way was discarded or lost its significance when Muhammad came. Muhammad is instructed to follow it. This does not establish a technical hierarchy among prophets. What the verse explicitly affirms is the continuing relevance of millat Ibrāhīm, Abraham’s way of devotion.

Al-Nisa 4:125 places following Abraham’s way alongside surrender to God and doing good. Al-Baqarah 2:130 censures turning away from it. Abraham’s importance thus extends beyond being the ancestor of later prophets: his path continues to be presented as one to follow.

Al-Hajj also speaks of “the way of your father Abraham” (22:78). Calling him “father” in this address should not become evidence of racial superiority. The verse addresses believers collectively and connects them to Abraham’s way. Its context is devotion, surrender, and the responsibilities of belief; that relationship cannot be reduced to the exclusive privilege of a lineage.

Someone may be close to Abraham by descent yet fail to follow his path. Someone outside his line may draw close through following it. This is the principle expressed in the Qur’an’s identification of those with the closest claim to him.

Ethnic and racial superiority therefore cannot be justified by claiming Abrahamic ancestry. The Qur’an presents peoples and tribes as a means for people to know one another, while making mindfulness of God the measure of honor before Him (Al-Hujurat 49:13). A family’s historical selection can be real without making every member morally or spiritually superior to everyone else.

Abraham’s continuing legacy in the pilgrimage

Abraham’s standing in the Qur’an is more than a historical title. His path remains present in the call to worship God alone and in connection with the House that the Qur’an associates with him and Ishmael—the sanctuary at the center of the pilgrimage.

Immediately after describing Abraham’s imamah, the Qur’an turns to the House and his station:

Al-Baqarah 2:125

وَإِذْ جَعَلْنَا الْبَيْتَ مَثَابَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَأَمْنًا وَاتَّخِذُوا مِنْ مَقَامِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ مُصَلًّى وَعَهِدْنَا إِلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ أَنْ طَهِّرَا بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْعَاكِفِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

“When We made the House a place of return and safety for people: ‘Take Abraham’s station as a place for salat.’ We charged Abraham and Ishmael: ‘Purify My House for those who circle it, those who remain there in devotion, and those who bow and prostrate.’”

“For people” appears here too. Abraham is made an imam for people, and the House is made a place of return and safety for people. The proximity of these statements connects his standing with worship that is not confined to one family or tribe. Again, the breadth of his legacy does not rest on the repetition of a word alone. The command to follow his way and the invitation to people to undertake the pilgrimage reinforce it.

“Abraham’s station” here refers to a place associated with worship. The Arabic expression maqām Ibrāhīm should not be treated as linguistically identical to the office of imamah. Its relevance to this discussion is the continuing association of Abraham’s name and memory with the House and people’s worship.

Al-Baqarah 2:127 describes Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundations of the House. The next verse recalls their prayer to remain surrendered to God, for a community surrendered to Him to arise among their descendants, and to be taught their rites. Their prayer for a messenger to be raised among those people follows in 2:129.

This sequence matters. Abraham prays for his descendants, yet the path attributed to him is not confined to them. The following verses discuss his way, his surrender to the Lord, and his and Jacob’s instructions to their children to remain faithful in their devotion (Al-Baqarah 2:130, 2:131, 2:132, 2:133). They pass on a legacy through instruction and counsel, not through an automatic guarantee of their descendants’ worthiness.

The sequence closes by recalling the responsibility of each generation. Al-Baqarah 2:134 emphasizes that earlier generations have the consequences of their own deeds, while later generations are assessed by theirs. Even the legacy of prophets cannot take the place of a person’s own actions.

In Al-Hajj, the invitation to people to undertake the pilgrimage also appears in connection with the House (22:26, 22:27). People of different lands, languages, and communities turn toward the House associated with Abraham and Ishmael. What brings them together in this worship is participation in the devotion and path the Qur’an attributes to Abraham, rather than descent from him.

Seen in this light, the pilgrimage is one enduring expression of Abraham’s legacy. At the House associated with his name, people recall a path founded on surrender to God and rejection of partners alongside Him. This return does not mean following Abraham instead of God. It means following the way Abraham himself walked in service to the one God.

Imamah and leadership after Muhammad

This Qur’anic account provides a basis for returning to the disagreements about imamah that developed after Muhammad’s death. Some concerned who should lead the community; others concerned religious authority, spiritual standing, and the right to guide. Over time these questions sometimes became so intertwined that the title imam itself was taken as evidence of a special rank before God.

Yet the Qur’an distinguishes among its uses of imamah. Moses’ scripture is called an imam; Abraham is made an imam for people; prophets descended from him become leaders toward guidance; an oppressed population in the account of Pharaoh is designated for leadership and inheritance; and Pharaoh and his followers are called imams who invite people toward the Fire.

These uses are not interchangeable. Conclusions about one cannot be transferred to another without evidence. In particular, leading a society must be distinguished from holding a specific divine covenant. A person may occupy a position of political or social leadership without that fact proving that God has made them an imam of guidance.

Some theological interpretations, especially Shi‘i arguments concerning imamah, also appeal to passages about ulū al-amr, those entrusted with authority or responsibility, to establish the authority of particular imams. We must distinguish the Qur’anic expression itself from the people later identified as its referents.

Al-Nisa 4:59 speaks of obedience to God, the Messenger, and those entrusted with authority from among the believers. It directs that disputes be referred back to God and the Messenger. In 4:83, those entrusted with authority appear in connection with examining and assessing reports about the community’s security or alarm. These verses concern their responsibilities, but they do not use imam in the same expression, nor do they specify the names or number of particular imams.

Distinguishing these terms does not mean an imam can never be among those entrusted with authority. A person might in practice hold both roles. That possibility does not establish that imam and ulū al-amr are two Qur’anic names for a single, predetermined office. Such a claim needs evidence beyond treating two expressions as interchangeable.

The same care is needed with terms such as walī (protector or guardian), khalīfah (successor), and malik (king or ruler). In particular settings they may overlap with leadership and authority, but each has its own range of uses. Without examining context, we cannot gather them under one theological category and then impose that category’s implications on every verse.

Nor does the Qur’an deny the existence of multiple imams or imams from a particular line of descent. Some of Abraham’s descendants did attain that standing. Leadership therefore cannot be confined to Abraham alone; equally, the presence of imams within a family does not establish an unconditional hereditary right for every member.

The passages examined do not, by themselves, provide a complete system for determining Muhammad’s political successor. Any particular claim about his succession requires evidence suited to that claim. The presence of the word imam in the Qur’an does not validate every later system of imamah; neither can these passages establish an absolute rejection of every form of leadership or succession.

This question is not confined to a single school or sect. Whenever religious legitimacy is made to depend on ancestry, family reputation, a religious title, or followers’ assertions, the same Qur’anic question arises: toward what path does this person lead, and what evidence establishes the truth of that call?

The Qur’an gives clear descriptions of leaders toward guidance: guiding by God’s command, doing good, establishing salat, giving zakat, worshiping, exercising patience, and holding certainty concerning His signs (Al-Anbiya 21:73; Al-Sajdah 32:24). These descriptions should not become a mechanical checklist for appointing every leader in every period. They do show that Qur’anic guidance involves more than claiming a title: it is connected to the direction of a person’s call and conduct.

The Qur’an also warns against blind following. Al-Ahzab 33:67, 33:68 describes people who, at the accounting, attribute their straying to obedience to their chiefs and leading figures. Choosing a leader does not bring personal responsibility to an end. Following someone else does not remove accountability for the path one has taken.

Numbers of followers, social influence, political power, and membership in a prominent family therefore cannot, by themselves, establish an imam’s rightful claim. A claim to guidance must be assessed through the Qur’an’s own evidence and criteria, rather than simply the claimant’s name or rank.

Returning to Abraham’s imamah

Abraham’s place in the Qur’an cannot be confined within the religious disputes of later periods. He is made an imam for people; his path is described as hanif, directed toward God alone and free of associating partners with Him; and Muhammad is commanded to follow it. At the same time, the Qur’an does not reserve imamah for Abraham and speaks of other leaders toward guidance.

What distinguishes his legacy is its presence both among later prophets descended from him and in a path open to others to follow. Those with the closest claim to him are those who follow him, not those who merely bear his name or ancestry.

Many disputes over imamah have blurred precisely this distinction: following an imam is different from claiming ownership of his rank. A person may claim affiliation with a great imam, but without following his path, that affiliation cannot take the place of truth.

Returning to Abraham’s imamah does not deny other imams or eliminate the need for social leadership. It means recognizing again the example the Qur’an itself presents—one whose value extends beyond ethnicity and sectarian identity and is bound to devotion to God alone, surrender, and guidance.

If Abraham is made an imam for people, his path cannot be monopolized by a family or religious identity. If God’s covenant does not extend to wrongdoers, no one can guarantee a share in it merely through bloodline, title, or ancestral legacy.

The Qur’an does not separate imamah from the reality of following, but neither does it call every act of following guidance. Leaders toward guidance are identified through God’s command, goodness, and devotion. Leaders toward error may bear the same title and attract large followings while leading people toward the Fire.

Abraham stands as an outstanding example: an imam for people whose path remains open to follow, without his rank or worth becoming a hereditary privilege for others. His legacy is a way to follow, not a title with which to claim superiority. No ancestry or assertion can replace the truth of that path.
