Immediately after declaring freedom of faith, the Qur’an states in Al-Baqarah 2:256:

فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ

2:256

“Whoever rejects taghut (false authority that oversteps its rightful limits) and believes in The God has grasped the firmest handhold.”

What is taghut, and why does the Qur’an place rejecting it alongside faith in God? Does the word refer only to idols, Satan, and oppressive rulers? Or does it point to something deeper, whose beginnings can arise within any being capable of choice?

Taghut (false authority that oversteps its rightful limits) is not merely the name of an idol, Satan, or a tyrannical ruler. It can be a person, power, or authority that exceeds its proper bounds and puts its own will and command in the place of truth and justice. Such transgression can begin within any being capable of choice: in the selfishness, sense of superiority, and self-worship that Iblis (the figure who refused God’s command to bow before Adam) revealed when he said, “I am better than him.” When this inward disposition gains power and influence, it can lead to domination, imposed belief, and violations of human rights. Conscience, the recognition of truth, and a sense of justice, by contrast, call us to measure our desires against what is true. Within the divine test, the possibility of transgression is a precondition of genuine choice; following taghut is neither necessary nor worthy of approval. A person can choose selfishness and false authority or consciously turn away from them. That choice carries responsibility and shapes both the course of life and the person’s ultimate fate. Thus, rejecting taghut means freeing oneself from subservience to any inward or outward force that seeks to displace truth. Faith in The God means consciously choosing truth, justice, and devotion to God over self-worship and blind obedience.

To understand taghut, we must look beyond visible structures of power to the source of transgression: the disposition that can lead us to selfishness, a sense of superiority, and the preference of our own wishes over truth and justice.

The story of Iblis offers one of the Qur’an’s clearest accounts of how such transgression begins. First, however, we need to understand the word itself.

What Does Taghut Mean?

Taghut belongs to the Arabic root family ṭ-gh-y (exceeding a limit or crossing a boundary). This is the basic meaning connecting the words in that family.

The Qur’an says in Al-‘Alaq:

كَلَّا إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لَيَطْغَىٰ

96:6

“No indeed! The human being transgresses,”

أَنْ رَآهُ اسْتَغْنَىٰ

96:7

“when he sees himself as self-sufficient.”

These verses expose a major source of transgression. People may interpret wealth, knowledge, power, or social standing as proof that they need nothing beyond themselves, and begin to exceed their rightful limits.

Al-Haqqah 69:11 uses a word from the same root to describe water:

إِنَّا لَمَّا طَغَى الْمَاءُ حَمَلْنَاكُمْ فِي الْجَارِيَةِ

69:11

“When the water rose beyond its bounds, We carried you in the sailing vessel.”

Water can exceed its bounds without committing a moral rebellion. The root’s basic meaning is therefore crossing a limit. In the conduct of a being capable of choice, this can become a violation of truth and justice.

But this raises an important question: Who determines the limit?

If each person makes personal preference the measure, someone may claim as a right precisely what they condemn as transgression in another.

The Qur’an says in Ar-Rahman:

وَالسَّمَاءَ رَفَعَهَا وَوَضَعَ الْمِيزَانَ

55:7

“He raised the heaven and established the balance,”

أَلَّا تَطْغَوْا فِي الْمِيزَانِ

55:8

“so that you do not transgress in the balance.”

وَأَقِيمُوا الْوَزْنَ بِالْقِسْطِ وَلَا تُخْسِرُوا الْمِيزَانَ

55:9

“Maintain the weight with justice, and do not diminish the balance.”

These verses connect transgression with balance and justice. We must not upset the measure of justice or distort what is right to serve our own wishes.

An-Nisa 4:135 likewise commands believers to uphold justice even against themselves, their parents, or their relatives.

Exceeding a limit is therefore not only a matter of having too much of something. It can also mean disrupting the proper relationship between values. Love for family matters, but it must not stand above justice. Respect for a scholar or leader is commendable, but it must not require unquestioning acceptance of an erroneous claim.

Transgression begins when people raise their wishes, interests, or authority beyond their proper place and give them priority over truth and justice.

We must also distinguish between tughyan (transgression beyond a limit) and taghut (an authority that embodies such overstepping).

Tughyan means crossing a limit. In the Qur’an’s explicit uses of taghut, however, it appears as an object of worship or service, an arbiter, a governing or protective authority, or something trusted and followed.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 calls for rejecting taghut. Al-Baqarah 2:257 places it in a position of protective or governing authority. An-Nisa 4:60 speaks of seeking its judgment, and An-Nisa 4:76 of fighting for its cause. Al-Ma’idah 5:60 also refers to serving taghut.

In these uses, taghut is more than an isolated wrongful act: it is an authority or power that has exceeded its rightful bounds and assumed a position to which it has no right.

That authority may be a person, a political power, a religious institution, an idol, or even a desire that someone allows to govern their thought and conduct.

To understand how such authority emerges, we need to examine the inward source of transgression.

Who Led Iblis to Disobey?

The story of Iblis is one of the Qur’an’s clearest accounts of how transgression arises.

In Al-Baqarah 2:34, God describes the command to bow before Adam and Iblis’s response:

أَبَىٰ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ وَكَانَ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ

2:34

“He refused, acted arrogantly, and was among those who rejected the truth.”

Iblis had heard God’s command. He knew God, spoke with Him, and was not unaware of His existence or sovereignty. Yet he disobeyed.

This raises a fundamental question:

If Iblis tempted Adam, who tempted Iblis? What moved him to disobey?

If every act of disobedience must originate in another being’s temptation, we must find yet another tempter to explain that tempter’s disobedience. But the Qur’an identifies no other being as the tempter behind Iblis’s initial refusal.

Al-Kahf 18:50 states:

كَانَ مِنَ الْجِنِّ فَفَسَقَ عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهِ

18:50

“He was one of the jinn (beings distinct from humankind), and he departed from his Lord’s command.”

The act of disobedience is attributed to Iblis himself.

In Al-A‘raf 7:12, when God questions him about his disobedience, Iblis replies:

أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِنْهُ خَلَقْتَنِي مِنْ نَارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُ مِنْ طِينٍ

7:12

“I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay.”

His answer reveals the inward source of his refusal:

“I am better than him.”

Iblis made himself the measure of superiority and worth. Instead of testing his judgment against truth, he judged God’s command through his own conviction of superiority.

By arguing that the substance from which he was created was superior, he claimed a standing that supposedly entitled him to refuse a rightful command.

Here we encounter a fundamental source of transgression: self-centeredness, a sense of superiority, and the preference of one’s own will over truth.

Iblis first yielded to his own inward transgression and then became a tempter of others.

We should therefore not assume that every transgression must originate in an outside being. Pride, selfishness, or self-interest can lead a being capable of choice to resist truth from within.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an also records Iblis saying:

بِمَا أَغْوَيْتَنِي

15:39

“Because You led me astray…”

But this is Iblis’s own accusation—the claim of the very being who responded to God’s command with arrogance. Recording that accusation does not mean endorsing it.

As-Saff 61:5 also states:

فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا أَزَاغَ قُلُوبَهُمْ

61:5

“When they deviated, God caused their hearts to deviate.”

Here, their own deviation precedes its divine consequence. We should not transfer responsibility for our choices entirely to a tempter, external circumstances, or divine will.

The story of Iblis shows that knowing God exists does not, by itself, protect a person or any being capable of choice against transgression. Someone may recognize truth yet refuse to accept it because of selfishness and a desire for superiority.

For a fuller examination of this scene, see the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:34.

Inward Taghut: When the Self Takes the Place of Truth

Everyone has desires, needs, and attachments. We seek life, security, affection, respect, progress, and success. None of these desires is inherently taghut.

The problem begins when someone elevates a desire so far that truth and justice lose their value beside it.

In An-Nazi‘at, the Qur’an contrasts two orientations:

فَأَمَّا مَنْ طَغَىٰ

79:37

“As for the one who transgressed,”

وَآثَرَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا

79:38

“and preferred the life of this world…”

By contrast:

وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ وَنَهَى النَّفْسَ عَنِ الْهَوَىٰ

79:40

“But as for the one who feared standing before his Lord and restrained the self from desire…”

These verses connect transgression with giving priority to worldly life, and contrast it with restraining the self from desire.

This contrast is crucial to understanding the inward source of transgression.

We can assess our desires against truth and justice, or we can set truth and justice aside to obtain what we want.

Al-Jathiyah 45:23 also asks:

أَفَرَأَيْتَ مَنِ اتَّخَذَ إِلَٰهَهُ هَوَاهُ

45:23

“Have you seen the one who has taken his own desire as his god?”

The same theme appears in Al-Furqan 25:43.

Here the Qur’an explicitly describes desire being made an object of worship. Although these verses do not directly call the self or desire “taghut,” together with the story of Iblis they show that the logic of transgression can arise within a being capable of choice.

In examining the origins of taghut, we can therefore speak of an inward tendency toward self-worship and overstepping limits. If it gains control of the will, personal desire becomes an authority placed above truth.

Ordinary life offers many examples.

A father’s love for his children is commendable. He wants them to live comfortably. But if he takes bribes, wrongs others, or appropriates public property to provide his family with luxuries, he has used family affection to justify placing private advantage above justice.

The affection itself is not taghut. Transgression begins with the decision that my family’s comfort matters more than other people’s rights.

An-Nisa 4:135 commands justice even against ourselves, our parents, or our relatives.

At-Tawbah 9:24 likewise weighs attachment to parents, children, spouses, relatives, property, and trade against love for God and His way.

The Qur’an does not ask us to destroy our natural affections and attachments. It asks us to keep them in their proper place and not allow them to rule over truth and justice.

Consider someone who has defended a religious belief for years. One day, clear evidence shows that their interpretation is mistaken. If they knowingly refuse to acknowledge the truth simply because they fear losing their reputation, position, or followers’ respect, what is preventing them from correcting themselves?

The problem is no longer merely insufficient information. Self-interest and attachment to status have taken control of their judgment.

Al-Baqarah 2:206 states:

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُ اتَّقِ أَخَذَتْهُ الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ

2:206

“When he is told, ‘Be mindful of God,’ sinful pride takes hold of him.”

An-Naml 27:14 also describes people who denied the signs out of injustice and arrogance, although inwardly they were certain of them.

Sometimes the barrier to accepting truth is not ignorance. It is unwillingness to accept what is true because pride, advantage, or a sense of superiority stands in the way.

One of the clearest signs that transgression has gained control is therefore a conscious insistence on putting oneself above truth, even after the error has become clear.

Yet this criterion must not become a tool for accusing others. We do not know all their motives and intentions. Someone may sincerely seek truth without having reached the conclusion we consider correct.

An argument should be assessed through evidence, not through claims about its speaker’s hidden motives.

Al-Hujurat 49:12 warns against much suspicion. An-Nisa 4:94 also commands careful examination and clarification.

Recognizing inward transgression should therefore lead us first to examine and correct ourselves, rather than pronounce judgment on other people’s inner lives.

Freedom of Choice, the Possibility of Transgression, and the Divine Test

Why has God allowed human beings the possibility of choosing against what is right?

Al-Mulk 67:2 says that He created death and life to test people as to which of them acts best.

Al-Insan 76:2 describes human creation and testing. Al-Insan 76:3 then turns to guidance and the possibility of choice:

إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا

76:3

“We showed him the way, whether he is grateful or ungrateful.”

In a world where human conduct is tested, the possibility of choosing wrongly must be real.

If a person were fundamentally incapable of disobedience, selfishness, or choosing a wrong path, their obedience would not have the same meaning as the conscious choice of someone who prefers what is right despite being able to disobey.

In this sense, the possibility of transgression is a precondition of genuine freedom within humanity’s moral test.

This does not mean that transgression itself is good, or that people must commit injustice to attain moral maturity. Being able to do something is different from needing to do it.

A person can wrong another. The value of choosing justice lies in doing so despite that possibility.

Nor does the possibility of transgression imply a power independent of God. This entire field of choice—its laws, possibilities, and consequences—exists within the creation and sovereignty of The God.

The study Why Did God Create a World in Which Satan and Taghut Can Act? examines this connection between freedom, the possibility of error, recognition of truth, and human responsibility.

Adam and Iblis: The Error and the Response to It

The stories of Adam and Iblis show that the ability to disobey does not, by itself, determine the fate of a being capable of choice.

Both disobeyed a divine command, but the character of their disobedience and their responses were different.

Of Adam, Ta-Ha 20:115 says:

فَنَسِيَ وَلَمْ نَجِدْ لَهُ عَزْمًا

20:115

“He forgot, and We found no firm resolve in him.”

Ta-Ha 20:121 explicitly describes Adam’s disobedience. His error was real, but the Qur’an connects it with forgetfulness and weakness of resolve.

Of Iblis, by contrast, it speaks of arrogance and refusal.

The difference becomes clearer after the disobedience.

Iblis persisted in his claim of superiority and blamed God for his own misguidance.

Adam and his spouse, however, said:

رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَا أَنْفُسَنَا

7:23

“Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves.”

Adam accepted responsibility and turned back. Ta-Ha 20:122 describes God choosing him, accepting his repentance, and guiding him.

The decisive difference was not simply that both could disobey. After doing wrong, one chose correction; the other continued in superiority and defiance.

We too may stumble, be deceived, or make mistakes through ignorance. But once the truth becomes clear, a new choice confronts us: will we accept responsibility and correct the error, or persist in it to protect our pride and interests?

This subject is explored in detail in The Garden of Eden: A Place of Growth and Development.

How Does Inward Transgression Become Outward Domination?

Transgression may begin within an individual, but its effects do not always remain confined to that person.

When a self-centered person gains political, social, economic, or religious power, they may seek to impose their wishes on others.

The inward logic of transgression can then take outward form as a power or authority that embodies taghut.

Pharaoh provides a clear Qur’anic example.

In An-Nazi‘at 79:24, he declares:

أَنَا رَبُّكُمُ الْأَعْلَىٰ

79:24

“I am your highest lord.”

In Az-Zukhruf 43:51, he also boasts to his people of his dominion over Egypt and the rivers flowing beneath him, displaying his power and possessions.

This is the danger connected with imagined self-sufficiency in Al-‘Alaq 96:6 and Al-‘Alaq 96:7.

People may see their power and resources as so great that they no longer think themselves bound by accountability, justice, or limits.

A ruler does not become taghut merely by possessing power. The problem begins when the ruler treats that power as an absolute personal possession, violates other people’s rights, and places personal will above justice.

Religious authority carries the same danger.

A religious scholar can guide and investigate. But no scholar is entitled to present a personal interpretation, without evidence, as God’s definitive ruling.

At-Tawbah 9:31 criticizes those who have taken their religious scholars and monks as lords besides God.

Aal ‘Imran 3:79 also states:

مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُؤْتِيَهُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ ثُمَّ يَقُولَ لِلنَّاسِ كُونُوا عِبَادًا لِي مِنْ دُونِ

3:79

“No human being to whom God gives the Book, judgment, and prophethood may then tell people, ‘Be servants of me instead of God.’”

If even someone granted scripture and prophethood has no right to call people into servitude to himself, how could other religious leaders or custodians claim such a right?

Authority becomes taghut when a person or institution moves beyond guiding, judging, or administering affairs and claims absolute control over other people’s thought, faith, or destiny.

This danger is not confined to governments and religious institutions.

Within a family, parents have a right to educate and guide their children. That does not amount to absolute ownership of their children’s conscience and faith.

In the media, popularity, follower counts, or social influence may give someone such authority that supporters accept every statement without examination.

Popularity, media, law, tradition, and power are not inherently taghut. The danger arises when one of them takes the place of evidence, truth, and justice.

Outward taghut gains power when a will or authority that exceeds its limits becomes a means of ruling over others.

Are the Followers of Taghut Responsible Too?

The domination exercised by taghut cannot be explained solely through a ruler’s wishes. In many cases, other people’s obedience and cooperation also help it spread.

Az-Zukhruf 43:54 says of Pharaoh:

فَاسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُ فَأَطَاعُوهُ

43:54

“He treated his people as insignificant, and they obeyed him.”

The verse describes both Pharaoh’s conduct and the response of those who obeyed him.

In Al-Ahzab 33:67, a group says on the Day of Judgment:

رَبَّنَا إِنَّا أَطَعْنَا سَادَتَنَا وَكُبَرَاءَنَا فَأَضَلُّونَا السَّبِيلَا

33:67

“Our Lord, we obeyed our masters and great ones, and they led us astray from the way.”

Following powers that mislead is therefore also part of human responsibility.

The responsibility of followers should not, however, be equated with that of people who possess greater power and freedom of action.

The Qur’an distinguishes between someone who knowingly assists injustice for personal advantage or status and someone genuinely constrained by pressure, threats, or incapacity.

An-Nisa 4:98 and An-Nisa 4:99 make separate provision for vulnerable people who have neither the means to act nor a way out. An-Nahl 16:106 likewise distinguishes genuine coercion from inward acceptance of rejection.

We cannot therefore treat everyone living under an oppressive government as responsible for, or complicit in, its oppression.

Nevertheless, someone who can understand and choose must ask: am I forced into silence by genuine fear and incapacity, or am I legitimizing false authority to preserve my advantage, position, and comfort?

Sometimes taghut rules through force. Sometimes it attracts followers through deception, benefits, prejudice, and enticing promises. In either case, responsibility must be assessed in relation to a person’s awareness, capabilities, and actual circumstances.

Taghut across Its Qur’anic Uses

The Qur’an’s explicit uses of the word taghut reveal a broad field of meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 places rejection of taghut alongside faith in God.

Al-Baqarah 2:257 describes God as the protecting guardian of believers, while the powers of taghut are guardians of those who reject the truth, taking them from light into darkness.

An-Nisa 4:51 speaks of believing in jibt (false objects of belief or reverence) and taghut.

An-Nisa 4:60 describes people who seek judgment from taghut despite having been commanded to reject it.

An-Nisa 4:76 contrasts fighting for God’s cause with fighting for the cause of taghut.

Al-Ma’idah 5:60 condemns serving taghut.

An-Nahl 16:36 places avoiding taghut alongside serving God.

Az-Zumar 39:17 gives good news to those who avoid serving taghut and turn back to God.

These uses show that taghut is not simply the name of one particular being. It concerns the human relationship with authorities that become objects of belief, service, judgment, guardianship, allegiance, and struggle.

The common element is that something exceeds its rightful place and assumes an authority to which it has no right.

We should therefore distinguish the source of transgression from the emergence of taghut.

Transgression can originate within a being capable of choice. In its explicit Qur’anic uses, however, taghut appears as an authority that is followed or served.

These levels are interconnected. Selfishness and a sense of superiority can lead people to establish or accept false authority. False authority, in turn, can strengthen transgression in others through fear, deception, and promised advantages.

How Does Legitimate Authority Differ from Taghut?

Rejecting taghut does not mean rejecting every law, government, guide, scholar, or social authority.

Shared life requires order, law, knowledge, consultation, and judgment. The Qur’an does not overlook these needs.

An-Nisa 4:58 commands that trusts be entrusted to those entitled to them and that judgment between people be just.

An-Nisa 4:59 speaks of obedience to God, the Messenger, and those entrusted with authority. Yet the same verse directs disputes back to God and the Messenger.

Human authority therefore cannot consider itself independent of truth or beyond examination.

Luqman 31:15 does not prescribe absolute obedience even to parents. If they press their child to associate something with God, they must not be obeyed in that matter, although they must still be treated kindly in worldly life.

This clearly distinguishes respect from blind obedience.

We can respect parents, learn from scholars, observe just laws, and cooperate with public officials. None of these relationships requires surrendering absolute control of conscience and faith to someone else.

Legitimate authority must act within the scope of its responsibility, uphold justice, and remain answerable to truth.

Aal ‘Imran 3:159 calls even God’s Messenger to consult the believers. Qaf 50:45 reminds him that he is not a coercive ruler over people, and Al-Ghashiyah 88:22 states:

لَسْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِمُصَيْطِرٍ

88:22

“You are not a controller over them.”

Legitimate guidance and government are therefore different from taking possession of people’s consciences and imposing belief.

Legitimate authority serves truth and justice. Authority that embodies taghut seeks to make truth and justice serve itself.

This is one of the clearest criteria for distinguishing them.

Distinguishing Nafs, Taghut, Shaytan, and Sultan

These four concepts help us understand the field of human choice, but they do not mean the same thing.

Nafs (the self) concerns the person and the inward life of desire, understanding, inclination, and choice. The self is not inherently taghut: a person can reform and purify it.

Ash-Shams 91:7, Ash-Shams 91:8, Ash-Shams 91:9, and Ash-Shams 91:10 speak of the self, its awareness of wrongdoing and moral vigilance, and the success or failure connected with its cultivation.

Tughyan (overstepping a limit) occurs inwardly when personal desire is elevated so far that truth and justice are displaced. Taghut, in its Qur’anic uses, concerns an authority in which such overstepping has taken form.

Shaytan (a tempter who entices and misleads) plays the role of deceiver and caller toward error in this discussion. Al-An‘am 6:112 speaks of devils among humans and jinn (beings distinct from humankind). Temptation and deception are therefore not confined to one particular being. A human being can also draw others toward falsehood through speech, conduct, and influence.

Yet a tempter does not remove the individual’s responsibility to choose.

An-Nahl 16:99 and An-Nahl 16:100 state that Satan has no authority over those who believe and trust in their Lord. His authority concerns those who take him as a patron and turn to him.

Ibrahim 14:22 also records Satan’s words on the Day of Judgment:

وَمَا كَانَ لِيَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ سُلْطَانٍ إِلَّا أَنْ دَعَوْتُكُمْ فَاسْتَجَبْتُمْ لِي

14:22

“I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you answered me.”

An invitation or temptation must not be confused with compulsion. We remain responsible for our response.

Sultan (authority, compelling proof, or power to prevail) has a range of Qur’anic uses. In some verses, such as Ar-Rum 30:35, it refers to evidence or proof.

Ghafir 40:35 and Ghafir 40:56 describe people who dispute God’s signs without such authority; the latter verse also speaks of pride within them.

The connection matters. People may argue against truth without evidence, driven only by self-importance and a desire for superiority.

Reason, fitrah (the human being’s innate disposition), divine signs, careful reflection, the examination of evidence, and willingness to correct mistakes become crucial in responding to this condition.

Thus, nafs is the self in which understanding and choice take place; tughyan is overstepping a limit; taghut is authority that oversteps its bounds; shaytan is a tempter calling toward error; and sultan, in the context of discerning truth, is proof and the authority of evidence.

For fuller discussions, see Why Did God Create a World in Which Satan and Taghut Can Act? and Logic and Sultan in the Qur’an.

Why Is Rejecting Taghut Linked with Faith in God?

Al-Baqarah 2:256 states:

فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ

2:256

“Whoever rejects taghut (false authority that oversteps its rightful limits) and believes in The God…”

Here, rejecting taghut comes before faith in God in the wording of the verse.

In An-Nahl 16:36, however, the order is:

أَنِ اعْبُدُوا وَاجْتَنِبُوا الطَّاغُوتَ

16:36

“Serve The God and keep away from taghut (false authority that oversteps its rightful limits).”

These two formulations show why word order should not be turned into an invented chronological requirement, as though everyone must first complete a separate stage of rejecting taghut and only afterward come to faith.

What matters most is the connection between these two commitments.

Someone who acknowledges God as the sole object of worship cannot at the same time give absolute command to a desire, power, or authority opposed to truth.

Conversely, escaping one false authority is not enough if we then replace it with our own desires or another power.

Someone might escape the control of a fanatical leader, only to become so convinced of their own superiority that they accept no argument or evidence. They have fled one false authority but become captive to self-worship.

Intellectual independence is not achieved by declaring, “Whatever I want is right.”

Intellectual independence means being able to recognize and consciously follow truth without being enslaved to fear, prejudice, advantage, or power.

Az-Zumar 39:17 gives good news to those who avoid serving taghut and turn back to God. Az-Zumar 39:18 then describes servants who:

يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقَوْلَ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ أَحْسَنَهُ

39:18

“Listen to what is said and follow the best of it.”

This connection links freedom from taghut with thinking, listening, and discerning truth.

An independent thinker is not someone who needs no one else. Such a person learns, listens, and consults, while refusing to make any human authority a substitute for truth.

Rejecting taghut and believing in God are therefore two connected directions of one choice: freedom from false authority and a conscious turning toward The God.

Taghut and Freedom of Faith

Any account of taghut is incomplete without the principle “There is no compulsion in religion.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:255, known as the Throne Verse, God describes His sovereignty, knowledge, power, and complete self-sufficiency. Immediately afterward, Al-Baqarah 2:256 rejects compulsion in religion and speaks of the distinction between sound guidance and error becoming clear.

This sequence has deep significance.

The very God who rules all existence has placed human beings in a field of understanding and choice. A person can accept truth or turn away, but the choice has consequences.

Al-Kahf 18:29 states:

فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِنْ وَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ

18:29

“Let whoever wishes believe, and whoever wishes reject.”

The same verse also warns of the fate of wrongdoers.

Freedom of choice does not mean that truth and falsehood are equivalent or that responsibility disappears.

Within the world of testing, human beings can incline toward transgression or choose what is right. The reality of that choice gives moral responsibility its meaning.

No human being, however, has the right to seize another person’s freedom in God’s name.

A power that seeks to rule over faith and conscience through force, threats, torture, imprisonment, or coercive pressure must itself be examined against the criteria for taghut.

In Yunus 10:99, God reminds even His Messenger that his task is not to compel people to believe.

If faith must arise from understanding and will, no power can force genuine belief into another person’s heart.

Freedom of faith is therefore more than a legal or social issue. It concerns the nature of understanding, will, responsibility, and the divine test.

For a detailed discussion, see the principle of no compulsion in religion and No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey.

How Can We Recognize Taghut in Our Own Lives?

Reading about Pharaoh and Iblis is not enough. We must also be able to recognize the same logic in our own lives.

When we refuse to accept something as true, we should ask: do we have a sound reason to disagree, or do we simply not want to admit that we were mistaken?

When we defend a leader, scholar, or tradition, have we accepted it because of evidence and its merits, or has attachment made us unwilling to examine it?

When we possess power or an advantage, do we use it within the bounds of justice, or imagine that our position entitles us to violate other people’s rights?

When we encounter someone who disagrees, do we seek to make truth clear through evidence, or to force acceptance of our interpretation through humiliation, threats, and pressure?

Before using these questions to condemn others, we should use them to examine ourselves.

People can make mistakes, fail to recognize truth, or be deceived despite good intentions. These conditions must not be equated with conscious transgression and arrogance.

But once an error becomes clear, a new choice arises: correction and return, or insistence on one’s own wishes at the expense of truth.

Taghut draws strength from the moment we seek to change truth to suit our wishes instead of correcting ourselves in response to it.

Conclusion: Freedom from Servitude to Taghut

Taghut in the Qur’an is not merely the name of an idol, ruler, or external being. It concerns the crossing of limits and the assumption by a power or authority of a position to which it has no right.

Such transgression can originate within a being capable of choice, when selfishness, pride, and personal wishes are placed above truth and justice.

The story of Iblis shows that disobedience does not always require an external tempter. He first imagined himself superior, then judged a rightful command by that conviction of superiority.

The story of Adam, however, shows that freedom of choice—and even wrongdoing—does not condemn a person forever. We can recognize error, accept responsibility, turn back, and choose the right path.

Within the divine test, the possibility of transgression is a condition of free choice. Following taghut is not the inevitable destiny of a being capable of choosing.

The choice begins inwardly, but its effects can spread throughout society. When selfishness acquires power, it can become oppressive government, authoritarian religious leadership, economic corruption, imposed belief, and violations of others’ rights.

Conversely, recognizing truth, upholding balance and justice, restraining desire, and refusing false authority can free us from this servitude.

The Qur’an does not ask us to destroy our desires, affections, and natural relationships. It asks us to place none of them above truth and justice.

Nor does it ask us to discard every guide and social authority. It asks us not to give any human power or authority absolute command over our conscience and faith.

The possibility of transgression makes free choice within the test possible. Freedom from servitude to taghut comes through understanding and consciously choosing what is right.

This is the connection Al-Baqarah 2:256 makes between rejecting taghut and believing in The God.

True freedom does not mean recognizing no authority beyond our own wishes. It means allowing no desire, power, or false authority to displace truth in our thought, conscience, and choices.
