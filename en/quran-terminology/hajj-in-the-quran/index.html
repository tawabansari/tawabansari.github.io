Qur’an Terminology

Hajj in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Way, the Rites, and People’s Rights

نسخه فارسی

1. Introduction

Every year, people from across the world bear considerable expense and the hardships of travel to reach Mecca. They come to the House that the Qur’an connects with Abraham and his hanif way: turning toward God alone. Before leaving, they may have dozens of questions about where to enter ihram, the consecrated state associated with pilgrimage; what to wear; how many circuits to make around the House; how to throw pebbles at the jamarat, the ritual stoning sites; when to cut their hair; and other ritual details. Someone might follow every instruction carefully without ever asking why the House was established, what Abraham called people to, or what hajj is meant to change in a person’s thinking and conduct.

In the Qur’an, hajj encompasses more than travel to a sacred place or a sequence of ceremonies. God describes the House as a place of return and safety for people (Al-Baqarah 2:125), addresses the invitation to hajj to people (Al-Hajj 22:27), speaks of their benefits, remembrance of God, and feeding those in need (22:28), and calls the Kaaba a support for people’s well-being (Al-Ma’idah 5:97). The House and its rites belong within a life of devotion. The Kaaba is a real building, and circling it and observing other sacred rites have their place. Their value, however, cannot be separated from devotion to God alone, mindfulness of Him, safety, care for others, and Abraham’s way.

The difficulty begins when that order is reversed and ritual details overshadow the foundations of hajj. How can trimming nails or cutting hair attract elaborate rulings and multiple forms of expiation while exclusion from the Sacred Mosque, imposed belief, religious monopoly, and the exclusion of other seekers of God receive less scrutiny? How can people insist on precise numbers of pebbles and circuits while remaining silent about violations of undivided devotion, Abraham’s hanif orientation, and freedom to worship? If an act’s outward form survives while its foundations are abandoned, can we still unconditionally identify it with Abraham’s hajj?

This reversal also requires examining how divine commands become mixed with human interpretations, historical customs acquire the force of religious obligations, and religious authority becomes bound to political and economic power. Over time, an extensive body of rules, categories, prohibitions, and conditions of validity has developed around hajj. Many details have become so deeply established that asking for their Qur’anic basis is sometimes treated as opposition to hajj itself. Instead of testing inherited practices and rulings against the Qur’an, people read the Qur’an through those rulings, assuming that what requires proof is already an unquestionable command of God.

The issue extends beyond accumulating ritual detail. Who has the right to legislate in God’s name, obstruct others’ devotion, or reserve a House established for people to the followers of one prescribed religious way, sect, or political power? The Qur’an emphasizes Abraham’s hanif way, rejects compulsion in religion, and condemns preventing remembrance of God. It also censures placing human beings in the position of lords with authority to prescribe religion. These standards cannot be used exclusively against other faiths. Every government, jurist, school, or community claiming such authority in God’s name must face the same test—even if it calls itself Muslim, a follower of Abraham, or a servant of God’s House.

This raises a further question. If God’s House is established for people, and the Qur’an recognizes different prescribed ways and communal rites while affirming that the One worshiped is the same, can one community’s detailed legal rules be made a universal condition for taking part in the House’s sacred observances? A Jew, a Christian, or someone following the hanif way of undivided devotion who approaches the House would then face a choice: adopt that community’s particular religious practice or regard themselves as excluded from rites rooted in worship of the one God. The issue is neither setting aside clear Qur’anic rulings nor ignoring specific restrictions on entry to the Sacred Mosque. It is the imposition of requirements that have not been established as God’s commands.

Reform nevertheless does not require rejecting hajj or abandoning Abraham’s House. Just as the Kaaba’s sanctity cannot be reduced to stone and walls, the meaning of hajj is not recovered by discarding every outward act. We must first understand its place and purpose through the Qur’an, then assess each rite and ruling in that light. What God has prescribed must be distinguished from what people have added in His name. A custom’s age, a ruling’s popularity, and an institution’s power do not independently establish a command’s divine origin.

That is this study’s starting point. It seeks neither to invent a new prescribed religious way nor to replace one collection of customs with another. It examines hajj through the Qur’an: the hajj to which Abraham called people and which the Qur’an associates with God’s oneness, the hanif way, mindfulness, safety, and human benefit. Before asking how many circuits a pilgrim must make, where to enter ihram, or which words to recite, we must ask what path the person is taking, whose authority they follow, and whether their thinking and conduct remain faithful to Abraham’s example. Someone may circle the House repeatedly while living far from his way.

2. The study’s foundational questions

If Qur’anic hajj is connected to devotion to God alone, Abraham’s hanif way, safety, mindfulness, and human benefit, how can we distinguish it from the customs and rulings that have accumulated around it? Before counting circuits, locating ihram boundaries, or specifying clothing, we need answers to questions about hajj’s foundations.

First, what does hajj mean and seek to accomplish in the Qur’an? Is it simply travel to Mecca and participation in ceremonies, or should the journey deepen devotion to God, mindfulness, and responsibility toward others? What benefits does the Qur’an describe, and why does it call the Kaaba qiyāman li-l-nās, a support for people’s well-being? What has someone gained of this worship if they perform every outward act while remaining distant from its stated purposes?

Second, how does hajj relate to Abraham and his hanif way? Why does the Qur’an connect the invitation with him and refer to his station? What understanding and practice did Abraham leave to those who follow him, having turned away from associating partners with God and serving others in His place? Does following him mean only repeating movements attributed to him, or does it also require undivided devotion, freedom in serving God, and resistance to blind following? How can someone circle Abraham’s House while abandoning the principles for which he stood?

Third, what place do the House and Abraham’s station occupy? Why, after describing Abraham’s imamah—his role as a leader to be followed—does the Qur’an identify the House as a place of return and safety and instruct people to take his station as a place for salat? Salat, examined further below, is prayerful devotion that the Qur’an calls people to establish. How are Abraham’s covenant, salat, purification of the House, and his path connected? Does presence at a sacred place alone make someone his follower?

Fourth, for whom is God’s House established, and who is fit to oversee it? When the Qur’an describes the House and the Sacred Mosque as “for people,” what responsibilities follow for their custodians? Can a government, family, or religious school reserve such a House for its own followers? How do freedom of belief, people’s safety, and the prohibition on obstructing remembrance of God relate to hajj? When the Qur’an criticizes exclusion from the Sacred Mosque and measures its rightful guardianship by mindfulness of God (Al-Anfal 8:34), who can claim an unrestricted right to decide who enters?

Fifth, how do sacred symbols and rites relate to mindfulness and the public good? Why do the hajj passages connect circling the House, salat, offerings, and remembrance with responsibility toward the needy and benefits for people? Is their spiritual value exhausted by form and number, or must their effects on devotion and conduct also be considered?

Sixth, for whom, and at what time, is hajj prescribed? The Qur’an makes ability a condition, but does ability mean only having the fare? How do physical capacity, psychological well-being, safety, family responsibilities, and actual circumstances enter the assessment? When it speaks of “whoever is able to find a way to it” (Ali ‘Imran 3:97), are an accessible and safe route part of that ability? Can one fixed financial standard apply both to someone near Mecca and to a low-income person far away? Why does the Qur’an describe “known months,” and how do they relate to the sacred months and the known and numbered days?

Seventh, who has authority to prescribe religious rules and rites? May people declare something obligatory or forbidden in God’s name when He has not done so? If the Qur’an criticizes making religious leaders into lords, does that standard concern only Jews and Christians, or must Muslims, jurists, governments, and religious schools face it too? How does informed trust in a religious guide differ from giving that guide authority to legislate religion? How should Al-Tawbah 9:28 be understood within its treaty context and alongside the Qur’an’s distinction between the People of the Book—communities associated with earlier scripture—and those who associate partners with God? Can a ruling concerning a specified group be extended to every other faith without evidence?

Eighth, which rites has God prescribed, and which details were added later? How do hajj and umrah differ, and do their legal classifications have clear Qur’anic support? What does the Qur’an say about circling the House, Safa and Marwah, Arafat, the Sacred Monument, offerings, shaving the head, and other acts? What do “ending tafath” and “fulfilling vows” mean in Al-Hajj 22:29, and what limits does the text specify? Does it prescribe numbers of circuits or pebbles, particular garments, entry points for ihram, set formulas, or the various expiations? Without Qur’anic evidence for a detail, on what basis can it be obligatory or its omission invalidate hajj? Is choosing a custom personally the same as attributing it to God as religious law?

Ninth, what responsibilities does hajj create toward society and those in need? In light of the Qur’an’s emphasis on human benefit, feeding the poor, the House’s safety, and mindfulness, how should we assess high costs, pilgrimage commerce, the distribution of offerings, and restrictions on access? How does legitimate trade differ from monopolizing worship for profit? When can political barriers, visas, and quotas become unjust exclusion from the Sacred Mosque, and how can that be distinguished from necessary measures for safety and order? Can a House established for people’s well-being become an instrument of sectarian superiority, discrimination, or profiteering while hajj’s purposes are still claimed to be fulfilled?

All these questions return to one issue. If hajj’s foundations—devotion to God alone, the hanif way, safety, mindfulness, freedom to worship, and people’s rights—are violated, what religious credibility can exact performance of outward details confer? Can correct counts of circuits and pebbles compensate for violating principles the Qur’an itself emphasizes?

This inquiry first establishes those foundations, then assesses customary rites and requirements against them. Without first identifying the governing principles, debate over subsidiary details may obscure the very truth hajj is meant to reveal instead of bringing us closer to it.

3. Method and standards

The Qur’an is this study’s primary standard for understanding hajj and evaluating its rites. We do not begin with current practice and then search for verses to validate each component. We first ask what the Qur’an says, what purposes it identifies, what acts it prescribes, and what limits it establishes. Present-day practices are then measured against those findings. Antiquity, widespread acceptance of a legal ruling, or agreement within a religious community does not by itself establish divine authority.

The first step is to examine the Qur’an’s language. Before accepting technical definitions of hajj, umrah (a separate visit to the House), nusuk (rites or acts of devotion), shaʿāʾir (sacred signs or observances), haram (sacred or inviolable), hady (an offering directed to the sanctuary), ability, way, and hanif, we examine their roots and ranges of meaning. Their occurrences are compared in context. A root’s meaning is not assumed to be identical in every derivative, and later legal terminology cannot decide the Qur’an’s meanings in advance. We also distinguish what an early audience could understand from a word from its specialized definition in later jurisprudence. The later prevalence of a legal definition does not prove that all its restrictions and obligations were present in revelation from the beginning. Each verse must be read with surrounding statements and other relevant passages; extracting a sentence can attribute to the Qur’an a meaning its fuller discourse does not support.

The second step is to identify hajj’s foundations. The Qur’an connects the House with devotion to God alone and rejection of partners (Al-Hajj 22:26), describes it as a place of return and safety (Al-Baqarah 2:125), places resident and visitor on an equal footing in the Sacred Mosque (Al-Hajj 22:25), and presents the Kaaba as a support for people (Al-Ma’idah 5:97). These principles belong alongside Abraham’s hanif way, freedom of religious choice, mindfulness, remembrance, and human benefit. Hajj cannot therefore be assessed through outward movements alone. We must first establish what those movements serve and whether the rules and institutions surrounding them accord with these foundations.

This yields the study’s governing principle:

The burden of proof rests on whoever presents an act as God’s command, an obligatory rite, or a condition of valid hajj—not on whoever asks for its Qur’anic basis.

That principle applies consistently. Anyone claiming a required circuit count, exclusive locations for entering ihram, a compulsory formula, or an act whose omission invalidates hajj must demonstrate the claim’s Qur’anic basis. The absence of a ruling in the Qur’an does not oblige us to seek its obligatory status in hadith or jurisprudence. Our question is what God has asked of people, rather than how to justify every existing detail. Voluntarily observing a custom must also be distinguished from imposing it as divine command. Silence about a detail neither commands nor prohibits it by itself, but silence cannot establish an obligation or a condition of validity.

Consider an ordinary instruction: “Bathe and wash your body.” Does it require wetting the entire body first, shampooing twice, scrubbing the arms three times, and washing the feet last? Would another sequence make the bath invalid? Such details are different ways to carry out the instruction, not requirements imposed by the person giving it. Someone may choose a method according to their needs, resources, and judgment. They cannot attribute their preferred method to the speaker and demand that everyone follow it.

The same reasoning applies to hajj. An act prescribed by God must be performed, but that does not establish every customary detail of its performance as His command. People may have adopted methods that passed from generation to generation. Their age and prevalence do not make them divine law. Where God prescribes an act without specifying particular details of execution, those details cannot be made obligatory in His name or their omission treated as invalidating worship.

The Qur’an offers a vivid example of the consequences of adding question after question to a command in the account of the Israelites and the cow (Al-Baqarah 2:67, 2:68, 2:69, 2:70, 2:71). The initial command was to slaughter a cow. They repeatedly asked about its age, color, and other characteristics, and God’s answers specified further details, until, the Qur’an says, they almost failed to carry out the command. The account neither rejects all questions asked to understand a command nor invalidates details that God Himself prescribed in response. Its relevance here is that a basic command should not be turned, without grounds, into a chain of complicated conditions. In that account, God Himself supplied the later details. Where the Qur’an gives no such command concerning a detail of hajj, who has the right to take His place, declare that detail obligatory, and pronounce worship invalid if it is omitted?

This distinction is more than scholarly caution. It concerns the Qur’an’s explicit warning against inventing claims about God. Al-Nahl 16:116 forbids labeling fabrications lawful or forbidden and attributing them to Him. Yunus 10:59 asks whether God gave permission or people are inventing claims about Him. Choosing a personal or customary practice is different from declaring it His certain command. A ruling attributed to God without divine support raises more than a disagreement about method: the claim requires evidence of His authorization. This warning applies equally to everyone. No leader, jurist, or institution is exempt because of religious standing.

When assessing rites, we distinguish explicit Qur’anic commands, conclusions reasoned from several passages, details for which the Qur’an gives no ruling, and rulings that contradict a clear command. Religious claims do not all have the same degree of textual clarity. A clear ruling must be accepted; an inference must disclose its reasoning and limits; an unsupported requirement must not be presented as God’s certain instruction. The aim is not to replace one set of human rulings with another under the Qur’an’s name.

Religious authority requires the same scrutiny. Al-Tawbah 9:31 criticizes taking religious scholars and monks as lords, while Al-Shura 42:21 describes partners prescribing religion without God’s permission. These standards cannot concern only Jews and Christians. Any government, institution, jurist, or school granting humans independent authority to prescribe religion must face the same test, whether it identifies as Muslim or otherwise. Criticism of a demonstrable act or legislative claim must not become an unsupported condemnation of every member of a faith or family. The standard concerns conduct and claims that can be established, not people’s names or identities.

To avoid selective readings, passages that may appear to point in different directions will be examined together. Al-Tawbah 9:28, prohibiting those who associate partners with God from approaching the Sacred Mosque, is an important example. Ignoring the treaty context at the chapter’s beginning and verses naming the People of the Book separately from those who associate partners can lead to unwarranted generalization. Al-Baqarah 2:105 distinguishes the two designations, as does Al-Tawbah 9:28, 9:29. One verse cannot simply place every scriptural community under the other designation. The precise reach of each ruling must nevertheless be established from the passages together, rather than from our own religious assumptions. The same method applies to other disputed hajj questions.

Historical sources, reports, legal accounts, and transmitted practice may help explain how rites developed, but their role differs from the Qur’an’s. They can show how people performed hajj in different periods, where schools disagreed, and the circumstances in which customs spread. We should also investigate whether details shaped by geography, travel resources, clothing, or earlier ways of life subsequently became religious requirements. Where supported by historical evidence, such a development can distinguish a practical solution for particular circumstances from a universal divine command. Yet a historical report of an act does not itself prove that God required it of everyone or made its omission invalidate hajj. Nor should an old practice automatically be equated with every detail of its later legal elaboration. History helps explain how customs arose; the Qur’an remains the final standard for attributing a ruling to God.

This method extends beyond individual pilgrims. Regulations, restrictions, and administrative structures must also be tested against Qur’anic principles. Since the Sacred Mosque is presented as being for people and exclusion is criticized (Al-Hajj 22:25; Al-Anfal 8:34), claims of monopoly or exclusion require examination. Necessary safety and service arrangements must be distinguished from religious discrimination, unjust barriers, and profiteering. Claims about current conditions must rest on verifiable assertions and credible evidence. Accusing people of injustice requires the same care that attributing commands to God demands.

For each subject, the study first offers one or two clear Qur’anic witnesses with explanation. Where fuller treatment is needed, it refers readers to a dedicated study. Discussions of devotion to God alone, associating partners, faith, the hanif way, qibla or orientation, Abraham’s imamah, din and shariah, and freedom of belief are therefore connected to existing research rather than entirely repeated. The relevant findings are used where the argument requires them.

We begin neither by assuming every customary rite is correct nor by resolving to reject them all. What the Qur’an affirms will be accepted, what conflicts with its clear principles examined critically, and what lacks sufficient grounds for religious obligation left without a claim to divine command. Foundations and details must retain their proper relationship. Before judging circuit counts, pebbles, and similar details, we must assess the principles and authority in whose name the rulings are issued. Precision in outward details cannot compensate for violating hajj’s most fundamental principles.

A return to Qur’anic hajj begins by understanding God’s command and assessing human claims against it. Human interpretation cannot become the standard that determines what His command is allowed to mean.

4. Hajj in the Qur’an: meaning, purpose, and place

The root ḥ-j-j: setting out for the House

Hajj belongs to the Arabic root ḥ-j-j. One recognized meaning in this family concerns intending or heading toward a particular person or place. In hajj, that intention involves going to the House for a recognized act of worship. Other forms, such as ḥujjah and muḥājjah, concern evidence, argument, and disputes conducted through reasoning. The Qur’an describes Abraham’s argument with someone who disputed with him about his Lord (Al-Baqarah 2:258), and elsewhere disputes about Abraham himself (Ali ‘Imran 3:65, 3:66). In Al-Qasas 28:27, ḥijaj denotes years.

These uses cannot be reduced to a single meaning without evidence. Sharing a root does not establish that hajj to the House means debating or presenting arguments. Its specific Qur’anic usage must guide us. In Al-Baqarah 2:158, performing hajj is connected to the House and appears alongside umrah. Ali ‘Imran 3:97 concerns a real House that people must be able to find a way to reach. Qur’anic hajj is therefore neither simply an inward decision nor a concept detachable from travel and presence at the House.

Knowing the geographical destination, however, does not mean understanding the purpose. A person may know the route, make the effort, and complete the journey while overlooking what the House was established to serve. The root introduces intention and approach; the verses explain what that intention should seek.

A conceptual connection can be drawn here with “setting one’s orientation upright” in Al-A‘raf 7:29, without treating the expressions as lexically identical. The journey to the House is a real outward movement, yet it can also recall the direction of a person’s thinking and devotion. A pilgrim travels toward the House; the deeper question concerns whom they turn toward in heart and conduct. This relationship is explored in Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Qur’an.

Abraham: hajj begins with devotion to God alone

The Qur’an does not present hajj as an institution first established by Muhammad. Abraham is connected with the House and the invitation long before him. The sequence in Al-Hajj clarifies the foundation of that invitation:

Al-Hajj 22:26

وَإِذْ بَوَّأْنَا لِإِبْرَاهِيمَ مَكَانَ الْبَيْتِ أَنْ لَا تُشْرِكْ بِي شَيْئًا وَطَهِّرْ بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْقَائِمِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

“When We assigned Abraham the site of the House: ‘Associate nothing with Me, and purify My House for those who circle it, those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate.’”

Al-Hajj 22:27

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ يَأْتِينَ مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ

“Proclaim hajj among people. They will come to you on foot and on every mount made lean by travel, arriving from every distant route.”

The order matters. Before the invitation comes rejection of partners with God and purification of the House. Whose House it is, and the devotion for which it stands, must first be clear; people are then called to it. Devotion to God alone is thus foundational to the Qur’anic presentation, not a secondary virtue alongside the rites.

If the House is purified for serving the one God, can people and religious institutions be granted independent authority there to define religion and declare things lawful or forbidden? Al-Shura 42:21 connects prescribing religion without God’s permission with assigning partners, while Al-Kahf 18:26 denies Him any partner in judgment. Purifying the House therefore requires examining associations of partners in obedience and religious legislation, beyond cleaning the building or removing stone idols. Identifying a particular instance still requires argument. The subject is examined in The Qur’anic Root sh-r-k.

Abraham is more than a builder or restorer of a House. He chose God’s service against inherited beliefs and pressure from his surroundings. The Qur’an calls him hanif, and Ali ‘Imran 3:67 explicitly describes him as neither Jewish nor Christian nor one who associated partners with God. In Al-Baqarah 2:135, when adherence to Judaism or Christianity is proposed as the path to guidance, the response points to Abraham’s hanif way. His path depends on turning toward truth and surrendering to God, rather than a sectarian name.

His prayer displays the same connection. He first asks God to keep him and his children from worshiping idols, then prays concerning his descendants near the House: for salat to be established, people’s hearts to turn toward them, and provision for them (Ibrahim 14:35, 14:36, 14:37). The House remains connected to God’s oneness, salat, and people’s needs.

This connection extends beyond Abraham’s biography. After describing the invitation, benefits, and some rites, Al-Hajj returns to the hanif orientation and rejection of partners:

Al-Hajj 22:31

حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ ۚ وَمَنْ يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَكَأَنَّمَا خَرَّ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ أَوْ تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي مَكَانٍ سَحِيقٍ

“Be hanif toward God, associating no partners with Him. Whoever associates partners with God is as though they had fallen from the sky, to be snatched by birds or swept by the wind into a distant place.”

The verse’s position among the pilgrimage passages is significant. After discussing its acts, the Qur’an returns to the foundation of Abraham’s invitation: turning toward God alone. Being hanif is therefore a living standard within the rites, not merely a historical memory or a word of praise for Abraham.

Following Abraham cannot be exhausted by repeating movements later attributed to him. Anyone who circles his House while granting people independent power to prescribe religion must ask how faithful they remain to his way. Ancestry and family name alone do not confer God’s covenant; physical presence at Abraham’s House alone does not make someone hanif. Yusuf 12:106 even describes belief in God mixed with associating partners. No religious identity, title, or claim of Abrahamic affiliation exempts anyone from testing their beliefs and conduct against devotion to God alone.

The House and Abraham’s station: a place for people

In Al-Baqarah, Abraham is tested and made an imam for people; the account then turns to the House and his station:

Al-Baqarah 2:125

وَإِذْ جَعَلْنَا الْبَيْتَ مَثَابَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَأَمْنًا وَاتَّخِذُوا مِنْ مَقَامِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ مُصَلًّى وَعَهِدْنَا إِلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ أَنْ طَهِّرَا بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْعَاكِفِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

“When We made the House a place of return and safety for people: ‘Take Abraham’s station as a place for salat.’ We charged Abraham and Ishmael: ‘Purify My House for those who circle it, those who remain there in devotion, and those who bow and prostrate.’”

Return, people, safety, Abraham’s station, salat, and purification for worshipers appear together. The House is presented as a place people return to and find safety in, extending beyond the site of an annual ceremony.

The expression mathābatan li-l-nās concerns return and recurring resort. People arriving from far away approach a place that should recall devotion to God and safety. If fear, humiliation, discrimination, or religious monopoly makes that same place inaccessible, what relationship remains between those conditions and the Qur’anic purpose of the House?

Safety cannot be reduced to protection from bodily violence or theft. Reading these passages with the rejection of compulsion in religion (Al-Baqarah 2:256) and obstruction of worship raises questions about freedom of belief and a pilgrim’s peace of mind. Does someone humiliated for their beliefs, afraid to express them, or compelled to adopt the custodians’ interpretation truly enjoy safety worthy of God’s House? The statement “whoever enters it is safe” does not itself enumerate every visitor’s right and freedom. This question arises from relating the House’s safety to other Qur’anic principles; its practical implications require careful examination.

Abraham’s station is described as connected to salat. The expression is not lexically identical to his imamah, or leadership, in the previous verse. Yet their connection matters: Abraham is made an imam for people, and the House is then a place of return and safety for people. Within this sequence, his station recalls someone who walked the path of devotion to God. It does not authorize a family or community to claim ownership of his legacy.

In Al-Baqarah 2:124, Abraham’s question about his descendants receives the answer that God’s covenant does not extend to wrongdoers. This neither excludes every descendant nor guarantees the covenant to all. Ali ‘Imran 3:68 identifies those who follow Abraham as having the closest claim to him. Closeness is not determined solely by descent, affiliation, or control of a sacred place; conduct and direction matter. See Imamah in the Qur’an: Abraham’s Example and the Question of Leadership.

Salat at Abraham’s station must likewise remain connected to life and conduct. Al-‘Ankabut 29:45 associates salat with restraint from indecency and wrongdoing. Someone who establishes it there while persisting in injustice, arrogance, and imposed belief must examine the relationship between their worship and its Qur’anic purpose. This does not deny salat’s practical form; it recalls the connection between form, meaning, and effect. See Salat in the Qur’an for fuller discussion.

In Al-Baqarah 2:127, 2:128, Abraham and Ishmael raise the House’s foundations and ask God to make them surrendered to Him, bring forth a surrendered community among their descendants, and show them their rites. The rites are plainly significant: Abraham asks to learn them. Yet that request stands within a prayer for surrender and acceptance of repentance. The next verse asks for a messenger to recite God’s signs, teach scripture and wisdom, and cultivate people’s purification (2:129). Learning, purification, and human growth stand alongside the House and its rites. The rites remain within devotion and do not replace it.

Ali ‘Imran makes this pattern clearer. Before introducing the House, 3:95 commands following Abraham’s hanif way. Verses 3:96, 3:97 then describe the House at Bakkah as blessed and a source of guidance for all peoples, mention Abraham’s station and safety, and prescribe hajj for those able to find a way to it. Again the sequence begins with Abraham’s path, then turns to the House and pilgrimage. Al-Hajj 22:25 presents the Sacred Mosque as being for people, placing the resident alongside the visitor. Al-Ma’idah 5:97 calls the Kaaba and its associated sacred protections a support for people.

“For people” recurs meaningfully. The invitation, the House, its safety, and its sustaining role concern people, rather than a family’s ownership or a nation’s superiority. The legal scope of this general language, including its relationship to Al-Tawbah 9:28, requires separate examination. Yet any claim to exclusive control must answer to that inclusive Qur’anic language.

Custodianship: does control of the House establish fitness to oversee it?

The Qur’an offers a clear example separating control of the Sacred Mosque from rightful custodianship:

Al-Anfal 8:34

وَمَا لَهُمْ أَلَّا يُعَذِّبَهُمُ اللَّهُ وَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَمَا كَانُوا أَوْلِيَاءَهُ ۚ إِنْ أَوْلِيَاؤُهُ إِلَّا الْمُتَّقُونَ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

“Why should God not punish them while they bar others from the Sacred Mosque, although they are not its rightful guardians? Its guardians are only those mindful of God, but most of them do not know.”

Al-Anfal 8:35

وَمَا كَانَ صَلَاتُهُمْ عِنْدَ الْبَيْتِ إِلَّا مُكَاءً وَتَصْدِيَةً ۚ فَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ

“Their salat at the House was nothing but whistling and clapping. Taste the punishment for what you used to reject.”

People could be present at the House, perform ceremonies, and control others’ access, yet have their claim to guardianship rejected. The first verse places exclusion alongside that claim and identifies mindfulness as the standard. The next criticizes their form of worship at the House.

Possession of a sacred place does not establish fitness to oversee it, just as ceremony beside it does not independently establish sound devotion. This standard cannot apply only to the past while exempting present custodians. Every government, family, or institution administering the House must answer to mindfulness, justice, and the prohibition on obstructing people. Establishing an actual violation nevertheless requires specific evidence about conduct.

Al-Baqarah 2:114 likewise concerns preventing remembrance of God in places of worship and seeking their ruin. Protecting a mosque means preserving its function in devotion, beyond keeping its walls intact.

Al-Tawbah 9:107, 9:108, 9:109, 9:110 describes a mosque built to cause harm and division, evaluating its foundation against mindfulness of God. A building’s designation as a mosque, or its outward upkeep, does not establish the soundness of its builders’ purposes or behavior. This example concerns a building’s foundation and value; it must not be turned without evidence into an entry rule for the Sacred Mosque.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 rejects compulsion in religion. Al-Hajj 22:39, 22:40 concerns defense against oppression and unjust expulsion, naming monasteries, churches, Jewish places of prayer, and mosques together as places where God’s name is frequently remembered. Defending worship and resisting injustice are not confined to protecting one building or religious community.

The next verse, Al-Hajj 22:41, describes those granted power in the land through establishing salat, giving zakat (purification through giving and care for others), enjoining what is right, and restraining wrongdoing. Power and administrative control cannot therefore signify religious superiority by themselves. Their holders must be assessed against these responsibilities.

These passages do not independently settle every entry rule for the Sacred Mosque. They do provide standards for examining monopoly claims. Freedom of belief, human safety, and the prohibition on obstructing remembrance belong within the hajj discussion. See No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Task Is to Deliver.

Why hajj? Human benefit, remembrance, and care for those in need

After commanding the proclamation of hajj, the Qur’an explains why people come:

Al-Hajj 22:28

لِيَشْهَدُوا مَنَافِعَ لَهُمْ وَيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ فِي أَيَّامٍ مَعْلُومَاتٍ عَلَىٰ مَا رَزَقَهُمْ مِنْ بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْبَائِسَ الْفَقِيرَ

“So that they may witness benefits for themselves and remember God’s name on known days over the livestock He has provided for them. Eat from it and feed the destitute poor.”

The purpose is not confined to one act or benefit. The Qur’an speaks of benefits without listing every kind in that phrase, then connects remembrance immediately with eating from the offering and feeding the poor. Remembrance and human well-being remain together.

The benefits may be spiritual, social, and material; no single meaning should be imposed without evidence. Al-Baqarah 2:198 permits seeking God’s bounty during pilgrimage, so trade and economic benefit are not rejected in themselves. Legitimate benefit must nevertheless be distinguished from monopolistic profit, exclusion, and exploitation of religious need.

Al-Ma’idah 5:97’s “support for people” extends beyond individual gain. The House and its sacred protections concern the stability of human life: safety, livelihood, cooperation, public good, and responsibility toward others. How can hajj’s benefits be discussed while disregarding people deprived of necessities to afford it, or subjected to pressure and discrimination around it?

Surat Quraysh connects the Lord of the House to two fundamental needs:

Quraysh 106:3, 106:4

فَلْيَعْبُدُوا رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ

الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُمْ مِنْ جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُمْ مِنْ خَوْفٍ

“Let them worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and made them safe from fear.”

Addressing Quraysh, these verses turn attention from the House to its Lord and identify food and safety as His gifts. Alongside the hajj passages, the implication is clear: the House should recall the God who frees people from hunger and fear. It should not become a means of making others hungry, fearful, or humiliated.

The question is therefore more than how many people performed hajj or how much was spent improving the sanctuary. What does the House contribute to people’s safety and well-being? If someone claims to serve it while their conduct produces hunger, fear, and deprivation, can outward service alone prove fidelity to its Lord?

The connection with iman—faith—also becomes clearer. Qur’anic iman is more than a communal label or spoken claim; it must become evident in honesty, trustworthiness, good action, and conduct. The words for faith and safety belong to the root family ʾ-m-n, although their uses should not all be flattened into one meaning. A House designated as a place of safety cannot be understood apart from responsibility for others’ security. See Iman in the Qur’an for a fuller examination.

Social benefit does not, however, mean that hajj can simply be replaced by charity, assistance to the poor, or good deeds performed anywhere. The Qur’an identifies a House and real rites for hajj. The concern is to preserve their connection to their stated purposes, not remove their tangible forms.

Sacred signs and rites: the form of worship in the service of mindfulness

The Qur’an affirms sacred observances. It identifies Safa and Marwah as God’s signs (Al-Baqarah 2:158), speaks of circling the House (Al-Hajj 22:29), includes offering animals among the sacred signs (22:36), and connects honoring them to mindfulness in the heart (22:32). Attention to inward meaning cannot justify abandoning every outward act.

At the same time, the form must not displace its purpose:

Al-Hajj 22:37

لَنْ يَنَالَ اللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَاؤُهَا وَلَٰكِنْ يَنَالُهُ التَّقْوَىٰ مِنْكُمْ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرَهَا لَكُمْ لِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

“Neither their flesh nor their blood reaches God. What reaches Him is your mindfulness. Thus He has made them available to you, so that you may proclaim His greatness for guiding you. Give good news to those who do good.”

The verse explains the offering from within the passages prescribing it. The animal is slaughtered, God’s name remembered, its meat eaten, and a share provided to those in need. None implies that God needs flesh or blood. Mindfulness is fundamental to the act’s meaning.

Abraham and his son’s trial in Al-Saffat 37:102, 37:103, 37:104, 37:105, 37:106, 37:107 likewise concerns surrender to God and the ransom substituted for the son’s slaughter. It can direct attention toward relinquishing attachments and placing God’s command before personal desire. Yet every rule for pilgrimage offerings, or detail of later narratives, cannot be derived from that account. Its precise relationship to hady and sacrifice in hajj will be examined in the discussion of rites.

The distinction extends beyond offerings. Al-Baqarah 2:177 does not confine goodness to facing east or west; it proceeds to faith, care for those in need, keeping commitments, salat, zakat, and endurance. It does not declare outward orientation meaningless. It warns against reducing goodness to a direction or movement. Someone facing the correct location while neglecting justice and commitments must still assess their conduct against the reality of goodness.

Al-A‘raf 7:29 brings justice, upright orientation, and sincere devotion together:

Al-A‘raf 7:29

قُلْ أَمَرَ رَبِّي بِالْقِسْطِ ۖ وَأَقِيمُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ عِنْدَ كُلِّ مَسْجِدٍ وَادْعُوهُ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ ۚ كَمَا بَدَأَكُمْ تَعُودُونَ

“Say: My Lord commands justice. Set your faces upright at every place of worship and call upon Him, making your devotion sincerely His. As He first brought you into being, you will return.”

Presence in a place of worship is connected here to justice and sincerity. Al-Baqarah 2:112 likewise joins surrendering one’s face or orientation to God with doing good. If someone turns their body toward the House while their thinking and conduct turn toward power, prejudice, or superiority, what have they gained from that outward movement?

Al-Nisa 4:125 brings surrendering one’s orientation to God, goodness, and following Abraham’s hanif way into a single verse. It clarifies the role of conduct and orientation alongside Abraham’s example, without eliminating the real House or prescribed pilgrimage acts.

This discussion does not deny the qibla’s real direction or the Kaaba’s location. As Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Qur’an explains, physical direction and the orientation of one’s life must neither be confused nor substituted for each other. Direction and sacred observance have their place, but do not exhaust devotion.

In the same verse that discusses crescents and the timing of hajj, Al-Baqarah 2:189 separates goodness from a particular outward manner of entering houses and locates its criterion in mindfulness. Al-Baqarah 2:197 calls mindfulness the best provision while discussing hajj. These repeated connections make it a standard within the rites themselves, rather than a supplementary recommendation after they end.

Al-Hajj 22:34 and 22:67 describe rites for different communities. Different forms of worship do not necessarily imply different objects of worship. After mentioning communal rites, 22:34 affirms one God and surrender to Him. Al-Shura 42:13 similarly calls different prophets’ communities to uphold din—their devotion and religious commitment—and avoid division within it. Al-Ma’idah 5:48 speaks of different prescribed ways and paths, then calls people to compete in doing good.

In Al-Hajj 22:34 and 22:67, God is the one who appoints rites for communities. Their existence does not establish independent human authority to prescribe religion. Equally, a rite’s familiarity does not prove, without further evidence, that all historical details of its performance were made obligatory by God.

This distinction becomes especially significant in relation to the House. The Qur’an calls it “a place of return for people” (Al-Baqarah 2:125), directs the call to hajj to people (Al-Hajj 22:27), and describes the Sacred Mosque as being “for people” (22:25). If the same Qur’an speaks of the rites of different communities in 22:34 and 22:67, and places different prescribed ways alongside the call to excel in good deeds in Al-Ma’idah 5:48, on what basis should one school’s detailed legal rules become the universal standard for performing the House’s observances? Would that requirement not force other seekers of God to choose between staying away from these rites and adopting one community’s particular prescribed way?

The distinction here is between the rites themselves and the ways they are performed. What God prescribes is binding on those addressed by that command. But not every count, sequence, or verbal formula transmitted in the name of tradition can be treated, without evidence, as His command to everyone. In light of the House’s purpose for people and the diversity of prescribed ways, leaving some details unstated can be understood as meaningful latitude for carrying out the command in different circumstances and traditions. This is an inference about the wisdom of that latitude drawn from the passages together; the Qur’an does not explicitly give this as the reason for leaving every such detail unstated. The specific ruling in Al-Tawbah 9:28 must also be examined in its own place and context.

We should neither erase every distinction among historical forms of worship nor treat one later legal form as the whole of din. Differences in rites require examination without turning devotion into competition for sectarian superiority. The relationships among din, islam as surrender, and shariah as a prescribed way are explored in Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Qur’an.

Al-An‘am 6:159 and Al-Rum 30:32 warn against dividing religion into separate factions. Differences in prescribed ways and practices must not be confused with factionalism or a monopoly on religious truth. At the close of Al-Hajj, 22:78 brings Abraham’s way together with the absence of undue hardship in religion, salat, zakat, and holding fast to God. His way remains connected to the other responsibilities of devotion.

Ability, timing, and personal responsibility

The Qur’an identifies a condition when prescribing hajj:

Ali ‘Imran 3:97

فِيهِ آيَاتٌ بَيِّنَاتٌ مَقَامُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ ۖ وَمَنْ دَخَلَهُ كَانَ آمِنًا ۗ وَلِلَّهِ عَلَى النَّاسِ حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ مَنِ اسْتَطَاعَ إِلَيْهِ سَبِيلًا ۚ وَمَنْ كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ عَنِ الْعَالَمِينَ

“In it are clear signs, including Abraham’s station. Whoever enters it is to be safe. Hajj to the House is owed to God by people who are able to find a way to it. Whoever rejects—God has no need of any of the worlds.”

“Whoever is able to find a way to it” does not restrict ability to possessing a specified amount of money. The wording requires considering the person’s capacity and the possibility of reaching the House. Physical health, route safety, financial resources, psychological condition, and family circumstances can affect that capacity. These are potential applications of the condition, not a detailed list supplied by the verse itself.

One measure cannot assess everyone. A resident near Mecca and a low-income traveler supporting a family in a distant country face different circumstances. Even two people with similar assets may differ greatly in health, safety, and responsibilities. The Qur’an establishes the condition without prescribing a fixed amount, distance, or table for its application. Honest assessment must take actual responsibilities into account, avoiding both rigid financial thresholds and invented excuses to evade responsibility.

Sometimes the obstacle is imposed by others rather than arising from personal health or finances. If religious exclusion, selective restrictions, or unjustified charges beyond necessary services prevent someone otherwise able to travel, two matters must be distinguished. That person may now lack an actual route to hajj, but the obstruction does not thereby become legitimate. The Qur’an both conditions hajj on finding a way (Ali ‘Imran 3:97) and condemns barring the Sacred Mosque (Al-Anfal 8:34; Al-Hajj 22:25). An inaccessible route can affect the pilgrim’s responsibility without removing the responsibility of those unjustly blocking it. Distinguishing necessary safety and administrative measures from unjust barriers requires separate examination.

Hajj also has a temporal setting. Al-Baqarah 2:189 identifies lunar crescents as markers of time for people and hajj, while 2:197 speaks of known pilgrimage months. Their precise identity, relationship to sacred months, and the Qur’anic basis of present calendar restrictions will be examined in the rites section. For now, the point is that hajj is connected to both a real destination and a recognized time.

Ability to travel is only part of the question. Conduct after undertaking the responsibility also matters:

Al-Baqarah 2:197

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ ۚ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ ۗ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ ۗ وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ ۚ وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

“Hajj is in known months. Whoever undertakes hajj in them must refrain from rafath, fusuq, and jidal during hajj. Whatever good you do, God knows it. Take provisions, but the best provision is mindfulness. Be mindful of Me, people of understanding.”

The verse emphasizes conduct rather than beginning with counts of movements. Rafath concerns sexual conduct or indecent sexual speech; fusuq concerns disobedience or transgression; and jidal concerns disputation or contentious argument. They are named together before the call to goodness and mindfulness. Ritual observance remains an ethical responsibility. Correct movements cannot excuse carelessness in speech, conduct, or relationships.

Nor does this make prescribed acts insignificant. The Qur’an emphasizes conduct while also discussing completion of hajj and umrah, circling the House, remembrance, offerings, and other acts. The issue is not choosing between action and mindfulness. Action must not stand in for mindfulness.

Can service to the House replace faith and good action?

Al-Tawbah provides a particularly clear standard for assessing form and meaning:

Al-Tawbah 9:19

أَجَعَلْتُمْ سِقَايَةَ الْحَاجِّ وَعِمَارَةَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ كَمَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَجَاهَدَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۚ لَا يَسْتَوُونَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ

“Have you made providing water to pilgrims and maintaining the Sacred Mosque equal to believing in God and the Last Day and striving in His way? They are not equal before God. God does not guide wrongdoing people.”

Even water provision and maintenance, for all their practical worth, are not placed on the same footing as faith and striving in God’s way. Service to pilgrims and care of the House deserve respect, but do not independently certify spiritual superiority or replace faith.

Someone investing heavily in the sanctuary while departing from justice and mindfulness cannot cite construction alone as proof of legitimacy. An institution’s or government’s service role does not exempt its conduct from Qur’anic assessment. The standards applying to ordinary people apply to the custodians too.

This reaches the study’s central concern. If even direct service and maintenance cannot be equated with faith and action in God’s way, how can precision in circuits, pebbles, clothing, and other details alone establish the fulfillment of hajj’s meaning?

Hajj in its proper place

Taken together, the verses describe worship with a real location, time, and acts, whose meaning extends beyond those outward forms. It begins with Abraham’s call to God alone, connects to the House and his station, gathers people from afar, concerns their safety and benefit, and asks them to enter this path with remembrance, mindfulness, and responsibility toward others.

Hajj is neither worship of the Kaaba nor unquestioning submission to its administrators. The Kaaba is a real and inviolable House, not a deity. Abraham guides and exemplifies devotion without taking God’s place. The rites must retain their Qur’anic position: neither eliminated nor magnified until God’s oneness, the hanif way, and people’s rights disappear beneath them.

We now have a clearer standard. The Qur’an affirms acts and sacred observances while situating them alongside the hanif way, mindfulness, faith, justice, safety, and human benefit. Correct movements do not validate every belief or behavior. Administration of the House does not give a government or institution independent authority to prescribe religion or monopolize devotion to God.

The first question is therefore how the journey relates to God’s command, Abraham’s way, mindfulness, and people’s rights, rather than simply the count of circuits or thrown pebbles. Any number or sequence God has prescribed retains its significance, but the divine authority of each requirement must be established separately.

With these foundations clearer, customary rites can be assessed. The next section asks which acts are actually God’s commands, which are human choices or traditions, and which requirements or institutions conflict with the principles established for hajj and the House. Mere transmission from the past does not answer those questions.

God’s House must be understood through Abraham’s hanif way. Reaching the House does not, by itself, mean walking his path.

5. Assessing current hajj practices through Qur’anic standards

The preceding section connected hajj with devotion to God alone, Abraham’s hanif way, safety, mindfulness, and human benefit. We must now ask how far present practice accords with those principles. Do its rules and institutions still serve those purposes, or have some displaced God’s command?

Foundations must take precedence over subsidiary questions. Before disputing circuits, garments, locations for throwing pebbles, or prayer formulas, we must identify who may prescribe these rules and whether the system enforcing them honors the Qur’an’s foundations. Exact performance cannot compensate for violating God’s oneness, justice, freedom to worship, or people’s rights.

A. Testing the foundations

Does devotion to God alone mean only avoiding stone idols?

When assigning Abraham the House, God commands him to associate nothing with Him and purify it for worshipers (Al-Hajj 22:26). Among the subsequent passages on sacred observances, He again calls for being hanif toward God and rejecting partners (22:31).

Yet shirk, associating partners with God, encompasses more than bowing before statues or believing in several creators. Al-Shura 42:21 concerns prescribing religion without God’s authorization. Al-Tawbah 9:31 criticizes making religious scholars and monks into lords. Muslims cannot be exempted from a standard applied to Jews and Christians.

The Qur’an also gives examples of attributing invented rules to God. Al-Ma’idah 5:103 states that He did not institute particular restrictions people imposed concerning livestock. Al-A‘raf 7:28 depicts inherited conduct being called God’s command, while 7:31, 7:32 challenges prohibitions on adornment and provision He produced for His servants. A custom or prohibition does not acquire divine origin through prevalence, even when practiced in religion’s name.

A ruler, jurist, or institution making unsupported rules obligatory in God’s name, or claiming independent power to determine the lawful and forbidden, must face this standard. The question applies equally to the religious and political authorities governing hajj, including the House of Saud, and to schools associated with juristic opinion, hadith-based authority, and other religious approaches. Identifying as Muslim, a religious scholar, or a servant of the two sanctuaries confers no immunity.

Not every legal disagreement or mistaken interpretation can immediately be called shirk. What requires demonstration is the granting of independent religious legislative authority to someone other than God, or attribution of an unauthorized command to Him. See The Qur’anic Root sh-r-k for its implications for judgment, obedience, and religious prescription.

The first question is therefore who may declare throwing pebbles a divine command and prescribe invalidity or expiation for omitting it, before asking how many pebbles to throw.

Does administering the Sacred Mosque mean owning it?

Al-Hajj 22:25 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ الَّذِي جَعَلْنَاهُ لِلنَّاسِ سَوَاءً الْعَاكِفُ فِيهِ وَالْبَادِ ۚ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ فِيهِ بِإِلْحَادٍ بِظُلْمٍ نُذِقْهُ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

“Those who reject and bar others from God’s way and the Sacred Mosque—which We established for people, with resident and visitor equal there—and whoever intends wrongful deviation there: We shall make them taste painful punishment.”

The Sacred Mosque is established for people, not presented as the exclusive property of a nation or family. Resident and visitor are placed on an equal footing.

Administration, security, cleanliness, and orderly access remain necessary. But management differs from absolute ownership or authority to deprive people of rights. Taking charge of the House cannot give a government independent authority over people’s devotion.

Al-Anfal 8:34 likewise criticizes those barring others while lacking rightful guardianship. Mindfulness of God is the standard, rather than political control, military strength, or family affiliation.

Restrictions that deny access through religious discrimination, self-interest, or unjustified reasons must answer to these verses. Measures necessary to protect life and safety are different from unjustly barring God’s House.

Does Al-Tawbah 9:28 authorize excluding all People of the Book?

This requires separate examination. Confusing mushrikin, those who associate partners with God, with the People of the Book may yield a conclusion the Qur’an does not state.

Al-Tawbah 9:28 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّمَا الْمُشْرِكُونَ نَجَسٌ فَلَا يَقْرَبُوا الْمَسْجِدَ الْحَرَامَ بَعْدَ عَامِهِمْ هَٰذَا ۚ وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ عَيْلَةً فَسَوْفَ يُغْنِيكُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ إِنْ شَاءَ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

“You who believe: those who associate partners with God are unclean, so they must not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year of theirs. If you fear poverty, God will enrich you from His bounty, if He wills. God is knowing and wise.”

The verse names “those who associate partners,” not “the People of the Book.” That distinction cannot be ignored.

Al-Baqarah 2:105 distinguishes the designations:

مَا يَوَدُّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ وَلَا الْمُشْرِكِينَ أَنْ يُنَزَّلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ يَخْتَصُّ بِرَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَاللَّهُ ذُو الْفَضْلِ الْعَظِيمِ

“Those who reject among the People of the Book, and those who associate partners, do not wish any good from your Lord to be sent down to you. But God singles out for His mercy whom He wills. God possesses immense bounty.”

Within Al-Tawbah itself, 9:28 discusses those who associate partners, while 9:29 identifies the other group as “those given the Book.” The Qur’an distinguishes these terms when addressing and identifying its audiences.

Al-Hajj 22:17 also names Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magians, and those who associate partners separately. Distinct designations do not independently settle anyone’s right of entry, but they prevent us from merging every group into one legal category without evidence.

The chapter’s opening context matters. Al-Tawbah 9:1, 9:2, 9:3, 9:4, 9:5, 9:6, 9:7, 9:8, 9:9, 9:10, 9:11, 9:12, 9:13 discusses treaties with those who associate partners, some parties’ faithfulness, others’ breaches, and aggression against believers. Verse 9:4 requires honoring agreements with faithful parties; 9:6 commands protection for a person seeking refuge and safe passage to a place of security. Verse 9:13 concerns treaty-breaking and plans to expel the Messenger.

Al-Tawbah 9:7 also requires continued fidelity to agreements made at the Sacred Mosque while the other party remains faithful. In this context, treatment does not rest solely on a religious label: honoring commitments matters. This observation alone nevertheless cannot conclusively define the scope of 9:28’s prohibition.

The ruling should therefore remain connected to the chapter’s historical relationships and conflicts. Conversely, context alone does not establish, without argument, that its wording applies exclusively to treaty-breakers. Its precise reach must be examined through the passages together.

Al-Tawbah 9:17, 9:18 concerns maintaining places of worship and fitness for that responsibility; 9:28 concerns approaching the Sacred Mosque. Maintenance and custodianship rules must not automatically be equated with entry rules. Al-Ma’idah 5:2 also refuses to make hostility toward a group that previously obstructed access a license for aggression. These passages inform the House’s sanctity and people’s rights without erasing the specific command of 9:28.

Najās denotes uncleanness or impurity, but the verse does not restrict it explicitly to physical contamination. Neither can it simply be translated “treaty-breaking” without evidence. Its relationship to shirk, the conduct condemned in the chapter, and the scope of exclusion must be examined. The ending, “if you fear poverty,” also recognizes economic consequences. None of these observations justifies turning najās into an inherent label for every religious or political opponent.

The firmer conclusion is that “those who associate partners” cannot automatically encompass every scriptural community. The Qur’an may characterize particular beliefs or conduct among the People of the Book as shirk, as in Al-Tawbah 9:31. Describing conduct in those terms is different from placing every member of a religious community under one legal ruling.

Anyone using 9:28 to exclude all Jews, Christians, or other seekers of God must first establish why its ruling includes those groups. Different prescribed religious ways, or rejection of a particular legal school, do not themselves supply that evidence.

Equally, “for people” must not be read so broadly that 9:28’s specific command disappears. Both must be examined in place: neither expanding the designation without grounds nor denying the particular ruling.

This connects to Iman in the Qur’an and the Reflections on Al-Baqarah, which examine faith, rejection, associating partners, and religious identity as distinct questions.

Can people be compelled to follow a particular school at God’s House?

Al-Baqarah 2:256 rejects compulsion in religion. Yunus 10:99 asks whether the Messenger would compel people to believe. If he is not tasked with imposing faith on conscience, on what grounds could a government or religious group claim that power?

A House connected to safety, the hanif way, and sincere devotion must not become a place for humiliating people over religious differences or forcing acceptance of the custodians’ beliefs. Preserving public order and preventing harm differ from policing personal belief or imposing one interpretation.

Al-Hajj 22:39, 22:40 concerns resistance to oppression and protection of places where God is remembered, including monasteries, churches, Jewish places of prayer, and mosques. Protecting worship and resisting injustice are not confined to one religious identity.

Freedom of belief and the inviolability of worship cannot be discarded while precision in legal details is presented as complete fidelity to Abrahamic hajj. See No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Task Is to Deliver.

Does service to hajj establish the custodians’ legitimacy?

Al-Tawbah 9:19 does not equate providing water and maintaining the Sacred Mosque with faith and striving in God’s way. Even indispensable, valuable services do not establish spiritual superiority or validate all the providers’ beliefs and conduct.

Roads, accommodation, water, and building maintenance can be genuine services. They do not exempt an institution from accountability for justice, mindfulness, people’s rights, or rulings issued in God’s name.

Service must be distinguished from domination. A custodian is responsible for protecting the House and enabling worship, but service does not confer ownership of people’s religion.

The same applies to individuals. Repeated hajj, expenditure on pilgrims, and numerous devotional acts do not independently validate someone who persists in injustice, arrogance, or imposed belief.

What standing do ritual details have when the foundations are violated?

If a system substitutes human religious authority for God’s command, unjustly obstructs the House, restricts freedom of devotion, or uses its administration for religious superiority, can precise circuit counts, ihram locations, and other details establish complete fidelity to Qur’anic hajj?

The preceding verses show why observing a subsidiary detail cannot compensate for violating a foundation. An authority at odds with Qur’anic principles cannot establish its detailed rulings’ divine origin merely by asserting them. Each ruling must be assessed independently.

This does not make every historical custom intrinsically wrong. The question concerns attributing it to God and imposing it as a religious duty. A customary act may be compatible with the Qur’an, but compatibility alone establishes neither obligation nor invalidity if omitted.

With that distinction in place, we can examine the rites without losing the central question in subsidiary disagreements.

B. Twenty questions about the rites

1. Is hajj obligatory for everyone in every circumstance?

Ali ‘Imran 3:97 prescribes hajj to the House for those able to find a way to it. The expression does not confine ability to possession of a specified sum of money.

Physical and psychological condition, route safety, resources, and real family responsibilities can affect ability. Someone who must leave a family in need, take on heavy debt, or abandon indispensable care for dependents is not necessarily in the same position as someone able to travel without those burdens.

Where a government or institution unjustly blocks access, the pilgrim’s circumstances must be distinguished from the obstructer’s responsibility. The absence of an actual route can affect ability without making the barrier legitimate.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 prescribes an offering when completion of hajj or umrah is prevented. Al-Fath 48:25 describes believers barred from the Sacred Mosque and offerings prevented from reaching their destination. Obstruction is a real Qur’anic concern. Yet rules for someone who has begun and is then prevented must not automatically be extended to everyone unable to travel in the first place.

The Qur’an establishes ability as a condition without fixing an amount of money or placing everyone in identical circumstances.

2. Has God prescribed hajj exactly once in a lifetime?

The Qur’an states the obligation and condition of ability without specifying how many times each person must perform hajj over a lifetime.

Without further argument, we cannot say that God explicitly fixed the number at one. Neither does the Qur’an command every able person to repeat hajj annually.

The sufficiency of one performance to discharge the obligation can be a matter of inference, but must not be confused with an explicit numerical prescription. Voluntary repetition is not intrinsically prohibited; an annual obligation attributed to God requires evidence.

3. Are hajj and umrah the same act of worship?

The Qur’an names them separately. Al-Baqarah 2:196 commands completion of both for God, while 2:158 speaks of performing hajj to the House or umrah.

The designations are distinct despite their shared connection to the House and some practices. These verses alone do not establish that timing is their only difference.

Umrah is not described through “known months” in the same way as hajj. The absence of that restriction does not validate every detail of contemporary umrah law. Moreover, 2:196 commands its completion; whether that establishes an independent obligation on every able person requires careful examination of the command’s force.

Umrah and ʿimārah, maintenance or habitation, belong to the root ʿ-m-r, but a shared root does not make them identical. References to maintaining the Sacred Mosque cannot independently establish umrah’s detailed rules.

The distinction between hajj and umrah is Qur’anic. Naming them does not establish every subsequent classification and requirement.

4. Is hajj confined to a few dates in Dhu al-Hijjah?

Al-Baqarah 2:197 places hajj in “known months,” while 2:189 identifies lunar crescents as time markers for people and pilgrimage.

Al-Hajj 22:28 speaks of known days, Al-Baqarah 2:203 of numbered days, and Al-Tawbah 9:3 of the day of the greater hajj. These expressions require examination together.

Hajj clearly has a prescribed temporal setting. But 2:197 does not list the names or number of its months, and the eighth through thirteenth days of Dhu al-Hijjah, the month traditionally associated with the main pilgrimage rites, are not specified in that form in the Qur’an.

Al-Tawbah 9:36 identifies four sacred months. Al-Ma’idah 5:97 places the Sacred House, sacred month, offerings, and their identifying garlands together as a support for people; 5:2 likewise connects the sacred month, offerings, and people heading toward the House. The connection among sacred time, place, offerings, and route security is Qur’anic. Yet “the Sacred House” describes the House; it does not independently establish that all four sacred months are exactly the pilgrimage months. Their relationship requires other passages, including those on known and numbered days.

Al-Tawbah 9:37 discusses nasīʾ, postponement, and treating months as lawful or sacred in different years. This matters for the sacred-month system and alteration of its protections. It cannot alone identify the precise pilgrimage months or the time limits for every rite.

Confining every act to a few dates therefore requires Qur’anic reasoning if presented as God’s certain command. Conversely, “known months” alone does not prove that every rite can be performed equally on every day of those months. The effects of concentrating activity on safety, crowds, and ability should also be examined, without turning a practical conclusion into a Qur’anic ruling.

5. Does the Qur’an prescribe the complete system of tamattuʿ, ifrad, and qiran?

Al-Baqarah 2:196 discusses benefiting from umrah until hajj and, under specified conditions, prescribes an offering or fasting when none can be found.

That ruling has a clear textual basis. The complete three-part legal system—tamattuʿ, ifrad, and qiran, categories governing how hajj and umrah are undertaken—with all its distinct conditions, sequences, and rulings is not listed in that form in the Qur’an.

The verse ends with a qualification concerning those whose families are not resident at the Sacred Mosque. Its scope must be examined in relation to benefiting from umrah until hajj, offerings, and fasting, rather than indiscriminately applied to every pilgrimage rule. A practical distinction between residents and nonresidents does not itself establish unequal rights to devotion or access.

What 2:196 actually states must therefore be distinguished from the full later legal classification. A technical legal name cannot turn additional rules into God’s certain commands.

6. What is ihram, and which restrictions are Qur’anic?

The Qur’an recognizes the state described as hurum and gives rules for it. Al-Ma’idah 5:95, 5:96 prohibits land hunting in that state and describes remedies for deliberate hunting. Al-Baqarah 2:197 prohibits rafath, fusuq, and jidal for someone undertaking hajj: sexual or indecent conduct, transgression, and contentious dispute.

Al-Ma’idah 5:1, 5:2 also refers to this protected state and leaving it. The state has textual support, but those expressions alone do not determine which garments, words, or formalities bring someone into it. Nor should a person’s consecrated state automatically be equated with the sanctity of months or the House’s location.

These are actual Qur’anic rulings open to examination.

The broader legal lists of ihram prohibitions cannot simply be placed on the same footing. Restrictions concerning mirrors, perfume, particular footwear or coverings, and expiations for details on which the Qur’an gives no ruling require independent support.

The existence of ihram must also be distinguished from the procedure for entering it. The Qur’an does not specify every opening formality.

7. Are two white cloths and specified entry boundaries obligatory?

The Qur’an neither prescribes white pilgrimage clothing nor identifies two particular cloths as the only valid garments. It does not name today’s geographical miqat locations, the boundaries used for entering ihram.

Suitable, simple, or uniform dress may be a practical choice. Making one form a condition of valid hajj requires divine authorization. Similarly, designated starting locations presented as God’s certain requirement need Qur’anic support.

This does not permit disregard for cleanliness, appropriate covering, or respect for others. The distinction is between real human responsibilities and special requirements claimed as divine rites.

8. Must the talbiyah and pilgrimage prayers follow fixed wording and counts?

The pilgrimage passages emphasize remembrance, including Al-Hajj 22:28 and Al-Baqarah 2:198 and 2:203. Al-Baqarah 2:201 also records a prayer for good in this life and the next.

Remembrance, prayer, and attention to God belong within hajj. Yet the Qur’an does not prescribe the familiar talbiyah, the pilgrim’s spoken response to God’s call, in its complete current wording as the only required opening formula. Nor does it assign a separate compulsory prayer and count to every circuit, stage of movement between Safa and Marwah, or pebble.

People can remember God in language and prayers they understand. Without Qur’anic evidence, no one should make a particular phrase a condition of validity or invalidate another’s worship for omitting it.

9. Is circling the House prescribed? Must there be seven circuits?

Circling the House, or tawaf, is clearly present. Al-Baqarah 2:125 and Al-Hajj 22:26 mention those who circle it, while 22:29 commands circling the Ancient House.

These verses do not specify seven circuits, the precise direction, each circuit’s starting point, or the detailed rules associated with them.

The distinction matters. The absence of a number does not justify abandoning tawaf, whose basis is explicit. Anyone claiming only seven circuits are valid, while six or eight invalidate worship, must identify where God established that condition.

The Qur’an supplies explicit numbers elsewhere: three days of fasting during hajj and seven after returning in 2:196, for example. Specifying numbers for some acts but not tawaf is a distinction worth taking seriously. Silence about a number does not, however, prohibit choosing seven circuits.

10. Are kissing the Black Stone and particular tawaf movements obligatory?

The pilgrimage verses do not require kissing, touching, or gesturing toward the Black Stone as conditions of valid tawaf. They also do not specify men leaving a shoulder uncovered, moving quickly during particular circuits, or several other familiar details.

Such acts may have histories or symbolic meanings, but history alone does not make them obligatory. Choosing an act voluntarily differs from imposing it on others as God’s command.

No optional custom justifies harming, pressing against, or violating the rights of other pilgrims. Human dignity and the prohibition on injustice cannot be disregarded to reach a stone or perform a movement.

11. What does the Qur’an prescribe about Abraham’s station and salat after tawaf?

Al-Baqarah 2:125 instructs people to take Abraham’s station as a place for salat. That connection has clear Qur’anic support.

The verse does not specify exactly two rakʿahs, units of formal prayer, after every tawaf with a particular legal sequence and conditions. The command should neither be denied nor supplemented without argument by details it does not state.

Abraham’s station is a place connected with the House and worship. Remembering his devotion to God alone can deepen presence there, but that spiritual reading must not displace the verse’s spatial meaning.

For salat’s meaning and relationship to conduct, see Salat in the Qur’an.

12. Are Safa and Marwah Qur’anic observances? Is sevenfold movement prescribed?

Al-Baqarah 2:158 identifies Safa and Marwah as God’s sacred signs and discusses going around or between them for someone performing hajj or umrah.

They cannot be removed from Qur’anic sacred observances. The wording, however, says there is “no blame” in performing the act. This does not by itself command obligation: 2:198 similarly removes blame from seeking bounty during hajj without making trade compulsory. Conversely, absence of blame alone does not establish that the act could never be obligatory. The relationship of this wording to an obligation of saʿy, the movement between Safa and Marwah, requires examining the passages together. Every legal condition of validity cannot be derived from it alone.

The verse does not specify seven traversals, how to count outward and return journeys, running in a designated part, or fixed prayers.

The sacred observance must be distinguished from details of execution. The Qur’an identifies Safa and Marwah; anyone making a specific number or manner a definite validity condition must provide its Qur’anic evidence.

13. Are Arafat and the Sacred Monument mentioned?

Al-Baqarah 2:198 describes departure from Arafat and remembrance at al-Mashʿar al-Haram, the Sacred Monument or sacred place of observance. Verse 2:199 also describes departing with the people and seeking forgiveness.

Presence at Arafat, departure from it, and remembrance at the Sacred Monument therefore have clear Qur’anic support.

These verses do not specify exact starting and ending hours at Arafat, a particular date, or detailed validity rulings for missing a prescribed time window.

The names Muzdalifah and Mina are not given in these verses. Geographical identification and associated transmitted practices must be examined separately from explicit commands.

Here too, incomplete specification of details does not remove the underlying act; neither does familiarity turn every detail into God’s instruction.

14. Does the Qur’an prescribe throwing pebbles at the jamarat?

The pilgrimage passages give no command to throw stones at the jamarat, identify three pillars or throwing sites, require seven pebbles, or distribute the throwing over specified days.

Nor does Abraham and his son’s trial in Al-Saffat 37:102, 37:103, 37:104, 37:105, 37:106, 37:107 describe Abraham stoning Satan at three places as pilgrimage rites.

The Qur’an alone therefore cannot establish this practice as a divine obligation or its omission as grounds for invalidating hajj.

Anyone assigning it symbolic meaning must distinguish that interpretation from God’s command. Symbolism alone cannot establish obligation, expiation, or a validity condition.

This illustrates the governing principle: the person asserting obligatory stone-throwing bears the burden of proof, rather than the person asking for Qur’anic evidence.

15. What place do offerings have, and are they equally obligatory for every pilgrim?

Offerings have clear Qur’anic support. Al-Baqarah 2:196 discusses hady in specified circumstances; Al-Hajj 22:28, 22:34, and 22:36 connect offerings, remembrance of God’s name, and feeding those in need.

Al-Hajj 22:33 also discusses benefits until an appointed time and the offerings’ destination in relation to the Ancient House. It must be read with 2:196 when examining where hady belongs, without deriving every later geographical detail and classification from it.

The conditions matter. Al-Baqarah 2:196 addresses obstruction of completion and benefiting from umrah until hajj. For someone in the latter circumstance unable to obtain an offering, it prescribes three days of fasting during hajj and seven upon return.

Its rulings cannot be indiscriminately extended to every pilgrim without examining those conditions.

Al-Hajj 22:37 explains that neither meat nor blood reaches God: mindfulness matters. The preceding passages also prescribe eating from the offering and feeding others.

An offering is neither a meaningless ceremony to discard at will nor an act whose entire purpose is fulfilled by slaughter alone, regardless of mindfulness and the needy person’s rights.

16. Must every Muslim slaughter an animal annually on Eid al-Adha?

The Qur’an clearly discusses offerings within hajj. It does not state, in this form, a command requiring every Muslim in every land to slaughter an animal on a particular date each year.

Al-Kawthar 108:2 speaks of nahr, slaughter or sacrifice. That verse alone does not establish the festival’s date, annual frequency, and a universal obligation for everyone.

People may spend their wealth to feed the poor or assist those in need, including slaughtering an animal, at any time of year. This can be generosity and goodness. Making it an annual compulsory rite for everyone requires separate Qur’anic grounds.

For pilgrimage offerings, the Qur’an also does not command that pilgrims personally perform the slaughter. What must be preserved is fulfillment where prescribed, remembrance of God’s name, and responsibility for the offering’s benefit. Entrusting its execution to someone else does not itself establish invalidity. Delegation must nevertheless not result in ignorance about its destination, waste, or loss of the meat.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 describes hady reaching its destination, and Al-Ma’idah 5:95 describes an offering reaching the Kaaba. These emphasize destination and fulfillment without providing every operational detail of slaughter.

17. Do trimming nails, beard, or hair invalidate an offering?

The Qur’an contains no explicit ruling that trimming nails or a beard invalidates the offering.

It does give a specific rule for shaving the head in Al-Baqarah 2:196: refrain until the offering reaches its destination, with compensation through fasting, charity, or a sacrificial rite for illness or an affliction of the head. These three options are not substitutes for shaving. They are compensation connected to the exception for illness or affliction. The rule’s scope must also be examined alongside obstruction and the verse’s other conditions.

Al-Fath 48:27 describes shaved or shortened hair in the vision of safe entry to the Sacred Mosque. It does not specify the full legal rules concerning body hair, beards, and nails.

A particular rule about the head and hady must not become a general catalogue of prohibitions on all body hair and nails, with unsupported expiations or invalidation attached to each.

Al-Hajj 22:29 also speaks of ending tafath. Arabic dictionaries associate the word with dishevelment, dirt, and uncleanness, and its removal. Identifying its specific ritual applications—shaving, trimming nails, and other acts—has also depended on exegetical reports; some lexicographers have noted limited independent linguistic evidence. The Qur’anic expression should therefore not immediately be converted into a fixed catalogue of prohibitions, expiations, and invalidity rules for all hair and nails.

18. Must a woman be accompanied by a male mahram?

Ali ‘Imran 3:97 states ability as a condition in a general address to people. It does not prescribe a separate condition that a woman be accompanied by a male mahram, a close relative whom she may not marry.

Travel safety, physical capacity, and actual circumstances matter for women and men alike. Making a male relative’s accompaniment a universal condition of women’s valid or obligatory hajj requires Qur’anic evidence.

Nor does the Qur’an specify the full customary legal restrictions on women’s clothing, face coverings, gloves, and related matters as special ihram rules. General obligations concerning clothing and conduct must be distinguished from claims of pilgrimage-specific prohibitions.

The absence of a condition does not license disregard for women’s safety. Concern for safety does not independently authorize a fixed, gender-specific religious rule either.

19. Can another person perform hajj in our place?

Ali ‘Imran 3:97 connects hajj to the individual’s ability, while Al-Najm 53:39 attributes a person’s striving to that person.

Al-An‘am 6:164 and Fatir 35:18 likewise connect responsibility to each individual and deny bearing another’s burden. These reinforce personal responsibility without independently settling every form of representation or assistance.

The Qur’an does not prescribe a separate ruling allowing one person’s hajj to discharge another person’s obligation.

Helping someone reach hajj differs from performing their worship in their place. Paying travel expenses, assisting a person with limited ability, or accompanying them is one matter. Claiming that the duty itself transfers to someone else is another.

Hajj by proxy, presented as a prescribed substitute for another’s duty, therefore requires Qur’anic support. Without it, the arrangement cannot be declared a certain divine ruling.

20. Are all rules for ending hajj and invalidating it Qur’anic?

Al-Hajj 22:29 mentions ending tafath, fulfilling vows, and circling the House. Al-Baqarah 2:200 calls for remembrance after completing the rites; 2:203 concerns remembrance on numbered days and the possibility of leaving after two days or remaining longer.

Hajj thus has stages and a recognized completion. But the complete later classifications—such as first and second tahallul, stages of release from restrictions, exact act counts, differently named tawafs, and extensive grounds of invalidity—are not specified in that form.

For example, tawaf al-nisa, a named circuit rite with particular rules in some legal schools, is not named in the Qur’an. Neither visiting Medina or the Prophet’s grave nor drinking Zamzam water is made a condition of completing hajj.

Some such practices may be chosen as visits, historical remembrance, or voluntary acts. They must not be declared obligatory or necessary for valid hajj without Qur’anic support.

What the examination of rites establishes

Qur’anic hajj must be distinguished from the entire body of current detailed rulings. The Qur’an identifies hajj, ability, timing, circling the House, Safa and Marwah as sacred signs, presence at Arafat, remembrance at the Sacred Monument, specified offering rules, the hunting prohibition in ihram, abstention from rafath, fusuq, and jidal, and other particular acts and limits.

Many counts, classifications, detailed prohibitions, fixed formulas, clothing requirements, and invalidity rules do not appear with that same specificity. The two sets cannot be presented indiscriminately as equally God’s commands.

The conclusion must not be reversed. We do not say that tawaf is unimportant because no circuit count is given, or that everyone who has thrown pebbles in the past sinned because that rite is not prescribed in the Qur’an. The issue is that no person or institution may impose its interpretation or custom as God’s obligatory command without evidence.

Above all, hajj’s spiritual integrity cannot be reduced to counts. Someone completing outward rites while embracing unauthorized religious legislation, compulsion, arrogance, injustice, or exclusion cannot establish fidelity to Abraham’s way merely through precision in details.

The same applies to custodians. No government or school can compensate for violating the Qur’an’s principles for the House and devotion by emphasizing subsidiary rulings.

Recovering Qur’anic hajj means restoring rites to their proper place and the authority to prescribe religion to the one God. It does not mean rejecting the rites.

6. Hajj, social responsibility, and people’s rights

Hajj places responsibilities on more than the individual pilgrim. Just as pilgrims must assess intentions, conduct, and rites against God’s command, those controlling the House and its facilities must answer to the same standards. The Qur’an invites people while describing the House as safe, the Sacred Mosque as for people, and the Kaaba as supporting their well-being. Assessing hajj therefore requires attention to the conditions of arrival, costs paid, and rights potentially denied.

This is particularly significant today. Many pilgrims depend on money, government authorizations, service companies, transport, and multiple regulations. Some arrangements are necessary for safety and orderly attendance. Without justice and accountability, the same mechanisms can enable discrimination, monopoly, and profiteering. Qur’anic standards should help distinguish serving people from controlling them.

A House for people: what do custodians owe them?

Al-Hajj 22:25 describes the Sacred Mosque as “for people,” adding that resident and visitor are equal there. It does not give Meccan residents inherently greater rights through proximity or depict distant visitors as people allowed to worship only by the generosity of those in power.

That equality is fundamental. Al-Ma’idah 5:97 places the Kaaba, the Sacred House, sacred month, offerings, and their garlands within an arrangement supporting people. Sanctity concerns the rights and interests of those for whom the House exists, beyond the building itself.

Administration does not mean ownership. A government may provide security, cleaning, roads, water, healthcare, and accommodation without thereby owning God’s House or independently defining the limits of people’s devotion. Just as ancestry does not automatically convey Abraham’s covenant, political control of the Sacred Mosque does not confer spiritual authority over everyone approaching it.

Political borders, visas, and travel quotas affect access today. But can different citizenship or residence establish different fundamental rights to worship God? If privileges are unavailable to outsiders solely because of nationality, their necessity and Qur’anic justification require examination.

Equal rights need not entail identical administrative arrangements. Different rules may be necessary to manage limited facilities, prevent crowding, and protect life. Such rules must serve people rather than make one country’s citizens the House’s owners and everyone else lesser claimants. The institution establishing restrictions must demonstrate their necessity and fairness.

Al-Anfal 8:34 makes mindfulness the criterion for guardianship and criticizes obstruction. Al-Tawbah 9:19 distinguishes water provision and maintenance from faith and striving in God’s way. Valuable service alone therefore cannot validate every custodial action or ruling.

Are the resources and authority assembled to administer the House serving people, or are people being used to preserve custodians’ power and interests? Every custodian, including the House of Saud, must face that question under the same standard and on demonstrable evidence. “Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques” can name a responsibility; the title is no immunity from criticism.

Partners in religious legislation: does the Qur’an’s standard apply only to others?

Authority to prescribe religion is central to this inquiry. Al-Tawbah 9:31 criticizes taking scholars and monks as lords; Al-Shura 42:21 describes partners prescribing religion without God’s authorization. These concern granting human beings the final authority to determine religion, beyond questions of statues or denying God’s existence.

The question directed at others must also be directed at ourselves. If Jewish and Christian communities are criticized for making religious leaders into lords, are adherents of hadith-based authority or juristic opinion exempt? If a jurist, transmitter, leader, or ruler declares obligatory or forbidden what God has not prescribed, and followers treat it as equivalent to His command, does the same problem of associating partners in religious legislation arise?

The question must be addressed explicitly to the House of Saud and its associated religious institutions too. If custodians claim authority to define religion, impose a school, or exclude people through rulings whose divine authorization is unproven, can Muslim identity or service to the sanctuaries exempt them from 9:31 and 42:21?

Al-Nahl 16:116 forbids attributing fabricated permissions and prohibitions to God. Yusuf 12:106 warns that belief in Him can coexist with assigning partners. Acknowledging one God does not alone guarantee undivided devotion in judgment, obedience, or religious legislation.

The question must therefore be stated plainly: if assigning people a share in divine legislative authority is shirk, are some currents of hadith-based and opinion-based jurisprudence, and institutions governing hajj, exposed to that same Qur’anic criticism?

This is no license to declare every follower of a school or member of a family guilty without examination. Consulting a scholar, using a historical report, or considering an inference openly identified as human interpretation is not identical to making someone a lord. The problem begins when human opinion assumes the standing of an independent divine command and disagreement is treated as opposition to God.

The standard must apply equally. A scriptural community’s name does not automatically condemn it; a Muslim name does not confer immunity. Every claim to prescribe religion, whoever makes it, must be assessed through the Qur’an. See The Qur’anic Root sh-r-k.

Safety at the House: protecting people or restricting them?

Safety is among the House’s clearest Qur’anic features. Al-Baqarah 2:125 calls it a place of return and safety; Ali ‘Imran 3:97 speaks of the entrant’s security. Quraysh 106:3, 106:4 describes its Lord as the one who feeds against hunger and secures against fear.

Safety requires practical action. Large gatherings in limited space need planning, drinking water, medical care, safe routes, crowd management, and accident prevention. Freedom of entry cannot justify endangering others; invoking security cannot automatically legitimate every restriction.

Entry permits and crowd regulations therefore do not independently establish injustice. Their purpose, the danger addressed, proportionality, equal enforcement, and possibilities for challenging mistaken decisions must be examined.

Contemporary Saudi administration uses permits and operational rules for entry and rites, identifying safety and crowd management among their purposes. Those purposes can accord with protecting pilgrims. A legitimate aim does not independently establish that every method is right. Rules that become instruments of religious discrimination, selective exclusion, or unjustified gain require separate assessment.

A pilgrim’s security also extends beyond bodily and financial safety. Read with the rejection of compulsion (Al-Baqarah 2:256), the House’s safety raises freedom of devotion. People approaching it to worship God should not be humiliated for different interpretations or forced to accept custodians’ beliefs. Maintaining order and preventing harm differ from policing conscience and imposing a religion.

Al-Ma’idah 5:97 also connects the Sacred House with the sacred month, drawing attention to protected times and routes. If examination of the pilgrimage months establishes a wider permissible period for some acts, its potential to reduce crowding and improve safety deserves study. That operational conclusion must not be assumed before the timing question is established.

Access: who may block the way to the House?

Al-Anfal 8:34 and Al-Hajj 22:25 criticize exclusion from the Sacred Mosque. Al-Baqarah 2:114 characterizes preventing remembrance in places of worship as injustice. Access is therefore no marginal concern.

Excluding someone who wishes to worship at the House with their own resources and without endangering others requires justification. Where exclusion rests on rejecting a legal school, not belonging to the governing religious group, or differing personal beliefs, its Qur’anic basis must be identified.

Al-Tawbah 9:28 is particularly important because it may be invoked to justify exclusion. Understanding it requires more than extracting one sentence from its setting.

Najās in 9:28: corrupted conduct or a label for excluding opponents?

Al-Tawbah 9:28 states that “those who associate partners with God are unclean” and must not approach the Sacred Mosque after that year. The description najās is followed by the restriction.

Who were these people, and why was the ruling addressed to them? The opening of Al-Tawbah concerns treaties, broken commitments, aggression against believers, and particular relationships. It requires fidelity toward those honoring treaties (9:4), protection for someone seeking refuge (9:6), and describes treaty-breakers who planned to expel the Messenger (9:13). This behavioral and historical setting cannot be severed from the discussion of impurity and exclusion.

Najās concerns uncleanness or impurity, but the verse does not explicitly say these people’s bodies are materially more contaminated than others’. Neither is “treaty-breaking” a straightforward translation of the word: that would be a contextual inference. Above all, the description must not become a racial, blood-based, intrinsic, or unchangeable characteristic assigned to every opponent in belief.

If the impurity is understood in connection with shirk and conduct condemned in the chapter, its application must depend on establishing the relevant condition, rather than surname, ethnicity, or an opponent’s label. The legal scope of exclusion still requires separate examination. Treaty-breaking matters to the context, but the wording of 9:28 does not explicitly restrict the ruling only to treaty-breakers.

Al-Baqarah 2:105 and Al-Tawbah 9:28, 9:29 distinguish the People of the Book from those who associate partners. All Jews, Christians, and other seekers of God cannot be subsumed into the latter without evidence. This does not mean a member of a scriptural community could never commit shirk. It means communal identity cannot automatically be equated with a particular belief or act.

Those calling others unclean must also submit to the self-examination the Qur’an demands. If sharing God’s authority to judge and prescribe is one form of shirk, are people granting independent legislative power to their religious leaders immune? Can a ruler or scholar who invents permissions and prohibitions in God’s name and denies rights to those rejecting them rely on Muslim identity to escape criticism of shirk and spiritual corruption?

If shirk and its corruption are standards of judgment, our own beliefs and conduct must face them first. How can we accuse others of impurity while overlooking possible elevation of jurists, reports, or rulers into partners in God’s authority to prescribe religion?

This does not pronounce all adherents of hadith or opinion-based schools, or all custodians, unclean—just as 9:31 does not authorize calling every member of a scriptural community an associator without examination. Nor does possible shirk in religious legislation automatically make 9:28’s entry restriction applicable to every person accused of it. Establishing the conduct and determining a ruling’s scope are separate steps.

The conclusion is the need to abandon double standards. Impurity must not become a device for expelling religious or political opponents while comparable conduct by one’s own leaders is ignored. The Qur’anic standard must apply to everyone.

See Iman in the Qur’an, The Qur’anic Root sh-r-k, and No Compulsion in Religion for connected inquiries.

Ability: does everyone face the same journey?

Hajj is prescribed for whoever can find a way to the House (Ali ‘Imran 3:97). The command fixes neither a sum of money nor a distance, and supplies no list restricting ability to financial assets.

Possessing a fixed amount cannot make everyone able regardless of other circumstances. Invented excuses must not evade responsibility, but religious obligation must not be used to force people into debt, pressure, and deprivation for their families either.

Ability also depends on external conditions. Someone physically and financially capable may face insecurity, a closed route, political prohibition, or a cost beyond their actual means.

The person’s blocked circumstances and the institution’s responsibility for the barrier are separate. An unjust closure may remove practical ability without becoming legitimate.

Where costs exclude large numbers, how much pays for necessary services, and how much reflects commercial structures, monopoly, or changeable decisions? Slogans cannot answer this. Actual costs and the methods used to set them require examination.

Trade during hajj: legitimate benefit or exploitation of worship?

The Qur’an does not prohibit trade and livelihood during hajj as such:

Al-Baqarah 2:198

لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَنْ تَبْتَغُوا فَضْلًا مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ ۚ فَإِذَا أَفَضْتُمْ مِنْ عَرَفَاتٍ فَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ عِنْدَ الْمَشْعَرِ الْحَرَامِ ۖ وَاذْكُرُوهُ كَمَا هَدَاكُمْ وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ لَمِنَ الضَّالِّينَ

“There is no blame upon you for seeking bounty from your Lord. When you depart from Arafat, remember God at the Sacred Monument. Remember Him as He guided you, although before that you were among those astray.”

Economic activity does not inherently conflict with worship. Food, accommodation, transport, healthcare, sanitation, and other necessary services can benefit pilgrims while providing legitimate income to those supplying them.

Legitimate trade, however, is not the same as exploiting people's religious needs. When someone takes advantage of a lack of alternatives to impose unjustified charges, monopolizes services so that people must pay unfair prices, or sells in religion's name something they have no right to own, that conduct must be assessed against justice and people's rights.

There is also an important distinction between charging for services and putting a price on the right to worship. Travelers need water, food, transport, and accommodation, and providing these costs money. But the right to serve God is not a commodity or the property of a government or company. No institution should confuse the services it provides with ownership of the way to God.

Financial transparency therefore matters. A pilgrim paying a substantial sum is entitled to know how much covers travel, accommodation, food, transport, healthcare, and other facilities, and how much goes toward administration and providers' profits. Such transparency makes it easier to distinguish actual costs from economic exploitation.

At the end of Al-Tawbah 9:28, following the prohibition stated in that verse, the Qur’an addresses anxiety about poverty. This shows that the ruling concerning access to the Sacred Mosque also had economic implications. Yet economic anxiety must not become a pretext for changing God's ruling, just as earning revenue cannot, by itself, justify every restriction or monopoly.

Our criticism, then, concerns any arrangement that puts financial gain ahead of people's rights, their safety, and the purposes of hajj—not earning an income during the hajj season as such.

“A means of support for people”: does the economy of hajj help people flourish?

In Al-Ma’idah 5:97, the Qur’an describes the Kaaba as qiyāman li-l-nās, “a means of support for people.” The expression belongs to the Arabic root q-w-m, associated with standing, remaining upright, and maintaining an ordered existence. Al-Nisa 4:5 also uses qiyām of wealth: material resources that sustain people's lives and livelihoods.

The two uses need not have identical meanings in every respect. Nevertheless, the connection between qiyām and the maintenance of material and social life cannot be ignored. When the Qur’an places the House, the sacred month, and the offerings within an arrangement that sustains people, it is entirely appropriate to ask about hajj's economic and social effects.

If vast resources are collected from pilgrims but most benefits pass to a small number of groups without clear accountability, we must ask how well that situation serves this purpose. Does hajj strengthen people's livelihoods and security, or is its economy managed chiefly for the benefit of those holding monopolies? Do ordinary people and those in need also share in the benefits of this great gathering?

These questions do not imply that profits earned by hotels, transport companies, or other service providers inherently violate the Qur’an. Income from genuine service must be distinguished from gains accumulated through monopoly, discrimination, or exploitation. Nor can we assert, without data and investigation, that all the benefits of the actual hajj economy are concentrated in a small group.

What the Qur’an gives us is a standard of assessment: the House is established to sustain people; people are not there to sustain the power and wealth of a particular group.

The sacrificial offering: who receives the meat?

One of the clearest connections between hajj and social responsibility appears in the verses about offerings. Al-Hajj 22:36 states:

وَالْبُدْنَ جَعَلْنَاهَا لَكُمْ مِنْ شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ فِيهَا خَيْرٌ ۖ فَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا صَوَافَّ ۖ فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرْنَاهَا لَكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

“We have made the sacrificial camels among God's sacred symbols for you; in them there is good for you. So mention God's name over them as they stand in rows. When their sides have fallen, eat from them and feed the needy person who does not ask and the one who comes seeking help. Thus We have made them subject to you, so that you may give thanks.”

Al-Hajj 22:28 likewise commands feeding the impoverished person in need. Al-Hajj 22:37 then makes clear that neither the meat nor the blood reaches God: what matters is human mindfulness of Him.

These verses do not reduce the offering to killing an animal. God's name is to be remembered; people benefit from His provision; those in need must receive a share; and mindfulness and gratitude must remain at the heart of the act.

If a pilgrim pays for an offering without knowing whether the animal was properly sacrificed or what became of its meat, questions of transparency and responsibility remain. The Qur’an does not, however, require each pilgrim personally to carry out every stage of slaughter, packaging, and distribution. With such large crowds, organized facilities for slaughter, preservation, and distribution can prevent waste and help the meat reach people in need farther away.

An industrial or organized process does not, by itself, prove that an offering lacks mindfulness of God. Equally, payment alone does not guarantee that all its Qur’anic purposes have been fulfilled. The practical criteria must be clear: was the offering properly carried out? Was the meat safely preserved? Did people in need actually benefit? Were resources used responsibly?

The present hajj system includes official programs for carrying out offerings and distributing their meat. Their existence should not be ignored, nor should anyone claim without investigation that all the offerings are wasted. At the same time, announcing a program does not replace an independent assessment of its effectiveness, transparency, and actual results.

If the Qur’an connects offerings with feeding people in need, reports on their implementation should show how much meat was consumed, how much was preserved and distributed, who benefited, and how much was wasted. Such information is necessary for a fair assessment.

Pilgrims, people in poverty, and responsibilities close to home

Another question concerns the relationship between spending on hajj and the real needs of those around the pilgrim.

The Qur’an does not, in principle, force a choice between visiting God's House and caring for people in poverty. On the contrary, the hajj passages themselves connect remembering God's name and making offerings with feeding those in need. The command in Al-Hajj 22:36 to feed both the needy person who does not ask and the one who comes seeking help shows that an offering's benefits must extend beyond the person making it.

Nevertheless, a person's circumstances may create a real conflict between responsibilities. Suppose someone could undertake hajj only by spending the family's essential savings, neglecting parents in need, or taking on a heavy debt. In that situation, the question is whether the person has the required means. The Qur’an prescribes hajj for those able to find a way to it, not for someone whose journey would expose their dependants to serious harm.

We must be equally precise in the other direction. The existence of poverty in the world does not, by itself, mean that hajj ceases to be required of everyone who has the means. The Qur’an speaks both of hajj and of giving and caring for those in need. These responsibilities must be understood in their proper relationship: neither should become a pretext for abolishing the other, and fulfilling one must not cause us to forget the other.

A pilgrim must not imagine that the journey replaces every duty toward family, people in poverty, and society. Al-Baqarah 2:177 connects righteousness with faith, giving, keeping commitments, and steadfastness. Hajj therefore cannot justify disregarding other people's rights.

The same question arises on a wider social scale. In the region where hajj takes place, and elsewhere in the world, many people face poverty, war, displacement, and food shortages. The humanitarian crisis in Yemen and the suffering of hungry and displaced people in other lands remind us that the need for food and safety does not end when the hajj ceremonies conclude.

When so many people need food and the necessities of life, how can we speak of a sacred observance that the Qur’an connects with feeding those in need, yet remain indifferent to what happens to the meat and to the possibility of helping hungry people?

This does not mean that all the meat must be sent to one particular country, or that offerings distributed elsewhere are worthless. Nor may we claim without evidence that the meat fails to reach people in need. The central issue is responsible use of resources and an examination of actual outcomes.

An offering should draw people closer to remembrance of God and responsibility toward His creatures. If an observance has been established with that purpose, we should at least be able to ask whether those in need benefit from it and expect a clear answer.

Does taking part in hajj endorse the government that administers it?

This question matters especially to people who disagree with the policies or religious interpretations of the government administering the House.

On one hand, the Qur’an does not make hajj dependent on a particular government. The House belongs to God, and Abraham's invitation is addressed to people. Disagreement with a government or criticism of its conduct does not, in itself, constitute opposition to hajj.

On the other hand, we cannot be wholly indifferent to how our actions and resources affect others. Al-Ma’idah 5:2 commands cooperation in goodness and mindfulness of God, not in wrongdoing and aggression. Where a particular act of cooperation genuinely serves injustice, its moral implications require examination.

Paying necessary travel costs or using a country's services, however, differs from knowingly endorsing every policy and belief of its government. A person cannot be deemed a supporter of every political and religious action of the authorities simply because they travel to Mecca. Such a conclusion requires a demonstrated connection between that person's action and the alleged assistance in injustice.

Conversely, performing the rites must not lead a pilgrim to abandon criticism of oppression and monopolization, or to imagine that the sanctity of the House makes every decision of those in power sacred.

Abraham's hanif way is one of serving God with undivided devotion, not unconditional loyalty to a government or religious institution. We can honor the House while judging its custodians' conduct by the Qur’an. Indeed, this distinction is essential if God's House is not to become a symbol of one group's power.

How can the social responsibilities of hajj be assessed?

This discussion yields criteria for assessing the administration of hajj. They are inferred from the Qur’anic passages taken together; we are not presenting every practical detail of their implementation as a direct regulation of revelation.

First, people's rights and dignity must guide decisions. No authority should confuse administration of the House with absolute ownership of it. Every restriction on access needs a clear reason, a necessity that can be examined, and a fair means of implementation.

Second, security must genuinely protect pilgrims' lives and dignity. Crowd management, medical facilities, and prevention of danger are necessary. Security must not become a cover for imposing a religious school, humiliating people, or selectively excluding them.

Third, charges should be transparent and proportionate to the services provided. If hajj is organized through commercial packages, it should be possible to examine their cost components, service quality, providers' profits, and reasons for price differences. These questions demand fairness and accountability; they do not oppose trade.

Fourth, the benefits of hajj should be visible to people. The Qur’an itself speaks of “benefits for them.” We should therefore ask what this gathering offers pilgrims, people in need, the host community, and others, and how monopoly and waste can be prevented.

Fifth, no service should become the basis for claiming unquestionable religious legitimacy. Supplying water, constructing facilities, organizing journeys, and providing amenities are valuable. Yet Al-Tawbah 9:19 reminds us that such services do not replace faith and action in God's cause. Mindfulness of God matters as much for the House's servants as for its visitors.

Sixth, the custodians' religious claims must also be examined. No religious institution may use its historical standing, number of followers, or political power to make unauthorized rulings obligatory in God's name. The standard used in Al-Tawbah 9:31 to criticize taking human beings as lords must also be applied to Muslims' own rulings and authorities.

These criteria neither create a new shariah for hajj nor turn administrative details into divine commands. Their purpose is to assess human decisions against the principles of devotion, justice, safety, and people's rights set out in the Qur’an.

Hajj should sustain people, not subjugate them

The Qur’an describes the Kaaba as “a means of support for people.” We should return to that expression as this discussion draws to a close. The House is more than somewhere people move around: it is associated with sanctity, safety, public benefit, and remembrance of God.

A House established to sustain people must not become an instrument for breaking their dignity. If the Sacred Mosque is for people, access to it must not, without justification, depend on a family's or sect's claim to ownership. If hajj is prescribed for remembrance of God and human benefit, the number of acts performed and the cost of travel must not so overshadow it that mindfulness, justice, and responsibility toward people in need are forgotten.

At the same time, criticism of distortions must not lead us to deny hajj itself. The Qur’an that speaks of people's rights and freedom to serve God also sets out the House, circling it, offerings, the timing of hajj, and its other rulings. Reform means distinguishing God's command from the claims and structures people have built around it, not abandoning that command.

What must change is the place we give our standards. Custodians must regard service as a responsibility, not a license to dominate. Pilgrims must regard the journey as devotion to God, not a certificate of superiority. Society must be able to distinguish what God has prescribed from what human beings impose in His name.

This examination must begin with ourselves. If we criticize others for taking their leaders as lords, we cannot exempt our own jurists and transmitters of reports from the same standard. If we speak of the impurity of associating partners with God, we must not turn it into a label for degrading opponents while overlooking that same association in our own practices of religious legislation and obedience. If we affirm that the House is for people, we must respect that principle for those outside our own school or beliefs too, except where a specific restriction is established with valid grounds and a clearly defined scope.

The House God established to sustain people must not become a means of dominating those very people. Those who judge others in the name of God's oneness must first ensure that they have not themselves handed His authority over to human beings.

7. Conclusion: restoring hajj to its Qur’anic place

This study has argued that the present crisis in understanding and practising hajj cannot be reduced to the number of circuits around the House, the fabric worn in ihram, or the throwing of pebbles. At its heart lies the question of religious authority and the right to legislate. Have the legal and political institutions that make these detailed rites obligatory in God's name themselves remained faithful to the Qur’anic foundations of God's oneness, Abraham's undivided devotion, freedom to serve God, and people's rights?

The findings come together in two fundamental principles:

First: secondary rules cannot stand on the ruins of their foundations.

On what authority can an institution prescribe and impose the details of worship in God's name if it violates His oneness, human freedom, the sanctity of the House, and the equality of people—the resident and the visitor alike? An authority that compromises the foundations of hajj through monopolization and the assignment of God's legislative authority to others cannot rely on its own power to dictate the boundaries for entering ihram or the number of pebbles as God's certain will. Rulings that such an authority invents or imposes on religion without Qur’anic grounds lack divine authority and must be set aside. Only what is established from the Qur’an itself, explicitly or through sound reasoning from its passages taken together, derives its authority from God's command.

Second: the Qur’an is the measure of shariah; shariah is not the measure of the Qur’an.

We are not obliged first to accept the heavy weight of legal rulings, historical reports, and inherited customs, and then strain the Qur’an's verses to justify them. The direction of guidance is the reverse: every religious claim must establish its case before the Qur’an. This yields the study's governing rule:

The burden of proof rests with whoever presents an act as God's command, an obligatory rite, or a condition for valid hajj—not with the person asking for Qur’anic evidence.

The preceding sections have shown how certain legal rulings and interpretations come into conflict with Qur’anic foundations:

  • The Qur’an connects hajj with Abraham's undivided devotion and worship of God alone. Yet when those claiming to defend God's oneness grant their jurists and leaders an independent right to legislate religious obligations, they expose themselves to the same criticism of associating partners with God in legislation that they direct at others. This standard applies equally to schools associated with juristic opinion, schools claiming hadith-based authority, and religious institutions allied with political power.
  • The Qur’an rejects compulsion in religion. Imposing a religious school or policing beliefs in the name of protecting the House's sacred precinct conflicts with that fundamental principle.
  • The Qur’an describes God's House as being “for people” and as a place of safety. Whenever access for those seeking God depends on religious identity, political nationality, or unjust restrictions, people's shared rights become subordinate to the discretion of those in power.
  • The Qur’an names the People of the Book and those who associate partners with God as distinct categories, and Al-Tawbah 9:28 must be understood in context. Groundless extension of the labels of association and impurity opens the way to excluding people because of their religious identity. Those who judge others by these standards cannot overlook the danger of assigning God's legislative authority to others within their own communities.
  • The Qur’an connects Abraham's station with salat and presents his undivided devotion as a model for serving God. When presence at a sacred site replaces following Abraham's way of thought and life, the outward form of worship overtakes its substance.
  • The Qur’an connects salat with turning away from wrongdoing, and offerings with mindfulness of God, feeding those in need, and human benefit. Yet in ritual-centred readings, the form of an act can so overshadow its substance that its moral purpose is pushed aside.
  • The Qur’an speaks of continuity in devotion to God alongside differences in prescribed ways. When a school of law makes its own historical form of worship the sole measure of serving God, differences in shariah become a claim to exclusive ownership of religion and a basis for judging others' faith.

This shows why imposing one school’s unproven requirements is more than a disagreement about the form of the rites. Such imposition can turn a House established for people into the exclusive domain of one community’s prescribed way, forcing other seekers of God to choose between staying away from the observances and accepting that community’s rulings. While affirming the oneness of the God worshiped, the Qur’an also recognizes different rites and prescribed ways. From this perspective, leaving some details unstated can be understood as latitude in carrying out the command rather than a deficiency in it—latitude that human beings have no right to remove by attributing their own rulings to God.

These deep divisions cannot be repaired by producing ever larger legal manuals and adding further exceptions and qualifications. An institution caught in such conflicts at the level of principle does not acquire divine legitimacy by enforcing greater strictness in secondary rules.

Restoring hajj to its Qur’anic place means neither denying God's sacred observances nor inventing a new shariah. It means dismantling the idols of religious legislation, ending sectarian monopoly, recognizing the Kaaba again as a place of safety, devotion, and human benefit—a means of support for people—and restoring the rites to the place the Qur’an assigns them. Hajj should free people from servitude to rulers, jurists, and official religious schools, bringing them back to worship of God alone, justice, and responsibility toward His creatures.

Hajj follows Abraham's way only when God's command takes precedence over human rulings, foundations over secondary details, and Abraham's undivided devotion over inherited customs. Circling God's House, without fidelity to Abraham's way and commitments, does not by itself make anyone wholly devoted to God alone.
