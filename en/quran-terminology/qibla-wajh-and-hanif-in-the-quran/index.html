Qur’an Terminology

Qibla, Wajh, and Hanif in the Qur’an

The stone-built Kaaba embodies the qibla; Abraham’s hanif way gives it meaning

نسخه فارسی

The Kaaba is familiar to Muslims everywhere. People worship facing it from thousands of miles away and endure long journeys to reach it. Yet many have lost sight of the purpose for which this House was established. That distance between devotion and understanding raises the question at the heart of qibla: when we turn toward this House, are we merely changing the position of our bodies, or the direction of our service to God?

The Qur’an calls the Kaaba al-Bayt al-Haram, the inviolable House. Its sanctity, however, is not exhausted by its walls and stones. The very verse that identifies it as the inviolable House says that it sustains people and enables them to stand:

Al-Ma'idah 5:97

جَعَلَ اللَّهُ الْكَعْبَةَ الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ قِيَامًا لِلنَّاسِ وَالشَّهْرَ الْحَرَامَ وَالْهَدْيَ وَالْقَلَائِدَ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ لِتَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

Translation: The God made the Kaaba, the inviolable House, a support for people, together with the inviolable month, the offerings, and their garlands. This is so that you may know that the God knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth, and that the God knows all things.

Here the House and its significance appear together. The Kaaba is unquestionably a real building, yet it is also qiyaman li-l-nas: something by which people are sustained. The same root, q-w-m, appears in the command to establish Salat. In the Qur’an’s language, Salat is to be established and kept alive, rather than simply recited and brought to an end. That distinction matters: the outward performance of worship does not encompass its entire reality.

The same root connects other parts of this discussion. The Qur’an speaks of setting one’s wajh toward din as a hanif (Ar-Rum 30:30), calls Abraham’s way an upright din (Al-An‘am 6:161), and asks believers to stand firmly for justice (An-Nisa 4:135; Al-Ma’idah 5:8). These related forms do not all mean the same thing. They do, however, show how the language of standing, establishing, and sustaining repeatedly extends beyond bodily movement to the way people uphold devotion and justice.

If the Kaaba becomes no more than a mass of stone that we must face, we have lost the House’s meaning. If, on the other hand, qibla is severed from this House altogether, we lose the visible sign through which that orientation is recognized. The Kaaba is a house; al-Bayt al-Haram names it in its inviolability, while al-Masjid al-Haram names the sacred precinct with the House at its heart. These names are not rivals. Each brings a different aspect of the same reality into view.

Qibla is commonly treated as virtually synonymous with “the direction of prayer.” But does the Qur’an itself define it that way? Its principal qibla passage, Al-Baqarah 2:142, 2:143, 2:144, 2:145, 2:146, 2:147, 2:148, 2:149, 2:150, never uses the word Salat. Yunus 10:87, by contrast, designates the homes of Moses’ people as a qibla and includes the command to establish Salat. The observation about Salat’s absence therefore concerns Al-Baqarah 2:142, 2:143, 2:144, 2:145, 2:146, 2:147, 2:148, 2:149, 2:150 specifically, not every verse containing qibla. Al-Baqarah 2:145 speaks of following a qibla, just as an earlier verse speaks of following a religious way. The Qur’an commands a turning of the wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram, yet also says that east and west belong to God and that wherever one turns, there is God’s wajh. It does not confine righteousness to facing east or west. In the midst of this discussion it calls people to outdo one another in good works. Taken together, these features require closer attention to the Qur’an’s own language before qibla is reduced to a geographical direction.

Some researchers take the opposite position: qibla, they argue, is fundamentally a community’s focus, destination, and intellectual orientation rather than a bodily direction. Some go further and interpret al-Masjid al-Haram itself as a symbol of peace and coexistence rather than a tangible place.1 Two questions must be kept distinct here. The physical reality of al-Masjid al-Haram in the Qur’an is beyond doubt: the text speaks of departing from it, entering it, people present there, the House’s foundations, circumambulation and Hajj, and people being prevented from entering. Yet a place’s physical reality does not by itself establish that qibla means the angle of a body facing that place. A real place can also embody a way of life, an identity, and a profound intellectual orientation.

That distinction guides this study. The Kaaba is a real House and al-Masjid al-Haram occupies real geographical space, but Al-Baqarah does not explain the change of qibla in geographical terms alone. It connects that change with following, guidance, faith, milla—a religious way—ahwa’—desires—and Abraham’s path. On this reading, turning toward the Kaaba can express an inward reorientation: a community must take its bearings neither from Judaism nor from Christianity, but from Abraham’s uncorrupted, hanif way. Stone does not replace meaning; it remains a sign charged with recalling that meaning.

What this essay sometimes calls a “symbol” or “sign” belongs to a field the Qur’an expresses through words such as sha‘a’ir and ayat. It calls Safa, Marwa, and the sacrificial animals of Hajj God’s sha‘a’ir and describes the House as containing clear signs. The relation between sign and meaning is therefore not an outside theory imposed on the text. The Qur’an itself connects these signs with the hearts’ taqwa. The discussion of Surah Al-Hajj will return to this connection in detail.

A harder question follows. What should happen when a symbol survives but what it represents is distorted or emptied out? The Qur’an associates this House with God’s oneness, Abraham’s hanif way, the sustaining of people, security, taqwa, feeding those in need, keeping God’s way open, and defending places where people seek Him. If the House is instead made to stand for religious monopoly, imposed belief, blind prejudice, political maneuvering, and the sanctification of human reports, should the symbol be abandoned, or its lost meaning restored?

This question reaches deeper than the direction of qibla itself. Someone may still stand precisely toward the same House while no longer following the way for which it was established. The body’s orientation may remain unchanged while the mind’s allegiance changes completely. In that situation, the problem lies not in the Kaaba’s coordinates but in the beliefs and meanings people bring to their act of turning toward it.

This study takes the Qur’an alone as the measure of competing interpretations. A recurring weakness in inherited and juristic readings is their preservation of outward form at the expense of purpose. Qibla becomes the direction of prayer; establishing Salat becomes merely performing prayers; Hajj becomes a sequence of mechanical acts; and the House becomes a building one must face. The forms survive, while the wisdom and path they were meant to communicate are often forgotten.

Returning to the Qur’an matters precisely because it reconnects form with meaning. The Kaaba, Salat, and the rites each have their place, but the character and value of each depend on the ends the Qur’an gives them. The Kaaba finds its meaning in Abraham’s way and the sustaining of human community. Salat should keep the remembrance of God alive and express itself in conduct. Rites become something more than lifeless movements when joined to taqwa and concern for others.

The central question is thus whether qibla is merely the direction in which a body is positioned, or a sign of a larger orientation that should align the whole course of our devotion with Abraham’s hanif way. If the outward qibla survives while its meaning is distorted, does the Qur’an call for changing the qibla or restoring what it means?

Qibla in the Qur’an: from facing the Kaaba to following Abraham’s hanif way

The root q-b-l: facing and turning toward

Qibla derives from the root q-b-l. Its central associations include facing, being in front of something, and turning toward it. Words such as muqabil, something opposite us, and iqbal, an approach or turning toward something, belong to the same family. Before any technical religious definition is considered, the root introduces the idea of being directed toward something.

Qur’anic usage supports this field of meaning. In Joseph’s story, whether his shirt was torn from the front or the back becomes evidence in establishing the truth: min qubul stands opposite min dubur. In Al-Hijr and As-Saffat, the people of the Garden are mutaqabilin, facing one another. The same family appears in Al-Baqarah’s discussion of turning toward east and west. (Yusuf 12:26; Al-Hijr 15:47; As-Saffat 37:44; Al-Baqarah 2:177)

These examples do not yet settle qibla’s technical meaning. What the root securely establishes is a connection with turning toward something and taking up an orientation. It does not decide whether that orientation must be bodily or can also involve thought, allegiance, and following. That question requires examination of qibla itself in the Qur’an, rather than the projection of a predetermined juristic definition onto its root.

Related Semitic languages also preserve this sense of facing and turning toward.2 Yet the direction of worship in pre-Qur’anic traditions is less straightforward than sometimes assumed. Hebrew scriptures beyond the Torah describe prayer toward Jerusalem, but the Torah itself contains no clear command establishing a particular physical qibla for prayer. Nor do the Gospels prescribe a geographical qibla. In Jesus’ conversation with the Samaritan woman, the dispute over Mount Gerizim and Jerusalem is answered by moving the focus from location to worship “in spirit and truth.” These matters require closer examination before “the qibla of the People of the Book” in Al-Baqarah can be treated as simply the name of a bodily direction.

Qibla occurs only seven times in the Qur’an: six occurrences in Al-Baqarah 2:142, 2:143, 2:144, 2:145 and one in Yunus 10:87. That small number is an advantage. All its uses can readily be considered together. A definition that cannot account both for Al-Baqarah and for the homes of Moses’ people in Yunus has not captured the word’s full Qur’anic range.

Before the qibla discussion begins

The discussion in Al-Baqarah 2:142 does not appear without preparation. The surah has already addressed claims to exclusive salvation, surrendering one’s wajh—one’s being—to God, obstructing places of worship, Abraham’s story, the House, and rival claims to guidance. By the time qibla enters the passage, the reader is already engaged with questions of the path to follow, the example to adopt, and religious allegiance.

Al-Baqarah 2:120 sets out this context particularly clearly:

Al-Baqarah 2:120

وَلَنْ تَرْضَىٰ عَنْكَ الْيَهُودُ وَلَا النَّصَارَىٰ حَتَّىٰ تَتَّبِعَ مِلَّتَهُمْ ۗ قُلْ إِنَّ هُدَى اللَّهِ هُوَ الْهُدَىٰ ۗ وَلَئِنِ اتَّبَعْتَ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ بَعْدَ الَّذِي جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ ۙ مَا لَكَ مِنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ وَلِيٍّ وَلَا نَصِيرٍ

Translation: The Jews and the Christians will not be pleased with you until you follow their religious way. Say: the God’s guidance is the guidance. If you follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, you will have neither protector nor helper against the God.

Here a community’s approval is directly tied to following its milla. In verse 145, much the same language returns, but qibla takes the place of milla. Following is again at issue, as is the warning against replacing divine guidance with other people’s desires after knowledge has arrived.

Another correspondence appears between the two passages. Verse 120 says that Jews and Christians will not be pleased with the Messenger until he follows their milla; verse 144 speaks of a qibla with which he will be pleased. The shared root proves nothing by itself. Within the larger passage, however, it brings into focus the contrast between seeking communities’ approval and accepting an orientation given by God.

Qibla and milla are not synonyms, and the Qur’an never says they are identical. Yet their placement in closely matching verbal structures cannot be incidental. In both cases the issue goes beyond knowing a geographical bearing. Whose way does a person accept, and whose approval leads that person to redirect a life?

Abraham enters between these passages. Al-Baqarah 2:130 describes the person who turns away from his way as foolish. In verse 131, Abraham answers his Lord’s call by declaring his surrender to the Lord of all worlds. In verse 135, when becoming Jewish or Christian is offered as the route to guidance, the Qur’an presents another course: the way of Abraham, the hanif.

A few verses later, qibla is introduced through the objection of the foolish: what has turned these people away from the qibla they previously followed? The association is significant. Before addressing qibla, the Qur’an has already measured foolishness against turning away from Abraham’s path and released guidance from the monopoly of denominational names.

The objections to the change of qibla therefore concern more than a new bodily direction. On this reading, turning toward the Kaaba visibly expresses a larger reorientation: believers must define themselves independently of Jewish and Christian religious ways, in continuity with Abraham’s uncorrupted, hanif path. Beneath the outward turn lies a deeper question of fidelity. Will people cling to habits inherited from their own community, or accept guidance that asks them to rebuild their religious and intellectual orientation around truth?

Changing the qibla: a different building, or a different frame of thought?

Al-Baqarah 2:142

سَيَقُولُ السُّفَهَاءُ مِنَ النَّاسِ مَا وَلَّاهُمْ عَنْ قِبْلَتِهِمُ الَّتِي كَانُوا عَلَيْهَا ۚ قُلْ لِلَّهِ الْمَشْرِقُ وَالْمَغْرِبُ ۚ يَهْدِي مَنْ يَشَاءُ إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

Translation: The foolish among the people will say: what has turned them from the qibla they were upon? Say: to the God belong east and west; He guides whom He wills to a straight path.

The objection explicitly concerns qibla, but the answer moves beyond east and west to the straight path. If the entire issue were a geometrical bearing, one would expect the answer to remain at that level. Instead, the Qur’an emphasizes that both east and west belong to God, and that guidance to the straight path is what matters. Al-An‘am 6:161 connects that same straight path with an upright din, the milla of Abraham as a hanif. From the beginning, qibla is discussed in terms of a path to follow, rather than an inherent sanctity belonging to east or west.

This does not make the Kaaba’s place unimportant. It sharpens the question: why should Abraham’s House, of all places, embody this orientation? If stone and walls were ends in themselves, “to the God belong east and west” would be a strange answer. If the House signifies a path, the answer makes sense. God is not confined within east or west; an orientation acquires its value from the way of life through which a person’s identity is formed.

The next verse develops the point:

Al-Baqarah 2:143

وَكَذَٰلِكَ جَعَلْنَاكُمْ أُمَّةً وَسَطًا لِتَكُونُوا شُهَدَاءَ عَلَى النَّاسِ وَيَكُونَ الرَّسُولُ عَلَيْكُمْ شَهِيدًا ۗ وَمَا جَعَلْنَا الْقِبْلَةَ الَّتِي كُنْتَ عَلَيْهَا إِلَّا لِنَعْلَمَ مَنْ يَتَّبِعُ الرَّسُولَ مِمَّنْ يَنقَلِبُ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ ۚ وَإِنْ كَانَتْ لَكَبِيرَةً إِلَّا عَلَى الَّذِينَ هَدَى اللَّهُ ۗ وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيُضِيعَ إِيمَانَكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِالنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

Translation: Thus We made you a middle community, so that you might be witnesses over people and the Messenger a witness over you. We appointed the qibla you were upon only to distinguish those who follow the Messenger from those who turn back on their heels. It was indeed difficult except for those the God guided. The God would not let your faith go to waste; He is compassionate and merciful toward people.

The Qur’an explicitly states why qibla became a divine test: to distinguish following the Messenger from returning to the past. The discussion is not exhausted by a body turning a few degrees. Its surrounding vocabulary is following—*ittiba‘*—turning back, guidance, and faith. If bodily rotation were the entire issue, a geometrical bearing would not carry this weight of meaning.

The verse’s ending matters too. It does not say that God will not let their Salat go to waste. It says imanakum: their faith. The choice of word places more than the form of a ritual at stake. Changing qibla concerns how people respond to guidance, which path they follow, and whether they remain faithful to what they have recognized as true.

The Qur’an also leaves the previous qibla unnamed. It says only “the qibla you were upon.” It does not name Bayt al-Maqdis or Jerusalem, or quote an earlier command to face that location. Any identification must therefore be established outside the Qur’an’s explicit wording. For this study, what the text supplies is enough: there was a previous qibla, and changing it tested fidelity and following.

We now reach the verse on which much of the familiar understanding of qibla rests:

Al-Baqarah 2:144

قَدْ نَرَىٰ تَقَلُّبَ وَجْهِكَ فِي السَّمَاءِ ۖ فَلَنُوَلِّيَنَّكَ قِبْلَةً تَرْضَاهَا ۚ فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ ۚ وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ ۗ وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ لَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّهُ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ ۗ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا يَعْمَلُونَ

Translation: We see your wajh turning in the sky; We shall turn you to a qibla that pleases you. Turn your wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram, and wherever you are, turn your faces toward it. Those given the Book know that it is the truth from their Lord. The God is not unaware of what they do.

We have often read this verse too quickly and concluded that it refers simply to bodily rotation. Once the Qur’an’s use of wajh is examined alongside the following verse, that certainty becomes harder to sustain. Wajh is also the word used for surrendering one’s whole being to God and for Abraham’s orientation toward the Creator of the heavens and earth. The destination here is al-Masjid al-Haram, a real place with the Kaaba—the inviolable House—at its heart. Yet a real destination does not settle whether turning the wajh means only moving one’s face and body, or directing one’s whole being toward the way that place represents.

Here the distinction between qibla and its embodiment becomes important. Al-Masjid al-Haram and the Kaaba are tangible places, as numerous Qur’anic verses confirm. But a tangible destination does not restrict qibla to the angle of the body during Salat. A real place can visibly represent a religious and intellectual orientation. For centuries people have represented beliefs through flags, houses, cities, prayer niches, and shrines. A symbol’s material reality does not confine the meaning it carries to its physical form.

There is therefore a serious possibility that turning toward the Kaaba outwardly expressed a much deeper change. The earliest believers had to demonstrate that they did not receive their identity and orientation from Jewish or Christian religious ways, but measured them against Abraham’s hanif path. Even if this orientation was bodily enacted during Salat, the body served the meaning. Each act of standing toward the Kaaba could remind the community where its true allegiance lay and from which source its way of life should take direction.

The next verse gives this reading further strength:

Al-Baqarah 2:145

وَلَئِنْ أَتَيْتَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ بِكُلِّ آيَةٍ مَا تَبِعُوا قِبْلَتَكَ ۚ وَمَا أَنْتَ بِتَابِعٍ قِبْلَتَهُمْ ۚ وَمَا بَعْضُهُمْ بِتَابِعٍ قِبْلَةَ بَعْضٍ ۚ وَلَئِنِ اتَّبَعْتَ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ ۙ إِنَّكَ إِذًا لَمِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

Translation: Even if you brought every sign to those given the Book, they would not follow your qibla. You are not a follower of theirs, nor do some of them follow the qibla of others. If you followed their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, you would indeed be among the wrongdoers.

Here qibla is something to follow, rather than merely a physical direction to face. Forms of following appear four times in a short passage: they do not follow your qibla; you do not follow theirs; some do not follow one another’s qibla; and following their desires would make you a wrongdoer.

Read alone, the verse might be taken to mean following a command about geographical direction. A few verses earlier, however, the same verbal structure appears with a different word:

Al-Baqarah 2:120: «حَتَّىٰ تَتَّبِعَ مِلَّتَهُمْ ... وَلَئِنِ اتَّبَعْتَ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ بَعْدَ الَّذِي جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ» — Until you follow their milla … and if you follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you.

Al-Baqarah 2:145: «وَمَا أَنْتَ بِتَابِعٍ قِبْلَتَهُمْ ... وَلَئِنِ اتَّبَعْتَ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ» — You are not a follower of their qibla … and if you follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you.

One verse speaks of following a milla, the other of following a qibla; both end with the same warning about desires and knowledge. The words are not lexical equivalents, but the Qur’an places them in a shared field: religious identity and allegiance.

Another linguistic feature deserves attention. The Qur’an speaks of the qibla one was “upon.” The same construction occurs with guidance, a clear path, and shariah. In Al-Jathiyah 45:18, the Messenger is placed upon a shariah concerning the matter and immediately commanded to follow it rather than the desires of those without knowledge. This parallel does not determine qibla’s meaning by itself, but again connects the passage’s language with path, shariah, following, and desires.

This correspondence also changes how verse 144 is read. It stands between two discussions of following: before it, qibla tests who follows the Messenger; after it come following a qibla and following desires. The command to turn the wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram occurs at the center of this sequence. It is therefore more natural to understand that turn as the outward expression of a much larger orientation, rather than the whole meaning of qibla.

This raises a historical question about the People of the Book. If qibla in verse 145 meant only a bodily direction, one would expect such directions to be clearly prescribed in the religious laws addressed. Yet the Torah of Moses contains no clear command requiring worshippers to turn their bodies toward a particular geographical point. Later Hebrew scriptures describe turning toward Jerusalem and Solomon’s Temple, but that is not an original prescription in the Torah itself. The four Gospels likewise prescribe no physical qibla for Christians. In John 4, Jesus responds to the dispute over Gerizim and Jerusalem by freeing worship from an exclusive location and speaking of worship in spirit and truth. “Their qibla” cannot therefore be reduced without a strong argument to physical bearings supposedly legislated in the Torah and Gospel.

This does not mean that Jews and Christians never adopted particular directions of worship. Later religious traditions could develop or establish such directions. A textual study beginning with the Qur’an, however, cannot silently import later traditional concepts into Qur’anic words.

Different orientations, but a shared race in goodness

Al-Baqarah 2:148

وَلِكُلٍّ وِجْهَةٌ هُوَ مُوَلِّيهَا ۖ فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ ۚ أَيْنَ مَا تَكُونُوا يَأْتِ بِكُمُ اللَّهُ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Translation: For each there is an orientation toward which one turns. So outdo one another in good works. Wherever you may be, the God will bring you all together. The God has power over all things.

In the midst of the qibla discussion, this verse recognizes different orientations and then immediately redirects the competition: outdo one another in good works. Believers are not asked to boast about whose direction is superior. A particular orientation may identify a community, but human worth is measured by goodness and the good people do.

The ending also bears on place: wherever people are, God will gather them. Alongside Al-Baqarah 2:115 and Al-Hadid 57:4, this recalls that different locations do not place anyone outside God’s domain. Directions may differ; the final authority remains one.

Al-Ma’idah 5:48 later repeats this principle in closely related language. Communities have different prescribed ways and paths, yet the command remains to outdo one another in goodness. Different ways of proceeding must not become a struggle over who owns God. God is one; goodness is the true field of distinction.

Al-Baqarah 2:149 and 2:150 repeat the command to turn toward al-Masjid al-Haram. That repetition certainly establishes the center’s importance. The purposes stated at the end of verse 150 must receive equal attention:

Al-Baqarah 2:150

وَمِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ ۚ وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَيْكُمْ حُجَّةٌ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِي وَلِأُتِمَّ نِعْمَتِي عَلَيْكُمْ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

Translation: From wherever you set out, turn your wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram; wherever you are, turn your faces toward it, so that people may have no argument against you, except those among them who do wrong. Do not fear them; be mindful of Me, so that I may complete My blessing upon you and you may be guided.

Guidance is again the command’s goal. The verse also speaks of removing people’s grounds for argument, refusing to fear them, and being mindful of God. The change of qibla thus has profound consequences for the community’s social life. Its identity and path are not to be adjusted to win other groups’ approval. Turning toward the Kaaba becomes a powerful symbolic act: faces turn toward one center to recall that the final authority is divine guidance, not the pressures and demands of other communities.

This unifying symbol can be entirely physical and very likely was so in ritual practice. Yet Al-Baqarah 2:142, 2:143, 2:144, 2:145, 2:146, 2:147, 2:148, 2:149, 2:150 never once says, “Do this while performing Salat.” That significant silence must not be filled with later reports and interpretations. Other verses establish a relationship between Salat and the Kaaba, but the narrow equation “qibla equals the direction of the body during prayer” is not one the Qur’an explicitly formulates.

Shatr: turning toward a center, rather than specifying a geometrical angle

In the qibla passage, shatr occurs three times alongside “al-Masjid al-Haram” and does not recur elsewhere in the Qur’an. Arabic lexicons and early poetry transmitted in Islamic-era sources record meanings of side, direction, and turning toward something.3 Since pre-Islamic poetry reaches us through later written sources, the dating and authenticity of individual examples require the same care as other early linguistic evidence. Even so, the verses’ own phrases—“wherever you are” and “from wherever you set out”—make the spatial sense “toward al-Masjid al-Haram” entirely natural.

This essay therefore does not deny the spatial dimension of that expression. The point is different: shatr does not by itself yield a meticulously measured angle, nor can that spatial dimension exhaust qibla’s meaning. Shatr indicates the direction of a real destination, while the surrounding verses continue to speak of wajh, following, guidance, faith, and desires.

The distinction matters because some interpretations ask the word to carry more than it can bear. They imagine a precise geometrical line extending from a person’s forehead to a particular point on the Kaaba. These verses specify no degrees, discuss no meridian, prescribe no measuring instrument, and do not even name the Kaaba directly in the command itself. “Shatr al-Masjid al-Haram” is the ordinary language of turning toward a focus or center, not the technical language of surveying.

Even if shatr is accepted as indicating the direction of a real place, wajh still requires examination. The command does not say “rotate your body.” It says “turn your wajh.” Elsewhere in the Qur’an, that word encompasses the whole person.

Wajh: when the face expresses the whole person

A few verses before the qibla discussion, God says:

Al-Baqarah 2:112

بَلَىٰ مَنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَلَهُ أَجْرُهُ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

Translation: Yes: whoever surrenders their wajh to the God while doing good will have their reward with their Lord. No fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.

No one would reasonably reduce “surrenders their wajh to the God” to placing the skin of the face against the ground. Here wajh expresses the person’s being, will, capacity to choose, and overall direction in life. An-Nisa 4:125 places the same expression alongside doing good and following Abraham’s hanif way. Before the qibla verses begin, the reader has therefore encountered wajh in a sense extending far beyond the bodily face.

Abraham’s story brings this breadth of meaning into sharper view:

Al-An'am 6:79

إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ حَنِيفًا ۖ وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

Translation: I have turned my wajh, as a hanif, toward the One who brought the heavens and earth into being; I am not among those who associate partners with Him.

Abraham is plainly not aligning his face with a geographical coordinate in order to locate God. The Creator of the heavens and earth is not confined to north, south, east, or west. Abraham is declaring the direction of his devotion and the course of his existence. Ar-Rum 30:30 and Yunus 10:105 use the same language concerning din: establish your wajh toward din as a hanif. Din is not a point on the globe toward which a bodily face can turn.

That semantic field cannot simply be discarded when we encounter the command to turn the wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram. The sanctuary is certainly real, but wajh can carry the weight of a person’s entire religious orientation. The bodily face may also turn as an expression of that inward commitment. That is precisely the power of a symbol: bodily action can recall a truth to the person performing it. The failure comes when the body turns but inward allegiance remains unchanged. The symbol has then been emptied of its meaning.

Al-A‘raf 7:29 brings this connection into the setting of worship:

قُلْ أَمَرَ رَبِّي بِالْقِسْطِ ۖ وَأَقِيمُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ عِنْدَ كُلِّ مَسْجِدٍ وَادْعُوهُ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ ۚ كَمَا بَدَأَكُمْ تَعُودُونَ

Al-A'raf 7:29

Translation: Say: my Lord has commanded justice. Establish your faces at every place of worship, and call upon Him, making your din sincere for Him. As He brought you into being, so you will return.

Justice, establishing the wajh, the place of worship, and sincerity in din appear together in one structure. Presence in a mosque cannot diminish the duty of justice; outward worship cannot replace inward sincerity. What must be set upright is the person’s whole wajh and being, not merely the neck and body.

God’s wajh: qibla is not God’s geographical address

Al-Baqarah 2:115 establishes a boundary that no interpretation of qibla may cross:

Al-Baqarah 2:115

وَلِلَّهِ الْمَشْرِقُ وَالْمَغْرِبُ ۚ فَأَيْنَمَا تُوَلُّوا فَثَمَّ وَجْهُ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Translation: To the God belong east and west. Wherever you turn, there is the wajh of the God. The God is all-encompassing, all-knowing.

Whatever qibla ultimately means, it is not an address at which God resides. Turning toward the Kaaba does not mean that the one, incomparable God sits behind its stone walls. Before discussing qibla, the Qur’an dismantles that misconception. East and west alike belong to Him; wherever people turn, they do not leave the reach of His presence and boundless knowledge.

Qibla is His command, not His address.

The full force of this statement emerges when the command itself is understood as serving human growth. If all our care goes into locating an exact bodily bearing while our inward orientation lacks justice, devotion to the one God, and Abraham’s way, we may have found the right address while losing the destination.

The homes of Moses’ people: when houses themselves become qibla

The only occurrence of qibla outside Al-Baqarah appears in Moses’ story:

Yunus 10:87

وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ وَأَخِيهِ أَنْ تَبَوَّآ لِقَوْمِكُمَا بِمِصْرَ بُيُوتًا وَاجْعَلُوا بُيُوتَكُمْ قِبْلَةً وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Translation: We revealed to Moses and his brother: provide houses in Egypt for your people, make your houses a qibla, establish Salat, and give good news to the believers.

This verse is crucial to any definition of qibla. It does not say “build your houses facing the qibla.” It says make the houses themselves a qibla. If qibla is understood only as a distant geographical point with which bodies must align, this use in Yunus becomes decidedly unnatural.

The verse supplies no architectural plan. It does not tell people to build houses directly opposite one another or to look at one particular house during Salat. Under Pharaoh’s oppression, the homes of Moses’ followers acquire a new significance, and the command to establish Salat accompanies this change. The houses become centers of cohesion, solidarity, and endurance for the community.

This verse alone may not prove that qibla is always entirely intellectual. It does, however, unsettle the assumption that qibla simply means turning a body toward a single building. A qibla can be the focus through which a community preserves its identity and direction.

Do the Torah and Gospels prescribe a bodily qibla?

This question matters for Al-Baqarah 2:145, though not because “their qibla” must necessarily appear as an explicit command in the Torah or Gospels. The Qur’an says that the People of the Book have qiblas they follow. Their foundational texts therefore need to be examined for the relationship they establish between worship, sacred place, and religious identity.

The Torah—the five books of Moses—does provide for a sacred center of ritual. Deuteronomy speaks of the place God chooses for His name, to which people travel for their rites. Yet it offers no clear command equivalent to “wherever you are, turn toward this place when you pray.” Turning toward Jerusalem appears later in Kings and Daniel, rather than as a command within the Torah itself.

The four Gospels likewise prescribe no fixed bodily qibla for Jesus’ followers. More significant than that silence is his conversation with the Samaritan woman in John 4. The dispute concerns two real sacred centers, Gerizim and Jerusalem. Jesus does not designate one as a new qibla; he turns the discussion toward worship in spirit and truth. Real places can embody a community’s religious identity, yet the reality of worship is not imprisoned within them.

This examination does not by itself prove that “their qibla” in Al-Baqarah had no spatial dimension. It shows that, among the People of the Book, sacred centers and communal identity could be intertwined. That conclusion fits this essay’s thesis: a real place and an intellectual orientation need not compete; a place can embody a path.

Salat and the House: was turning toward the Kaaba a symbolic language?

The absence of the word Salat from Al-Baqarah 2:142, 2:143, 2:144, 2:145, 2:146, 2:147, 2:148, 2:149, 2:150 does not mean that the Kaaba and Salat are unrelated. Other Qur’anic passages make that relationship clear.

Al-Baqarah 2:125 says:

وَإِذْ جَعَلْنَا الْبَيْتَ مَثَابَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَأَمْنًا وَاتَّخِذُوا مِنْ مَقَامِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ مُصَلًّى ۖ وَعَهِدْنَا إِلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ أَنْ طَهِّرَا بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْعَاكِفِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

Al-Baqarah 2:125

Translation: When We made the House a place of return and security for people: take Abraham’s station as a place of Salat. We charged Abraham and Ishmael: purify My House for those who circle it, those who remain there, and those who bow and prostrate.

In Ibrahim 14:37, Abraham settles his descendants near “Your inviolable House” and says, “Our Lord, so that they may establish Salat.” Al-Anfal 8:35 likewise speaks of “their Salat at the House.” Salat and the House are thus plainly connected within the Qur’an.

An important distinction remains. Al-Baqarah 2:142, 2:143, 2:144, 2:145, 2:146, 2:147, 2:148, 2:149, 2:150 does not explicitly say, “Turn toward al-Masjid al-Haram during Salat.” That connection is inferred by reading several sets of verses together: the qibla verses command turning the wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram wherever one is; Al-A‘raf 7:29 speaks of establishing the wajh at every place of worship; Al-Baqarah 2:125 connects Abraham’s station with a place of Salat; Ibrahim 14:37 places the establishment of Salat beside the inviolable House; and Al-Anfal 8:35 mentions Salat “at the House.” Together these strengthen the connection, but an inference must still be distinguished from the Qur’an’s express wording.

Read together, the verses suggest that if believers stood toward the Kaaba during Salat, that act could have been a daily enactment of their intellectual and religious allegiance. The body faced Abraham’s House so that the person would remember to direct heart and mind, as a hanif, toward the Lord of that House. The symbol served its meaning.

“Why face the Kaaba?” then receives a deeper answer. God is not located on that side, and a stone building does not need our reverence. The House should recall the path a community has chosen to follow. If Salat fulfilled this role, each establishment of it renewed a commitment to that path.

Hanif: freedom from inherited qiblas

Abraham’s greatness in the Qur’an does not consist merely in raising a building’s walls. Before he is the builder of the Kaaba, he is a person who reclaims his mind from society and his forebears, entrusting it to God alone.

He grew up among people who had given their objects of worship the authority of tradition and history. When he questioned them, they repeatedly appealed to their ancestors. Abraham did not mistake a belief’s age for evidence of its truth. He questioned what his father held sacred, did not exempt his people’s idols from examination, and was not stopped even by the splendor of star, moon, and sun. His destination was the One who brought the heavens and earth into being.

In the Qur’an, hanif is not the name of a third denomination alongside Judaism and Christianity. Against the invitation to become Jewish or Christian in order to be guided, Al-Baqarah 2:135 says: rather, the milla of Abraham, the hanif. Aal ‘Imran 3:67 insists that Abraham was neither Jewish nor Christian: he was a hanif who surrendered. An-Nisa 4:125 places following Abraham’s hanif way beside surrendering one’s wajh to God. Al-An‘am 6:79 portrays Abraham turning his wajh, as a hanif, toward the Creator.

A hanif does not leave every tradition in order to remain forever directionless. Freedom from captivity to tradition makes it possible to receive direction from truth. Freedom precedes surrender: released from the pressure of community, ancestry, and habit, a person consciously turns toward the one God.

This takes qibla beyond a geographical dispute. In Al-Baqarah, its change is discussed amid Judaism, Christianity, Abraham’s way, and following desires. The emerging community had to resist defining its religious identity through the approval of earlier communities. Turning toward Abraham’s House, even when enacted bodily, symbolized this independence: becoming a hanif, rather than becoming Jewish or Christian.

The inviolable House: a building that must represent a way

The Qur’an presents the Kaaba as more than a cube. The verse that names it also states its function:

Al-Ma'idah 5:97

جَعَلَ اللَّهُ الْكَعْبَةَ الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ قِيَامًا لِلنَّاسِ وَالشَّهْرَ الْحَرَامَ وَالْهَدْيَ وَالْقَلَائِدَ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ لِتَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

Translation: The God made the Kaaba, the inviolable House, a support for people, together with the inviolable month, the offerings, and their garlands. This is so that you may know that the God knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth, and that the God knows all things.

Qiyaman li-l-nas gives the House a significance far greater than an address on a map. An-Nisa 4:5 uses qiyam for the wealth God has made a means of sustaining life. The Kaaba, too, should sustain people. A stone structure around which people circulate, but which strengthens no foundation of their lives, has not fulfilled the whole purpose the Qur’an assigns to it.

Aal ‘Imran connects that purpose directly with Abraham’s way. First it says:

قُلْ صَدَقَ اللَّهُ ۗ فَاتَّبِعُوا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

Al-Imran 3:95

Say: the God has spoken the truth. Follow Abraham’s way as a hanif; he was not among those who associated partners with Him.

It then immediately speaks of the House:

إِنَّ أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ لَلَّذِي بِبَكَّةَ مُبَارَكًا وَهُدًى لِلْعَالَمِينَ

Al-Imran 3:96

Translation: The first House established for people is the one at Bakkah, blessed and a guidance for all worlds.

The Qur’an goes on to describe clear signs within the House:

Al-Imran 3:97

فِيهِ آيَاتٌ بَيِّنَاتٌ مَقَامُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ ۖ وَمَنْ دَخَلَهُ كَانَ آمِنًا ۗ وَلِلَّهِ عَلَى النَّاسِ حِجُّ الْبَيْتِ مَنِ اسْتَطَاعَ إِلَيْهِ سَبِيلًا ۚ وَمَنْ كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ عَنِ الْعَالَمِينَ

Translation: In it are clear signs, Abraham’s station; whoever enters it is secure. Pilgrimage to the House is owed to the God by those able to find a way to it. Whoever rejects— the God has no need of the worlds.

The sequence is telling: follow Abraham’s way, then the introduction of the House. The House cannot be separated from his path. Calling it a guidance for all worlds requires it to offer something that guides. “Clear signs” again introduces the language of signification: something within this House must point beyond its stone and structure. If the Kaaba were merely a travel destination, “guidance for all worlds” would seem an extravagant description.

Al-Baqarah 2:125 calls the House a place of return and security for people and charges Abraham and Ishmael with purifying it. When they raise its foundations a few verses later, their prayer is not for stronger building materials. They ask for surrender, a community that surrenders, rites, a messenger, teaching, and purification. As the walls rise, Abraham looks through stone and mortar toward the human being.

Surah Quraysh does not command worship of the House. It says:

فَلْيَعْبُدُوا رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ ۝ الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُمْ مِنْ جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُمْ مِنْ خَوْفٍ

Quraysh 106:3, 106:4

Translation: Let them worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and secured them against fear.

The House is honored, but it is not an object of worship. Even its remembrance is connected with two basic human needs: food against deprivation and safety against fear. This is how its role as a support for people becomes real in human life.

Surah Al-Hajj: the Kaaba’s charter of meaning

Surah Al-Hajj is among the clearest places to ask what this House should represent in the world. It does not allow the Kaaba to be detached from people, God’s oneness, freedom of devotion, taqwa, or care for those in need.

Al-Hajj 22:25

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَالْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ الَّذِي جَعَلْنَاهُ لِلنَّاسِ سَوَاءً الْعَاكِفُ فِيهِ وَالْبَادِ ۚ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ فِيهِ بِإِلْحَادٍ بِظُلْمٍ نُذِقْهُ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

Translation: Those who reject and obstruct the way of the God and al-Masjid al-Haram—which We made for people, resident and visitor alike—and whoever intends wrongful deviation there: We shall make them taste a painful punishment.

The verse does not begin with “for people,” but it arrives there: al-Masjid al-Haram is for people. Residents and those arriving from elsewhere stand equal. Birthplace, political power, wealth, or possession of the Kaaba’s keys cannot give anyone a greater claim to it. The Qur’an removes the House from claims of exclusive ownership.

The verse also places obstruction of God’s way alongside obstruction of al-Masjid al-Haram. Access to the House and access to God’s way are not unrelated. Preserving the Kaaba’s walls while closing God’s way to people is not service to the Kaaba.

The next verse turns to Abraham:

Al-Hajj 22:26

وَإِذْ بَوَّأْنَا لِإِبْرَاهِيمَ مَكَانَ الْبَيْتِ أَنْ لَا تُشْرِكْ بِي شَيْئًا وَطَهِّرْ بَيْتِيَ لِلطَّائِفِينَ وَالْقَائِمِينَ وَالرُّكَّعِ السُّجُودِ

Translation: When We assigned Abraham the site of the House: associate nothing with Me, and purify My House for those who circle it, those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate.

Before circumambulation, sacrifice, pilgrimage clothing, or travel, the House’s first principle is associate nothing with Me. Millions may circle it, but if a human being, scholar, particular report, nation, government, or leader occupies a position whose command rivals God’s, the House’s purpose has been violated. Abraham’s Kaaba is, above all, a House of devotion to God alone.

The second command is to purify the House. This cannot mean only dusting its stones. When the preceding command expressly rejects shirk, the House’s purity cannot be severed from God’s oneness. It must be kept free of whatever draws people away from the one God.

Only after these foundations are laid does the call go out:

وَأَذِّنْ فِي النَّاسِ بِالْحَجِّ يَأْتُوكَ رِجَالًا وَعَلَىٰ كُلِّ ضَامِرٍ يَأْتِينَ مِنْ كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ

Al-Hajj 22:27

Proclaim Hajj among people: they will come to you on foot and on every lean mount, arriving from every distant pass.

The invitation is addressed to people, who come from the most distant places. But for what purpose?

لِيَشْهَدُوا مَنَافِعَ لَهُمْ ... فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْبَائِسَ الْفَقِيرَ

Al-Hajj 22:28

So that they may witness benefits for themselves … eat from them and feed the poor in distress.

To witness benefits for themselves, remember God’s name, and feed the poor in distress. Hajj is more than a crowd’s mechanical movement around stone. Social benefit and feeding hungry people belong at the heart of its rites.

A few verses later, the surah returns to its first principle:

Al-Hajj 22:31

حُنَفَاءَ لِلَّهِ غَيْرَ مُشْرِكِينَ بِهِ ۚ وَمَنْ يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَكَأَنَّمَا خَرَّ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ أَوْ تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي مَكَانٍ سَحِيقٍ

Translation: Be hanifs for the God, associating nothing with Him. Whoever associates partners with the God is like someone falling from the sky, snatched away by birds or swept by the wind to a distant place.

Hajj without hanif devotion has lost the Kaaba’s spirit. The Abraham who purifies the House is also the example of a person who escapes inherited objects of worship and turns wholly toward God.

The Qur’an then prevents ritual from becoming a vehicle for sectarian pride:

Al-Hajj 22:34

وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا لِيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَىٰ مَا رَزَقَهُمْ مِنْ بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ ۗ فَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ فَلَهُ أَسْلِمُوا ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُخْبِتِينَ

Translation: For every community We appointed a rite, so that they might remember the God’s name over the livestock He provided them. Your God is one God: surrender to Him, and give good news to the humble.

Ritual forms may differ between communities, but the One worshipped is the same. This message stands within Surah Al-Hajj itself. A House embodying hanif devotion cannot become a means of claiming exclusive ownership of God. Forms differ; surrender to the one, incomparable God is what ultimately connects them.

Sha‘a’ir: the Qur’an’s language for what we call symbols

This essay has repeatedly used “symbol.” The Qur’an has its own language for this field: sha‘a’ir. It names Safa and Marwa among God’s sha‘a’ir (Al-Baqarah 2:158), and Surah Al-Hajj says:

Al-Hajj 22:32

ذَٰلِكَ وَمَنْ يُعَظِّمْ شَعَائِرَ اللَّهِ فَإِنَّهَا مِنْ تَقْوَى الْقُلُوبِ

Translation: Thus it is. Whoever honors the God’s sha‘a’ir: this comes from the hearts’ taqwa.

A few verses later, the sacrificial animals themselves are expressly included among these signs:

Al-Hajj 22:36

وَالْبُدْنَ جَعَلْنَاهَا لَكُمْ مِنْ شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ فِيهَا خَيْرٌ ۖ فَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا صَوَافَّ ۖ فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرْنَاهَا لَكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

Translation: We made the sacrificial camels among the God’s sha‘a’ir for you; in them is good for you. Remember His name over them as they stand in rows. When they have fallen on their sides, eat from them and feed both the unasking needy and those who ask. Thus We subjected them to you, so that you might be grateful.

These verses are central to the argument. A sha‘irah is real and visible, but its value does not end with its physical substance. Honoring the signs is connected to the hearts’ taqwa, while sacrifice takes its meaning from remembrance, goodness, and feeding people. The House is likewise described through clear signs. “Symbol” here therefore names nothing alien to Qur’anic language: it concerns signs whose outward form should carry meaning.

Sacrifice repeats precisely this lesson:

Al-Hajj 22:37

لَنْ يَنَالَ اللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَاؤُهَا وَلَٰكِنْ يَنَالُهُ التَّقْوَىٰ مِنْكُمْ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرَهَا لَكُمْ لِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

Translation: Neither their flesh nor their blood reaches the God; what reaches Him is your taqwa. Thus He subjected them to you, so that you might magnify Him for His guidance to you. Give good news to those who do good.

This may be the clearest key to the relationship between symbol and meaning. Sacrifice is a real act: an animal is physically slaughtered. Yet the Qur’an explicitly says that neither flesh nor blood reaches God. What matters is taqwa. The outward form is not abolished, but it is never allowed to take the place of the reality it should express.

Qibla may work in precisely this way. A body physically faces the Kaaba, but the one God has no need of a particular angle of our necks. If facing the Kaaba expresses a hanif orientation, the act draws its value from the meaning it should keep alive within us. A bearing without inward allegiance is as empty as sacrificial blood without taqwa.

Freedom of devotion and the sanctity of other places of worship

Surah Al-Hajj does not confine itself to rites within the sanctuary. A few verses later, its defense of people suffering injustice widens the scope considerably:

Al-Hajj 22:39, 22:40

أُذِنَ لِلَّذِينَ يُقَاتَلُونَ بِأَنَّهُمْ ظُلِمُوا وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ نَصْرِهِمْ لَقَدِيرٌ ۝ الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقُولُوا رَبُّنَا اللَّهُ ۗ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا ۗ وَلَيَنْصُرَنَّ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَنْصُرُهُ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَقَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

Translation: Permission is given to those against whom war is made, because they have been wronged; the God is able to help them. They were expelled from their homes without right, only for saying, “Our Lord is the God.” Had the God not checked some people by means of others, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which His name is often remembered would have been demolished. The God will surely help those who help Him; He is strong, mighty.

The same surah that explains the Kaaba and Hajj also recognizes the sanctity of other people’s places of worship. Monasteries, churches, and synagogues are not worthless in the Qur’an’s view. If the inviolable House embodied a sect that refused to recognize others’ right to worship, this passage in Surah Al-Hajj would be profoundly incongruous.

The Qur’an’s defense of freedom of belief goes further. “No compulsion in din” does not permit faith to be imposed by force. Yunus 10:99 asks the Messenger whether he would compel people to become believers. Az-Zumar 39:18 praises those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it. The road to God does not run through terror and the suppression of questions. Faith has value when people can hear, weigh, and freely surrender.

If Abraham’s House represents this din, its purpose is not to force everyone into one prescribed posture. Under its meaning, people should be able to find their way to God without fear or coercion, just as Abraham found his way by breaking the hold of his people’s intellectual idols.

When the sign takes the place of its meaning

A distinction is needed between signs the Qur’an identifies and the sacred associations later woven around them. It names the Kaaba, the inviolable House, Abraham’s station, Safa, and Marwa; it expressly includes sacrificial animals among God’s signs. Yet it never names the Black Stone. Nowhere does it command kissing or touching a black stone, or attributing supernatural power to one.

That silence does not, by itself, establish that touching the stone is forbidden. It does establish a limit: the status the Qur’an gives the House as a whole cannot simply be transferred to every object and custom associated with it. The Kaaba has a defined purpose in the Qur’an. Historical additions require their own examination.

This example shows how a religious symbol can be buried beneath accumulated custom and superstition. A House initially embodying God’s oneness becomes surrounded by objects, reports, claims of special merit, and ritual details. These can grow until the House’s original message disappears behind them. A person may then fight others to touch a piece of stone while moving far from the command to associate nothing with God.

The objection is not to a piece of stone, which makes no claims of its own. The question is whether people still understand why they face this House.

Righteousness extends beyond east and west

Al-Baqarah 2:177 offers one of the Qur’an’s clearest distinctions between outward form and the substance of din:

لَيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا ۖ وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ ۗ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ

Al-Baqarah 2:177

Translation: Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward east and west. Rather, righteous is the person who believes in the God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; who gives wealth, despite loving it, to relatives, orphans, those in need, travelers, those who ask, and for freeing people in bondage; who establishes Salat and gives Zakat. They fulfill their pledges when they pledge, and are steadfast in poverty, suffering, and battle. These are the truthful; these are the people of taqwa.

The Qur’an does not deny east and west. It says righteousness is not exhausted by these orientations. Righteousness is freed from mere appearance and located in faith, generosity, freeing the captive, establishing Salat, Zakat, fidelity to covenants, and steadfastness.

The verse sets a limit on what qibla can mean. An exact geographical bearing does not make a person righteous if they break pledges, deny the needy their due, and commit injustice. A community may adorn the Kaaba with fine stone and gold, yet violate God’s oneness, freedom of faith, people’s rights, and taqwa. The House’s splendor cannot compensate for the loss of its meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:189 follows the same approach. It rejects entering houses from the back as a measure of righteousness and directs people to enter through their doors. The Qur’an can retain an outward practice while refusing to make that practice the sole measure of goodness. The same reasoning applies to qibla: the outward form can remain, but that form is not our god.

Can the House’s meaning change?

The Kaaba’s walls may remain, its covering be renewed each year, circumambulation continue, and millions establish Salat facing it, while its significance in people’s actual lives changes. The Qur’an itself presents precisely this possibility.

Al-Anfal 8:34 says:

وَمَا لَهُمْ أَلَّا يُعَذِّبَهُمُ اللَّهُ وَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَمَا كَانُوا أَوْلِيَاءَهُ ۚ إِنْ أَوْلِيَاؤُهُ إِلَّا الْمُتَّقُونَ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

Al-Anfal 8:34

Translation: Why should the God not punish them when they obstruct al-Masjid al-Haram, though they are not its guardians? Its guardians are only the people of taqwa, but most of them do not know.

The warning applies in every age. People may control the sanctuary, regulate access, and hold its keys, yet the Qur’an may say of them: they are not its guardians. Physical control does not confer spiritual entitlement. Possessing a door’s key is not a title to guardianship of the House.

The next verse does not exempt even Salat performed beside the House from criticism:

وَمَا كَانَ صَلَاتُهُمْ عِنْدَ الْبَيْتِ إِلَّا مُكَاءً وَتَصْدِيَةً ۚ فَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ

Al-Anfal 8:35

Translation: Their Salat at the House was nothing but whistling and clapping. Taste the punishment, then, for your rejection.

The House may be the right House while the Salat beside it is wrong and emptied of meaning. A place can retain its sanctity while people invert what it represents. This is what “changing the House’s meaning” means here. The expression al-Bayt al-Haram has not acquired a new dictionary definition; rather, the purpose the House should embody becomes buried beneath alien ideas.

At-Tawbah 9:17, 9:18, 9:19 likewise refuses to let outward service to the sanctuary replace faith and striving in God’s way. Maintaining al-Masjid al-Haram and providing water to pilgrims are valuable, but the Qur’an does not automatically equate them with faith. Construction, expansion, management, and service can be beneficial without proving the integrity of those who undertake them.

The question is thoroughly Qur’anic: who determines what the House represents today? Does the Kaaba still embody what Surah Al-Hajj describes, or have society and political power dressed it in a different meaning?

When a symbol’s meaning changes: abandonment or restoration?

We now reach the essay’s central dilemma. If facing the Kaaba physically expresses a spiritual orientation, and that orientation has been distorted in practice, should a believer turn away from the physical direction too?

The Qur’an offers no such model. Where the House is defiled, its response is to restore its meaning.

God does not tell Abraham to find another house because shirk has gathered around this one. He says:

«أن لا تشرك بي شيئا وطهر بيتي».

Al-Hajj 22:26

Associate nothing with Me, and purify My House.

Nor are Abraham and Ishmael told to distance themselves from the House. They are commanded:

«طهرا بيتي».

Al-Baqarah 2:125

Purify My House.

In Al-Anfal, when unworthy people control al-Masjid al-Haram and obstruct access, the Qur’an rejects their claim to guardianship, rather than invalidating the House itself.

An important objection remains: the Qur’an describes one change of qibla, so why could another change not follow a further distortion of its meaning? The answer lies in the direction of the change described. Immediately before the qibla passage, Al-Baqarah discusses Abraham’s milla and then turns to al-Masjid al-Haram. The change is a return to the House connected with Abraham. The same Qur’an knows that people may be barred from the sanctuary and that those controlling it may not be its rightful guardians; even so, it does not cancel the House’s sanctity. Al-Baqarah 2:125 and Al-Hajj 22:26 answer defilement with purification, not the search for another house.

The response is therefore purification rather than abandonment. A distorted qibla calls for the recovery of its original meaning, not the invention of a replacement.

The spiritual significance is considerable. If the Kaaba becomes an instrument of sectarian superiority, if a House meant for people becomes the possession of a political system, if hanif devotion fades beneath prejudice and the proclamation of one God is smothered by reports and intermediaries, turning away from the Kaaba is not the remedy. We must turn again toward the Lord of this House and reclaim its meaning from those who have appropriated it.

Facing the Kaaba can then acquire a profound meaning of protest. Standing toward the same House, a person refuses to concede it to any sect, state, or religious institution. They do not prostrate before stone, but worship its Lord. They surrender to the House’s true owner, rather than its political ruler. Facing the House becomes an act of restoring its forgotten meaning.

The qibla should not be replaced; its meaning should be restored.

An intellectual qibla: when bodily direction and the source of authority diverge

If qibla is more than a geographical direction, another question follows: where do people actually receive their bearings? Who determines truth and falsehood for them? Whose words do they accept without examination? When inherited custom clashes with truth, where do they stand?

The Qur’an has its own vocabulary for these questions: following, milla, desires, guardianship, obedience, legislation, and lawful and unlawful. “Intellectual qibla” serves here as an umbrella for these concepts. It is not a claim that the Qur’an literally uses qibla to mean an intellectual authority.

At-Tawbah 9:31 warns that people can take scholars and monks as lords in place of God. An-Nahl 16:116 denies the human tongue authority to declare things lawful or unlawful without God’s permission. Yunus 10:59 asks directly whether God has authorized such declarations or whether people are inventing lies about Him. Ash-Shura 42:21 speaks of partners who legislate in din without His authorization.

These verses are not hostile to scholars or knowledge. The Qur’an itself tells those who do not know to ask people of remembrance and calls for a group to gain understanding in din. Asking is not the problem. The failure comes when final authority to judge is handed to someone else. A scholar may illuminate a path, but cannot take the place of its ultimate source of light.

An-Nisa 4:59 preserves the same distinction. It speaks of obeying those entrusted with authority, but directs disputes back to God and His Messenger. No person or institution receives an unquestionable final word.

We can therefore understand how someone may physically face the Kaaba while an intellectual and religious qibla has been established elsewhere.

Catholicism and Rome as a center of authority

The Gospels prescribe no particular bodily qibla for Christianity. Yet Rome became Catholicism’s principal center of religious authority. Catholic canon law gives the Bishop of Rome supreme, full, and immediate authority over the whole Church, while the Trinity stands at the center of Catholic doctrine.4

From a Qur’anic standpoint, the criticism does not concern Rome itself. A person living there whose heart surrenders to God alone is not altered by geography. The question is where final authority over belief resides. The Qur’an presents Jesus as saying:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ رَبِّي وَرَبُّكُمْ فَاعْبُدُوهُ ۗ هَٰذَا صِرَاطٌ مُسْتَقِيمٌ

Al-Imran 3:51

Translation: The God is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him. This is a straight path. (Aal ‘Imran 3:51)

Al-Ma’idah also criticizes the divinity of Christ and the Trinity. If an institution’s account of God is accepted as final despite the Qur’an’s rejection of it, then, in this essay’s terms, a person’s intellectual orientation has turned toward that institution—even without ever physically facing the Vatican. The Vatican provides a concrete illustration of an intellectual qibla: a physical place representing beliefs and authority. It helps explain how al-Masjid al-Haram, too, can be both a real place and the sign of a way.

Jerusalem: politics in the shadow of a sacred city

Jerusalem holds a profound place in Jewish heritage, although the Torah of Moses prescribes no fixed bodily direction for prayer. Later Hebrew writings made the city and its Temple a focus of prayer. Yet prophets within those same writings repeatedly warn that taking refuge in the Temple cannot erase injustice. Jeremiah condemns those who invoke “the Temple of the Lord” while oppressing foreigners, orphans, and widows and shedding innocent blood.

The principle remains relevant: sacred land cannot justify oppression. Political Zionism places the State of Israel and Jerusalem at the heart of national identity, emphasizing attachment to the land and settlement there.5 This is a political and national project and must, like other political projects, be measured against justice.

Al-Ma’idah 5:8 sets the standard so firmly that even hostility cannot excuse injustice:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُونُوا قَوَّامِينَ لِلَّهِ شُهَدَاءَ بِالْقِسْطِ ۖ وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ عَلَىٰ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا ۚ اعْدِلُوا هُوَ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَى

Al-Ma'idah 5:8

Translation: You who believe, stand firmly for the God as witnesses to justice. Do not let hatred of a people lead you to abandon justice. Be just: that is nearer to taqwa.

In recent years, international bodies have documented displacement, home demolitions, and harm to civilians.6 No religious interpretation of promise or land can remove human life, homes, or children from the claims of justice. When “holy land” grows so absolute that living people count for nothing before it, territory has become a political qibla.

This criticism is not directed at people for being Jewish. It follows precisely the Qur’anic standard that forbids hostility toward a people from producing injustice. Criticism of political Zionism is distinct from antisemitism, just as criticism of historical caliphates, the Vatican, or Saudi rule is distinct from hostility toward the adherents of their religions.

Christian Zionism: when prophecy displaces the human being

In parts of the evangelical world, support for Israel is connected with interpretations of the end times. Research identifies expectations of fulfilled biblical prophecy and Jesus’ approaching return as important motivations for some within this movement.7

Hope for the future is not the problem. The crisis begins when living people and their suffering become instruments in an end-times scenario. If war, seizure of land, or displacement is treated as necessary merely because it is thought to fulfill prophecy, ethics has been sacrificed to interpretation.

The Qur’an grants no such permission. No promise suspends justice, and no expectation of the end times makes innocent blood less serious. When an interpreter becomes so absorbed in a scheme of history’s ending that human suffering fades from view, the intellectual qibla is no longer human right and God’s approval, but the interpreter’s own scenario.

Shi‘ism: the Kaaba ahead, authority elsewhere

Twelver Shi‘i tradition recognizes the Kaaba as the bodily qibla, while its system of taqlid connects much of an individual’s practical religious conduct to a marja‘’s rulings. Najaf and Qom have become major centers of that authority; Karbala occupies an exceptional place in Shi‘i emotional and ritual identity.8

Adherents explain that a jurist does not invent a new ruling for God but seeks to derive a ruling from religious sources. That explanation deserves fair consideration. This essay’s question begins at the next stage: can the follower examine the grounds of that inference? Does the ruling remain open to criticism against the Book, or does the fatwa itself become the point at which questions stop?

Consulting scholars and specialists is not inherently wrong. No one discovers all knowledge from the beginning. The danger begins when “the marja‘ said” replaces “what is the evidence?” A person’s status then exempts an opinion from examination against the Book, and asking for its grounds becomes an act of disrespect.

In that setting, someone may physically face the Kaaba but receive authoritative direction elsewhere. Qom and Najaf can become juristic qiblas—not because Shi‘is face them during Salat, but because final determinations of lawful, unlawful, and religious obligation effectively issue from them.

Karbala, too, can function in two ways. Remembering Husayn can express fidelity to justice and resistance to oppression. But when emotional attachment to his name overtakes the justice for which he died, the sign has displaced its meaning: people weep for Husayn while remaining silent about injustice inflicted on others. Intercession must be measured by the same Qur’anic standard. The Qur’an assigns it to God and makes it dependent on His permission, while rejecting reliance on unauthorized intercession. (Al-Baqarah 2:48, 2:123, 2:255; Yunus 10:3; Saba 34:23; Az-Zumar 39:44) If a leader’s name becomes an unconditional guarantee of salvation, trust has again shifted from God toward a created being. The House associated with Abraham, who left ancestral imitation behind, should not return people to the captivity from which he freed himself.

Sunni tradition: when reports and schools displace the criterion

Within Sunni tradition, authority takes a somewhat different form. Four legal schools, large hadith collections, and centuries of commentary and rulings form an extensive body of scholarship. That historical inheritance and scholarly labor cannot simply be dismissed. Yet neither the age nor the scale of human discourse turns it into revelation.

The problem arises when a school determines an answer in advance and the Qur’an is then read within that predetermined answer. If an apparent conflict between a verse and a report is met by reinterpreting the verse until the report survives, the criterion and the material being tested have exchanged places.

The Qur’an’s questions about attributing rulings to God—An-Nahl 16:116, Yunus 10:59, and Ash-Shura 42:21—apply to anyone, not only Shi‘is or Sunnis. At the same time, the Qur’an establishes following the Messenger, presents him as an example, and speaks of his explaining what has been revealed. (Al-Ahzab 33:21; An-Nahl 16:44) Al-Hashr’s command to take what the Messenger gives occurs in the context of distributing fay’. (Al-Hashr 59:7) The issue here is therefore not whether to follow the Messenger. It is which reports actually came from him, what they mean, and how they should be assessed. The Qur’an presents itself as a detailed Book for judgment (Al-An‘am 6:114) and treats attribution of words and rulings to God as a grave responsibility. Labeling a text “hadith” must not close inquiry. If a report becomes so sacrosanct that the Qur’an needs its permission to establish its own authority, the source of religious legislation has effectively shifted. Someone may turn toward the Kaaba five times a day while the mind remains wholly surrendered to inherited schools. This is the division between form and meaning with which the essay began.

Custodians of the Two Sanctuaries: serving the House or owning its meaning?

The question becomes more sensitive when it reaches Mecca itself. Those administering al-Masjid al-Haram control access, services, security, construction, and the detailed arrangements of Hajj. Managing millions of people is plainly an unavoidable practical responsibility. But the Qur’an has already drawn a boundary: administrative control of the House is not ownership of its spiritual meaning.

Al-Hajj 22:25 makes the sanctuary for people and places resident and visitor on equal terms. Al-Ma’idah 5:97 calls the Kaaba a support for people. Al-Anfal 8:34 denies guardianship to those who practically controlled the sanctuary, reserving it to the people of taqwa. At-Tawbah 9:19 insists that services to pilgrims and physical maintenance alone do not establish superiority or truth.

Understood as a service role, “Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques” names a serious responsibility. It does not grant a special divine right to determine the Kaaba’s meaning. Whoever organizes visas, transportation, hotels, and security remains answerable to the House’s Qur’anic purpose.

Today Hajj is organized through permits, service companies, and travel packages. The official Nusuk platform offers categories differing in service and price.9 Meeting the costs of managing such numbers is natural, and the Qur’an does not prohibit trade during Hajj (Al-Baqarah 2:198). But trade during Hajj and turning Hajj itself into a commodity are different matters. Paying for food, lodging, and travel is one thing; pilgrimage becoming an increasingly stratified experience whose comfort and service depend on wealth is another. Different packages do not by themselves establish an ethical violation. Nevertheless, “for people” and equality between resident and visitor require questions about the boundary between necessary administration and privilege based on wealth.

The Kaaba may now be more elaborately adorned, protected, and administered than at any earlier time. Its black-and-gold covering is striking; enormous towers surround it; millions move around it with remarkable organization. But the Qur’an’s question concerns neither the splendor of the covering nor the size of the courtyards. Does this House still embody what Surah Al-Hajj says it should?

The same Qur’anic standards must be used to examine current conditions. Does access and administration reflect a House meant for people? Does equality between resident and visitor have practical ethical and legal force, or is it merely a historical phrase? Does the surrounding religious environment tolerate questions and the examination of inherited reports? Independent reporting on religious freedom in Saudi Arabia records serious restrictions on departure from official interpretations and on freedom of belief. Such evidence does not replace Qur’anic judgment, but it shows that these questions are not merely hypothetical.10

If a documented examination finds the House approaching a political or religious monopoly in practice, then what it represents in lived experience has changed. The Qur’an has not changed its definition; human structures have obscured the meaning it should embody. A House that should express hanif devotion and freedom can become an official showcase for a particular religious reading. A House for people can be constrained by one state’s power. A center that should focus attention on the one God can become so surrounded by human rulings and reports that questioning grows difficult.

This criticism does not overlook welfare services, security, or construction in the sanctuary. A government can manage crowds and cleanliness efficiently while remaining far from the Kaaba’s Qur’anic meaning. At-Tawbah 9:19 warns precisely that outward service does not answer every spiritual question.

Freedom of religion and thought in Abraham’s House

The Qur’an declares no compulsion in din, reminds the Messenger that people cannot be forced into faith, praises hearing different words and following the best, and places monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques together under protection in Surah Al-Hajj. How, then, could Abraham’s House symbolize imposed belief and intellectual rigidity?

Freedom of thought does not make every claim true. Abraham himself rejected many of his people’s beliefs. But his path began with questions: he examined, reflected, and asked for evidence. A House associated with that path should not fear inquiry and criticism.

Here form and meaning can separate. People may stand in impeccably ordered rows facing one way while a single interpretation is dictated to them all. The rows may be straight, yet the spirit of hanif devotion has been broken.

Should we change the bodily direction?

The question can now be stated directly: if the Kaaba has come to represent something distant from the Qur’anic House, should believers turn away from it during Salat?

Taken together, the verses yield a firm no. That answer is not a defense of existing conditions.

We do not replace the qibla because the fault does not lie in the House itself. The Qur’an still calls it guidance for all worlds, security, a House for people, and a support for people. The problem lies with those whose actions obscure its meaning. If the House’s owner has not changed, why abandon the House?

Facing the Kaaba can become testimony rather than routine: I stand toward this House, but receive its meaning from neither governments, denominations, reports, nor commerce. I receive it from the Qur’an that connects it with Abraham, God’s oneness, taqwa, and people’s rights.

Keeping the bodily qibla can thus mean reclaiming the sign from those who distort it, rather than accepting the status quo. The Qur’an itself offers the example: people controlled the sanctuary and performed Salat beside the House, but it recognized neither their guardianship nor their emptied-out Salat. Human deviation does not dissolve the House’s sanctity; it makes the restoration of its meaning more urgent.

God did not tell Abraham to replace the House. He told him to purify it.

That may be the essential answer toward which this entire essay leads.

Questions for a geometrical definition of qibla

If qibla is understood simply as an imaginary line from a person’s body to the Kaaba’s building, several straightforward questions expose the limits of that definition.

Which way should someone inside the Kaaba face?

Someone outside can face the House. Inside it, the supposed destination surrounds the worshipper. The Qur’an designates no particular wall as that person’s qibla.

This silence does not negate bodily direction. It does highlight that the command says toward al-Masjid al-Haram, not “toward the Kaaba.” Someone inside the Kaaba is already at the heart of the sanctuary toward which the command directs them. “Which wall is the qibla?” is therefore not a question generated by the verse’s wording. Defining qibla as an absolute straight line to a particular wall does not make the definition more faithful to that wording.

Above all, even inside the Kaaba, none of its walls is God. Surah Quraysh commands worship of the Lord of this House. Even surrounded by its walls, a person must turn their wajh toward its Lord.

A spherical earth and the obsession with bearings

On a spherical earth, “toward Mecca” still has a physical meaning, and routes to it can be calculated. At the point directly opposite the Kaaba, however, there is more than one equally long route to it. Moreover, people in the first Islamic century did not possess today’s instruments for measuring degrees and seconds of arc.

These observations do not invalidate qibla. They put geometrical obsession in perspective. The Qur’an uses shatr: toward, in the direction of. Modern precision can help people find a bearing, but the instrument must not take the place of worship itself.

If Salat is meant to establish the remembrance of God, then the moment concern about a degree of geographical error outweighs remembrance, the means has overtaken the end.

The previous qibla in the Qur’an

Al-Baqarah 2:143 says only that the Messenger had previously been upon a qibla. The Qur’an leaves it unnamed.

None of the verses concerning the change names Bayt al-Maqdis or Jerusalem. Al-Isra 17:1 mentions al-Masjid al-Aqsa, but never calls it the previous qibla. A study taking the Qur’an alone as its criterion should not force later historical reports into the text’s silence.

The point matters especially if qibla also bears a communal and intellectual meaning in this context. “The qibla you were upon” should not be reduced without argument to a coordinate. The Qur’an leaves its location and the details of the earlier command unstated. Just as we should not declare it non-spatial without evidence, we cannot add details the text has not supplied.

Muslim reports about Bayt al-Maqdis are important historical material, but they are not Qur’anic evidence. Jewish prayer toward Jerusalem before Islam is also a historical fact. That parallel may illuminate the setting in which some reports emerged, but it does not by itself establish that the Qur’an’s unnamed earlier qibla was Jerusalem.

What can archaeology establish?

The architecture of the Prophet’s Mosque, Quba Mosque, and the Mosque of the Two Qiblas is sometimes used to identify the earlier qibla. These buildings, however, have undergone repeated expansion and reconstruction; no untouched building from the earliest years of the migration survives there today. Present prayer niches and alignments cannot by themselves supply a definitive plan of those first structures.

An independently dated, undisturbed early layer showing two clear stages of changed orientation would be important evidence. The lack of such evidence does not invalidate historical reports, but neither does it permit reconstructed architecture to stand in for conclusive historical documentation.

The same caution applies to opposing theories. Different alignments in some early mosques or new geographical calculations cannot alone prove that the first qibla lay somewhere else. Research is valuable when its conclusions remain within the limits of its evidence.

Qibla remains alive when its meaning remains alive

The threads of the argument can now be brought together.

In Al-Baqarah, qibla appears among milla, following, desires, knowledge, faith, and guidance. That setting concerns a community’s identity and allegiance more than instructions for a compass. Verse 145 presents qibla as something followed, in a structure strikingly parallel to verse 120’s discussion of following a milla.

Yet al-Masjid al-Haram remains a physical place. The Kaaba is the inviolable House: people travel to it, enter it, are sometimes barred from it, circle it, and perform Hajj. Establishing qibla’s intellectual and spiritual dimensions requires neither denying this physical reality nor turning the Kaaba into an imaginary abstraction.

The central point is that a real physical place can embody a real orientation of thought and devotion.

The Kaaba is built of stone, but its meaning is neither stone-like nor lifeless. It should embody a House for people, security, guidance for all worlds, and a support for human life. It is associated with Abraham, the hanif who never placed father, people, or inherited tradition above truth. Surah Al-Hajj begins its instructions for the House by rejecting shirk, calls people to pilgrimage, establishes the poor’s share in its rites, distinguishes flesh and blood from taqwa, brings ritual diversity under the oneness of God, and defends monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques against destruction.

If a House with that purpose becomes an instrument of monopoly, imposed interpretation, political power, or stratified commerce, its meaning in practice has changed, even if the Qur’an’s verses and the Kaaba’s walls remain intact.

Here the argument parts company with abandoning the Kaaba. The Qur’an does not direct us to discard the House because its custodians are corrupt. Al-Anfal denies the usurping custodians’ claim to guardianship, rather than stripping the House of sanctity. In Al-Hajj, God commands Abraham to purify the House, not to build a replacement.

Returning to qibla is therefore more than returning to stone and brick. It is returning to the meaning those stones are meant to bear.

When a believer faces the Kaaba during Salat, the act can renew a daily covenant: I do not face the government administering this House, the denomination dominant around it, or the accumulated reports surrounding it. I face a sign charged with recalling Abraham’s hanif way.

The bodily qibla then serves as the body’s language for remembering that meaning, rather than competing with spiritual orientation.

The body says: I am directed toward this House.

The wajh says: my heart turns toward its Lord.

The path says: I follow Abraham’s hanif way.

Conduct must then demonstrate that these words are more than empty claims.

Qibla and unity: standing together to remember

If bodily orientation toward the Kaaba expresses qibla outwardly, one of its purposes becomes clearer. People of different cities, languages, ancestries, and backgrounds take one direction during Salat. This can express unity with remarkable simplicity: all stand before one God, and no nation, family, or clerical institution owns the path between us and Him.

Yet this expression of unity can turn into its opposite. People may face one House while condemning every other denomination as lost. Their bodies align while their hearts scatter in conflicting directions. If every group claims exclusive possession of salvation’s key, the bodily qibla produces only outward unity while intellectual allegiance generates division and resentment.

In the midst of the qibla passage, Al-Baqarah 2:148 recognizes different orientations and immediately commands competition in goodness. It turns disagreement away from a bloody contest of identity and toward good works. Al-Ma’idah 5:48 takes the same approach to different prescribed ways: God could have made everyone one community, but did not; people should therefore outdo one another in good.

The Kaaba cannot, on this reading, serve as a flag of collective vanity or superiority. The first House established for people is for all people, not a possession through which one group may claim to own God. Guidance for all worlds must reach beyond denominational labels. A support for people cannot fulfill that role only for one sect.

Turning toward the Kaaba should therefore break down arrogance and religious pride. All stand in one row toward one direction because no one possesses a private qibla: king and beggar, scholar and layperson, Arab and non-Arab. Someone who leaves Salat imagining an exclusive ownership of truth has seen the row’s physical form while missing its message.

Unity of qibla means more than matching clothes and synchronized movements. Its substance is that final authority belongs to one truth, and no community may install itself in God’s place. Surah Al-Hajj makes the point plainly: your God is one God, so surrender to Him.

Restoring the House’s meaning: criticism must begin with ourselves

If this essay leads only to the accusation that a government, denomination, or institution has distorted the House’s meaning, it has traveled only halfway. Before pointing outward, hanif devotion requires a person to look inward and recognize hidden qiblas within the self.

Someone may sharply criticize Saudi rule while blindly following a favored religious authority. Another may reject juristic imitation but accept an intellectual’s words without question. A critic of hadith-centered religion may give a personal reading such sacred status that no clear verse can correct it. An idol is not only a statue before which others kneel. Any idea, person, or belief exempted from examination can become one.

Abraham’s example begins here. His achievement was more than breaking wooden and stone idols: he reclaimed the direction of his own mind from his people’s traditions. An appeal to ancestral custom did not convince him. Majority habit did not become evidence. In the scenes of star, moon, and sun, no celestial splendor escaped scrutiny; what rises and sets cannot be his wajh’s final destination. Hanif devotion refuses to exempt anything from questioning merely because it is great, ancient, popular, or powerful.

Restoration of the House must begin in that spirit. God’s authority and human authority must be clearly distinguished. What God has not declared lawful or unlawful must not be entered into din under His name. Where the Qur’an recognizes plurality and different paths, sectarian narrowness must not imprison them in a single rigid form. What God leaves to human choice must not become an imposed belief.

The next step is to take “for people” seriously. The poor should not become wistful spectators of wealth being generated around a House meant for them. If Hajj serves people’s benefit, that public benefit should shape its administration. If sacrifice is connected with feeding the needy, pilgrimage cannot close its eyes to surrounding poverty. If the House is a center of security, human blood and life cannot be less inviolable than its stones.

Restoring the House’s meaning means hearing again the question implicit in Surah Quraysh: who is the Lord of this House? If our hearts answer “the one God,” no cleric, government, dynasty, leader, or report may share His absolute right in matters of divine legislation and worship.

Freedom of thought: Abraham’s House does not fear questions

Freedom of thought here does not mean absolute relativism or abandoning standards of truth. The Qur’an challenges false claims, argues against shirk, and rejects many paths. But criticizing ideas and coercing people are fundamentally different acts.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 declares no compulsion in din. Yunus 10:99 asks the Messenger: would you compel people to become believers? Az-Zumar 39:18 praises those who listen and follow the best of what is said. Al-Isra 17:36 warns against following what one does not know.

These teachings form a person who listens, weighs with reason, and then follows freely. Such a person can identify with Abraham’s way. Their faith is not the product of fear of questions.

A House bearing Abraham’s name should not fear free thought. If everything may be discussed except the ruling religious establishment; if human reports become absolutely immune to scrutiny; if a Muslim feels danger, hesitation, or shame at asking elementary Qur’anic questions, that environment is alien to the freedom of hanif devotion, however immaculate its architecture.

When Surah Al-Hajj calls for protecting monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques, more than brick and stone is at stake. It defends the human right to turn toward God through the ways people know. How could the House at the center of pilgrimage in that same surah become a symbol of silencing those who seek truth?

Restoration therefore means more than changing Hajj’s administration. It means renewing its spiritual environment: a place where people can emerge freely from shirk, move beyond inherited prejudice, hear different views, weigh evidence, and consciously entrust their wajh and being to their Lord.

The conclusion of this inquiry

The opening question was what qibla means within the Qur’an. The familiar answer confines it to the geographical direction of prayer. Examining the verses shows that the text does not settle for so restricted an account. In Al-Baqarah, qibla tests following and surrender; it is something followed, appears alongside milla and desires, and is directly connected to faith and guidance. The command to turn the wajh toward al-Masjid al-Haram comes within this very discussion.

Together, these indications lead to a different conclusion: qibla is, before and beyond a geometrical angle, the intellectual orientation and communal identity of a people. The Kaaba and al-Masjid al-Haram can physically embody that orientation because the Qur’anic House is connected with Abraham’s way, devotion to God alone, enduring security, the sustaining of human life, and hanif freedom.

If the earliest believers’ bodily orientation toward the Kaaba during Salat is inferred from the verses together, that practice can be understood as a daily declaration of independent identity. Their way was defined through Abraham’s hanif milla, rather than through the approval of Jews and Christians. The bodily turn expressed a deeper inward turning: the turning of the wajh.

Yet history teaches that a symbol can endure while its substance changes. The Qur’an describes people who controlled the sanctuary without deserving guardianship, and Salat beside the House reduced to whistling and clapping. Possessing the House, sweeping and serving it, or even worshipping beside its walls does not guarantee fidelity to its way.

The question is no different today. The House may be dressed in black silk and gold while hanif freedom is buried beneath religious monopolies, political power, large commercial interests, and fabricated human reports. The fault is not in its stones. Our faces may be aligned toward the Kaaba while our wajh and intellectual qibla have turned far elsewhere.

The Qur’anic response is not estrangement from the House. It is a return to the original charge to Abraham: restore devotion to the one God at its center and purify it. Purification reaches beyond breaking stone idols to removing whatever becomes an intermediary or barrier between people and the one, incomparable God.

We do not need to build a new qibla. Our responsibility is to understand this one anew and more deeply.

We stand toward the Kaaba, but we do not worship it.

We honor the House wholeheartedly, but recognize no state, king, or denomination as its absolute owner.

We observe the rites carefully, while refusing to substitute sacrificial flesh and blood for taqwa.

We learn from scholars, while handing no human being the exclusive right of divine legislation.

We respect inherited learning, while following Abraham in testing that inheritance against truth.

If the House’s meaning has been buried beneath history, we do not turn our backs on it. We set to work bringing that meaning back to life.

Perhaps this is qibla’s deepest significance: knowing where our wajh and whole being are directed, beyond the position of our faces.

Qibla is His command, not His address.

The final question may therefore reach beyond “Which way do you stand during prayer?” to something more searching:

As you stand facing the Kaaba, which way are you truly following in your heart and mind?
