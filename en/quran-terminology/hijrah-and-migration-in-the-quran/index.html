Introduction

Home is more than a place to live. It is where people are born, grow up, establish families, work, and form relationships with others and their surroundings. Childhood memories, language, culture, relatives, and possessions become bound up with that place. Leaving home and country can therefore be one of the hardest decisions a person makes.

Yet attachment can become excessive. People may become so devoted to their land, community, history, or religion that they refuse to leave even when their lives and futures are threatened. Honor, ethnic pride, religious prejudice, or fear of being called cowardly or disloyal may lead them to reject every alternative to staying.

Consider a population living under relentless bombardment. Homes are destroyed, children die, hospitals and schools disappear, and young people lose their education and prospects. Against artillery, tanks, and devastating weapons, they have few means of defense—perhaps little more than stones and sticks.

Is staying and being killed always courageous in such circumstances? Does someone who takes their children to a safer country betray their people and homeland? Could preserving life, gaining knowledge, and developing the capacity to continue resisting oppression be a wiser choice?

Personal attachment is only part of the problem. Political, religious, or communal leaders sometimes stir people’s emotions, presenting remaining under bombardment and sacrificing oneself as proofs of faith, honor, and loyalty. Those leaders and their families may enjoy safety and resources while demanding that ordinary people resist without effective defenses or a clear way to protect their lives and futures.

We must then ask: Are leaders responsible only for encouraging resistance and sacrifice, or must they also plan to save lives, find shelter, make migration possible, and preserve the capacities of future generations?

Defending a homeland and confronting injustice must not be confused with sending people to a futile slaughter. Resistance in place may be both possible and necessary. But an overwhelming imbalance of power, siege, and destruction of essential services can make protecting lives and securing a safe exit the priority. No leader has the right to use people’s lives to preserve their own power, reputation, or political and religious ambitions.

At the same time, not everyone can migrate. Many people lack knowledge of escape routes, money for travel, contacts abroad, or information about who might receive them. Borders may be closed, leaving even those who want to leave with no way to safety.

Governments, influential people, those with resources, and potential host communities have responsibilities here. They should work to open safe routes, arrange admission, cover costs, and create opportunities for a new life, rather than urging people with no options to remain and be sacrificed.

The issue extends beyond war. Some societies deny people education, freedom of thought, expression, and opportunities to develop. A girl may be barred from school, a young person denied work because of their beliefs, or a writer or researcher threatened for expressing an idea. If a freer environment is genuinely available, why should attachment to a birthplace consume a person’s entire future?

Migration is not always driven by war or religious persecution either. People may move to find work, escape poverty, secure food or education, or build a better life.

The Qur’an describes several kinds of movement. Moses left Egypt to save his life and found work and a family in Midian. Jacob and his family came to Egypt amid severe economic hardship. The Israelites escaped Pharaoh’s domination and traveled toward another land. Joseph lived among people with different beliefs and took on economic and social responsibilities there.

Migration nevertheless involves more than the migrant’s needs and rights. People in the destination country also have rights. Seeking a better life does not justify deception, lies, forged documents, bribery, or violating others’ rights. After arrival, migrants must respect agreements, just laws, public property, and the rights of the host community.

The receiving society, in turn, must treat migrants fairly and take seriously asylum seekers’ genuine need for safety.

What does the Qur’an say about these questions? When does migration become a responsibility? Who is exempt? Must a migrant eventually return to their first homeland? Is living among people of different faiths permissible? Does responsibility end when a person reaches safety?

Answering these questions requires reading together passages about migration, departure, expulsion, refuge, livelihood, freedom to worship, entry into another land, trustworthiness, and striving in God’s cause.

Evidence and discussion

1. Hijrah: leaving and creating distance

Hijrah (leaving an environment, including migration away from oppression) belongs to the Arabic root h-j-r. In Qur’anic usage, this family of words is associated with leaving, separation, avoidance, and distance. The root occurs 31 times in 27 verses across 17 chapters, but not every occurrence describes moving from one country to another.

In Maryam 19:46, Abraham’s father tells him to stay away for a long time. Al-Muzzammil 73:10 instructs withdrawal in a gracious manner. An-Nisa 4:34 uses the root in the context of marital relations and separation in bed.

Al-Muddaththir 74:5 calls for leaving al-rujz (a term associated with defilement or punishment, whose precise sense requires contextual study). Whatever its exact meaning in this passage, the instruction concerns turning away, rather than geographical migration.

In Al-Mu’minun 23:66, Al-Mu’minun 23:67, turning away from God’s verses is accompanied by arrogance and the expression tahjurun (a form that can refer here to offensive speech or abandonment). Its setting of nighttime conversation may connect it with objectionable speech, while its relationship to distancing oneself from the verses also deserves attention.

In Al-Furqan 25:30, the Messenger complains that his people have treated the Qur’an as mahjuran (abandoned or neglected). The point is not geographical separation from the book, but setting it aside and distancing oneself from its truths.

Leaving and distance thus connect these usages, while context determines their form and character. When hajaru (they migrated) and muhajir (one who migrates) appear alongside homes, land, expulsion, shelter, and struggle, physical migration is clearly in view.

Travel for trade, learning, or livelihood need not use the same vocabulary. The Qur’an also speaks of “traveling through the earth,” “journeying in the land,” and “dispersing through the earth.”

Hijrah in its specific Qur’anic contexts should therefore be distinguished from migration as a broader human phenomenon. Moving to earn a living, gain knowledge, or build a better life can nevertheless be a legitimate and beneficial choice.

2. Faith, migration, and striving: their first connection in the Qur’an

In the Qur’an’s chapter order, Al-Baqarah 2:218 is the first verse explicitly referring to migration:

«إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا وَجَاهَدُوا فِي سَبِيلِ أُولَٰئِكَ يَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ»

Al-Baqarah 2:218

“Those who believe, and those who migrate and strive in the way of The God, hope for the mercy of The God; and The God is forgiving and merciful.”

The preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:217, discusses obstructing God’s way, exclusion from the Sacred Mosque, and fighting by those seeking to turn believers away from their faith. Faith, migration, and striving now appear together as a connected response.

Faith may place someone at odds with an environment that denies freedom of thought, expression, and worship. Migration can then protect faith, life, and the possibility of continuing to live.

The verse also speaks of jihad (sustained striving that can include armed struggle in a military context). Its root, j-h-d, concerns exerting one’s capacity, persistent effort, and enduring difficulty. Qur’anic jihad is not invariably warfare: in Al-Furqan 25:52, God speaks of a great struggle through the Qur’an:

«وَجَاهِدْهُمْ بِهِ جِهَادًا كَبِيرًا»

Al-Furqan 25:52

“Strive against them through it with a great striving.”

Communicating truth and standing intellectually against falsehood can therefore also be forms of striving. Other verses do use jihad in a military setting. Neither reducing every occurrence to warfare nor denying its military meaning where the context supports it is justified.

The connection between migration and striving in Al-Baqarah 2:218 raises a further question: once people reach safety, what do they do with their new freedom and resources? Do they pursue only comfort and private advantage, or use the opportunity to grow, understand truth, assist others, and challenge oppression?

The verse ends with hope for divine mercy. Even migration and striving for what is right should not become grounds for arrogance, self-congratulation, or claiming guaranteed salvation.

3. Leaving oppression: is staying always virtuous?

An-Nisa 4:97 is among the Qur’an’s clearest passages about the responsibility to migrate:

«إِنَّ الَّذِينَ تَوَفَّاهُمُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ ظَالِمِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ قَالُوا فِيمَ كُنْتُمْ قَالُوا كُنَّا مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ فِي الْأَرْضِ قَالُوا أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ وَاسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا فِيهَا فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ وَسَاءَتْ مَصِيرًا»

An-Nisa 4:97

“When the angels take the souls of those who have wronged themselves, they ask, ‘What situation were you in?’ They reply, ‘We were oppressed in the land.’ The angels say, ‘Was the earth of The God not spacious enough for you to migrate within it?’ Their refuge is Hell—an evil destination.”

The phrase zalimi anfusihim (wronging themselves) is especially significant. These people plead their oppressed condition, but are reminded that God’s earth was spacious and migration was possible. In this context, remaining under oppression when escape is available becomes another form of wronging oneself.

“Was the earth of The God not spacious?” is a direct question. God’s earth is not confined to one’s house, city, community, or birthplace. Attachment to a homeland can become so strong that no other future seems imaginable. Communal and religious leaders may also label departure a betrayal and demand that people remain even at the cost of their destruction.

The Qur’an does not, however, treat staying as virtuous in every situation. Where an environment obstructs life and worship and a real opportunity to leave exists, attachment to a homeland should not prevent escape and safety.

Al-‘Ankabut 29:56 also says:

«يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ أَرْضِي وَاسِعَةٌ فَإِيَّايَ فَاعْبُدُونِ»

Al-‘Ankabut 29:56

“O My servants who believe, My earth is indeed spacious, so worship Me alone.”

Here the spaciousness of the earth is connected with serving God. A piece of land should not be treated as so sacred that a person sacrifices life itself and the possibility of worship for it.

The phrase “migrate within it” also locates migration throughout God’s spacious earth. It need not always mean crossing a modern political border. Moving to another city or region within the same country may also provide a route to safety and freedom.

4. When does migration become a responsibility?

The Qur’an neither commands everyone to leave every country nor treats migration as a matter of personal preference in all circumstances. An-Nisa 4:97, An-Nisa 4:98, An-Nisa 4:99, An-Nisa 4:100, An-Nahl 16:41, An-Nahl 16:110, and Al-‘Ankabut 29:56 suggest several criteria.

First, the environment must be considered. Is someone living under oppression and repression? Are worship, free thought, and acting on what they understand to be true obstructed? Are their life and future seriously threatened?

Next come the person’s actual options. Is there a way out? Is somewhere willing to receive them? Can they bring themselves and their family to a safer setting? Do they have the capacity to travel and meet basic needs afterward?

It is also necessary to ask whether safety and freedom can be protected through organization within the existing environment. In Yunus 10:87, God tells Moses and Aaron to establish houses for their people in Egypt and maintain Salat (the Qur’anic practice of prayer and devotion). At that stage, the emphasis is on organizing and continuing worship there; elsewhere in Moses’ story, departure from Egypt is commanded.

The Qur’an therefore makes neither staying universally virtuous nor leaving an identical duty for everyone. Where oppression closes off life and worship, a genuine route to freedom exists, and remaining exposes a person to harm and ruin, migration can become a serious responsibility.

Someone with no way out, or someone able to protect themselves and others through the resources available locally, is in a different situation.

5. Those who cannot leave

An-Nisa 4:97 cannot be read apart from the two verses that follow it. An-Nisa 4:98 states:

«إِلَّا الْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ وَالنِّسَاءِ وَالْوِلْدَانِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ حِيلَةً وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ سَبِيلًا»

An-Nisa 4:98

“Except the oppressed among men, women, and children who have no means to act and cannot find a way.”

An-Nisa 4:99 then speaks of the possibility of their being pardoned.

Two expressions deserve particular attention: “they have no means to act” and “they cannot find a way.” Some people lack the capacity to leave; others do not know a route. They may lack travel funds, documents, transportation, or shelter. Borders may be closed, outside contacts absent, or no country willing to receive them.

Inability to migrate is therefore not simply a matter of poverty. Lack of information, a safe route, admission, or a practical solution also matters. A family trapped by siege, with no exit or no knowledge of where to seek refuge afterward, cannot be blamed for not migrating.

A person without a way to safety is not in the same position as someone with a real opportunity to leave. This distinction brings into view the responsibilities of those with greater capacity and resources.

6. Leaders’ responsibilities: saving lives rather than urging people toward slaughter

An-Nisa 4:75 describes men, women, and children trapped under oppression who ask God for a protector and helper. Al-Anfal 8:26 reminds believers that they were once few and oppressed, afraid of being seized, before they found shelter and assistance. Al-Anfal 8:72 places migrants alongside those who sheltered and supported them.

Rescuing oppressed people thus depends on more than the trapped individual’s decision. Escape may require help from people with greater knowledge, connections, and resources.

Imagine a city under devastating attack. Its residents have inadequate defenses, no safe shelter, and no way out. More families are destroyed each day, while their leaders continue to invoke honor, resistance, and sacrifice, presenting staying as a religious or national duty.

If those leaders possess political power, money, outside contacts, and decision-making authority, why devote all their efforts to continuing confrontation? Why not also plan for a ceasefire, safe civilian evacuation, admission elsewhere, treatment for the wounded, and the protection of the next generation?

Leaders are not entitled to send people to their deaths through emotional slogans and religious or ethnic prejudice. Their responsibility is to protect lives, prevent further harm, and make genuine routes to safety available.

Where effective defense is possible, its capacity and consequences must be assessed. Where civilians have a safe exit, political advantage, communal prestige, or claims of heroism should not deprive them of it. No leader may treat people’s lives as instruments for preserving personal power or standing.

This responsibility extends beyond government. Influential people, those with wealth, professionals, scholars, civic organizations, and people with contacts outside the danger zone may help find solutions. Obtaining accurate information and documents, locating a receiving country, negotiating residence, covering travel costs, or arranging study and employment can save a family’s life.

Historical accounts of Muhammad’s life also emphasize making migration possible. Some followers sought refuge in Abyssinia under a Christian ruler; arrangements for reception and support later made migration to Medina possible. The details belong to historical reports, while the Qur’anic principle of sheltering and assisting migrants is explicit in Al-Anfal 8:72 and Al-Hashr 59:9.

This experience suggests another point: a suitable destination need not share the migrant’s religion. Safety, justice, and freedom to serve God may be available in a society with different beliefs.

Making migration possible must not become forced displacement. People should, wherever possible, be able to choose between staying and leaving. A leader who forcibly removes people from their land also violates their rights.

Responsible leadership does not abandon people to a choice between death and surrender. It creates routes toward safety, education, greater capacity, and continued life.

7. Preserving life and continuing the struggle: is resistance without resources always wise?

People sometimes face devastating weapons with almost no means of defense. A young person may confront artillery and tanks with stones and sticks while leaders celebrate the confrontation as courageous and faithful. But is every confrontation wise, regardless of capacity and consequences?

In Al-Anfal 8:66, God takes the believers’ weakness into account and lightens the preceding requirement concerning confrontation. Al-Anfal 8:16, in a military context, exempts withdrawal for tactical purposes or to join another group from condemned flight.

These passages do not directly concern civilian migration, but show that capacity, planning, and actual circumstances matter in making decisions.

Al-Baqarah 2:195 prohibits casting oneself into destruction. Its context concerns giving and social responsibility; it should not be detached from that setting and turned into a ban on every risk or sacrifice. Nevertheless, avoiding thoughtless destruction is consistent with other Qur’anic principles.

Al-Ahzab 33:16 and An-Nisa 4:78 also remind people that flight cannot free them from death forever. Death’s inevitability does not make efforts to protect life pointless.

Aal Imran 3:156 and Aal Imran 3:168 criticize those who claim, after others have died, that staying with them or following them would have prevented death. We should therefore not blame those who stayed and were killed without understanding their circumstances, or confidently assert that migration would certainly have saved them.

Still, moving people away from killing, protecting children, and securing education and capabilities for the next generation may sometimes sustain resistance to oppression more effectively. Migration in such circumstances does not mean accepting aggression, surrendering the homeland, or abandoning a right to return.

Defending a right is different from sacrificing human lives to the futile consequences of leaders’ decisions and rivalries.

8. Migration for education, freedom of thought, and a future

Imagine a society in which some people are denied education, different ideas are suppressed, and free inquiry or discussion about truth is impossible.

A girl may be unable to continue studying; a young person may be barred from employment because of their beliefs; a writer or researcher may face threats for expressing an idea. If a suitable route to a freer environment exists, why sacrifice an entire future to attachment to a birthplace?

In another country, that person may study, acquire skills, achieve financial independence, and develop capabilities unavailable in the previous environment.

Az-Zumar 39:18 speaks of listening to different statements and following the best. At-Tawbah 9:122 connects learning about religion and informing others with a division of responsibilities. Moses’ journey in Al-Kahf 18:60, Al-Kahf 18:61, Al-Kahf 18:62, Al-Kahf 18:63, Al-Kahf 18:64, Al-Kahf 18:65, Al-Kahf 18:66 also concerns seeking understanding and learning.

Travel for education is not identical to migration away from repression. These passages nevertheless demonstrate the value of understanding and learning. Attachment hardened into prejudice should not cost someone every opportunity to develop in a setting that blocks education and freedom.

Not every ordinary difficulty makes migration necessary. The severity of restrictions, prospects for improvement, dangers of staying, and actual conditions for leaving all require consideration.

9. Migration for livelihood: Jacob and his family

Migration does not always arise from persecution or a threat to life. People may move for food, work, and better living conditions. Joseph’s story provides an important example.

Yusuf 12:47, Yusuf 12:48, Yusuf 12:49 describes abundant years followed by hardship and food shortages. In Yusuf 12:88, Joseph’s brothers ask for provisions, saying:

«مَسَّنَا وَأَهْلَنَا الضُّرُّ»

Yusuf 12:88

“Hardship has afflicted us and our family.”

They also mention the limited value of the goods they have brought. Their concern is a real need for food and the means to live.

In Yusuf 12:93, Joseph tells them:

«وَأْتُونِي بِأَهْلِكُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ»

Yusuf 12:93

“And bring your entire family to me.”

When his family arrives, he says in Yusuf 12:99:

«ادْخُلُوا مِصْرَ إِنْ شَاءَ آمِنِينَ»

Yusuf 12:99

“Enter Egypt in safety, if The God wills.”

The family’s relocation connects livelihood, invitation, admission, and security. Jacob and his family did not let loyalty to their former home prevent them from accepting an opportunity to live where more resources were available.

The example shows that moving for food, family safety, and better living conditions can also be a sound choice.

The Qur’an does not, however, describe this relocation with the verb hajara (to migrate). Jacob’s story illustrates migration in the broader sense; it does not establish that every economic journey is called migration in God’s cause.

10. Joseph in Egypt: living among people with different beliefs

Joseph’s life in Egypt illustrates a further point. He did not initially go there by choice: he was taken there and sold. After many difficulties, however, he attained a position in that same society. In Yusuf 12:55 he says:

«اجْعَلْنِي عَلَىٰ خَزَائِنِ الْأَرْضِ إِنِّي حَفِيظٌ عَلِيمٌ»

Yusuf 12:55

“Appoint me over the storehouses of the land; I am a knowledgeable guardian.”

Yusuf 12:56 describes God establishing him and giving him scope to act in that land.

Egyptian society did not share Joseph’s belief in one God. In Yusuf 12:39 he speaks of “different lords,” while Yusuf 12:76 uses the expression din al-malik (the king’s governing order or system). Yet Joseph lived there, accepted economic responsibility, and planned for the population to endure the difficult years.

The Qur’an does not condemn him for living among people with different beliefs. A difference between someone’s religion or religious law and that of the majority is therefore not, by itself, a reason they must leave. People may live, work, raise families, and cooperate in a society of differing beliefs while remaining faithful to God and their ethical commitments.

What matters is the possibility of living safely, serving God freely, and treating people justly. The Qur’an portrays Joseph’s extended settlement in Egypt but does not recount the details of his final years. His place of death should therefore not be attributed to the Qur’an without evidence.

The central point is that returning to one’s birthplace is not a condition of every migration. Permanent settlement elsewhere can also be a sound choice. The distinction between religion and religious law is explored in Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Qur’an: din (religion or an order of commitment and accountability), Islam (submission to God), and shariah (a prescribed path).

11. Moses in Midian: from escape to a new life

In Al-Qasas 28:20, a man warns Moses that a group has decided to kill him and urges him to leave the city. In Al-Qasas 28:21, Moses departs in fear and vigilance, asking God to save him from the unjust people.

Moses does not remain under a serious threat of death to prove his courage. In Al-Qasas 28:22 he heads toward Midian. There he encounters a family and helps them. In Al-Qasas 28:25 he is assured that he has escaped the unjust people. Al-Qasas 28:27 then describes an eight-year work agreement, with the option of completing ten years, in connection with marriage.

Leaving danger can thus open a new stage of life: safety, employment, family, and years of residence elsewhere. A migrant is not destined to remain permanently displaced, dependent, and needy. Building an independent, dignified life can be one purpose of migration.

Moses’ residence in Midian did not end his responsibilities. A further stage in his life and mission followed.

12. The Israelites’ departure from Egypt: freedom and a new land

In the account of Moses and the Israelites, departure from Egypt is connected with liberation from Pharaoh’s rule. Ta-Ha 20:77 and Ash-Shu‘ara 26:52 instruct Moses to lead God’s servants out by night. Their immediate purpose was escape from Pharaoh’s pursuit and oppression.

The story continues beyond leaving Egypt. In Al-Ma’idah 5:21, Moses calls on his people to enter the holy land. Al-Ma’idah 5:26 describes forty years of wandering for a group among them. Al-A‘raf 7:137 speaks of formerly oppressed people inheriting lands God had blessed.

Movement may begin as escape from oppression and become a search for somewhere to continue living. Yet finding a new destination is not always straightforward; difficulty and uncertainty may persist after departure.

An instruction concerning a particular event in Israelite history must not, without evidence, be turned into a general license to seize other people’s land.

13. Traveling for work and livelihood

The Qur’an also discusses traveling to earn a living. Al-Muzzammil 73:20 states:

«وَآخَرُونَ يَضْرِبُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ يَبْتَغُونَ مِنْ فَضْلِ »

Al-Muzzammil 73:20

“Others travel through the land seeking God’s bounty.”

Al-Mulk 67:15 calls on people to move through the earth and benefit from God’s provision. Al-Jumu‘ah 62:10 speaks of dispersing through the land and seeking God’s bounty after Salat (prayer and devotion). Al-Baqarah 2:198 does not prohibit seeking the Lord’s bounty in the context of pilgrimage either.

These verses do not directly discuss permanent residence abroad. They nevertheless recognize traveling in search of livelihood and resources. A person need not remain forever where work, food, shelter, or adequate living conditions are unavailable. They may seek an environment in which to work, support their family, and become independent through their efforts.

A legitimate purpose does not justify wrongful methods. Economic migration also calls for honesty, trustworthiness, and respect for others’ rights.

14. Choosing to leave and being forcibly expelled

Leaving and being driven out are linguistically distinct in the Qur’an, though both may describe one person’s experience. Al-Hashr 59:8 describes migrants expelled from their homes and possessions. Aal Imran 3:195 likewise brings together migration, expulsion, persecution, and struggle.

Someone can therefore be both a migrant and a victim of unjust expulsion.

Al-Hajj 22:40 condemns driving people from their homes without right. Al-Baqarah 2:84 and Al-Baqarah 2:85 also discuss expulsion in the context of broken covenants and injustice.

Migration to save life must therefore be distinguished from forcibly removing people from their homeland. The value of migration cannot legitimize forced expulsion. If aggression and oppression compel someone to leave home, responsibility for that injustice remains with those who expelled them.

Nor should migration mean compulsory abandonment of rights, property, or a right to return. Conversely, someone who has fled to safety elsewhere is not necessarily obliged to return to their birthplace. Security, living conditions, and free choice matter.

15. Entering another country: proper means and respect for others’ rights

Alongside movement through the earth, the Qur’an sets principles for entering other people’s space. Al-Baqarah 2:189 says:

«وَأْتُوا الْبُيُوتَ مِنْ أَبْوَابِهَا»

Al-Baqarah 2:189

“Enter houses through their doors.”

In its immediate context, this instruction concerns entering houses and is linked with mindfulness of God. A broader principle about using appropriate means to reach a goal may also be drawn from it, as discussed in the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:189.

An-Nur 24:27 connects entering others’ homes with obtaining a welcome and greeting the occupants. An-Nur 24:28 is more explicit: do not enter without permission, and if told to return, return. An-Nur 24:29 describes a different situation for nonresidential premises in which people have a legitimate use or interest.

Entering another person’s space is therefore connected with their rights and consent.

In Yusuf 12:67, Jacob advises his sons to enter through separate gates. This verse does not directly prescribe immigration law, but shows that how one enters a city, and planning that entry, mattered.

Joseph’s invitation to Jacob’s family and their safe entry into Egypt also deserve attention. Joseph holds a position of authority there and helps arrange their reception.

Taken together, these examples suggest an ethical principle for migration today: someone seeking to live elsewhere should use proper, recognized, lawful routes accepted by the destination society and respect its residents’ rights.

The Qur’an does not, however, directly legislate passports, visas, or modern citizenship systems. A country is also not legally identical to a private home. Rules concerning houses should therefore not be extended uncritically to every border question.

Alongside a society’s authority to regulate entry and residence stands the right of a person in danger to seek refuge. Migrants should not deceive or violate others’ rights to achieve their aims; receiving societies should not use regulations and power to oppress, discriminate, or unjustly deprive people of safety.

16. Offering refuge to someone in need, even across differences of belief

At-Tawbah 9:6 states:

«وَإِنْ أَحَدٌ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ اسْتَجَارَكَ فَأَجِرْهُ حَتَّىٰ يَسْمَعَ كَلَامَ ثُمَّ أَبْلِغْهُ مَأْمَنَهُ»

At-Tawbah 9:6

“If one of those who associate others with God seeks your protection, grant him protection so that he may hear the word of The God; then convey him to a place of safety.”

The verse has a particular context concerning relations with people who associate others with God. Yet its ethical point is significant: even a profound difference in belief does not, by itself, justify disregarding the safety of someone seeking protection. It requires more than receiving the request; it also speaks of bringing the person to safety.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:10 gives instructions concerning believing women who migrate. The same verse reminds readers that God knows their faith best. Examining an asylum seeker’s circumstances should therefore not become a pretext for humiliation, mistreatment, or definitive judgments about their intentions.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 and Al-Mumtahanah 60:9 distinguish those who have fought people because of religion and expelled them from their homes from those who have not. Treatment should not depend solely on a person’s religious, ethnic, or communal label.

A person’s need for refuge and safety cannot be made dependent simply on sharing the destination population’s beliefs.

Seeking refuge from a threat to life also differs from ordinary migration for employment or study. Someone fleeing war or persecution may be unable to obtain documents or complete every normal procedure. In such circumstances, governments and receiving institutions have an ethical responsibility to provide safe, lawful ways to request protection, rather than leaving a trapped person to choose between death and irregular entry.

17. Respect for law, ethical conduct, and keeping agreements

Migration does not release anyone from ethical responsibility. A person living in a new country must respect people’s rights, agreements, and fair public rules. Al-Ma’idah 5:1 states:

«أَوْفُوا بِالْعُقُودِ»

Al-Ma’idah 5:1

“Fulfill your agreements.”

Al-Isra 17:34 also commands fulfillment of commitments and reminds people that they will be questioned about them.

An-Nahl 16:90 commands justice and doing good, and forbids indecency, wrongdoing, and transgression. Al-A‘raf 7:199 calls for ma‘ruf (conduct recognized as right and good). Social life should therefore rest on justice, kindness, respect for rights, and avoidance of aggression.

These principles support respect for employment contracts, residence commitments, just laws, public regulations, and neighbors’ rights. Someone admitted under accepted conditions should not knowingly break their commitments or exploit others’ trust.

Respect for law is not blind obedience to every command. The Qur’an itself makes justice and right conduct the standard. Invoking law gives no government or institution unlimited authority to oppress, humiliate, or violate human rights.

Migrants should honor legitimate agreements and just laws. If they encounter injustice or discrimination, they should work for reform through proper and peaceful means.

18. Honesty and avoiding forged documents

Migration can involve great difficulties. A person may lack sufficient documents, be unfamiliar with lawful routes, or face complicated admission requirements. Difficulty reaching a goal does not turn lies and forgery into right conduct. Al-Ahzab 33:70 says:

«اتَّقُوا وَقُولُوا قَوْلًا سَدِيدًا»

Al-Ahzab 33:70

“Be mindful of The God and speak words that are right and sound.”

At-Tawbah 9:119 calls for being with the truthful. Al-Hajj 22:30 forbids false speech, and Al-Isra 17:36 warns against pursuing what one does not know.

These principles are inconsistent with fabricating identities, submitting forged documents, lying about one’s history, falsifying educational credentials or work experience, and concealing information to obtain benefits to which one has no right.

Those who provide migration services must not take people’s money through false promises, sales of forged documents, or concealment of travel risks.

Migrating for a better life is a legitimate aim; deception and lies do not become legitimate means of reaching it.

Forging information to take another person’s rights must nevertheless be distinguished from withholding private information from persecutors. Such situations require judgment attentive to danger, rights, necessity, and actual consequences.

19. Trustworthiness, public funds, and shared resources

An-Nisa 4:58 commands returning trusts to those entitled to them and judging fairly. Al-Baqarah 2:188 condemns taking others’ wealth wrongfully and using improper approaches to authorities to obtain it. An-Nisa 4:29 also prohibits wrongful appropriation and describes trade by mutual consent. Al-Anfal 8:27 forbids betraying trusts.

These principles apply equally to migrants and everyone else. People receiving public assistance, insurance, healthcare, education, or other support should respect fair conditions of eligibility. False income declarations, concealing assets to claim undeserved assistance, using another person’s identity, or fraudulently obtaining duplicate benefits take resources intended for those in need and the public.

Al-Mutaffifin 83:1, Al-Mutaffifin 83:2, Al-Mutaffifin 83:3, Al-Mutaffifin 83:4, Al-Mutaffifin 83:5, Al-Mutaffifin 83:6 condemns those who demand their full measure when receiving but give others less than they are due. The same standard matters in social life. People should not demand every right and resource from society while evading their fair share of responsibility.

Public money and services are held in trust for people’s benefit. They are not opportunities to exploit others’ trust and labor.

Officials in the receiving society bear the same responsibility. Refugee budgets, humanitarian aid, and public resources must not become vehicles for corruption, bribery, discrimination, or private gain. In the context of distributing wealth, Al-Hashr 59:7 addresses preventing its circulation solely among the rich, recalling the importance of fair access to resources.

20. Relations with the host community

Migrants may differ from residents in language, culture, food, clothing, customs, or belief. Those differences do not, by themselves, justify hostility, contempt, or deliberate social separation.

Al-Hujurat 49:13 connects differences between peoples and tribes with mutual acquaintance. Al-Hujurat 49:11 forbids ridicule and humiliation; Al-Hujurat 49:12 cautions against unjust suspicion, spying, and backbiting.

According to Al-Ma’idah 5:8, even hostility toward a group must not lead to injustice. Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 does not forbid kindness and justice toward those who have not fought or expelled people.

These principles also govern a migrant’s life in a new society. Migrants should respect neighbors, avoid causing harm, resolve disputes through dialogue and peaceful means, and use public facilities responsibly. They should not assume that their ethnicity, culture, or religion automatically makes them superior.

Host populations likewise should not demean migrants because of language, color, ethnicity, poverty, or belief. Healthy coexistence requires both sides to respect each other’s rights and dignity.

People can retain their beliefs while cooperating and forming friendships with those who believe differently.

21. Receiving communities: shelter and the means to become independent

Al-Anfal 8:72 mentions migrants alongside those who sheltered and helped them. Al-Hashr 59:8 describes impoverished migrants expelled from their homes and possessions.

Al-Hashr 59:9 describes people who loved those who migrated to them and felt no resentment over what the migrants received. They gave others preference even when they themselves were in need. An-Nur 24:22 also urges those with resources not to stop supporting relatives, people in need, and migrants in God’s cause.

Migration therefore cannot be accomplished through the migrant’s decision alone. A displaced person may have lost a home, possessions, employment, family, and support network. Reaching a safe country does not solve every problem. Shelter, food, treatment, language learning, employment opportunities, and social support may still be needed.

Receiving societies can help migrants become independent rather than permanently dependent on assistance. A reciprocal relationship can develop: the host community supports migrants, who in turn gain knowledge and capabilities through which to contribute to its economy, scholarship, and development.

Offering refuge involves more than opening a gate. Opportunities for a dignified life and personal development matter too. Residence status must also not become a means of subjecting migrants to economic exploitation, forced labor, threats, or abuse.

22. Migrant women: life, choice, and education

Qur’anic migration is not restricted to men. Aal Imran 3:195 explicitly states that no person’s work, male or female, will be lost, and then discusses migration, expulsion, and persecution.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:10 addresses believing women who migrate. In its particular context, it gives instructions about examining their situation and not returning them to the group they have left. It also addresses financial rights associated with marital relationships.

An-Nisa 4:98 includes women alongside men and children unable to migrate. A woman is therefore a person with faith, choices, and responsibilities, not merely an extension of men’s decisions.

A woman may seek refuge elsewhere to protect her life, freedom to worship, access to education, or escape from abuse. A receiving society should attend to her safety and rights and prevent exploitation, humiliation, or unjust return to danger.

Assistance to migrant women must not become a means of imposing beliefs, forcing personal dependence, or denying choice and education.

23. Leaving and staying: does everyone have the same duty?

Sometimes migration protects life and freedom. Sometimes organization and cooperation allow people to continue living where they are.

In Yunus 10:87, Moses and Aaron are instructed to establish houses for their people in Egypt and maintain Salat (prayer and devotion). At-Tawbah 9:122 does not call for all believers to depart together; it discusses a division of responsibilities, learning, and informing others. An-Nisa 4:98, An-Nisa 4:99 exempts those without the means or a route to migrate.

Whether to leave or stay must therefore be assessed against actual circumstances and responsibilities. A doctor may stay in difficult conditions to treat people. A teacher may remain to educate children. A family may migrate to save its children’s lives. Another person may have no way out at all.

Without understanding these circumstances, we cannot label one person brave and another cowardly. Not migrating is not always sinful, just as migration is not always an escape from responsibility.

Governments and influential people should provide information and genuine options for informed choice rather than impose one decision on everyone.

24. Migration among earlier prophets and believers

Leaving a threatening environment was not unique to Muhammad’s followers.

In Maryam 19:46, Abraham’s father threatens him and tells him to leave. In Maryam 19:48, Abraham speaks of withdrawing from them and what they invoke besides God. In Al-‘Ankabut 29:26, Lot believes, and the expression of migrating toward the Lord appears.

Al-Anbiya 21:71 describes God rescuing Abraham and Lot and bringing them to a blessed land. In Al-Qasas 28:21, Moses leaves the city after learning of the threat to his life. In Ash-Shu‘ara 26:21, he also speaks of fleeing the threatening environment.

In Al-Kahf 18:16, young people who have withdrawn from their community and its objects of worship seek shelter in a cave. Al-Kahf 18:20 describes the danger of being forced back into their people’s religion.

Seeking refuge away from pressure thus appears in the accounts of earlier prophets and believers too. None of these accounts means that people should flee every disagreement. The issue is understanding conditions, preserving freedom to serve God, and responding wisely to danger.

25. Must migrants eventually return to their first homeland?

Migration may be temporary or prolonged. Some return when danger ends; others settle permanently in their new country.

Al-Baqarah 2:218 does not make returning home a condition of migration in God’s cause. An-Nisa 4:100 even discusses someone who dies while migrating, whose reward rests with God.

Joseph’s settlement in Egypt and his family’s arrival show that life can continue in a new land. Moses’ years in Midian illustrate the same possibility.

If someone has been forcibly expelled, however, migration should not extinguish their rights. Al-Hajj 22:40 condemns unjust expulsion, and Al-Fath 48:27 speaks of safe entry into the Sacred Mosque.

Historical accounts of Muhammad and his followers describe migration from Mecca to Medina and the later reopening of access to Mecca, illustrating a change from exclusion to possible return. Return must not become a pretext for revenge, injustice, or imposing beliefs.

Migration can preserve life and capacity; return may be a future aim. Neither should violate others’ rights. Those who remain in their new country can still assist people in their first homeland from there.

26. Effort after migration: from personal safety to social responsibility

Al-Anfal 8:72 brings together faith, migration, striving with possessions and lives, shelter, and assistance. Al-Anfal 8:74 and At-Tawbah 9:20 repeat this connection.

An-Nahl 16:110 presents another sequence:

«ثُمَّ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لِلَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا فُتِنُوا ثُمَّ جَاهَدُوا وَصَبَرُوا إِنَّ رَبَّكَ مِنْ بَعْدِهَا لَغَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ»

An-Nahl 16:110

“Then your Lord, toward those who migrated after being persecuted, then strove and persevered—your Lord, after that, is forgiving and merciful.”

The verse describes migration after persecution, followed by striving and perseverance.

After reaching safety, people first need to establish life for themselves and their families. Housing, work, peace of mind, and education are genuine needs. But if safety leads only to consumption, accumulating wealth, and pleasure, with no concern for others’ suffering, a great opportunity for beneficial action may be lost.

Migrants can study, learn skills, become doctors, teachers, engineers, or researchers, establish media and educational institutions, and use new resources to help others. This effort may eventually lead to returning and contributing to their homeland’s development. It may also take the form of promoting education, defending rights, or reducing deprivation from their new society.

These are examples of beneficial effort, not claims that the Qur’an names each modern activity explicitly. Al-Furqan 25:52 describes a great struggle through the Qur’an, and Al-‘Ankabut 29:69 connects striving in God’s cause with guidance to His ways.

Striving in God’s cause can therefore neither be restricted to war nor extended automatically to every social activity regardless of intention and conduct.

Migration can preserve and rebuild human capacity. Effort afterward can put that capacity at the service of truth, justice, freedom, and people’s well-being.

The reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:202 also examines prayer, choice, acquiring capabilities, and action. Building a better future requires understanding, decisions, and effort. Wishing and praying do not replace making use of available means.

27. Trust in God without a guarantee of comfort

Tawakkul (trust in God while acting responsibly) is sometimes understood as remaining in danger and waiting for extraordinary rescue without using available means. The Qur’anic accounts offer a different picture.

Moses leaves the city when threatened. The people of the cave seek shelter. An-Nisa 4:100 speaks of leaving one’s home to migrate.

Al-‘Ankabut 29:56 recalls the spaciousness of the earth, and the following verses discuss death, reward, perseverance, trust, and provision. Al-‘Ankabut 29:60 observes that many creatures do not carry their own provision; God provides for them and for human beings.

These passages connect hope in God with the realities of human life and effort; they do not eliminate the need for work, planning, or understanding possible routes. A migrant needs information, documents, funds, safety, admission, and a plan. Working to secure these does not conflict with trust in God.

An-Nisa 4:100 also speaks of muraghaman kathiran wa-sa‘ah (many places of refuge and ample opportunity). Migrants may find new openings and places to live. The promise is not a guarantee of effortless comfort for every migrant: poverty, loneliness, language barriers, unemployment, and separation from family may remain.

Trust in God does not deny these difficulties. It accompanies responsible decisions, effort, and hope.

The relationship between divine permission, the order of creation, circumstances, and human choice is examined in Divine Permission in the Qur’an: The Order of Creation, Guidance, and Human Choice.

28. Difficult passages: does failure to migrate justify punishment?

An-Nisa 4:89 discusses migration in the context of relations with a group about whom there is disagreement and concern over hostility. It uses severe language about dealing with them. It cannot, however, be separated from An-Nisa 4:88, An-Nisa 4:89, An-Nisa 4:90, An-Nisa 4:91.

An-Nisa 4:90 exempts groups connected to a people with whom a treaty exists, or who refrain from fighting. The same verse gives no authorization to attack them if they withdraw from fighting and offer peace. An-Nisa 4:91 addresses groups that do not desist from persecution and aggression.

An-Nisa 4:89 therefore cannot establish that everyone who fails to migrate or join a particular community deserves to be killed.

Al-Anfal 8:72 also relates the position of those who have not migrated to the social obligations and mutual protection of the migrant community. Yet it discusses their requests for help concerning religion and makes honoring treaties with others a condition.

Migration in some circumstances was thus connected with the organization and responsibilities of a community involved in war. These particular provisions cannot be turned into a universal command to punish people who do not migrate.

The Qur’an distinguishes inability, personal choice, social agreement, and active hostility. A religious label must not erase those distinctions.

29. Migration, forgiveness, and mercy

The migration passages concern more than duty and hardship. They repeatedly offer hope of mercy and forgiveness.

In Al-Baqarah 2:218, those who migrate and strive in God’s cause hope for His mercy. An-Nisa 4:100 places with God the reward of someone who dies while migrating.

An-Nahl 16:41 speaks of a good place in this world and a greater reward in the Hereafter. An-Nahl 16:110 connects migration after persecution with striving, perseverance, forgiveness, and mercy.

Al-Hajj 22:58, Al-Hajj 22:59 promises good provision and a welcome destination to those who migrate in God’s cause and are then killed or die.

These promises show the significance of migration in God’s cause. They should not become permission for self-praise.

At-Tawbah 9:20 describes a higher standing before God for believers who migrate and strive. An-Najm 53:32, meanwhile, forbids declaring oneself pure.

Judgment of people’s intentions, capacities, circumstances, and ultimate fate belongs to God.

Someone who migrates with few resources and great danger may endure enormous hardship. Someone else may want to leave but lack any practical route. Neither should be demeaned merely for leaving or staying, without understanding their circumstances.

Conclusion

Hijrah in the Qur’an is more than a historical event or movement between countries. Across its passages, migration is connected with faith, escape from oppression, freedom to serve God, striving, perseverance, shelter, and social responsibility.

The stories of Joseph, Jacob, Moses, and the Israelites also show people moving to preserve life, find food, work, and safety, or build a new life. The Qur’an does not confine human life to a birthplace.

One of this study’s most important concerns, however, is remaining under oppression.

Attachment to homeland, territory, community, or religion can become so powerful that people refuse to leave danger even when escape is possible. Leaders may also stir emotions and portray remaining under bombardment and sacrificing oneself as evidence of faith and courage.

An-Nisa 4:97 confronts this outlook with a serious question: was God’s earth not spacious enough for you to migrate?

Remaining under oppression is not always virtuous. Where a genuine escape is available, people must consider their responsibility to protect life, freedom, and their future.

An-Nisa 4:98, An-Nisa 4:99 nevertheless exempts those without means or a route. People trapped by war, siege, poverty, or closed routes cannot be blamed for failing to migrate.

This is where governments and influential people bear particular responsibility.

Leaders should not use ethnic, religious, or political slogans to urge people to remain amid killing while providing no means to protect their lives. They should use power, knowledge, connections, and resources to save people, find refuge, arrange admission, and preserve future generations’ capacities.

Where effective resistance is possible, its conditions and consequences require assessment. Where civilians can be rescued, political prestige or factional interests should not deny them that opportunity. No ideal gives leaders authority to use people’s lives to maintain their own power.

Providing an opportunity to migrate must equally not become forced displacement or denial of rights to home and land. Wherever possible, people should have an informed choice between staying and leaving.

Migration extends beyond escaping oppression. People may move for livelihood, education, work, or better living conditions. They may also settle permanently among people with different beliefs, as Joseph’s life in Egypt illustrates the possibility of serving God and others within a different society.

The right to migrate nevertheless carries ethical responsibilities.

Migrants should seek proper entry, honor legitimate agreements and just laws, speak truthfully, avoid forgery and deception, and treat public resources and the host population’s rights as a trust.

Receiving societies, in turn, should respect migrants’ and refugees’ rights, avoid humiliation based on ethnicity or belief, and help provide safety, education, employment, and independence as far as they can.

Migration does not end responsibility. The Qur’an repeatedly connects it with striving, perseverance, and effort. In a safer environment, people can acquire knowledge, skills, economic independence, and social capacity to serve truth, justice, and others.

Returning to the first homeland may be one outcome of these efforts, but it is not a condition of every migration. People can also serve their communities of origin and others in need from their new country.

The Qur’anic evidence considered here supports the following principles:

First, remaining under oppression is not always courage, and migration is not always an escape from responsibility. Leaving danger can be necessary to protect life and continued freedom to serve God.

Second, migration can have different purposes, from escaping persecution and war to obtaining livelihood, education, and a better life.

Third, responsibility to migrate depends on actual capacity and opportunity. Someone without a route should not be blamed for that inability.

Fourth, governments and influential people should provide trapped populations with routes to safety and a secure future, rather than urge them toward slaughter without resources or planning.

Fifth, migrants and receiving societies have mutual rights and responsibilities. Honesty, justice, recognized good conduct, keeping agreements, and trustworthiness are necessary on both sides.

Sixth, migration can begin a new stage of development and effort. Preserving life and gaining capabilities may allow a person to serve truth, freedom, justice, and others more effectively.

On this reading, migration is neither a sign of cowardice nor, by itself, proof of superiority. Its value depends on circumstances, motives, real opportunities, methods, and conduct.

The earth of The God is not confined to any government’s borders or territory. Nor does human responsibility for truth, justice, and others’ rights end at a border crossing.
