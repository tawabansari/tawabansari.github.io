Introduction

The Arabic word rabb (lord, sustainer, and one responsible for care and direction) belongs to the root r-b-b. Its meanings connect nurturing and protection with oversight, management, ownership, and the ordering of affairs. Rububiyyah (lordship) describes the Lord’s standing and His relationship with beings sustained, governed, and directed by Him. Throughout this study, “Lord” therefore carries more than the sense of a ruler: it also conveys care, provision, and the sustaining of life.

Discussions of God’s lordship often emphasize creation, the nurturing of living beings, provision, and the governance of the universe. But that account remains incomplete without considering creation’s purpose, God’s guidance of human understanding and conduct, His authority to command, the standards by which life is lived, and the authority to establish religious obligations.

Similarly, shirk (associating others with God) is often discussed in terms of other deities, idol worship, or attributing divine powers to created beings. The Qur’an also addresses another form of association: taking fellow human beings as lords.

This study begins with rabb and related words, distinguishing them from other descriptions of God. It then considers divine lordship in creation, human life, guidance, freedom of choice, devotion, and the authority to command. Within that framework, it examines Qur’anic examples of treating people as lords, the role of God’s Messengers, the authority of religious and political leaders, and the place of Qur’anic principles in organizing shared life.

The argument is that the lordship of The God—the One worthy of worship—extends throughout existence and every stage of His creatures’ lives. Creation is purposeful, and creatures are not abandoned after coming into being. Recognizing God as Lord means reserving worship and the authority to legislate religion for Him, while accepting responsibility for our own thought, choices, and conduct. People can use reason, knowledge, experience, and consultation to organize their affairs in accordance with divine principles and make binding decisions for their common life. That competence does not entitle any person or group to turn its decisions into God’s decrees, command injustice or disregard for others, or impose a belief in God’s name.

1. What Rabb and Lordship Mean

Within the r-b-b word family, nurturing is connected with undertaking responsibility and managing affairs. Someone responsible for the care of a living thing provides what it needs to grow and survive and attends to its needs. The meaning thus also involves oversight, authority, and directing a process of development.

The Qur’an uses several forms from this word family in different contexts:

TermMeaning and Qur’anic use
RabbLord, nurturer, guardian, or master, according to context
RabbiMy Lord
RabbanaOur Lord
RabbukumYour Lord
Rabb al-‘alaminLord of all worlds
ArbabPlural of rabb: lords or masters
RabbaniyyunPeople described in 3:79 and 5:44 in connection with teaching, studying, and safeguarding God’s Book
RibbiyyunGroups described in 3:146 as accompanying Prophets and remaining steadfast in hardship; the precise meaning of the term is disputed
Raba’ibStepdaughters, the daughters of one’s wife from another marital relationship, in the marriage regulations of 4:23

Rububiyyah does not occur in the Qur’an in that form. It is a term used to describe the concept of lordship understood from the Qur’an’s uses of rabb.

The word families r-b-b and r-b-w have related meanings concerning growth and nurturing. Their semantic range and word formation are examined in the root studies r-b-b and r-b-w.

2. Lord, Deity, Creator, Owner, and Other Divine Descriptions

The final chapter of the Qur’an begins:

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ

114:1

Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of humankind,

مَلِكِ النَّاسِ

114:2

The Sovereign of humankind,

إِلَٰهِ النَّاسِ

114:3

The God of humankind.

The refuge sought is in the One God, who is also humanity’s Lord and Sovereign. These three verses, alongside other passages, distinguish descriptions of God that help clarify the meaning of rabb.

DescriptionMeaningQur’anic example

KhaliqCreator; one who brings into being6:102

Malik, with a short first vowelKing or sovereign114:2

The God says:

ذَٰلِكُمُ رَبُّكُمْ ۖ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ خَالِقُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ فَاعْبُدُوهُ ۚ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ وَكِيلٌ

6:102

That is The God, your Lord; there is no deity except Him, the Creator of everything. So worship Him; He is the guardian over everything.

Describing God as Creator concerns the bringing of beings into existence. Describing Him as Lord concerns His relationship of care, governance, and preservation toward creation. His divinity concerns the devotion and worship owed to Him alone.

In 6:102, these descriptions are brought together and lead to the command to worship Him.

These distinctions also matter for the principle of God’s oneness. His uniqueness concerns not only creation but also lordship, divinity, and sovereignty.

For a fuller discussion of divine names and descriptions, see The God, Lord, and the Language of Divine Transcendence.

3. Lord of All Worlds: The Universality of Divine Lordship

الْحَمْدُ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

1:2

All praise belongs to The God, Lord of all worlds.

The Lord whom a person addresses as “my Lord” does not belong exclusively to that person, their people, or their religious community. The exchange between Moses and Pharaoh makes the scope of His lordship explicit:

قَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ وَمَا رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

26:23

Pharaoh said: And what is the Lord of all worlds?

قَالَ رَبُّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُوقِنِينَ

26:24

He said: The Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatever is between them, if you have certainty.

Moses then says:

قَالَ رَبُّكُمْ وَرَبُّ آبَائِكُمُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

26:26

He said: Your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.

And continues:

قَالَ رَبُّ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

26:28

He said: The Lord of the east and the west and whatever is between them, if you would reason.

Pharaoh, the territory he rules, and the generations before him all fall within the lordship of the same Lord whom Moses identifies.

As discussed in the reflection on 1:2, this is fundamental to the Qur’an’s inclusive account of the Lord and His creatures.

God is the Lord of believers and deniers, human beings and other creatures. Water, air, sunlight, the earth’s resources, and the laws governing creation are not reserved for followers of one belief. In 17:20, the Qur’an speaks of the Lord’s giving to people with different desires and choices. Receiving these gifts does not itself establish divine approval of everything a person believes or does.

Nor should divine lordship be imagined as merely a larger version of human care and management. Human beings need knowledge, time, abilities, resources, and conditions to care for others, many of which lie outside their control. God’s lordship depends on no other being’s resources, knowledge, or power.

The foundations of this understanding are examined in Subhan and Tasbih: Divine Transcendence, Oneness, and the Order of Creation. Subhan expresses God’s freedom from imperfection; tasbih is the acknowledgment and affirmation of that transcendence.

4. Lordship, the Order of Nature, and the Purpose of Creation

When Pharaoh asked Moses about his and Aaron’s Lord, Moses answered:

قَالَ رَبُّنَا الَّذِي أَعْطَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ خَلْقَهُ ثُمَّ هَدَىٰ

20:50

He said: Our Lord is the One who gave everything its creation, then guided it.

Moses connects the creation of each thing with its guidance. Creatures are not abandoned once they exist: the conditions for their continued life, growth, and development are provided within the created order.

Under suitable conditions, a seed germinates. Its roots draw water and minerals from the soil, while its leaves use sunlight to produce what the plant needs to grow.

An embryo also develops through successive stages in the mother’s womb as its organs form. After birth, the human body uses food, resists pathogens, and repairs many injuries through a variety of mechanisms.

All these processes occur within the order and regularities of creation.

The God says:

سَبِّحِ اسْمَ رَبِّكَ الْأَعْلَىٰ

87:1

Affirm the transcendence of the name of your Lord, the Most High,

الَّذِي خَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ

87:2

Who created and gave due form,

وَالَّذِي قَدَّرَ فَهَدَىٰ

87:3

And who determined a measure and guided.

Creating, giving form, determining measure, and guiding are attributed here to the same Lord.

Discovering natural laws and understanding how living beings function acquaints us with aspects of this sustaining order. A physician studying disease and treatment, a farmer studying the conditions of plant growth, and a scientist investigating the movement and development of cosmic phenomena each examines part of creation’s regularity.

As discussed in Divine Permission in the Qur’an, identifying the causes and laws through which something happens is compatible with its occurrence by God’s permission.

God’s lordship concerns not only the order of creation but also its purpose.

The Qur’an asks:

أَفَحَسِبْتُمْ أَنَّمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ عَبَثًا وَأَنَّكُمْ إِلَيْنَا لَا تُرْجَعُونَ

23:115

Did you think that We created you aimlessly and that you would not be returned to Us?

It also asks:

أَيَحْسَبُ الْإِنْسَانُ أَنْ يُتْرَكَ سُدًى

75:36

Does the human being think that he will be left unattended?

The following verses in chapter 75 recall the stages of human creation and then speak of God’s ability to bring the dead to life.

Human creation, growth, the capacities made available to us, and our return to the Lord therefore belong together. Human beings were not created simply to spend a period in this world and then have their entire existence, and the consequences of good and evil conduct, disappear at death.

In 67:2, the creation of death and life is connected with testing people in the quality of their deeds. Earthly life offers an opportunity to grow, understand, choose, and act; the hereafter is a stage in which the consequences of this life are realized.

This understanding underlies the principle of faith in The God, the hereafter, and righteous action.

Faith in God recognizes that existence has a Creator and Lord. Faith in the hereafter recognizes creation’s purpose, the continuation of human existence, and accountability for our choices. Righteous action brings that understanding consciously into our lives and conduct.

5. Divine Lordship throughout Human Life

After referring to the Lord of all worlds in 26:77, Abraham describes several dimensions of his relationship with Him:

الَّذِي خَلَقَنِي فَهُوَ يَهْدِينِ

26:78

The One who created me, and He guides me;

وَالَّذِي هُوَ يُطْعِمُنِي وَيَسْقِينِ

26:79

And the One who feeds me and gives me drink;

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

26:80

And when I fall ill, He heals me;

وَالَّذِي يُمِيتُنِي ثُمَّ يُحْيِينِ

26:81

And the One who will cause me to die, then bring me to life;

وَالَّذِي أَطْمَعُ أَنْ يَغْفِرَ لِي خَطِيئَتِي يَوْمَ الدِّينِ

26:82

And the One who, I hope, will forgive my wrongdoing on the Day of Recompense.

Abraham brings together the different dimensions of our relationship with the Lord: our coming into existence and continued life, guidance, death, renewed life, and forgiveness.

He places his hope in the same Lord who provides for him today and heals him when he is ill. Despite his standing and virtues, Abraham does not treat forgiveness as something guaranteed to him; he hopes for it.

The opening chapter follows a related sequence. After identifying God as Lord of all worlds, it speaks of His mercy and then His ownership of the Day of Recompense.

Hope in divine mercy and guidance, and accountability before divine judgment, are both part of the servant’s relationship with the Lord.

These questions are explored further in Owner of the Day of Recompense: Why Final Judgment Belongs to God Alone.

6. Lordship, Guidance, Freedom, and Devotion

Alongside the conditions for coming into existence and staying alive, human beings possess the capacities to think, understand, decide, and choose.

The God says:

إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا

76:3

We have shown him the way, whether he is grateful or rejects it.

In human understanding and moral life, divine guidance accompanies freedom of choice. A person can recognize and accept a truth, or turn away even after its grounds have become clear.

Recognizing God’s lordship does not deny human freedom. That freedom is itself among the capacities the Lord has given us.

Divine Decree and Human Choice distinguishes the regularities of creation from human choice within the capacities we have received.

In addition to our ability to understand and reason, God has provided, through His Messengers, standards for distinguishing truth from falsehood, living rightly, and establishing justice. Accepting or rejecting this guidance remains a human choice; moral responsibility is bound up with that freedom.

To live as God’s servant is to respond consciously to the recognition of His lordship.

Noah’s invitation is expressed as follows:

لَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ فَقَالَ يَا قَوْمِ اعْبُدُوا مَا لَكُمْ مِنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرُهُ ۖ إِنِّي أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ

7:59

We sent Noah to his people, and he said: My people, worship The God; you have no deity other than Him. I fear for you the punishment of a tremendous Day.

Of Abraham, we read:

إِذْ قَالَ لَهُ رَبُّهُ أَسْلِمْ ۖ قَالَ أَسْلَمْتُ لِرَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

2:131

When his Lord said to him, “Submit,” he said, “I have submitted to the Lord of all worlds.”

When Jesus invites people to worship, he likewise makes his own standing before the Lord clear:

إِنَّ رَبِّي وَرَبُّكُمْ فَاعْبُدُوهُ ۗ هَٰذَا صِرَاطٌ مُسْتَقِيمٌ

3:51

Indeed, The God is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him. This is a straight path.

Jesus identifies recognizing the One God as Lord and worshipping Him as the straight path.

Muhammad’s invitation connects this same path with Abraham’s way:

قُلْ إِنَّنِي هَدَانِي رَبِّي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ دِينًا قِيَمًا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا ۚ وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

6:161

Say: My Lord has guided me to a straight path, an upright religion, the way of Abraham, devoted to what is right; he was not among those who associated partners with God.

In the opening chapter, believers praise the Lord of all worlds, dedicate their worship to Him, and ask Him to guide them along the straight path—the way of those whom He has favoured.

In 19:58, the guided and chosen Prophets are included among those favoured. In 4:69, the Prophets, the truthful, the witnesses, and the righteous are named among them. In 43:59, Jesus himself is described as a servant upon whom God bestowed favour.

The reflection on 3:51 examines this connection in detail. The straight path is the shared invitation of God’s Messengers; no people, denomination, or sect owns it exclusively.

The effects of this devotion also appear in our dealings with others. In 6:151, rejection of association with God appears alongside kindness to parents and the sanctity of human life. In 6:152, the Qur’an addresses orphans’ rights, honest measures and weights, fairness in speech, and fidelity to commitments. In 6:153, people are called to follow this very path.

Protecting an orphan’s rights, using honest scales in trade, and treating others justly are ways of following the straight path for which believers ask their Lord’s guidance each day.

7. The Authority to Command and Establish Religious Obligations

The authority to command is one of the fundamental dimensions of God’s lordship.

The God says:

إِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ يُغْشِي اللَّيْلَ النَّهَارَ يَطْلُبُهُ حَثِيثًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ وَالنُّجُومَ مُسَخَّرَاتٍ بِأَمْرِهِ ۗ أَلَا لَهُ الْخَلْقُ وَالْأَمْرُ ۗ تَبَارَكَ رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

7:54

Your Lord is The God, who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then established His dominion over the Throne. He covers the day with the night, which pursues it swiftly; the sun, moon, and stars are subject to His command. Truly, creation and command belong to Him. Blessed is The God, Lord of all worlds.

Here, creation and command are attributed to God in the context of His governance of existence.

In guidance and religious legislation, the Qur’an likewise makes the authority to declare things lawful or forbidden dependent on God’s authorization:

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ مَا أَنْزَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ رِزْقٍ فَجَعَلْتُمْ مِنْهُ حَرَامًا وَحَلَالًا قُلْ أَذِنَ لَكُمْ ۖ أَمْ عَلَى تَفْتَرُونَ

10:59

Say: Have you considered the provision The God has sent down for you, some of which you have made forbidden and some lawful? Say: Has The God authorized you, or are you inventing lies about The God?

A person may dislike a food and choose not to eat it. But saying that God has forbidden that food to everyone attributes a ruling to Him. Such an attribution requires divine evidence.

In 16:116, attributing human inventions about what is lawful and forbidden to God is also described as fabricating lies about Him.

The Qur’an further asks:

أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا لَمْ يَأْذَنْ بِهِ ۚ وَلَوْلَا كَلِمَةُ الْفَصْلِ لَقُضِيَ بَيْنَهُمْ ۗ وَإِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

42:21

Or do they have partners who have prescribed for them as religion what The God has not authorized? Were it not for the word of decision, judgment would already have been passed between them. The wrongdoers will have a painful punishment.

The verse connects unauthorized religious legislation with assigning partners to God. The expression min al-din (as part of religion) matters: the issue is what people prescribe for others as religion.

In 6:114, God’s authority in judgment is linked to His sending down the Book with its detailed explanation. This supports the principle of revelation as the authority for religious judgment.

God’s authority to command is not confined to declaring things lawful or forbidden. He has established principles for human life and relationships that also provide standards for assessing human commands, laws, and decisions.

إِنَّ يَأْمُرُ بِالْعَدْلِ وَالْإِحْسَانِ وَإِيتَاءِ ذِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَيَنْهَىٰ عَنِ الْفَحْشَاءِ وَالْمُنْكَرِ وَالْبَغْيِ ۚ يَعِظُكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ

16:90

The God commands justice, goodness, and giving to relatives, and forbids indecency, wrongdoing, and aggression. He admonishes you so that you may take heed.

People can make laws and regulations to manage their lives. That competence does not grant them the right to impose any obligation they please on others.

A command requiring people to oppress others, violate human rights, or commit wrongdoing contradicts the Lord’s command. No ruler, political institution, religious leader, or group can make such a command legitimate merely through power or status.

Recognizing God’s lordship therefore requires both reserving religious legislation for Him and observing divine principles in government and the administration of human affairs.

These foundations are examined in Lawful and Forbidden in the Qur’an: Divine Legislative Authority, the principle of justice, and the principle of ihsan—goodness beyond the minimum obligation.

8. Associating Partners with God by Taking Others as Lords

Shirk (associating others with God) is not confined to believing in another creator or worshipping a being other than God.

The Qur’an describes people who acknowledged God as Creator yet turned away from the truth:

وَلَئِنْ سَأَلْتَهُمْ مَنْ خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ لَيَقُولُنَّ ۖ فَأَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

29:61

If you ask them who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon, they will surely say, “The God.” How, then, are they turned away?

Acknowledging that God created the world does not, by itself, reveal whom someone treats as lord in practice.

Joseph said to his two fellow prisoners:

يَا صَاحِبَيِ السِّجْنِ أَأَرْبَابٌ مُتَفَرِّقُونَ خَيْرٌ أَمِ الْوَاحِدُ الْقَهَّارُ

12:39

My two companions in prison, are separate lords better, or The God, the One, the Overpowering?

مَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ إِلَّا أَسْمَاءً سَمَّيْتُمُوهَا أَنْتُمْ وَآبَاؤُكُمْ مَا أَنْزَلَ بِهَا مِنْ سُلْطَانٍ ۚ إِنِ الْحُكْمُ إِلَّا ۚ أَمَرَ أَلَّا تَعْبُدُوا إِلَّا إِيَّاهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

12:40

You worship besides Him nothing but names that you and your forefathers have named, for which The God has sent down no authority. Judgment belongs to The God alone. He has commanded that you worship none but Him. That is the upright religion, but most people do not know.

Joseph brings together taking lords, worship, evidence, and authority in judgment.

If someone grants another person an authority to command that belongs to the Lord, and places that person’s status, words, or wishes above divine guidance, their understanding of the Lord’s oneness has gone astray.

God also commands Muhammad:

قُلْ أَغَيْرَ أَبْغِي رَبًّا وَهُوَ رَبُّ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ ۚ وَلَا تَكْسِبُ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ إِلَّا عَلَيْهَا ۚ وَلَا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ۚ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ مَرْجِعُكُمْ فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

6:164

Say: Shall I seek a lord other than The God, when He is the Lord of everything? Every soul earns only to its own account, and no bearer of burdens bears another’s burden. Then to your Lord you will return, and He will inform you about that over which you differed.

The verse begins by rejecting the search for a lord other than God and ends with our return to Him. Between those statements, it affirms each person’s responsibility for their own conduct.

The connection matters: choosing another lord does not allow someone to hand over responsibility for their choices and actions.

Other forms of assigning equals or rivals to God are examined in Andad in the Qur’an. Andad means counterparts or rivals set alongside God.

9. Pharaoh: Claiming Lordship over People’s Lives and Consciences

Pharaoh provides a striking Qur’anic example of a human claim to lordship:

فَقَالَ أَنَا رَبُّكُمُ الْأَعْلَىٰ

79:24

Then he said: I am your highest lord.

How did Pharaoh exercise this claimed lordship over people’s lives?

In 43:51, he invokes his rule over Egypt and the material resources under his control. In 28:4, the Qur’an describes his arrogance, his division of the population into factions, and his oppression of one group. In 26:22, Moses confronts him with his subjugation of the Children of Israel.

Pharaoh used political power to impose his will. People did not enjoy equal rights under his rule: one community was subjected to oppression because of its group identity.

But his claim went beyond political rule and control of material resources.

When the magicians responded to Moses’ sign by believing in the Lord of Moses and Aaron, Pharaoh said:

قَالَ آمَنْتُمْ لَهُ قَبْلَ أَنْ آذَنَ لَكُمْ ۖ إِنَّهُ لَكَبِيرُكُمُ الَّذِي عَلَّمَكُمُ السِّحْرَ ۖ فَلَأُقَطِّعَنَّ أَيْدِيَكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ مِنْ خِلَافٍ وَلَأُصَلِّبَنَّكُمْ فِي جُذُوعِ النَّخْلِ وَلَتَعْلَمُنَّ أَيُّنَا أَشَدُّ عَذَابًا وَأَبْقَىٰ

20:71

He said: Have you believed in him before I gave you permission? He must be your chief who taught you magic. I will cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides and crucify you on palm trunks; you will know which of us is harsher and more enduring in punishment.

Pharaoh made even belief conditional on his permission. When people accepted a truth against his wishes, he used political power and threats of punishment to force them to abandon it.

They replied:

قَالُوا لَنْ نُؤْثِرَكَ عَلَىٰ مَا جَاءَنَا مِنَ الْبَيِّنَاتِ وَالَّذِي فَطَرَنَا ۖ فَاقْضِ مَا أَنْتَ قَاضٍ ۖ إِنَّمَا تَقْضِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا

20:72

They said: We will never prefer you to the clear evidence that has come to us, or to the One who brought us into being. So decree whatever you will decree; you can decree only in this worldly life.

They distinguished Pharaoh’s limited power in this world from God’s lordship. Pharaoh could punish them, but that ability gave him no authority to determine truth, faith, or belief for them.

This reveals a central distinction between legitimate government and a human claim to lordship.

A ruler can make decisions within the scope of a legitimate responsibility to administer society. A ruler cannot demand that people place his will above justice, human rights, or truth they have recognized.

If God commands justice and goodness and forbids oppression and aggression, no human office can turn injustice into a binding duty for others.

Pharaoh exemplifies someone who makes political power a means of controlling people’s lives, freedom, and conscience, claiming an authority beyond the proper limits of human responsibility.

As discussed in the reflection on 3:26, human ownership and government at every level are limited and dependent on the resources available to those exercising them.

Related forms of domination are explored in Taghut in the Qur’an—taghut denotes transgressive authority—and the principle rejecting tyranny and humiliation.

10. Religious Scholars and Monks: Taking Lords in Matters of Faith

Treating human beings as lords is not confined to political power. The Qur’an also speaks of people who placed their religious leaders in that position:

اتَّخَذُوا أَحْبَارَهُمْ وَرُهْبَانَهُمْ أَرْبَابًا مِنْ دُونِ وَالْمَسِيحَ ابْنَ مَرْيَمَ وَمَا أُمِرُوا إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُوا إِلَٰهًا وَاحِدًا ۖ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۚ سُبْحَانَهُ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

9:31

They took their religious scholars and monks as lords besides The God, and the Messiah, son of Mary, although they had been commanded only to worship one God. There is no deity except Him. Exalted is He beyond what they associate with Him.

In Pharaoh’s story, the ruler himself claimed lordship. In 9:31, the Qur’an describes followers who took human beings as their lords.

The distinction is important. Someone need not personally claim lordship in order to be treated as a lord by others.

Jesus invited people to worship his Lord and theirs, yet some placed him in the very position from which he directed people away.

Nor does taking a religious scholar as lord require believing that he created the universe or prostrating before him.

Reading 9:31 alongside 10:59 and 42:21 clarifies one form of this conduct: transferring the authority to legislate religion and determine what is lawful or forbidden to religious leaders.

When a scholar’s statement becomes a religious obligation without divine evidence, and people believe themselves answerable to God for following it, that scholar has been placed in a position whose authority belongs to the Lord.

In both examples—Pharaoh’s claim and the treatment of scholars and monks as lords—human beings exceed their proper authority, or their followers assign them a standing that belongs to God.

11. Guiding People toward the Lord, Rather Than Becoming Their Lords

The Qur’an values religious knowledge, deep understanding of religion, and teaching the divine Book. The purpose of that teaching is to know the Lord and His guidance.

The God says:

مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُؤْتِيَهُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ ثُمَّ يَقُولَ لِلنَّاسِ كُونُوا عِبَادًا لِي مِنْ دُونِ وَلَٰكِنْ كُونُوا رَبَّانِيِّينَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تُعَلِّمُونَ الْكِتَابَ وَبِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَدْرُسُونَ

3:79

It is not for a human being to whom The God gives the Book, judgment, and prophethood to then say to people, “Be my servants instead of The God’s.” Rather: be rabbaniyyin (people devoted to the Lord through learning and teaching), through your teaching of the Book and your study of it.

A rabbani scholar guides people toward understanding the Book and the Lord’s guidance.

In 5:44, the rabbaniyyun (learned people devoted to the Lord) and ahbar (religious scholars) judge according to God’s Book, with which they have been entrusted and to which they bear witness. In 5:63, these same groups are reproached for failing to restrain sinful speech and the consumption of unlawful wealth.

Religious knowledge creates responsibilities. It does not confer lordship over others.

The Qur’an also addresses the pursuit of deeper understanding:

وَمَا كَانَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ لِيَنْفِرُوا كَافَّةً ۚ فَلَوْلَا نَفَرَ مِنْ كُلِّ فِرْقَةٍ مِنْهُمْ طَائِفَةٌ لِيَتَفَقَّهُوا فِي الدِّينِ وَلِيُنْذِرُوا قَوْمَهُمْ إِذَا رَجَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَحْذَرُونَ

9:122

The believers should not all go forth together. Why, then, should a group from each community not go forth to gain deep understanding of religion and warn their people when they return to them, so that they may take care?

Tafaqquh means gaining deep understanding. This verse concerns acquiring religious understanding and communicating it to others.

In 16:43 and 21:7, people unaware that earlier Messengers were human are directed to ask those who possess knowledge of the reminder. The question addresses a particular lack of knowledge. It does not establish a class of people with exclusive authority over every religious matter. See the principle against establishing religious authority without evidence.

Reflection, investigation, and ijtihad (reasoned inquiry and judgment) in understanding religion remain open to everyone. Greater knowledge increases a person’s responsibility to teach and guide; it does not take away others’ right to think and understand.

This is the difference between a scholar devoted to the Lord and a religious lord: one helps people understand divine guidance; the other occupies the position of an independent source of religious commands.

Following the passage about devotion through teaching and study, God says:

وَلَا يَأْمُرَكُمْ أَنْ تَتَّخِذُوا الْمَلَائِكَةَ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ أَرْبَابًا ۗ أَيَأْمُرُكُمْ بِالْكُفْرِ بَعْدَ إِذْ أَنْتُمْ مُسْلِمُونَ

3:80

Nor would he command you to take the angels and the Prophets as lords. Would he command you to disbelief after you have submitted?

If the Qur’an rejects taking even angels and Prophets as lords, how could a jurist’s or teacher’s learning and status confer such authority?

12. Obeying the Messenger and Recognizing the Qur’an’s Authority

In the Qur’an, obedience to the Messenger does not conflict with worship of God, because the Lord is the source of the Messenger’s commission and the authority of his command.

The God says:

مَنْ يُطِعِ الرَّسُولَ فَقَدْ أَطَاعَ ۖ وَمَنْ تَوَلَّىٰ فَمَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ عَلَيْهِمْ حَفِيظًا

4:80

Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed The God. As for those who turn away, We have not sent you as their keeper.

In 3:50, Jesus speaks of making lawful some things previously forbidden to the Children of Israel and calls people to obey him. Immediately afterward, in 3:51, he declares God to be his Lord and theirs.

The same sequence appears in 43:63 and 43:64.

As Messenger, Jesus conveys rulings received from the Lord. Obedience to him in that role neither recognizes Jesus as Lord nor grants him an independent authority to legislate religion.

Of Muhammad, the Qur’an says:

وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُنَا بَيِّنَاتٍ ۙ قَالَ الَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ لِقَاءَنَا ائْتِ بِقُرْآنٍ غَيْرِ هَٰذَا أَوْ بَدِّلْهُ ۚ قُلْ مَا يَكُونُ لِي أَنْ أُبَدِّلَهُ مِنْ تِلْقَاءِ نَفْسِي ۖ إِنْ أَتَّبِعُ إِلَّا مَا يُوحَىٰ إِلَيَّ ۖ إِنِّي أَخَافُ إِنْ عَصَيْتُ رَبِّي عَذَابَ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ

10:15

When Our clear verses are recited to them, those who do not expect to meet Us say, “Bring a Qur’an other than this, or change it.” Say: It is not for me to change it on my own initiative. I follow only what is revealed to me. If I disobey my Lord, I fear the punishment of a tremendous Day.

Muhammad, whom people are called to obey in his capacity as Messenger, is himself answerable to the Lord’s command.

To understand obedience to the Messenger, we must return to the verses that establish it.

The God says:

قُلْ أَطِيعُوا وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ ۖ فَإِنْ تَوَلَّوْا فَإِنَّمَا عَلَيْهِ مَا حُمِّلَ وَعَلَيْكُمْ مَا حُمِّلْتُمْ ۖ وَإِنْ تُطِيعُوهُ تَهْتَدُوا ۚ وَمَا عَلَى الرَّسُولِ إِلَّا الْبَلَاغُ الْمُبِينُ

24:54

Say: Obey The God and obey the Messenger. If they turn away, he is responsible for what has been placed upon him, and you for what has been placed upon you. If you obey him, you will be guided. The Messenger’s responsibility is only al-balagh al-mubin (clear conveyance of the message).

The very verse commanding obedience also defines the Messenger’s responsibility through the expression al-balagh al-mubin (clear conveyance of the message).

The same connection between obedience and the duty of clear conveyance appears in 5:92 and 64:12.

What, then, was Muhammad commissioned to convey?

قُلْ أَيُّ شَيْءٍ أَكْبَرُ شَهَادَةً ۖ قُلِ ۖ شَهِيدٌ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَكُمْ ۚ وَأُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنُ لِأُنْذِرَكُمْ بِهِ وَمَنْ بَلَغَ ۚ أَئِنَّكُمْ لَتَشْهَدُونَ أَنَّ مَعَ آلِهَةً أُخْرَىٰ ۚ قُلْ لَا أَشْهَدُ ۚ قُلْ إِنَّمَا هُوَ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ وَإِنَّنِي بَرِيءٌ مِمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ

6:19

Say: What is greatest in testimony? Say: The God is witness between me and you. This Qur’an has been revealed to me so that by it I may warn you and whomever it reaches. Do you truly testify that there are other deities alongside The God? Say: I do not testify so. Say: He is only one God, and I am free of what you associate with Him.

Muhammad identifies the Qur’an itself as the means of conveying God’s warning to his contemporaries and to everyone the Qur’an reaches.

The Qur’an repeatedly describes itself as a clear and clarifying Book and a clear and clarifying Qur’an: a standard for distinguishing truth from falsehood. Al-Mubin and the Messenger’s Clear Conveyance examines the relationship between these descriptions and the Messenger’s duty. Al-mubin means clear or clarifying, according to context.

Obeying the Messenger in his commissioned role means obeying the message he brings from God. During his lifetime, Muhammad also judged disputes and administered community affairs, alongside conveying and teaching revelation. Those responsibilities, too, derived their validity from the framework of the Lord’s guidance.

After Muhammad’s death, the command to obey the Messenger remains valid for later generations. We obey his mission by following the same Qur’an he conveyed.

The Messenger must be distinguished from reports attributed to him. He bore divine revelation; accounts of his words and actions are human reports.

A historical report does not become revelation or an independent source of religious legislation merely because a system of hadith evaluation classifies it as sahih (authenticated under that system’s criteria). Hadith are transmitted reports about the Prophet’s words and actions. Such reports must be measured against the Qur’an, rather than used to change its clear rulings, restrict them, declare them abrogated, or impose new religious duties.

In 15:9, the Qur’an speaks of preserving the Reminder. In 25:30, the Messenger’s complaint concerns his people’s abandonment of the Qur’an.

Obeying the Messenger and the Authority Claimed for Hadith examines how confusing revelation with transmitted reports opens the way to parallel sources of religious legislation and the transfer of religious authority to human beings.

The reflection on 3:32 also distinguishes the standing of a Nabi (Prophet, a recipient of divine knowledge) from the responsibility of a Rasul (Messenger, commissioned to convey the message), in relation to the command to obey.

Fidelity to Muhammad’s mission means fidelity to the Qur’an. Obedience to the Messenger cannot justify transferring the authority of the very Book he was commissioned to convey to someone else.

13. Those Entrusted with Affairs: Knowledge, Competence, and Limited Authority

People need knowledge, experience, cooperation, management, and decision-making to organize their lives. The Qur’an recognizes different human responsibilities and areas of competence.

The God says:

إِنَّ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَنْ تُؤَدُّوا الْأَمَانَاتِ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهَا وَإِذَا حَكَمْتُمْ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ أَنْ تَحْكُمُوا بِالْعَدْلِ ۚ إِنَّ نِعِمَّا يَعِظُكُمْ بِهِ ۗ إِنَّ كَانَ سَمِيعًا بَصِيرًا

4:58

The God commands you to deliver trusts to those entitled to them, and when you judge between people, to judge justly. Excellent is what The God admonishes you to do. The God is Hearing, Seeing.

The next verse says:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَطِيعُوا وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنْكُمْ ۖ فَإِنْ تَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ فَرُدُّوهُ إِلَى وَالرَّسُولِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَحْسَنُ تَأْوِيلًا

4:59

You who believe, obey The God, obey the Messenger, and those entrusted with affairs among you. If you disagree over anything, refer it to The God and the Messenger, if you believe in The God and the Last Day. That is better and leads to the best outcome.

Who Are Uli al-Amr?

Uli al-amr (those entrusted with affairs or authority) should not be confined in meaning to political rulers, governors, kings, or religious leaders.

The expression draws attention to people responsible for an amr (an affair or matter). To understand their authority, we must first identify the matter concerned.

Another verse in the same chapter states:

وَإِذَا جَاءَهُمْ أَمْرٌ مِنَ الْأَمْنِ أَوِ الْخَوْفِ أَذَاعُوا بِهِ ۖ وَلَوْ رَدُّوهُ إِلَى الرَّسُولِ وَإِلَىٰ أُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنْهُمْ لَعَلِمَهُ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَنْبِطُونَهُ مِنْهُمْ ۗ وَلَوْلَا فَضْلُ عَلَيْكُمْ وَرَحْمَتُهُ لَاتَّبَعْتُمُ الشَّيْطَانَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

4:83

When a report concerning security or fear reaches them, they spread it. Had they referred it to the Messenger and to those entrusted with affairs among them, those capable of discerning its significance would have understood it. Were it not for The God’s favour and mercy upon you, you would have followed Satan, except for a few.

The subject is a report concerning security or fear. The Qur’an warns against spreading it without examination and directs attention to those qualified to assess it and determine what is true.

Here, istinbat (drawing out an understanding through examination) concerns that report. It is not the creation of new religious rulings.

Read alongside 4:58 and 4:59, this passage connects authority with the nature of the matter, the relevant knowledge, and a person’s responsibilities and powers.

For example, someone who becomes ill consults a physician for diagnosis and treatment, not the mayor. If the illness requires specialist knowledge, the patient seeks a physician with the appropriate training and experience, rather than assuming any physician is equally qualified.

Hospital inspection, compliance with health standards, and supervision of care quality are different matters. They fall to the responsible regulator or health ministry. Medical knowledge alone does not authorize every physician to carry out statutory hospital inspections.

A dispute over land ownership, debt, or breach of contract may require a judge and a court to make a binding decision. A Qur’an reciter or local religious teacher does not acquire judicial authority simply by possessing religious knowledge. Equally, holding judicial office does not qualify a judge to diagnose illness or prescribe treatment.

We turn to an engineer to assess a building’s structural soundness, to people with financial expertise for financial questions, and to duly authorized institutions to administer municipal affairs.

Expertise, authority to adjudicate, and executive power are distinct, although they may sometimes be combined. None independently gives a person general authority over every area of life.

When Joseph was invited to take responsibility, he said:

قَالَ اجْعَلْنِي عَلَىٰ خَزَائِنِ الْأَرْضِ ۖ إِنِّي حَفِيظٌ عَلِيمٌ

12:55

He said: Put me in charge of the storehouses of the land; I am a knowledgeable guardian.

Joseph invokes two qualifications: the ability to safeguard what is entrusted to him and the knowledge required. Responsibility should correspond to competence and capability.

See the principle of trust and competence in leadership and the principle of valuing knowledge while recognizing its limits.

Obedience within a Mandate, and Freedom to Choose Whom to Consult

Obedience to those entrusted with affairs operates within their legitimate responsibilities. Authority over one matter does not confer control over every part of people’s lives, thought, or beliefs.

We must also distinguish seeking someone’s expertise from complying with a decision made within their lawful authority.

A patient can seek a second medical opinion and decide on treatment with a better understanding of the options. A valid court decision in a legal dispute, or legitimate public safety and health regulations, carries binding force within civic responsibility.

In understanding religion, too, people can investigate for themselves, learn from others, and compare arguments. Choosing whom to consult is voluntary; no one is required to adopt a particular religious authority as the necessary route to understanding religion.

Although 4:59 commands obedience to those entrusted with affairs, it identifies God and the Messenger as the authorities to whom disputes are ultimately referred.

As argued earlier, for generations after Muhammad, returning to God’s guidance and Muhammad’s mission means returning to the Qur’an he conveyed.

There is no guarantee that everyone occupying a position of authority possesses knowledge, competence, honesty, integrity, or good intentions. They may misunderstand a situation, or make a decision appropriate only under particular conditions.

Their decisions must therefore be assessed against the Qur’an, divine principles, the limits of their mandate, and the evidence relevant to the matter itself.

14. Judgment, Qur’anic Principles, and Consultation

Qur’anic Standards for Judgment

In 4:58, delivering trusts to those entitled to them and judging justly are human responsibilities. The command includes everyone who judges, including those entrusted with public affairs.

Even Muhammad himself was required to observe divine standards when judging:

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ لِتَحْكُمَ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ بِمَا أَرَاكَ ۚ وَلَا تَكُنْ لِلْخَائِنِينَ خَصِيمًا

4:105

We have sent down the Book to you in truth, so that you may judge between people by what The God has shown you. Do not be an advocate for those who betray trust.

In 4:65, believers are held responsible for accepting Muhammad’s judgment; in 4:105, Muhammad is held responsible for how he judges. The verses that follow also prohibit him from defending those who betray themselves.

In 2:213, the sending down of the Book is connected with settling disputes. In 3:23, people are called to the judgment of God’s Book to resolve their differences.

The Qur’an also sets clear standards for adjudicating among the People of the Book (communities possessing earlier scripture). In 5:42, Muhammad may accept or decline a request to judge, but if he judges, he must do so equitably.

In 5:43, the Torah is described as containing God’s judgment. In 5:47, the People of the Gospel are called to judge by what God revealed in it. In 5:48 and 5:49, Muhammad is commanded to judge by what God has revealed and not follow people’s desires.

While recognizing different prescribed ways for different communities, 5:48 describes the Qur’an as confirming earlier scripture and as muhaymin over it (a guardian and authoritative witness).

As explained in the reflection on 3:4, the Qur’an is a furqan (a criterion distinguishing truth from falsehood). Judging among scriptural communities therefore involves both recognizing their different prescribed ways and the validity of what God revealed in earlier Books, and assessing rulings and claims attributed to those Books against the Qur’an.

This rests on the principle of a shared foundation of religion and diverse prescribed ways, explored in Din, Islam, and Shariah in the Qur’an: din concerns religion and accountable allegiance, Islam submission to God, and shariah a prescribed way.

If Muhammad was required to observe divine standards in judging, those entrusted with affairs must likewise judge and decide according to the Qur’an, justice, and divine principles. See judging with truth and equity.

Establishing a Rule Is Different from Applying It to a Case

The Qur’an itself sometimes leaves the application of a rule to human judgment. Concerning hunting while in ihram (the consecrated state associated with pilgrimage), it says:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَقْتُلُوا الصَّيْدَ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ ۚ وَمَنْ قَتَلَهُ مِنْكُمْ مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاءٌ مِثْلُ مَا قَتَلَ مِنَ النَّعَمِ يَحْكُمُ بِهِ ذَوَا عَدْلٍ مِنْكُمْ هَدْيًا بَالِغَ الْكَعْبَةِ أَوْ كَفَّارَةٌ طَعَامُ مَسَاكِينَ أَوْ عَدْلُ ذَٰلِكَ صِيَامًا لِيَذُوقَ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ ۗ عَفَا عَمَّا سَلَفَ ۚ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَيَنْتَقِمُ مِنْهُ ۗ عَزِيزٌ ذُو انْتِقَامٍ

5:95

You who believe, do not kill game while you are in ihram. Whoever among you kills it deliberately must compensate with an equivalent animal from livestock, as judged by two just people among you, as an offering reaching the Ka‘bah (the sanctuary in Mecca); or make expiation by feeding people in need, or by an equivalent amount of fasting, so that he may experience the consequence of his deed. The God has pardoned what is past; whoever repeats it, The God will exact retribution from him. The God is Mighty, Possessor of retribution.

Read with 5:96, the prohibition concerns land game while in ihram. God establishes the prohibition, the duty of compensation, and its criteria. Two just people determine the equivalent animal from among livestock.

They are not creating a new religious rule. They are deciding how an established divine rule applies to a particular case.

In 2:229, limits are set for divorce and financial relations between spouses. In 2:231, retaining or releasing a wife must conform to what is right, without causing harm. Recognizing appropriate conduct in such cases requires understanding people’s circumstances as well as Qur’anic principles.

This illustrates the importance of Qur’anic principles. The Qur’an gives principles, standards, and limits for life; it does not enumerate every instance of right and wrong conduct or every arrangement for managing life across all times and conditions.

Consulting people with relevant knowledge and authority can help identify how these principles apply, resolve disputes, and determine suitable ways to implement them.

A decision made under particular conditions may need revision when those conditions change. Revising it does not change God’s ruling.

As examined in Recognized Good, Wrongdoing, and Indecency in the Qur’an, Qur’anic principles provide standards for assessing changing customs, laws, conduct, and human decisions.

Consultation: Civil Obligations and Religious Obligations

The God says:

وَالَّذِينَ اسْتَجَابُوا لِرَبِّهِمْ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ

42:38

And those who respond to their Lord, establish Salat, conduct their affairs through consultation among themselves, and spend from what We have provided them.

The verse presents both responding to the Lord and managing shared affairs through shura (mutual consultation) as characteristics of believers. Salat (worshipful devotion, including its established practice) is discussed here alongside consultation and giving. In 3:159, Muhammad himself is commanded to consult people about their affairs.

People can deliberate and decide on traffic rules, taxation, the selection of a national leader, the length and limits of that office, budget priorities, and the allocation of public resources to education, health, security, and other social needs.

Decisions made through legitimate civic procedures, within the rights of community members, are binding on those members. Their civil force does not turn a council’s decision into a religious decree, an absolute declaration of what God permits or forbids, or a command equal in authority to the Qur’an.

For example, a road may have a speed limit of 100 kilometres per hour. Observing it is a driver’s civic responsibility. Changes to the road, population growth, or the need to protect life may lead to its reduction to 80 kilometres per hour. God’s ruling has not changed, nor has the council invented a new religious prohibition.

The principle of protecting human life remains constant; its implementation can change with circumstances and knowledge. The same distinction applies to tax law, commercial regulations, methods of selecting public officials, and budget priorities.

Through consultation, people can also define the responsibilities and powers of those entrusted with affairs. A public office confers authority for a specified responsibility, not unlimited control over people’s lives.

Even a unanimous council vote to compel a particular belief or religious affiliation cannot override the principle of no compulsion in religion. Nor does majority agreement to oppress a group make that oppression just.

The validity of decisions by councils and public authorities must be assessed by justice, trust, goodness, human rights and dignity, freedom of belief, and other Qur’anic principles.

The Qur’anic foundations are explored in consultation and participation in shared affairs.

Is This Authority Reserved for Particular Individuals?

The Qur’an’s standards for judgment and public administration clarify the responsibilities of those entrusted with affairs. Identifying particular people as the exclusive holders of that authority requires independent evidence.

Twelver Shi‘ism, a branch of Islam, identifies uli al-amr with a particular succession of Imams (religious leaders). It also has separate juristic grounds for consulting maraji‘ al-taqlid (jurists followed in religious practice) during the occultation, the believed absence of the last Imam.

One argument about uli al-amr concerns the wording of 4:59: the imperative ati‘u (obey) is not repeated immediately before that title.

But two titles sharing one verb does not establish equality of standing or authority. In 8:46, God and the Messenger also share a single verb commanding obedience. Moreover, 4:59 itself does not include those entrusted with affairs among the final authorities to whom disagreement is referred.

Under the principle of the authority of clear foundational verses, the Qur’an contains no explicit basis for restricting uli al-amr to those Imams or requiring people to follow particular religious jurists.

If following specified individuals were a religious obligation for all believers, The God would state that duty in the Book’s clear foundational verses. So consequential a requirement cannot rest on interpreting ambiguous passages or on indirect inferences.

Selection, leadership, and imamah (the role of an imam or guide) are examined in Imamah in the Qur’an.

Joseph’s story further illuminates the difference between divine lordship and human responsibilities.

Joseph, who challenged devotion to separate lords in prison, later sought responsibility for Egypt’s storehouses and participated in its administration. Drawing on 12:54, 12:55, and 12:76, the reflection on 3:28 explains that accepting administrative office and cooperating in public life does not transfer religious authority to political rulers.

The story also uses rabb for the king of Egypt. Understanding that usage requires noticing Joseph’s distinction between his own Lord and the master of the people he addresses.

In 12:37, Joseph speaks of knowledge taught to him by his Lord. In 12:38, he declares his adherence to the way of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. In 12:39 and 12:40, he challenges his fellow prisoners’ devotion to separate lords. Later, in 12:41 and 12:42, he calls one prisoner’s master that prisoner’s rabb.

The distinction also appears in Joseph’s exchange with the king’s messenger:

وَقَالَ الْمَلِكُ ائْتُونِي بِهِ ۖ فَلَمَّا جَاءَهُ الرَّسُولُ قَالَ ارْجِعْ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَاسْأَلْهُ مَا بَالُ النِّسْوَةِ اللَّاتِي قَطَّعْنَ أَيْدِيَهُنَّ ۚ إِنَّ رَبِّي بِكَيْدِهِنَّ عَلِيمٌ

12:50

The king said, “Bring him to me.” When the messenger came to him, he said, “Return to your master and ask him about the women who cut their hands. My Lord knows their scheming.”

Joseph distinguishes his addressee’s master from his own Lord. In view of his prayer concerning those women’s scheming in 12:33 and the Lord’s response in 12:34, “my Lord” at the end of 12:50 refers to God.

Using rabb to identify someone’s master does not amount to accepting that person’s divine lordship. Similarly, the Hebrew title rabbi is used for Jewish teachers and religious scholars; its meaning is understood from its linguistic setting and the human relationships involved.

What matters for divine oneness and association with God is not only the titles people use for one another, but the kind and extent of authority they actually grant them.

15. Taking Religious and Political Leaders as Lords Today

The Qur’an’s warning about taking scholars, monks, and other people as lords is not merely a lesson about past communities.

We, too, must not make lords of religious teachers, muftis (scholars who issue religious opinions), jurists followed in religious practice, rulers, princes, or a consensus of religious scholars.

If we demand evidence for other people’s leaders’ claims, we must apply that same standard to our own leaders.

If a mufti declares something forbidden without divine evidence, and people believe themselves religiously bound to accept it solely because of his office, they have transferred legislative authority to a human being.

If a ruler forces people to accept a particular belief or religious affiliation, he claims the same authority that Pharaoh claimed over the magicians.

If someone in power puts his will above justice and human rights and requires obedience to unjust commands, he exceeds the authority entrusted to him for administering people’s affairs.

If a jurist followed as a religious authority makes a statement incompatible with the Qur’an’s clear foundational teachings, his status cannot make that statement a valid divine ruling.

Scholarly agreement may arise from knowledge, experience, investigation, and collective deliberation, and people may benefit from it. But agreement among thousands does not turn a ruling into revelation. As explained in the principle that numbers do not establish truth, a claim’s validity depends on its evidence, not on how many people accept it.

Explaining the harm caused by a practice likewise does not require inventing new religious rulings.

For example, 2:219 describes both grave sin and benefits for people in khamr (intoxicants) and maysir (gambling), while declaring their sin greater than their benefit. In 5:90, they are described as defilement belonging to Satan’s work, with a command to avoid them. In 5:91, their effects include hostility, hatred, and distraction from remembering God and from Salat (worshipful devotion).

The reflection on 2:219 explains that khamr is not limited to grape wine and that ithm (sin or culpable wrongdoing) extends beyond physical or financial harm. In 7:33, ithm itself is among the things God has forbidden.

We can convey the Qur’an’s command and its stated reasons, use knowledge and experience to explain harmful consequences, and enact regulations to protect people’s rights and health. In each case, we must distinguish understanding harm, implementing divine principles, and inventing a new ruling in God’s name.

In 2:170, following ancestral beliefs without reason or guidance is criticized. In 33:67, people on the Day of Account attribute their misguidance to obeying their leaders and great figures.

Can those who invite others to follow them take responsibility for those followers on the Day of Account?

16. The Responsibility of Followers and Those Who Mislead Them

Concerning people who promise to take responsibility for others’ wrongdoing, the Qur’an says:

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّبِعُوا سَبِيلَنَا وَلْنَحْمِلْ خَطَايَاكُمْ وَمَا هُمْ بِحَامِلِينَ مِنْ خَطَايَاهُمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

29:12

Those who denied said to those who believed, “Follow our way, and let us carry your sins.” But they will carry none of their sins. They are certainly liars.

It immediately continues:

وَلَيَحْمِلُنَّ أَثْقَالَهُمْ وَأَثْقَالًا مَعَ أَثْقَالِهِمْ ۖ وَلَيُسْأَلُنَّ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَمَّا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

29:13

They will certainly carry their own burdens, and other burdens alongside their own; on the Day of Resurrection they will be questioned about what they fabricated.

Another passage says of those who mislead:

لِيَحْمِلُوا أَوْزَارَهُمْ كَامِلَةً يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَمِنْ أَوْزَارِ الَّذِينَ يُضِلُّونَهُمْ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ ۗ أَلَا سَاءَ مَا يَزِرُونَ

16:25

So that on the Day of Resurrection they may carry their own burdens in full, and some of the burdens of those whom they mislead without knowledge. How evil is what they bear!

In 29:12, the promise to carry followers’ sins is rejected. But 29:13 and 16:25 speak of an additional responsibility incurred through misleading others.

Someone who misleads is accountable both for personal conduct and for leading others astray. That additional responsibility does not cancel the followers’ responsibility.

Anyone who invents rulings for others in religion’s name must recognize the weight of their words and their effects on people’s lives. Followers must likewise not assume that surrendering religious judgment to others exempts them from thinking, understanding, and choosing responsibly.

This is connected with the principle of individual responsibility for wrongdoing.

17. Divine Lordship and Freedom of Belief, Thought, and Expression

The God says:

قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ كَلِمَةٍ سَوَاءٍ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ أَلَّا نَعْبُدَ إِلَّا وَلَا نُشْرِكَ بِهِ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَتَّخِذَ بَعْضُنَا بَعْضًا أَرْبَابًا مِنْ دُونِ ۚ فَإِنْ تَوَلَّوْا فَقُولُوا اشْهَدُوا بِأَنَّا مُسْلِمُونَ

3:64

Say: People of the Book, come to a word common to us and you: that we worship none but The God, associate nothing with Him, and do not take one another as lords besides The God. If they turn away, say, “Bear witness that we are those who submit.”

Worshipping God, associating no partner with Him, and refusing to take one another as lords form a common foundation of the Qur’anic invitation.

The ending matters too. If those addressed do not accept, believers are to declare their own submission, not compel others to accept the invitation.

In 2:256, the Qur’an rejects compulsion in religion. In 10:99, it challenges even Muhammad’s compelling people to believe.

If God, despite His universal lordship and power over creation, leaves the choice of faith and belief to human beings, no leader or ruler can take that choice away in His name.

Freedom of belief is connected with freedom to listen, think, and express a view.

In 39:17, God gives good news to those who avoid devotion to taghut (transgressive authority) and turn to Him. He then describes these servants:

الَّذِينَ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقَوْلَ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ أَحْسَنَهُ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ هَدَاهُمُ ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمْ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

39:18

Those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it. They are the ones The God has guided, and they are the people of understanding.

Recognizing the best argument requires opportunities to hear, think, compare, and choose. In 16:125 and 29:46, people are also directed to engage in discussion and argument in the best manner.

Together, these passages support the freedom to hear, express, and assess different views, as explored in freedom to listen and respectful dialogue.

Freedom of thought and expression allows people to criticize one another’s beliefs and arguments. Someone may argue from the Qur’an that a belief involves shirk (association with God), kufr (rejection of truth), or departure from divine guidance, and state the evidence. That judgment confers no right to attack the life or liberty of those who hold the belief.

Nor should kufr be understood merely through religious labels or affiliation. As discussed in Who Is a Kafir, and What Is Kufr?, the issue includes how a person encounters truth and rejects it. Kafir denotes a person characterized by such rejection; the study examines the term’s range and contexts.

The Qur’an says of those who repeatedly believe and then deny:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا ثُمَّ آمَنُوا ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا ثُمَّ ازْدَادُوا كُفْرًا لَمْ يَكُنِ لِيَغْفِرَ لَهُمْ وَلَا لِيَهْدِيَهُمْ سَبِيلًا

4:137

Those who believed, then denied, then believed, then denied, and then increased in denial—The God will neither forgive them nor guide them to a way.

The verse describes repeated movement between belief and denial, followed by deeper denial. It states their fate in relation to God’s forgiveness and guidance; it does not command people to kill them.

In 2:217, a person who turns back from religion and dies in denial is described as losing the value of their deeds in this world and the hereafter and remaining in the Fire.

As examined in the reflection on 2:217, these passages provide no warrant for killing someone simply for changing belief.

Difference of belief is not itself permission to fight. In 60:8, God does not forbid kindness and justice toward those who have neither fought believers over religion nor expelled them from their homes. This supports respect, kindness, and justice toward people of other religions.

The freedom we seek for our own inquiry and expression also belongs to someone who investigates the same question and reaches a different conclusion.

Freedom from one another’s domination in matters of belief is one practical consequence of recognizing the lordship of the One God.

These foundations are examined in No Compulsion in Religion: The Messenger’s Duty Is to Convey and the principle of freedom of belief.

18. Living the Relationship between Servant and Lord

Recognizing God’s lordship must extend beyond a theoretical or doctrinal discussion.

When ill, a person uses medical knowledge and available treatment while hoping for the Lord’s healing. When facing difficulty, a person draws on the capacities God has provided and seeks His help. After wrongdoing, a person accepts responsibility, works to repair the harm and reform their conduct, and asks the Lord’s forgiveness.

After their lapse, Adam and his wife said:

قَالَا رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَا أَنْفُسَنَا وَإِنْ لَمْ تَغْفِرْ لَنَا وَتَرْحَمْنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

7:23

They said: Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be among the losers.

They accept responsibility for their wrongdoing and turn directly to the Lord for forgiveness and mercy.

A person who has received guidance still needs the Lord’s guidance:

رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا وَهَبْ لَنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً ۚ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

3:8

Our Lord, do not let our hearts turn aside after You have guided us, and grant us mercy from Yourself. You are the Ever-Giving.

Accepting guidance at one stage of life does not remove the need for growth, deeper understanding, and self-correction later.

Recognizing divine lordship must also shape relationships. If judgment goes against ourselves or those close to us, 4:135 requires us to stand for justice. If we are hostile toward someone, 5:8 forbids that hostility from leading us into injustice.

The Lord from whom we seek guidance, justice, and mercy is also the Lord of other human beings.

We cannot claim to be His servants while disregarding others’ rights or using our power, knowledge, and social position to reduce them to servitude.

As explained in the reflection on 1:5, devotion to the One God is realized by consciously and responsibly directing our lives in the light of the Lord’s guidance.

Conclusion

Recognizing the lordship of the One God means recognizing Him as the Lord of all existence. The world and humanity were not created to be abandoned. Creation, care, growth, guidance, and return to the Lord are dimensions of a purposeful order.

That recognition requires reserving worship and religious legislation for God while accepting responsibility for our own thought, choices, and conduct. Obedience to His Messengers is fulfilled through following the divine guidance they brought.

This understanding frees people from servitude to other human beings without denying responsibility toward others or participation in managing shared life.

When entrusted to judge, teach, or administer public affairs, we must know the limits of our authority and act according to justice and divine principles. When others present a ruling in God’s name, we must ask for its evidence—even when they are among our closest and most trusted leaders.

Believers address God daily in the opening chapter as Lord of all worlds and dedicate their worship to Him. The sincerity of that acknowledgment must become visible in how we think, choose, and live.
