Ummi and Ummiyyin in the Qur’an: Having Scripture Is Not the Same as Knowing It

Muhammad is often called “the unlettered Prophet.” The Arabic word behind “unlettered” is ummi (a term concerning a person’s relationship to Scripture and scriptural learning). Its precise meaning is the subject of this study. A familiar interpretation holds that Muhammad could neither read nor write, both before receiving revelation and throughout the rest of his life. Sometimes this lifelong lack of literacy is presented as evidence that the Qur’an must have come from God. Yet the claim that someone did not acquire a skill is different from the claim that he could never acquire it, and neither should be attributed to the Qur’an without examining its words.

The Qur’an uses this term in several settings. In Al-Baqarah 2:78, some people within a community that possessed Scripture are called ummiyyun (a plural form of ummi) because they do not know the Book. In Al ‘Imran 3:20, ummiyyin (another grammatical form of the same plural) appear alongside those who have been given Scripture. In Al-Jumu‘ah 62:2, God raises a Messenger from among these people to teach them the Book and wisdom.

These passages raise a basic question. Does the term identify people who cannot read and write, people whose community has not received Scripture, or people who lack knowledge of Scripture? And how should we understand Muhammad’s description as ummi when, through revelation, he became a teacher of the Book?

We need to distinguish receiving Scripture, having access to it, reading it, understanding it, and acting on what one has learned. These activities and responsibilities are connected, but they are not interchangeable. A community may possess Scripture while many of its members remain unfamiliar with it. Someone may read it without understanding it well. Someone else may understand it and knowingly act against the truth they have recognized.

The word ummi belongs to the Arabic root family A M M, which also includes umm (mother or source), ummah (community), and imam (a leader or guide). Several explanations of its derivation have been proposed: a connection with ummah and a community without an earlier scriptural tradition; a connection with umm and a person’s original, untaught state; or an association with Umm al-Qura (literally “Mother of Settlements,” a designation associated with Mecca). Membership in the same root family does not, by itself, establish the historical derivation of a particular word. The linked root study examines this family in detail.

Ummi and its plural forms occur six times in the Qur’an: Al-Baqarah 2:78, Al ‘Imran 3:20 and Al ‘Imran 3:75, Al-A‘raf 7:157 and Al-A‘raf 7:158, and Al-Jumu‘ah 62:2. Each occurrence needs to be read in context before we decide what they have in common.

People without knowledge of Scripture within a scriptural community

Al-Baqarah 2:78 provides an especially important example. The surrounding verses distinguish people who understand the truth and deliberately distort it from people who lack knowledge of the Book itself.

أَفَتَطْمَعُونَ أَنْ يُؤْمِنُوا لَكُمْ وَقَدْ كَانَ فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ يَسْمَعُونَ كَلَامَ ثُمَّ يُحَرِّفُونَهُ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا عَقَلُوهُ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

Al-Baqarah 2:75

“Do you hope that they will believe you, when a group of them used to hear the word of The God and then distort it after understanding it, knowing what they were doing?”

The criticism here concerns people who have heard and understood the message. Their distortion follows understanding. The phrases “after understanding it” and “knowing what they were doing” establish this sequence. Their conduct differs from the mistake of someone who has not yet learned the truth.

A few verses later, the Qur’an describes another group among the same people.

وَمِنْهُمْ أُمِّيُّونَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ الْكِتَابَ إِلَّا أَمَانِيَّ وَإِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَظُنُّونَ

Al-Baqarah 2:78

“And among them are ummiyyun who do not know the Book, only imagined notions; they do nothing but conjecture.”

The opening words, “among them,” matter. These people belong to a community in which divine Scripture is already present. We therefore cannot conclude from this verse that everyone described as ummi must belong to a society with no revealed book.

The verse also states what is missing: knowledge of the Book. It does not explicitly say that these people cannot recognize written letters or write words. Someone may be literate and still know little about Scripture. They may even have read it repeatedly or memorized passages without learning to distinguish what the text says from what others have told them it says.

The word amani (wishes, imagined notions, or wishful expectations) helps explain their condition. It belongs to the Arabic root family M N Y. In Al-Baqarah 2:111, groups claim exclusive entitlement to Paradise; the Qur’an calls these claims their amani and demands evidence. An-Nisa’ 4:123 likewise rejects the idea that a person’s fate is determined by their own wishes or those of the People of the Book. These uses support “imagined notions” in Al-Baqarah 2:78, especially since the verse ends by saying that the people are merely conjecturing.

Classical Arabic also records a reading or recitation sense within this word family. This gives another possible interpretation of amani in Al-Baqarah 2:78: people whose acquaintance with the Book extends only to reciting or repeating its words. This possibility should not be discarded merely because it is less familiar, although the Qur’anic parallels for wishes and imagined notions are clearer. The root study examines both readings.

Under either interpretation, reading and knowing remain different. Someone may recite the Qur’an beautifully, complete it many times, and memorize numerous verses, yet respond to questions about their meaning by repeating inherited explanations without examining the text. The difficulty is not necessarily an inability to read. It is a failure to distinguish knowledge from assumption.

That failure becomes more consequential when others speak in the Book’s name. Al-Baqarah 2:79 criticizes people who produce a text with their own hands and then claim that it comes from God in order to obtain a benefit. Its placement beside the description of people lacking scriptural knowledge exposes a serious danger: readers unfamiliar with the Book may mistake someone else’s writings and claims for God’s words. The verse does not explicitly say that every person described in the preceding verse was a victim of those writers. The connection is an inference from the context, not a documented historical event that can be assigned to every individual.

Taken together, Al-Baqarah 2:75, Al-Baqarah 2:76, Al-Baqarah 2:77, Al-Baqarah 2:78, and Al-Baqarah 2:79 expose two different problems: deliberate distortion by those who know, and reliance on conjecture by those who do not. Both can occur within a community that possesses Scripture, but the people involved do not have the same knowledge or responsibility.

Those given Scripture and those outside its inherited traditions

Al ‘Imran 3:20 uses ummiyyin in a different arrangement. Whereas Al-Baqarah 2:78 describes people within a scriptural community, this verse addresses them alongside communities that have been given the Book.

فَإِنْ حَاجُّوكَ فَقُلْ أَسْلَمْتُ وَجْهِيَ وَمَنِ اتَّبَعَنِ وَقُلْ لِلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ وَالْأُمِّيِّينَ أَأَسْلَمْتُمْ فَإِنْ أَسْلَمُوا فَقَدِ اهْتَدَوْا وَإِنْ تَوَلَّوْا فَإِنَّمَا عَلَيْكَ الْبَلَاغُ بَصِيرٌ بِالْعِبَادِ

Al-Imran 3:20

“If they dispute with you, say: ‘I have submitted myself to The God, and so have those who follow me.’ Say to those who were given the Book and to the ummiyyin: ‘Have you submitted?’ If they submit, they have found guidance. If they turn away, your duty is only to convey the message. The God sees His servants.”

The invitation addresses two groups: those given the Book and the ummiyyin. There is no basis for simply replacing this distinction with “literate people” and “illiterate people.” A person whose community has not received Scripture may know how to read and write. A person who belongs to a scriptural community may lack knowledge of its Book.

Two expressions also deserve attention: ahl al-kitab (People of the Book, communities associated with revealed Scripture) and “those who were given the Book.” The first identifies a community through its relationship with Scripture and its religious tradition. The second emphasizes receiving Scripture. Neither implies that every member has studied it or acquired specialist knowledge. Al-Baqarah 2:101 describes some of those given Scripture casting God’s Book behind their backs as though they did not know. Al ‘Imran 3:19 describes division arising, through wrongful rivalry, after knowledge had come. Possessing the Book, acquiring knowledge, and responding to that knowledge are distinct matters.

Other passages help clarify the historical difference between communities in Al ‘Imran 3:20.

وَمَا آتَيْنَاهُمْ مِنْ كُتُبٍ يَدْرُسُونَهَا وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهِمْ قَبْلَكَ مِنْ نَذِيرٍ

Saba 34:44

“We had not given them books to study, nor had We sent them a warner before you.”

أَنْ تَقُولُوا إِنَّمَا أُنْزِلَ الْكِتَابُ عَلَىٰ طَائِفَتَيْنِ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا وَإِنْ كُنَّا عَنْ دِرَاسَتِهِمْ لَغَافِلِينَ

Al-An'am 6:156

“Lest you say: ‘The Book was sent down only to two communities before us, and we were unaware of their study.’”

These passages distinguish earlier recipients of Scripture from a community that had not itself received such a book. They do not imply that no books existed around the latter community, or that none of its members could read. Earlier Scriptures were available to others, but the people addressed had not themselves received that revelation and had remained unfamiliar with its study.

A strong reading of ummiyyin in Al ‘Imran 3:20 is therefore people outside the communities that had received earlier Scriptures. This also fits Al-Jumu‘ah 62:2, where a Messenger arises among them to teach the Book that is now reaching them.

Does this conflict with Al-Baqarah 2:78? Not necessarily. There, some members of a scriptural community lack genuine knowledge of the Book and receive the same designation. The term may refer in one setting to a community’s historical relationship with Scripture and in another to individuals who lack knowledge despite Scripture’s presence. Al-Baqarah 2:78 may even carry a rebuke: people who ought to know the Book, given their community’s inheritance, remain as unfamiliar with it as if it had never reached them.

Al ‘Imran 3:75 offers another instance of this social dimension.

وَمِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِقِنْطَارٍ يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِدِينَارٍ لَا يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ إِلَّا مَا دُمْتَ عَلَيْهِ قَائِمًا ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا لَيْسَ عَلَيْنَا فِي الْأُمِّيِّينَ سَبِيلٌ وَيَقُولُونَ عَلَى الْكَذِبَ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

Al-Imran 3:75

“Among the People of the Book is someone who, if you entrust him with a great amount of wealth, will return it to you. Among them is another who, if you entrust him with a single dinar, will not return it unless you keep standing over him. That is because they say: ‘We are not accountable concerning the ummiyyin.’ They knowingly attribute a lie to The God.”

The verse first distinguishes individual conduct within the People of the Book. Some return even a substantial sum faithfully. Others withhold a single dinar—a coin—unless its owner keeps demanding it. The latter justify their behavior by claiming that they owe no accountability toward the ummiyyin.

This claim fits a social reading of the term. They may have viewed the ummiyyin as outsiders to their religious community and denied their financial rights the protection they afforded fellow members. Historical Jewish texts contain examples of different financial rules for members of the community and foreigners. That comparison must not be expanded into a claim that those texts universally permit betrayal of a trust. Debt collection, interest on loans, and wrongful appropriation of entrusted property are different issues.

Another possibility is that people with scriptural learning were exploiting those without it. This can be compared with the setting of Al-Baqarah 2:78 and Al-Baqarah 2:79. However, Al ‘Imran 3:75 does not explicitly identify the ummiyyin as uninformed members of the same religious community. That possibility cannot be presented as the verse’s certain meaning.

What is explicit is the misconduct of some People of the Book toward the ummiyyin and their attribution of its justification to God. The Qur’an calls this a knowing falsehood. Once again, the subject is not whether the ummiyyin can read or write. It is the status others assign to them and the rights those people seek to disregard.

Scriptural learning and the people who teach it

So far, we have considered communities that received Scripture and people who lacked knowledge of it. Within scriptural communities, some people also devoted their lives to studying, teaching, and safeguarding the Book. Could ummi sometimes be understood in relation to those specialists?

To explore that question, we must distinguish rabbaniyyun (people devoted to the Lord and to teaching Scripture), ahbar (religious scholars), ruhban (monks), and hawariyyun (Jesus’ devoted disciples). These titles do not mean the same thing in the Qur’an. They cannot all be treated as synonyms for a religious scholar or specialist in Scripture.

Al ‘Imran 3:79 is particularly clear about studying and teaching the Book.

مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُؤْتِيَهُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ ثُمَّ يَقُولَ لِلنَّاسِ كُونُوا عِبَادًا لِي مِنْ دُونِ وَلَٰكِنْ كُونُوا رَبَّانِيِّينَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تُعَلِّمُونَ الْكِتَابَ وَبِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَدْرُسُونَ

Al-Imran 3:79

“It is not for a human being to whom The God gives the Book, judgment, and prophethood to then tell people: ‘Be servants of mine instead of The God.’ Rather: ‘Be devoted to the Lord, through your teaching of the Book and through your study.’”

Here, the description rabbaniyyin connects devotion to the Lord with teaching and studying Scripture. It concerns people engaged in these activities, rather than merely belonging to a religious community. Al-Ma’idah 5:44 similarly places the rabbaniyyun and ahbar in relation to the Torah, safeguarding God’s Book, bearing witness to it, and judging responsibly. These titles illuminate the standing of learned people within scriptural communities. Their word families are examined in the studies of R B B and H B R.

This suggests another way to examine Muhammad’s description as ummi. Before revelation, he did not occupy the position of a scholar trained within the traditions of the Torah and Gospel. The Qur’an also speaks of his lack of prior knowledge of the Book. His description can therefore be considered in relation to an absence of specialist training in earlier Scriptures.

A conceptual comparison, however, is different from an explicit contrast in the text. Al ‘Imran 3:20 does not directly contrast the ummiyyin with the rabbaniyyun or ahbar. It places them alongside “those given the Book.” Lack of specialist training may be one condition associated with being ummi, but it cannot alone define every occurrence.

The other titles require the same care. Ruhban, monks, belongs to a root associated with awe or fear and describes a religious way of life. A monk may be learned in Scripture, but the title itself does not necessarily mean a scriptural specialist. The hawariyyun are Jesus’ devoted followers who declare themselves ready to support God’s cause. Their title concerns commitment, faith, and assistance more directly than an academic rank.

For identifying people engaged in studying and teaching Scripture, rabbaniyyun and ahbar are therefore the more direct examples. Yet their knowledge does not place them beyond error or moral responsibility. Al-Ma’idah 5:63 criticizes them for failing to restrain sinful speech and wrongful consumption of wealth. At-Tawbah 9:31 criticizes treating scholars and monks as lords. Scriptural knowledge must not become an independent human authority set against God.

A learned person may understand the Book and still act against that knowledge when interests or prejudices are threatened. That person differs from someone who has not yet learned. One may recognize the truth and deliberately reject it; the other still needs teaching. The Qur’an distinguishes these situations when assessing human conduct.

Muhammad: from among the ummiyyin to a teacher of the Book

We now reach the best-known use of ummi. In Al-A‘raf 7:157 and Al-A‘raf 7:158, Muhammad is described as al-nabi al-ummi (the Prophet described as ummi). The first verse identifies him as the Prophet whose description the recipients of the Torah and Gospel find in their Scriptures. The next says that this Prophet believes in God and His words and calls people to follow him.

If ummi meant permanent inability to understand Scripture, how could the title still describe someone who now receives revelation and teaches the Book? This question concerns knowledge of Scripture; teaching it does not, by itself, establish an ability to write. Al-Jumu‘ah helps explain the relationship between Muhammad’s background and his mission.

هُوَ الَّذِي بَعَثَ فِي الْأُمِّيِّينَ رَسُولًا مِنْهُمْ يَتْلُو عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتِهِ وَيُزَكِّيهِمْ وَيُعَلِّمُهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحِكْمَةَ وَإِنْ كَانُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ لَفِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ

Al-Jumu'ah 62:2

“He is the One who raised among the ummiyyin a Messenger from among themselves, reciting His verses to them, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom, although before that they were clearly astray.”

Muhammad comes from among the same people he is commissioned to teach. The verse shows that an earlier lack of scriptural knowledge does not prevent later learning. A community without its own earlier revealed book now receives teaching through the Qur’an. The Messenger arises within that community and becomes the recipient and teacher of revelation.

His origin among the ummiyyin therefore does not conflict with the knowledge and responsibilities he acquires through revelation. A person’s background does not cease to be their background when they learn something new. The title can accordingly identify Muhammad’s relationship to the community and circumstances from which he emerged.

The Qur’an also addresses his knowledge before revelation.

وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ رُوحًا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا مَا كُنْتَ تَدْرِي مَا الْكِتَابُ وَلَا الْإِيمَانُ وَلَٰكِنْ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُورًا نَهْدِي بِهِ مَنْ نَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا وَإِنَّكَ لَتَهْدِي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

Ash-Shura 42:52

“In this way We revealed to you a spirit from Our command. You did not know what the Book was, nor faith; but We made it a light by which We guide whomever We will among Our servants. You indeed guide to a straight path.”

The statement concerns knowledge brought by revelation that Muhammad did not previously possess. This is not the same as an inability to recognize letters, read a written text, or learn to write. The subject is the Book and faith made known through revelation, rather than a report on all his educational abilities.

There is a meaningful connection with Al-Baqarah 2:78. There, some ummiyyun “do not know the Book.” Here, Muhammad did not previously know what the Book and faith were. Both passages concern an absence of scriptural knowledge, but revelation changes Muhammad’s situation. An-Nisa’ 4:113 also speaks of God teaching him what he had not known.

Before revelation, Muhammad did not possess knowledge of the Book he would receive. Through revelation, he acquired that knowledge and was commissioned to teach it. His description as ummi can thus be understood through his background and relationship to earlier scriptural traditions, rather than permanent exclusion from knowledge.

Does the Qur’an establish that Muhammad remained unable to read and write?

Al-‘Ankabut 29:48 is the passage most often cited to establish Muhammad’s illiteracy.

وَمَا كُنْتَ تَتْلُو مِنْ قَبْلِهِ مِنْ كِتَابٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ إِذًا لَارْتَابَ الْمُبْطِلُونَ

Al-'Ankabut 29:48

“Before it, you did not recite any book, nor write it with your right hand. Otherwise, those intent on falsehood would have had cause for doubt.”

“Before it” is crucial. The verse addresses Muhammad’s situation before the Book whose revelation is discussed in the surrounding passage. He had not previously recited a book or written it with his own hand. A prior practice of that kind could have given opponents grounds for alleging that the Qur’an had been copied from earlier writings.

The denial of a past practice does not establish a lifelong inability. Saying that someone had not previously recited or written a book does not tell us that he could never learn to read and write afterward. Equally, the possibility of learning does not prove that he actually acquired those skills. These are separate questions.

Al-Furqan 25:5 reports opponents alleging that Muhammad’s message consisted of ancient tales he had written down or arranged to have written down, which were dictated to him morning and evening. The accusation itself establishes neither that he personally wrote the material nor that he was unable to write. The following verse answers by identifying the divine source of revelation: it was sent down by the One who knows the secrets of the heavens and earth. In Al-An‘am 6:105 and An-Nahl 16:103, responses to allegations of learning from others likewise concern revelation, instruction, and language.

The absence of an appeal to Muhammad’s illiteracy in those replies does not prove his literacy. But neither can Al-‘Ankabut 29:48, describing the absence of earlier recitation and writing, simply be expanded into a statement that he could never read or write throughout his life.

Reading, writing, and learning are themselves valued in the Qur’an. Al-‘Alaq 96:1, Al-‘Alaq 96:2, Al-‘Alaq 96:3, Al-‘Alaq 96:4, and Al-‘Alaq 96:5 open with a command to read or recite and speak of teaching through the pen. Al-Qalam 68:1 begins with an oath by the pen and what people write. Al-Baqarah 2:282 gives detailed instructions for recording debts and obligations, assigning responsibilities to the writer, debtor, and witnesses.

Muhammad was commissioned to convey and act on this Qur’an. Its emphasis on learning, reasoning, reflection, and responsible recordkeeping gives no basis for claiming that he was barred from learning literacy. These are learnable skills, and no verse declares him permanently incapable of acquiring them. This observation concerns the possibility of learning; it is not historical proof that he learned to write.

The distinction between being able to learn and having demonstrably done so must remain clear. Muhammad may have used writers for letters, agreements, and other documents. Employing a writer does not itself prove illiteracy, just as teaching the Book does not prove that he personally wrote every document. Al-Baqarah 2:282 itself recognizes having a document written by another person.

The conclusion supported by these passages is precise and limited. The Qur’an states that Muhammad had no prior practice of reciting and writing a book and lacked the scriptural knowledge that revelation would bring. It does not explicitly settle his ability to read and write at the end of his life. Neither lifelong illiteracy nor his personal acquisition of writing can be presented as a historical fact conclusively established by these verses alone.

One finding nevertheless matters: Muhammad’s description as ummi does not mean an unchangeable inability to learn. He arose among the ummiyyin and, through revelation, became a teacher of the Book and wisdom.

Nor does the Qur’an make its authority depend on proving that the Prophet permanently lacked a particular skill. An-Nisa’ 4:82 invites reflection and examination of the text. Its origin and truth should be considered through its own evidence, rather than making an inability that it does not explicitly establish a necessary condition of its divine character.

Access to a book is not knowledge of its contents

The distinction between possessing a book and understanding it is not confined to religious history. It appears in ordinary life whenever access to information is mistaken for knowledge.

Imagine someone spending a few hours searching online, reading medical articles, and watching videos about disease. They then feel qualified to advise patients: “Avoid vaccination; all vaccines are harmful,” or “Masks are useless in every situation.” They may have read genuine scientific articles and learned terms such as virus, immunity, antibody, and clinical trial. But familiarity with vocabulary and scattered reading do not replace an understanding of research methods, experimental results, statistics, and the body of evidence as a whole.

The problem is not simply a shortage of information. It is a failure to recognize the limits of one’s knowledge while making categorical claims about matters requiring careful study. A person may have read material without acquiring the knowledge needed to support their conclusions.

Consider also someone confidently asserting that the earth is flat. Libraries contain works on physics, mathematics, geometry, and astronomy, while methods of observation and measurement are available for examination. Yet a person may collect technical vocabulary online and dismiss scientific calculations without learning the foundations needed to understand them. They may even argue about complex theories whose mathematical basis they cannot explain.

Someone who has spent years studying physics and mathematics, performing experiments and calculations, exposing findings to criticism, and defending a research argument has followed a different path to knowledge. The difference is not merely a university credential. It lies in how they learn, assess evidence, explain their reasoning, and correct mistakes. A doctorate does not make anyone infallible, and a lack of formal education does not make someone incapable of thought. A farmer with years of experience and study in a subject may know more about it than someone who has merely memorized a few technical terms.

These examples are not lexical definitions of ummi. They illustrate the distinction Al-Baqarah 2:78 draws between knowledge of the Book and reliance on imagined notions and conjecture. A book may be available while its reader trusts assumptions about it instead of learning what it contains.

The same danger exists in religious life. Someone may read the Qur’an daily, memorize large portions, and know dozens of religious teachers’ explanations. Yet when a verse conflicts with an inherited belief, they may immediately appeal to those explanations instead of examining the verse and its relationship with other passages. They are not necessarily unable to read. The question is whether they will test their interpretation against the Book or require the Book to conform to their inherited interpretation.

Al-Baqarah 2:170 criticizes following ancestors without reason when their example is set against what God has revealed. The study Revelation or Inherited Tradition? explores this issue. Learning from earlier generations is not inherently wrong; much human knowledge comes from others. The difficulty begins when another person’s statement becomes an unquestionable authority rather than a claim open to examination.

Even knowledge of the Book does not complete a person’s responsibility. Interests, power, or prejudice may lead someone to disregard a truth they recognize. Al-Jumu‘ah 62:5 compares those entrusted with the Torah who fail to carry its responsibility to an animal carrying books. The issue extends beyond ignorance to a failure to accept and fulfill what the Book requires.

A person who lacks knowledge and a person who knowingly acts against it are therefore in different positions. Al-Baqarah 2:75 and Al-Baqarah 2:78 already made this distinction: one group distorts after understanding, while another relies on imagined notions and conjecture. Al ‘Imran 3:19 also describes division driven by wrongful rivalry after knowledge has arrived.

Initial ignorance is not itself a sin. An-Nahl 16:78 reminds us that human beings emerge from their mothers’ wombs knowing nothing and are given the means of learning. Everyone begins without knowledge and gradually learns. But responsibility changes when a person encounters the Book, instruction, and clear evidence. Someone who has not had an opportunity to understand differs from someone who sees a route to knowledge yet insists on their assumptions.

Al-Isra’ 17:36 forbids following what one has no knowledge of. Al-Baqarah 2:111 demands evidence from those making claims, and Az-Zumar 39:9 distinguishes those who know from those who do not. This invitation to learning belongs to no exclusive social or religious class. Nor should ummi become a contemptuous label for people with limited literacy or formal schooling. Not knowing a subject does not deprive anyone of dignity. What matters is how they respond to their lack of knowledge and whether they are willing to learn. The related principle of valuing knowledge and recognizing its limits develops this responsibility.

What the passages establish

The Qur’anic uses of ummi cannot be adequately understood by applying “illiterate” mechanically to every occurrence. A central thread is the relationship of a person or community to revealed Scripture and knowledge of it.

Al-Baqarah 2:78 describes people within a scriptural community who lack knowledge of the Book and rely on imagined notions and conjecture. Al ‘Imran 3:20 and Al ‘Imran 3:75 place the ummiyyin in relation to communities that received earlier Scriptures, bringing the term’s social and historical dimension into view. Al-Jumu‘ah 62:2 describes a Messenger raised among them to teach the Book and wisdom. In Al-A‘raf 7:157 and Al-A‘raf 7:158, Muhammad remains the Prophet described as ummi even while receiving revelation and teaching it.

Together, these passages support a reading concerned sometimes with the absence of an earlier scriptural inheritance and sometimes with an actual lack of knowledge of a Book already present in one’s community. A lack of specialist training may also be relevant, but it should not be made the exclusive definition of every occurrence without evidence.

For Muhammad, Ash-Shura 42:52 states that he lacked prior knowledge of the Book and faith. Al-‘Ankabut 29:48 describes the absence of earlier recitation and writing of a book. Neither establishes a lifelong inability to learn literacy. The Qur’an presents him as arising among the ummiyyin and becoming, through revelation, a teacher of the Book and wisdom.

The significance of this study extends beyond defining a word or questioning a familiar account of Muhammad. Al-Baqarah 2:78 confronts every reader with a question: does having Scripture mean knowing it? And once we learn something from it, are we willing to measure our beliefs and conduct against that knowledge?

A book may be in the home, in a library, or available to everyone. Its name may be repeated daily while its contents remain unfamiliar. Conversely, someone with no background in scriptural learning may encounter teaching and clear evidence, recognize the truth, and act on it.

Having the Book is not knowing the Book. Knowing the truth does not discharge our responsibility unless we are prepared to follow it.

The study returns, finally, to the question in Al ‘Imran 3:20: “Have you submitted?”

It addresses both those already given Scripture and the ummiyyin now receiving the divine message. A book’s value is not realized merely by possessing, reading, or memorizing it. It must be understood and reflected upon, and what one recognizes as true must be met with responsible action.
