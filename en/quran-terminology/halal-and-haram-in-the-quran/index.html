1. Introduction

People decline particular foods, avoid certain clothes, or dislike particular practices. There is nothing objectionable about making such personal choices: health, taste, experience, affordability, upbringing, and caution can all inform them. The problem begins when someone crosses the boundary between preference and religious authority, claiming that God has forbidden a food or practice to everyone and then invoking religion and piety to compel others to follow that personal judgment. The issue has then moved beyond an individual decision about eating or dress. Someone has assumed the authority to issue a religious ruling in God’s name.

The first question should therefore be more fundamental than whether the food or behavior seems agreeable: who authorized you to declare it halal (permitted under divine law) or haram (forbidden under divine law) in God’s name? The Qur’an poses precisely this question: “Has God given you permission, or are you inventing claims about God?” (Yunus 10:59). The following verse warns about the outcome of fabricating claims against Him (Yunus 10:60). Attributing a ruling to the Creator is no ordinary expression of opinion. It is a substantial claim requiring clear divine evidence and authorization.

Ash-Shura 42:21 takes the question further: “Or do they have partners who have prescribed for them as religion what God has not permitted?” The passage concerns authorities instituting religion without the Lord’s permission. The issue reaches beyond a mistaken food choice or disagreement over conduct into shirk (assigning partners to God) in religious lawmaking. Someone who imposes a personal ruling as a divine command has done more than lengthen a list of prohibitions: the source of religious authority itself has been altered.

At-Tawbah 9:31 describes people taking their religious scholars and monks as “lords besides God.” The point is not to deny the value of learning or guidance. It confronts us with a boundary: when does learning from a scholar become handing over unrestricted authority over religion and conscience? If a religious leader’s statement becomes the final standard of permission and prohibition without requiring divine evidence, what remains of tawhid (affirming God’s oneness and exclusive divine authority)? Reading this passage alongside Ash-Shura 42:21 is essential. “Assigning partners in religious legislation” is an analytical description of the problem. The case must rest on the verses, rather than on the rhetorical appeal of the description.

This danger belongs to no single school or period. A community can become so accustomed to fatwas (scholars’ opinions on religious law), transmitted sayings, inherited practices, and sectarian boundaries that it stops seeking God’s actual commands in His Book. People may make practices obligatory or forbidden for themselves and others in the name of caution, piety, or devotion to the Prophet without establishing a sound connection to revelation. Excessive human prohibitions can also provoke the opposite reaction: dismissing God’s explicit commands along with the invented ones. Both errors begin with losing the distinction between God’s speech and human wishes.

The Qur’an gives concrete examples involving food and livestock. Al-Anam describes people allocating shares to God and to idols, inventing rules for crops and animals, and distinguishing permitted from forbidden animals by sex without evidence (Al-An'am 6:136, Al-An'am 6:137, Al-An'am 6:138, Al-An'am 6:139, Al-An'am 6:140, Al-An'am 6:141, Al-An'am 6:142, Al-An'am 6:143, Al-An'am 6:144). Against such claims it demands witnesses who can testify that God actually forbade these things (Al-An'am 6:150). These passages are more than an account of an ancient community. They provide a way to examine any society in which habit, interest, and inherited belief take on the appearance of divine commands. The striking feature of the Qur’an’s response is its challenge to the authority behind a prohibition, rather than the addition of further restrictions.

This is where food enters the present inquiry. Is a food’s forbidden status the same as our dislike of it? If one culture eats an animal that another finds objectionable, can the second culture’s taste legislate religion for the first? Must we await a transmitted report before recognizing the danger of spoiled food? Does permission to eat something impose a duty to eat it? When we avoid a food, how can we prevent personal avoidance from becoming a supposed permanent command from God?

The study begins with these practical questions, but its aim is not to produce another food list. It seeks the boundaries of God’s commands and human authority: a religious understanding that neither permits what God has forbidden nor closes, through unauthorized rulings, what He has opened to His servants. At its deepest level, permission and prohibition concern God’s oneness, honesty in attributing speech to Him, justice toward other people, and personal responsibility.

2. The Fundamental Questions

Understanding the Qur’anic boundaries of permission and prohibition requires a set of questions that can guide the argument throughout:

First, who has the authority to make something part of religion in God’s name? If God has granted permission, how can it be identified, and who bears the burden of producing evidence?

Second, is turning a human opinion, ethnic custom, or historical report into binding divine law merely a methodological error, or can it involve the Qur’anic concerns about assigning partners to God and taking human beings as lords? How does criticizing a system of religious lawmaking differ from judging an individual’s faith?

Third, what is the Prophet’s place in legislation and the duty of obedience? Does obeying the Messenger who brings revelation mean accepting without question every later report attributed to him? How should the statement that he “makes wholesome things lawful for them and forbids corrupt things to them” (Al-A'raf 7:157), together with At-Tawbah 9:29, be read alongside divine permission and his responsibility to convey the message?

Fourth, how does the Qur’an express prohibition? Must the word “forbidden” appear, or can an explicit negative command or instruction to avoid something also impose a binding restriction? How do we distinguish those requirements from moral recommendations, individual preferences, and personal decisions?

Fifth, what do halal (permitted), tayyib (good, wholesome, or fitting in context), and khabith (corrupt, impure, or objectionable in context) mean? Do the latter two identify fixed lists of food species, or qualities relevant across many areas of life? How do these descriptions relate to a legal ruling?

Sixth, what boundaries does the Qur’an itself set for food? How do prohibitions concerning carrion, blood, pork, and animals dedicated to another beside God relate to passages on slaughter, hunting, mentioning God’s name, the food of scriptural communities, and food from the sea?

Seventh, what roles do reason, experience, medical knowledge, culture, geography, and affordability play in choosing food? Are human regulations intended to prevent health or social harm equivalent to attributing a permanent religious prohibition to God?

Eighth, how can the principle of ease in religion coexist with explicit prohibitions, personal responsibility, and necessity, without ease becoming disregard for all limits or piety becoming endless restriction?

Ninth, how should we answer practical challenges such as: “Would you personally eat dog or donkey meat?” “Does that make wine permissible?” “Is everything science finds harmful religiously forbidden?” and “If the Prophet prohibited corrupt things, why should reports about him not establish prohibitions?”

Slogans and short lists cannot answer these questions adequately. Each answer requires the verses, careful attention to words and context, and an argument whose steps can be examined.

3. Method

The Qur’an is the primary standard of this inquiry. To identify a religious ruling, we first ask what a passage says, whom it addresses, its context, the form of its command or prohibition, and any qualifications or exceptions. We then bring related passages together. A phrase cannot be detached from its setting and turned into an unlimited rule. Nor can a verse be ignored because it fails to fit our assumptions.

We must also distinguish what the Qur’an states directly from what we infer. A conclusion may emerge from several passages without being the Qur’an’s own verbatim expression. Linguistic explanation, historical evidence, and scientific findings can improve our understanding without becoming independent sources of divine legislation.

Four levels of knowledge are therefore distinguished: direct textual statement—what revelation explicitly says; Qur’anic inference—an analysis developed by reading passages together; linguistic and historical explanation—information that helps clarify the text; and contemporary application—applying a principle to present circumstances.

The distinction matters when identifying both a rule and its reason or scope. Al-Baqarah 2:173 includes pork among prohibited foods: that is a direct statement. Saying that a particular medical harm is the sole reason for the prohibition is a different claim requiring independent evidence. Likewise, identifying a food as contaminated or capable of causing illness is important grounds for avoiding it. That finding alone does not establish that God permanently prohibited the entire food category, by name, for everyone at every time.

The distinction between divine judgment and human assessment leads to tayyib (wholesome or fitting) and khabith (corrupt or unwholesome). Al-Baqarah 2:168 places lawful and wholesome together in relation to food. Al-A'raf 7:157 describes the Messenger making wholesome things lawful and forbidding corrupt things. Before drawing legal conclusions, we must understand these words through their varied Qur’anic uses, rather than start with a ready-made legal classification.

This method does not discard historical and linguistic knowledge. It identifies the place and evidential limits of each. External evidence can illuminate how words were used and what different communities believed. But a historical report cannot, merely by being famous or attributed to the Prophet, independently create divine law. Conversely, rejecting a report’s independent legislative authority does not permit distorting a verse, disregarding Arabic grammar, or ignoring contrary evidence.

This boundary rests on the Qur’an’s claims about its own textual authority. Al-An'am 6:114 speaks of the Book sent down with its contents set out distinctly: “He is the One who sent down to you the Book, explained in detail.” Al-An'am 6:119 states that God has detailed what He has forbidden. An-Nahl 16:89 describes the Book as “an explanation of all things,” while Al-Ma'idah 5:3 speaks of religion being completed.

These descriptions matter to the case for the sufficiency of revealed guidance. They should not be interpreted as claiming that the Qur’an is a laboratory manual, a complete inventory of animals, or a medical textbook. Comprehensive religious guidance differs from listing every empirical instance. Nevertheless, anyone proposing a new set of permanent religious prohibitions without evidence from revelation must answer these passages’ fundamental challenge concerning the source and authority of the ruling.

An explicit statement provides firmer evidence than an indirect inference. Yet the absence of an item’s name from a list does not justify an unrestricted conclusion without considering general rules, circumstances, and exceptions. Each conclusion must be identified as an explicit statement, a clear implication of several passages, or an inference requiring further support. This discipline helps us avoid both inventing prohibitions and dismissing Qur’anic rulings through incomplete arguments.

The result is intellectual transparency: readers can distinguish direct revelation, interpretation, linguistic or empirical knowledge, and questions that remain open to scholarly discussion.

4. Findings

People are not free to declare whatever they like permitted, and whatever they dislike forbidden, in the Lord’s name. In the Qur’an, attributing a ruling to God requires clear permission and evidence. Ash-Shura 42:21 challenges partners who prescribe religion without His authorization; Yunus 10:59 asks whether God has permitted the claim or people are inventing it; An-Nahl 16:116 warns against false declarations of permission and prohibition. These passages move the question beyond culinary traditions and differences in taste into God’s oneness and the responsibility of speaking in His name.

An unfamiliar food is not the only possible danger. A greater one is inventing a prohibition God did not establish, treating it as sacred, and demanding obedience. A community may appear rigorous about food while grounding its rigor in custom, power, prejudice, or uncertain reports rather than revelation. The opposite response—rejecting explicit divine prohibitions in reaction to invented restrictions—is equally mistaken. Fidelity to the Qur’an requires neither adding restrictions to demonstrate severity nor subtracting commands merely to make things easier.

The linguistic inquiry shows how widely tayyib (wholesome, good, or fitting) and khabith (corrupt, impure, or unfit) apply: to land and wind, property and speech, conduct and food. Their particular qualities depend on context. They cannot invariably be equated with “legally permitted” and “legally forbidden.” An-Nisa 4:160 describes wholesome things forbidden to a particular group under particular circumstances. Al-A'raf 7:157 establishes a real connection between corrupt things and the Prophet’s prohibiting role, but gives nobody permission to turn personal disgust or ethnic custom into heavenly law.

The food passages explicitly address carrion, blood, pork, dedication to other than God, the circumstances of an animal’s death, slaughter, hunting, food from scriptural communities, and the restrictions of ihram (the consecrated state associated with pilgrimage). They must be read as a connected body of guidance. Ignoring a passage to secure a preferred conclusion is precisely what a Qur’an-centered inquiry must avoid. Al-An'am 6:145 emphasizes revealed boundaries against invented prohibitions, while Al-Maidah develops the circumstances and actions involved. Personal preference authorizes neither additions nor omissions.

Reason, experience, and modern science are allies in this understanding. They help distinguish safe food from spoiled food, dangerous circumstances from safe ones, and responsible decisions from recklessness. God’s commands themselves recognize necessity and real human limitations. But scientific assessments and personal choices should not quietly replace revelation. “I do not eat this” does not require the additional claim that God forbade it. A warning about contamination need not be converted into an eternal religious law.

Responsibility at the table also extends beyond avoiding a few substances. Economic justice, people’s rights, resistance to waste, protection of the earth, and responsible treatment of living beings matter. A food may not belong to a prohibited species or substance yet have been acquired by violating someone’s rights. One part of Qur’anic guidance cannot excuse neglecting another. Distinguishing a food’s classification from the morality of acquiring and using it preserves legal precision while taking ethics beyond superficial labels.

The answer “Everything inherited religious law forbids is certain” does not resolve authority; it merely conceals the question behind earlier generations’ names. The opposite shortcut, ignoring every textual qualification, is equally inadequate. We must read the passages together: both “Say: I find no…” in Al-An'am 6:145 and “Do not eat…” in Al-An'am 6:121 deserve full weight. A sound argument depends on treating the evidence comprehensively and consistently.

The fundamental boundary is this: God holds religious authority, while human beings have the responsibility to understand and apply His guidance. They do not become unauthorized partners in its legislation. Scholars may teach, specialists identify risks, communities regulate affairs to protect public rights, and individuals exercise their choices. None becomes immune to examination by calling a personal judgment God’s certain command. Every claim of divine obligation or prohibition must answer: “Has God authorized this?” Where God has provided room, human fear, habit, and power must not close it against His servants.

A responsible affirmation of God’s oneness in legislation means respecting the boundaries He sets rather than inventing new ones in His name. It means receiving His provision with gratitude, moral care, reason, justice, and restraint from waste. Our taste is not other people’s god; neither are the opinions of admired leaders. Human judgments cannot replace God’s Book.

These findings are examined through textual evidence, context, and argument in the research that follows. Readers can assess their foundations in the next section and then consult the responses to fundamental questions and common objections.

Obedience to the Messenger must also be distinguished from obedience to reports attributed to him. The Qur’an commands the former. It does not thereby certify every collection compiled in later generations. “Obey the Messenger,” tabyin (making a message clear), and “the Book and wisdom” must first be understood through the verses themselves, alongside the clear Book, clear conveyance, and the wisdom explicitly identified in the Qur’an. Anyone extracting a new, permanent divine law from a report must establish its attribution, legislative authority, and scope. The following research develops that argument and answers the principal objections.

5. The Research

5.1. The Authority to Legislate: Divine Permission and the Burden of Proof

Ash-Shura 42:21 asks about “partners” who prescribe something as religion without God’s permission. Two features belong together: the subject is introducing a rule as part of religion, and the defect is the absence of divine authorization. Human decisions or regulations should therefore not be classified as partnership with God simply because people made them. Communities plan, contract, legislate for social affairs, and distribute administrative responsibilities. The verse addresses the point at which a human rule becomes binding as “God’s religion” without its divine authorization being established.

Yunus 10:59 makes the issue concrete. People divide God’s provision into permitted and forbidden categories, and the verse asks whether God authorized them or they are inventing claims against Him. Permission is central. “I dislike this food” reports a preference. “This food harms my health” makes an empirical claim about a body. “God has forbidden this food to everyone” claims knowledge of a divine command. All three statements may lead to abstaining, but their grounds, sources, and burdens of proof are quite different.

An-Nahl 16:116 warns against tongues falsely declaring, “This is lawful and this is forbidden,” thereby fabricating claims about God. Al-A'raf 7:33 places speaking about God without knowledge alongside major prohibited wrongs. A person may or may not know that a food is harmful under certain conditions. Even genuine scientific knowledge does not, without evidence from revelation, turn a finding into a permanent divine ruling.

The Qur’an raises this question even with the Prophet: “Why do you prohibit what God has made lawful for you?” (At-Tahrim 66:1). The surrounding passage concerns seeking his wives’ approval and a personal oath (At-Tahrim 66:2). It should not be represented as an occasion on which he instituted a new law for everyone. Nevertheless, the principle is significant: human wishes and approval, even involving a prophet, do not turn God’s permission into divine prohibition. Al-Ma'idah 5:87 reinforces the point by explicitly telling believers not to prohibit the wholesome things God has permitted them.

A ruling’s soundness is not measured by whether it seems lenient or strict. An apparently accommodating opinion may permit in God’s name something He forbids; an opinion designed to display rigor may prohibit something He did not prohibit. Both must produce evidence of divine authorization. This standard is just precisely because it makes no exception for our likes or dislikes. Preference and personal advantage justify neither inventing a prohibition nor overturning an established one.

5.2. From Unauthorized Legislation to Taking People as Lords

At-Tawbah 9:31 describes people taking their scholars and monks as lords besides God, before affirming the oneness of the deity to be worshipped. The expression should not be confined to the outward worship of statues. Equally, respect for learning and benefiting from a scholar’s knowledge should not automatically be called partnership with God. The question is where final religious authority resides.

Learning from a knowledgeable person, consulting a specialist, or hearing a jurist’s or researcher’s opinion does not inherently conflict with God’s oneness. The problem arises when a person’s or institution’s statement itself becomes religion’s final measure: people are expected to obey in the name of faith even when no divine evidence supports the ruling. Read together, At-Tawbah 9:31 and Ash-Shura 42:21 reveal the danger of handing divine authority to people. This is a textual inference, however strongly supported; it is not a claim that every feature of later legal systems is explicitly described in those two verses.

The Qur’an elsewhere connects obedience without sound grounds with assigning partners. Al-An'am 6:121 prohibits eating that over which God’s name has not been mentioned, warns of deceptive prompting, and says, “If you obey them, you will indeed be associators.” Although the immediate context is food, the danger concerns obedience to an authority drawing people away from God’s command. Its application must remain faithful to that context. Not every disagreement or administrative and social instruction falls under this warning.

For that reason, criticizing unauthorized religious legislation must not become an instrument of takfir (declaring an individual an unbeliever). Attributing an unauthorized ruling to God is grave. Whether a particular person knowingly conceals truth, misunderstands, reasons incorrectly, acts from fear, or trusts another is a separate question. An influential scholar with extensive resources is also differently placed from someone who inherited a saying without a genuine opportunity to investigate it. Clear criticism of structures of religious power needs no unsupported judgment about people’s inward faith.

Al-Ahzab 33:67 portrays people saying at judgment: “Our Lord, we obeyed our leaders and our great ones, and they led us astray from the path.” Blind following does not eliminate personal responsibility. Nobody can transfer the whole duty of thinking and assessing evidence to famous, powerful, or beloved figures. The question of divine authority therefore concerns both those issuing rulings and those following them without asking for grounds.

Other passages clarify the connection. Yusuf 12:40 declares that judgment belongs to God alone; Al-Kahf 18:26 says that He admits no partner in His judgment; Al-Imran 3:64 calls people not to take one another as lords besides God. Hukm (judgment, command, or authority, depending on context) does not always mean religious legislation in the narrow legal sense. These passages concern God’s oneness and sovereignty and must be read alongside the specific passages on invented prohibitions. At-Tawbah 9:37, for example, condemns shifting the sacred months so that something is permitted one year and forbidden another. Its immediate subject differs from the scholars and monks of At-Tawbah 9:31, but it offers a further example of manipulating a ruling through custom and expediency. This connected reading identifies the danger without forcing unstated meanings into individual verses.

5.3. Food Prohibitions and the Question of God’s Oneness

Al-An'am 6:136, Al-An'am 6:137, Al-An'am 6:138, Al-An'am 6:139, Al-An'am 6:140, Al-An'am 6:141, Al-An'am 6:142, Al-An'am 6:143, Al-An'am 6:144 concerns more than slaughter technique or food safety. People allocate shares to God and their supposed partners, invent prohibitions concerning crops and livestock, forbid riding certain animals, and impose religious distinctions involving animals’ offspring and sex. Invented divisions of God’s provision appear alongside sacred language and rituals dedicated to partners.

The Qur’an challenges this system’s evidence rather than offering another list of cultural preferences. Were the two males forbidden, the two females, or what the females’ wombs contain? (Al-An'am 6:143, Al-An'am 6:144). Where are the witnesses that God commanded this? (Al-An'am 6:150). The point is plain: creating a classification and making it socially familiar does not establish its divine origin.

Al-Ma'idah 5:103 names bahirah, saibah, wasilah, and ham (traditional categories of livestock set apart under customary religious restrictions). It explicitly denies that God instituted them and condemns inventing claims about Him. Al-Ma'idah 5:104 then describes people responding to an invitation to revelation by invoking their ancestors’ ways. The central problem is not merely the number of restrictions. Inherited practice has displaced revealed evidence.

Al-Baqarah 2:168, Al-Baqarah 2:169, Al-Baqarah 2:170, Al-Baqarah 2:171, Al-Baqarah 2:172, Al-Baqarah 2:173 develops the relationship in another sequence. All people are invited to eat what is lawful and wholesome from the earth. Warnings follow against deceptive steps and speaking about God without knowledge. The passage then addresses blind adherence to ancestors even when they neither understood nor followed guidance. Only then come specified food prohibitions and the allowance for necessity. Food cannot be separated from the source of religious authority in this context. It reveals whether people learn from God or prefer inherited human speech because breaking a habit frightens them.

An-Nahl 16:114, An-Nahl 16:115, An-Nahl 16:116, An-Nahl 16:117, An-Nahl 16:118 follows a comparable sequence: eat lawful, wholesome provision and be grateful; recognize the stated food prohibitions and necessity allowance; do not falsely declare things lawful and forbidden. Read in order, the passages ask both what we should eat and on what authority we speak about God’s judgment.

Al-Imran 3:93 marks another boundary. It states that food was lawful for the children of Israel before the Torah, except what Israel had forbidden himself, and then asks the claimants to bring and read the Torah. Al-Imran 3:94 condemns attributing falsehood to God. “Forbidden himself” does not establish that all personal abstention is sinful. Its significance is the distinction between an individual restriction and a claim about universal divine law. What a person sets aside for himself becomes binding religion for others only through divine evidence.

The demand for knowledge is explicit in Al-Anam 6:143: “Tell me with knowledge, if you are truthful.” After describing people justifying their partnership and prohibitions, Al-An'am 6:148 asks whether they possess knowledge they can produce, then criticizes conjecture and guesswork. An-Nahl 16:35 similarly describes an appeal to God’s will to justify worship practices and prohibitions. The Qur’an therefore supplies more than a list: within these very discussions it teaches readers to require knowledge from anyone claiming to state God’s law.

5.4. Obeying the Messenger, His Judgments, and Later Reports

Two truths must be held together. The Qur’an explicitly commands obedience to the Messenger and presents him as the bearer of revelation entrusted with guidance. An-Nisa 4:80 and An-Nur 24:54 cannot be ignored. But the Messenger is not identical to a collection of reports attributed to him. A command heard directly from him during his lifetime was a historical and religious reality. A report transmitted generations later is a historical claim whose reliability must be assessed. Similar language does not remove the distance in time and knowledge between an event and a later account.

Al-A'raf 7:157 says that the Prophet makes wholesome things lawful and prohibits corrupt things. At-Tawbah 9:29 refers to what God and His Messenger have forbidden. These statements must be taken seriously: the Qur’an does not minimize his role in communicating and implementing commands. In both, however, he acts as one commissioned by God, not as an independent authority legislating without Him. Neither cancels the standard of divine permission in Ash-Shura 42:21 or the demand for authorization in Yunus 10:59.

Even if the Prophet issued binding executive and judicial instructions for his community, two questions remain. How do we know that a report now transmitted reproduces his actual instruction? And, assuming accurate transmission, how do we know it established a permanent religious law for every generation rather than an administrative decision suited to a particular situation? Attribution and scope are distinct problems. Quoting a report alone resolves neither.

Al-Hashr 59:7 contains the well-known instruction to take what the Messenger gives and refrain from what he forbids, within a discussion of fay’ (property coming under the community’s control without the military expedition described in the preceding verse). The instruction cannot be emptied of its connection with his authority. But anyone deriving an unrestricted rule across every area of religious law must explain the move from that financial context to the proposed scope. They must still establish that a later collection reproduces what the Prophet gave or prohibited. The verse names no later hadith (reports attributing words or actions to the Prophet) collection and supplies no blanket certification of its contents.

An-Nahl 16:44 concerns making clear what has been revealed to people. Explaining a message is not the same as establishing an independent legislative authority alongside it. This does not deny the Prophet’s teaching and explanations during his life. It asks where the enduring standard is found and how an attribution is proved. Al-Jathiyah 45:18 tells him to follow the shariah (a prescribed way) on which God has placed him. That direction does not support a picture of the Prophet inventing a parallel religion without divine permission.

The proposed second source of legislation must first be established. The Qur’an does not, in Al-Hashr 59:7, Al-A'raf 7:157, or elsewhere, identify another book of prophetic rulings alongside itself as a universal authority for future generations. This is a specific, examinable claim about the text. It does not imply that the Prophet never explained, judged, or commanded. Another question remains even where a report is historically accurate: can a particular executive instruction become permanent law for all humanity? That additional step requires its own evidence.

The histories of Qur’anic transmission and hadith transmission require separate investigation, undertaken elsewhere on Forqan. For this inquiry, the essential point is that “the Prophet said” cannot by itself impose a new law without establishing both the attribution and the ruling’s scope and permanence. Disagreement about a report’s reliability should not become contempt for obeying the Messenger. Respect for him, equally, must not be extended into immunity for every narrator and later interpretation.

This becomes especially important in arguments for obligatory adherence to hadith collections. A verse about obedience, a word such as clarification or wisdom, or a reliable chain is sometimes made to establish that later collections contain God’s certain commands for everyone. Each step needs separate evidence. First, what does the Qur’an say about the Messenger’s position? Second, does it designate another authority for later generations? Third, what establishes each report’s attribution, content, and scope? Unless these remain separate, the revered expression sunnah (the Prophet’s way or example) can take the place of an argument.

The first objection begins with “Obey God and obey the Messenger,” and concludes that people must therefore obey the collections of al-Bukhari, Muslim, and others. The premise is Qur’anic; the conclusion is not stated in that command. An-Nisa 4:80 identifies obedience to the Messenger with obedience to God. Al-Anfal 8:20 warns its hearers not to turn away while listening. An-Nur 24:54 joins obedience with each party’s responsibility and the Messenger’s duty of clear conveyance. None identifies a narrator, a chain of transmission, or a collection compiled centuries later as equivalent to the Messenger. Directly hearing his message and treating every later attribution as certain are not self-evidently identical. Their equivalence must be proved, not assumed and then used as evidence.

Al-Ma'idah 5:92 and At-Taghabun 64:12 also place obedience alongside al-balag al-mubin (clear conveyance of the message). An-Nahl 16:35, An-Nahl 16:82, and Al-'Ankabut 29:18 connect the messengers’ responsibility with conveying it. Meanwhile, the Qur’an describes itself as a clear Book (Yusuf 12:1), a clear Qur’an (Al-Hijr 15:1), the Qur’an and a clear Book (An-Naml 27:1), and an explanation of all things within the scope of guidance (An-Nahl 16:89). These descriptions illuminate the Messenger’s role as bearer of God’s clear message. Repeating the adjective mubin (clear and clarifying) does not by itself prove that no oral explanation occurred during his lifetime. But replacing clear conveyance with later, conflicting collections of reports requires evidence the phrase itself does not provide. Forqan’s study of the clear message and its conveyance examines this network of passages.

An objection may invoke An-Nisa 4:59, An-Nisa 4:65, or An-Nisa 4:105: the Messenger did more than recite; he judged disputes, and believers had to accept his judgment. That correctly recalls his judicial and executive role. But An-Nisa 4:105 describes his judgment as according to what God showed him; Al-Ma'idah 5:48, Al-Ma'idah 5:49 commands judgment by what God revealed. His authority cannot be detached from divine authorization. Referring disputes to God and the Messenger in An-Nisa 4:59 also does not issue permanent approval to every later hadith collection. A binding judgment in his lifetime establishes neither the accuracy of every surviving account nor the permanence of every decision tied to a particular dispute.

The second objection invokes tabyin (making clear). Because An-Nahl 16:44 instructs Muhammad to make clear what was revealed to people, it is argued that all authenticated reports necessarily explain and complete the Book’s laws. Yet the object of clarification is precisely what was revealed to them. The verse relates clarification to revelation, not to creating a separate book beside it. An-Nahl 16:89 itself calls the revealed Book an explanation. The Messenger certainly made the message clear in teaching, answering, and judging. But that is not the same proposition as “every statement attributed to him in a later collection is a certain, binding explanation of the Qur’an.” Transmission, attribution, and permanent legislative force still require proof. See Obeying the Messenger and The Clear Message and Its Conveyance.

The third objection rests on “the Book and wisdom”: the Book must be the Qur’an, it argues, and hikmah (wisdom) must therefore be a separate body of hadith. But naming two concepts together does not establish two independent books. The Qur’an itself supplies evidence. Al-Isra 17:22, Al-Isra 17:23, Al-Isra 17:24, Al-Isra 17:25, Al-Isra 17:26, Al-Isra 17:27, Al-Isra 17:28, Al-Isra 17:29, Al-Isra 17:30, Al-Isra 17:31, Al-Isra 17:32, Al-Isra 17:33, Al-Isra 17:34, Al-Isra 17:35, Al-Isra 17:36, Al-Isra 17:37, Al-Isra 17:38 teaches God’s oneness, parents’ rights, giving, restraint from waste, the prohibition of killing, avoiding unlawful sexual relations, care for orphans’ property, keeping commitments, fair measures, and refusing to follow without knowledge. Al-Isra 17:39 immediately identifies these teachings as part of the wisdom revealed by the Lord. Wisdom is also given to Luqman in Luqman 31:12 and discussed more broadly in Al-Baqarah 2:269. None of these refers to Muslim hadith collections. Al-Baqarah 2:129, Al-Baqarah 2:151, and Al-Jumu'ah 62:2 place teaching the Book and wisdom together without supplying the name or credentials of a new legislative source. The direct connection between Al-Isra 17:39 and the preceding instructions already defeats the claim that wisdom must mean hadith outside the Qur’an.

The fourth objection again invokes Al-Hashr 59:7. Respect for the Messenger’s command must accompany attention to its setting. Al-Hashr 59:6, Al-Hashr 59:7, Al-Hashr 59:8, Al-Hashr 59:9, Al-Hashr 59:10 concerns property, its distribution, and preventing wealth from circulating only among the rich. Receiving what the Messenger gives and refraining from what he prohibits occurs within that financial order. Even if a wider principle of obedience is inferred, further questions remain: how is a particular later report shown to reproduce that command, and how is its universal, permanent scope established? A sound Qur’anic instruction does not automatically certify every report attributed to its Messenger.

The fifth objection uses “He does not speak from desire” (An-Najm 53:3) to classify every sentence the Prophet ever spoke as revelation, and then extends that status to later reports. The setting of An-Najm 53:1, An-Najm 53:2, An-Najm 53:3, An-Najm 53:4, An-Najm 53:5, An-Najm 53:6, An-Najm 53:7, An-Najm 53:8, An-Najm 53:9, An-Najm 53:10, An-Najm 53:11, An-Najm 53:12, An-Najm 53:13, An-Najm 53:14, An-Najm 53:15, An-Najm 53:16, An-Najm 53:17, An-Najm 53:18 concerns his truthfulness in receiving and conveying revelation and his revelatory experience. It does not guarantee every historian’s report. Yunus 10:15 tells him to say that he cannot alter the revelation on his own initiative and follows only what is revealed. Al-An'am 6:50 and Al-Ahqaf 46:9 likewise describe his following revelation. Al-Haqqah 69:44, Al-Haqqah 69:45, Al-Haqqah 69:46, Al-Haqqah 69:47 warns about attributing invented speech to God. These passages distinguish revelation from personal wishes and speech. The Qur’an also corrects or questions decisions involving the Messenger, including At-Tahrim 66:1, At-Tawbah 9:43, and Al-Anfal 8:67, Al-Anfal 8:68. If every human decision he made were indistinguishable from a revealed ruling, what would such correction mean? Respect requires accepting the Qur’an’s account of him rather than elevating every attributed statement to revelation in order to defend a report.

The sixth objection proposes that the Prophet may have received divine information beyond the recited Qur’an. Al-Baqarah 2:143 concerning the earlier prayer direction, At-Tahrim 66:3 concerning information about a private disclosure, and Al-Hashr 59:5 concerning permission for a particular action are invoked. These passages should be examined on their own terms; divine information beyond the recited text need not be declared impossible in advance. Even if established in a particular case, however, two further steps remain: was it universal legislation for every generation, and did God designate later collections as its preserved and binding authority? None names such a collection. The possibility of a particular disclosure does not establish the certainty of thousands of subsequent historical attributions.

The seventh objection invokes the Messenger as a good example in Al-Ahzab 33:21. He was an example for believers, but the verse does not establish how later reports are authenticated or make every personal habit a religious obligation. Human life includes cultural and ordinary choices: favorite foods, ways of cleaning teeth, clothing, and everyday routines. Even an accurate account requires asking whether the behavior was made obligatory for everyone or reflected a person’s circumstances. Exemplary conduct is not equivalent to legal obligation in every detail. The chapter’s setting, including steadfastness in hardship, must also be considered. Extracting one expression does not authorize innumerable new requirements.

The eighth objection assumes the Qur’an is insufficient to organize life: without an independent hadith source, how are prayer units, pilgrimage details, commerce, medicine, punishment, and countless other matters to be regulated? This first assumes an inherited system of rules, sectarian details, and administrative expectations, then judges the Qur’an against it. The inquiry should proceed in the opposite direction: what has God made obligatory, what principles has He given, and what has He left to responsible human knowledge and decision? Health and social organization can be regulated without calling every regulation an eternal divine law. Continuing ritual practice can be studied historically and collectively, but even widespread practice does not establish that every disputed detail or associated report was divinely imposed. Hajj (pilgrimage) in the Qur’an and the study of obedience distinguish foundational requirements from later particulars.

The ninth objection concerns transmission: the same people transmitted the Qur’an and hadith, so accepting one without the other is contradictory. Yet “people transmitted it” says nothing by itself about the character of transmission. The Qur’an circulated through public recitation, memorization, writing, and collective instruction. Hadith reports, particularly individual-chain reports, often passed through limited chains with variations in wording and meaning. Calling both transmission cannot erase differences in evidence. Anyone claiming equivalence must identify a particular hadith text transmitted with the same public structure and safeguards from the earliest community to its present form. Historical precision remains necessary: the absence of a surviving manuscript does not prove that no companion ever wrote anything, and conflicting reports permitting or prohibiting hadith writing themselves require assessment. The contrast concerns the Qur’an’s collective transmission and the complex, gradual compilation of report collections. See The Transmission of the Qur’an and Hadith.

Here the claim of tawatur (widespread, independent transmission across successive generations) arises. Such transmission must be demonstrated for each specific claim; repeating the label does not prove it. Even strong historical evidence for a practice leaves three questions distinct: did it occur in the Messenger’s time; did he institute it as God’s command for everyone; and must its same scope and details bind every later setting? Proving the first does not prove the others. Likewise, a sound ethical or theological statement in a report does not confer revelatory status or legislative power on the collection containing it. A report affirming God’s oneness may be true, but that truth does not certify neighboring statements or their attribution.

The tenth objection says hadith scholarship has separated sound reports from weak ones through analysis of narrators and chains, leaving no room for doubt. These methods can assist historical investigation. But sahih (classified as sound under a scholar’s authentication criteria) is not equivalent to absolute freedom from doubt. Judgments about memory, integrity, continuity, and the possibility of meetings rest on historical evidence. Disagreement over authentication, conflicting texts, and the need to prefer one account reveal the method’s limits. A truthful narrator can mishear, paraphrase, forget a qualification of time or place, or blend interpretation with the original words. “Sound according to this scholar” differs from “certainly spoken by the Messenger and binding on everyone.” A claim of divine prohibition must explain how that evidential gap has been crossed.

The eleventh objection concerns probability. The Qur’an accepts witnesses in Al-Baqarah 2:282, requires examination of an unreliable person’s report in Al-Hujurat 49:6, and calls a group to gain understanding and warn its people in At-Tawbah 9:122. People necessarily act on differing degrees of confidence in trade, judgment, medicine, and administration. Zann (supposition or an assessment of likelihood, depending on context) also does not invariably mean groundless guessing; Al-Hujurat 49:12 refers specifically to some suspicions. But the present question is whether disputed historical evidence can support the certain, universal claim “God has forbidden this.” In the context of invented prohibitions, Al-An'am 6:143 demands knowledge, and Al-An'am 6:148 asks claimants to produce it before criticizing conjecture. Yunus 10:36 says conjecture cannot replace truth, and Al-Isra 17:36 prohibits following without knowledge. Accepting testimony in a financial dispute does not automatically authorize turning any plausible report into God’s certain, permanent law. Evidence must match the nature and scale of the claim.

The twelfth objection invokes naskh (abrogation or replacement of a ruling) and takhsis (restricting the scope of a general statement). It argues that an authenticated report can narrow or displace a Qur’anic ruling because the Messenger best understood God’s intention. First, however, the report’s attribution must be established; then its status as a permanent divine command; finally, the reason its interpretation should change the explicit verse. God’s right to change His ruling does not automatically authorize narrators or jurists to render a verse ineffective. Al-Baqarah 2:106 and An-Nahl 16:101 address abrogation or replacement of a verse. They cannot simply be turned into permission for a report lacking an independently examinable revealed text to cancel the existing Qur’an. See Abrogation in the Qur’an.

The thirteenth objection says conflicts in hadith collections are merely apparent and scholars can reconcile them, so the collections enjoy a guarantee equivalent to revelation. An-Nisa 4:82 asks readers to reflect on the Qur’an and says that, were it from other than God, they would find much inconsistency in it. Its wording is “much inconsistency,” and its direct subject is the Qur’an. Nevertheless, it makes textual coherence relevant to a claim of divine origin. If a collection is claimed to contain only error-free revelation, even one demonstrated, genuine contradiction with a clear Qur’anic command defeats that exceptionless claim. This does not prove every historical report in it fabricated. It means the collection cannot rely on its name to claim guaranteed revelation and independent legislative authority. Conversely, finding no contradiction would not alone establish divine authorization. Coherence and authorization are separate tests.

Are reports then rejected merely because they conflict with our reading of the Qur’an? No. Our reading must first be examined through language, context, related passages, and the possibility of our own mistake. An apparent conflict must not be called conclusive. But if a contradiction with a clear Qur’anic command remains after that examination, reverence for a transmitter’s name cannot override revelation. A serious claim of contradiction requires careful testing, neither automatic submission to a collection nor hasty rejection without reading.

Rajm (stoning to death) as a punishment for unlawful sexual intercourse is a significant test. Its defenders cite authenticated reports prescribing it for a muhsan (a person with a protected legal status, commonly understood here to require a qualifying marriage), while restricting An-Nur 24:2 to others. Some invoke a supposed stoning verse whose recitation was removed while its ruling remained. The Qur’an itself must come first. An-Nur 24:1 announces a chapter sent down and made obligatory, containing clear verses. An-Nur 24:2 then prescribes one hundred lashes for the woman and man guilty of unlawful sexual intercourse, without stating an unmarried-only qualification. An-Nur 24:6, An-Nur 24:7, An-Nur 24:8, An-Nur 24:9 addresses a husband’s accusation against his wife; An-Nur 24:8 speaks of averting “the punishment” from her. In the chapter’s immediate context, that refers back to the stated punishment; stoning cannot be substituted from the verse itself. An-Nisa 4:25 describes a group of women receiving half the punishment applicable to protected women. A numerical punishment can be halved; death cannot. The meanings of protection and the verses’ addressees require careful examination, but this network places a substantial burden on the claim for stoning.

An-Nisa 4:15, An-Nisa 4:16 also discusses punishment and a way God will provide. Adding a stoning report in place of the written Qur’anic ruling, without proving its revealed authority, does not resolve these passages. “Removal of recitation while retaining the ruling” raises a larger question: why should a lethal punishment in God’s name depend on a verse absent from the preserved, readable Qur’an? This does not deny God’s authority to legislate. It asks what divine evidence a human claimant possesses. The Qur’an records threats of stoning made by some peoples against messengers, but does not command stoning as the punishment for unlawful sexual intercourse. The relationships among An-Nur 24:1, An-Nur 24:2, An-Nur 24:3, An-Nur 24:4, An-Nur 24:5, An-Nur 24:6, An-Nur 24:7, An-Nur 24:8, An-Nur 24:9, An-Nisa 4:15, An-Nisa 4:16, An-Nisa 4:25, the protection of life, and the alleged missing verse are examined in Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Rule Derived from Reports?.

Stoning is therefore more than a peripheral dispute over one report. It tests whether written revelation remains the standard or is restricted by a ruling dependent on historical transmission for its origin and scope. If a report governs the Qur’an, that report becomes the final measure. Anyone defending the exceptionless certainty of an entire collection must answer this unresolved test. The greater a ruling’s consequences, especially where human life is at stake, the greater the responsibility to substantiate it.

The fourteenth objection says that accepting some reports consistent with the Qur’an, reason, or ethics concedes the authority of all hadith. This confuses the truth of a statement with its origin and authority. A historical book may contain sound advice, and an attributed saying about justice may agree with the Qur’an. Such statements can be examined and recognized as sound without proving their historical attribution or authorizing the collection to create new divine law. God’s oneness does not acquire its authority from a report simply because it appears there. The truth of one proposition guarantees neither every proposition in the book nor the book’s revelatory character.

The fifteenth objection says using dictionaries, pre-Islamic poetry, and historical reports to understand Qur’anic Arabic contradicts rejecting hadith authority. This confuses different functions of evidence. Early Arabic texts illuminate word meanings, grammatical structures, and context. Consulting poetry or texts outside Islamic tradition does not hand legislative authority to a poet or lexicographer. These are aids to understanding, and their texts, dates, and attributions also need scrutiny. Using a historical report to create a permanent prohibition or cancel a Qur’anic command crosses from interpretation into a claim of legislative authority. Linguistics and the study of reports can both use historical evidence without assigning it the same function. We understand the Qur’an through the language of its time; we do not replace it with that language’s other literature.

The critique here is not a war against history or human knowledge. Hadith collections can be studied as historical works. But making a report God’s certain command entails three distinct burdens: establishing attribution, establishing a binding divine command in its content, and establishing its application to present addressees and circumstances. No verse about obedience, clarification, wisdom, or example alone undertakes those three tasks for a particular collection. God’s Book remains the measure. Historical works and scholarly understandings carry the weight their evidence supports. See Forqan’s collections on The Completeness of the Qur’an and Hadith Critique.

5.5. How Do We Know a Ruling Is Actually God’s?

“I consider this wrong” and “God has forbidden this to everyone” make fundamentally different claims. The first can rest on experience, prudence, ethical reasoning, or professional assessment. The second claims knowledge of the Lord’s command and the authority to impose a religious obligation on other people’s conscience and conduct. “Our great scholars said so,” “It has always been this way,” and “Nobody in our community does that” establish a tradition or reputation. They do not establish God’s permission.

Al-An'am 6:150 states the demand plainly: bring the witnesses who testify that God prohibited this. The verse does not reject learning from others’ experience or knowledge. It establishes divine authority as the issue: testimony attributing a command to God must rest on divine evidence. Numbers, antiquity, and fear of social disapproval do not prove a prohibition.

At least four successive questions are needed. First, textual basis: does a reliable, explicit statement of revelation support the claim? Second, meaning: what do its words, sentence structure, and context establish? Third, scope: is it individual, tied to conditions such as pilgrimage consecration, or general? Fourth, application: does the present case genuinely involve the subject and conditions addressed by the passage?

For example, Al-Ma'idah 5:96 prohibits land hunting during pilgrimage consecration. That temporary restriction does not make the animal inherently forbidden to everyone in every situation. Conversely, permission to hunt outside that state does not cancel the restriction within it.

This sequence also prevents a sound ethical or health concern from being hastily relabeled as a permanent divine ruling. Cruelty to an animal, polluting water, or selling spoiled food can violate rights and cause harm. These wrongs should be explained through their actual ethical and rational grounds; we need not pretend the animal, substance, or process is named in revelation’s food list. A human action may be sinful while the substance itself is not inherently prohibited. A food may also be unsuitable for a person’s body or circumstances without any sin having occurred.

Al-Isra 17:36 and An-Najm 53:28 call attention to knowledge, supposition, and the responsibility not to substitute guesswork for truth. Rational use of historical evidence is not thereby abolished. The difficulty arises when historical information of varying confidence becomes God’s certain, eternal command for everyone. The weight of a religious obligation demands evidence proportionate to it.

5.6. How the Qur’an Prohibits: More Than One Word

The Qur’an does not express prohibition only through the verb harrama (to prohibit or make forbidden). Al-Baqarah 2:275 contrasts permitted trade with prohibited riba (a form of financial increase or exaction whose precise scope requires separate study). An-Nisa 4:23 identifies relationships within which marriage is forbidden. Al-A'raf 7:33 lists indecency, sin, unjust aggression, assigning partners, and speaking about God without knowledge. Al-An'am 6:151, Al-An'am 6:152, Al-An'am 6:153 begins by announcing what the Lord has forbidden and sets out ethical and social commands concerning God’s oneness, parents, indecency, human life, fair measures, and commitments. Religious prohibition cannot be confined to a few foods or a single grammatical form.

Al-Ma'idah 5:90 describes intoxicants, gambling, sacrificial installations, and divining arrows as defilement associated with Satan’s work, then commands: “Avoid it.” Searching only for the word “forbidden” while ignoring a direct command to avoid would reduce interpretation to a superficial word search. Conversely, every unfavorable description or ethical expression is not automatically a general prohibition of a substance or species. The verb, audience, object, scope, and context require separate examination.

This matters for wholesome things. Praise or the description tayyib (good or fitting) does not by itself state every condition of use. Describing something as khabith (corrupt or unfit) in a particular context does not authorize prohibiting everything one dislikes. A religious ruling emerges from the legislative statements and their context, rather than from attaching an isolated adjective to preferred examples.

5.7. Wholesome and Corrupt: Broad Qualities, Not Two Closed Lists

Al-Baqarah 2:168 and An-Nahl 16:114 place lawful and wholesome alongside each other. They are not simply interchangeable. The same word for wholesomeness describes wind, land, speech, and willingness. Yunus 10:22 refers to a favorable wind for sailing. Al-A'raf 7:58 contrasts good, productive land with poor land that yields little. An-Nisa 4:4 describes women willingly giving part of their marriage gift. These uses do not classify foods as religiously permitted or forbidden. Each identifies a desirable quality appropriate to its setting.

The opposite word also extends beyond “religiously forbidden” or “ritually impure.” Al-Baqarah 2:267 asks believers to give from their good earnings rather than select inferior property they would themselves accept only reluctantly. Quality and fairness in giving are at issue. Ibrahim 14:24 and Ibrahim 14:26 compare a good word and a corrupt word to good and bad trees, extending the contrast into speech and thought. Al-Anbiya 21:74 applies corrupt things to the objectionable conduct of Lot’s people. Reducing the word to unpleasant-smelling meat or spoiled food would ignore much of its Qur’anic range.

Nor should every standard of goodness be surrendered to taste. A landowner’s affection does not make saline soil fertile, and a consumer’s culture does not make contaminated water safe. Conversely, disliking a food does not prove it harmful to others. These descriptions can concern sensory, functional, moral, and empirical qualities. Each requires evidence suited to the particular subject.

An-Nisa 4:160 is decisive for distinguishing quality from legal status. It describes wholesome things previously permitted but subsequently forbidden to a group because of wrongdoing. If “wholesome” always meant “legally permitted,” the statement would lose its point. A descriptive quality and a relation to permission or prohibition are distinct aspects of a thing. They may coincide in one situation and diverge in another. By asking for food that is both lawful and wholesome, the Qur’an also directs attention beyond the bare question of whether it is prohibited to its quality and suitability.

Forqan’s separate studies of these roots examine their linguistic and textual evidence in detail. For present purposes, the result is clear: neither word is itself a table of animal names. Neither personal liking nor cultural aversion can determine the divine status of a species without evidence.

An objection may invoke Al-Maidah 5:4: when people ask what is permitted, the answer is that wholesome things are permitted. Has wholesomeness therefore become a legal criterion? Yes, the verse establishes a meaningful connection; it cannot be dismissed as legally irrelevant description. But the connection neither makes the two words identical nor gives every person unlimited power to define the opposite category. An-Nisa 4:160 still distinguishes wholesome quality from a changed legal status. Meaning begins with language and context; the ruling depends on the Qur’an’s legislative statements together. Neither inquiry should replace the other.

5.8. Does Al-A'raf 7:157 Authorize an Endless List of Prohibitions?

The passage must be read as a whole. It describes a prophet who commands recognized good, forbids wrong, permits wholesome things, prohibits corrupt things, and removes people’s burdens and shackles. It then calls for faith in him, support for him, and following the light sent down with him. The relationship between these qualities and permission or prohibition is real. It cannot be dismissed by saying that “corrupt” is merely a description of hygiene.

Yet it does not follow that anyone in any period can label whatever seems unpleasant “corrupt” and thereby issue eternal divine law for everyone. The agent in the verse is the Messenger introduced through divine commission and revealed light. A person with different tastes, a religious institution, or a socially influential figure does not acquire that position merely by claiming to recognize corruption. The requirement for authorization in Ash-Shura 42:21 and the warnings against fabrication in Yunus 10:59 and An-Nahl 16:116 remain in force.

The word khaba’ith (corrupt or unwholesome things) does not name animal species in this verse. Its word family also describes conduct, speech, inferior property, and poor land. Without clear revealed evidence, it cannot be reduced to animals disliked by one culture. But the opposite claim also needs caution: the absence of a named animal does not mean that no prohibition derived from the verse can be discussed. We must ask whether other passages establish its application and scope, and how the descriptive quality supports the legal conclusion.

The expression “what God and His Messenger have forbidden” in At-Tawbah 9:29 belongs within this framework. The verse’s setting concerns relations with scriptural communities and the political and religious circumstances addressed there. Here we examine only the expression concerning prohibition, without erasing that broader context. Naming God and the Messenger together can describe a command conveyed by one sent by God. The conjunction alone proves neither two independent lawgivers nor the insignificance of the Messenger’s role. Anyone deriving independent authority for later collections must establish their attribution and scope and explain the inference from this context. That preserves the dignity of the Messenger’s commission without automatically turning historical transmission into certain revelation.

The passage offers no license for taste to legislate. It must be read alongside Al-A'raf 7:32, which asks who has prohibited God’s adornment and wholesome provision, and Al-A'raf 7:33, which prohibits speaking about Him without knowledge. One cannot use Al-A'raf 7:157 to forbid every disliked thing while disregarding those explicit challenges. Together they demand careful application, divine authorization, and accountability for attributing rules to God.

Empirical inquiry can identify contaminated water, poisoned food, or meat made unsafe through poor storage. Such food is unsuitable in those circumstances. Where a particular hazard can genuinely be removed and safety established, the danger in that instance does not prove every example of the species eternally forbidden. Responsible judgment requires avoiding known harm to oneself and others. That is different from claiming that all food of a particular kind is permanently prohibited in every condition. Al-A'raf 7:157 should cultivate responsibility toward what is corrupt, rather than open a route for conjecture and preference to speak in God’s name.

The verse’s reference to lifting burdens and shackles further clarifies its setting. Al-Imran 3:50 describes Jesus permitting some things previously forbidden to the children of Israel; An-Nisa 4:160 and Al-An'am 6:146 describe restrictions on earlier communities. Permitting wholesome things and removing prior burdens are genuine parts of prophetic guidance. The prohibition of corrupt things must nevertheless be taken equally seriously. The Messenger conveys restrictions as God’s commissioned representative. The question is what revealed evidence places within them, rather than how later tastes and customs can continually enlarge their scope.

5.9. Reading the Food Prohibitions Together

Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-An'am 6:145, and An-Nahl 16:115 use related formulations to identify carrion, blood, pork, and what is dedicated to other than God. Their repetition emphasizes clear divine boundaries. The foundational categories are stated directly rather than left for people to reconstruct from scattered reports and competing opinions.

Al-An'am 6:145 is particularly important: “Say: I find nothing forbidden to an eater who eats it in what has been revealed to me…” except the specified items. It follows the criticism of invented livestock prohibitions. Its force lies in answering unsupported claims: anyone adding to the revealed restrictions must identify the divine authorization. It is more than casual advice; it directly answers those inventing prohibitions for others.

A careful reading must also include Al-Ma'idah 5:3. Alongside carrion, blood, pork, and dedication to other than God, it addresses animals killed by strangling, blows, falls, goring, and predators, with an exception concerning animals properly slaughtered. It also forbids sacrificial dedication at stone installations and allocation by divining arrows. Some clauses explain circumstances of death; others concern religious practices and human actions. Divining arrows are not a food substance at all. These distinctions should neither be flattened into a few names nor converted without evidence into a new inventory of prohibited species.

The defensible conclusion is that the Qur’an identifies the principal prohibited foods and explains circumstances, ritual acts, and use elsewhere. Conditions and exceptions must be respected. “This animal is not named, so no condition can apply” is as hasty as “I dislike it, so God must have forbidden it.” The task remains to assess the source of the claim and all relevant textual evidence.

Some restrictions have particular addressees or circumstances. Al-An'am 6:146 reports restrictions concerning the Jewish community and connects them to wrongdoing. Al-Ma'idah 5:96 prohibits land hunting during pilgrimage consecration while permitting sea game and its food for residents and travelers. Hunting an animal can thus be permitted in one state and prohibited in another. Legal status is not always the expression of an unchanging corrupt essence in a species. Human action, personal status, time, and place can matter.

The five circumstances in Al-Ma'idah 5:3—strangling, a blow, falling, goring, and predation—concern the animal’s condition and timely intervention, especially in light of the slaughter exception. They are not five new species. Sacrifice at installations concerns ritual; divining arrows do not name food. Comparison with Al-An'am 6:145 therefore requires distinguishing each clause’s object and kind of rule. The restrictive wording of Al-Baqarah 2:173 and An-Nahl 16:115 matters within its context, but cannot be expanded into permission to ignore pilgrimage restrictions, human conduct, or harm to others.

5.10. Does the Revealed List Establish a Presumption of Permission?

A text’s restrictive list must be distinguished from an absence of all limits. Al-An'am 6:145, read alongside the criticism of invented livestock prohibitions, establishes a strong barrier against unsupported additions. Al-Baqarah 2:168 invites all people to eat lawful, wholesome things from the earth; An-Nahl 16:114 joins lawful provision with gratitude. These are positive statements of access to blessings and a demand for evidence of prohibition, rather than merely silence about a few animals.

Yet “This animal is not in the list” differs from “Every act involving this animal is permitted in every circumstance.” It may have died in a way that makes it carrion, been dedicated in another’s name, been hunted during pilgrimage consecration, or been obtained and supplied through injustice. Some rules concern the substance, others its condition, and others the human act. A sound synthesis identifies each rule’s actual subject.

For example, permission for livestock in Al-Ma'idah 5:1 does not logically imply that every other creature is prohibited. Affirming permission for one group does not deny it to all others. Al-Ma'idah 5:96 explicitly permitting sea game confirms that permission is not confined to livestock. Conversely, sea-game permission cannot alone settle every land animal’s status and conditions. Those questions require the complete network of passages rather than incomplete analogies.

The argument is therefore that God identifies prohibited food in answering those who invent restrictions, specifies conditions concerning animals and human behavior elsewhere, and warns against unauthorized additions. The burden of proving a new prohibition lies with its claimant. This does not disregard textual qualifications. It protects them against being replaced by personal wishes.

Describing pork as rijs (defilement or an objectionable impurity in this context) in Al-An'am 6:145 does not authorize claiming that a medical conjecture has discovered the sole and final reason for God’s ruling. An explicit prohibition does not need a newly announced scientific rationale each day. Nor may science be invoked to manufacture equivalent religious prohibitions for other foods. Research into actual food hazards has value in its own field, but changing empirical hypotheses should not be imposed on the Qur’an as its unstated legislative purpose.

5.11. Carrion, Blood, and the Animal’s Condition

In ordinary usage, carrion refers to an animal that dies before proper preparation for consumption. But the exact scope in these passages requires reading Al-Ma'idah 5:3 with Al-An'am 6:145. A modern definition should not dictate every conclusion. Al-Ma'idah 5:3 distinguishes circumstances of death and introduces the slaughter exception, showing that the manner of death and timely human intervention matter.

Three passages refer simply to blood, while Al-An'am 6:145 specifies daman masfuhan (blood poured out or flowing). The qualification must not disappear. The relationship between flowing blood and natural moisture in meat requires linguistic, physical, and preparation-related examination. Conversely, the qualification is not a pretext for consuming blood in every other form or disregarding contaminated food. The aim is to identify the text’s actual boundary rather than evade it.

The same care applies to tadhkiyah (the slaughter or preparation referred to in the exception). Al-Ma'idah 5:3 states the exception; Al-Ma'idah 5:4 permits hunting with trained animals and commands mentioning God’s name; Al-Hajj 22:36 concerns mentioning His name over sacrificial animals, eating from them, and feeding others. These passages do not make the way meat is obtained irrelevant. Hunting, slaughter, sacrifice, and avoidance of dedication to another are all addressed. But making a particular technical method, hand movement, tool, or minor customary detail an eternal condition also requires evidence.

Health knowledge can help assess methods of killing, transporting, and storing food animals. Its value should not be confused with an explicit revealed command. Where reliable evidence supports a safer technique, it can be chosen on that basis without pretending the technique is specified in the Qur’an. Conversely, traditional tools alone cannot guarantee that meat remains wholesome after improper storage.

The comparison concerning blood begins with the wording itself. Al-An'am 6:145 qualifies what the shorter formulations of Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Ma'idah 5:3, and An-Nahl 16:115 leave unqualified. Understanding the shared ruling requires retaining that detail. Flowing, poured-out blood is not linguistically identical to all natural meat moisture or every other fluid. This distinction is nevertheless no license for unsafe consumption. The religious inquiry must identify blood and the qualification accurately, while the health inquiry assesses the actual food.

5.12. Mentioning God’s Name: Every Food, or Animals and Sacrifice?

Al-An'am 6:118 says, “Eat of that over which God’s name has been mentioned.” The word “slaughter” is not present. A translation silently inserting it would mix interpretation with the verse’s wording. Faithful translation must distinguish the statement itself from the explanatory reading “that over which God’s name was mentioned at slaughter.”

The word’s absence does not, however, prove that every food—fruit, vegetables, bread, and meat—is necessarily addressed in exactly the same way. The surrounding verses discuss food prohibitions, wrongdoing, disputes, and unsupported restrictions. Al-Ma'idah 5:4 also connects mentioning God’s name with hunting by trained animals. Animal food and its associated practices cannot therefore be excluded from the inquiry. A broader application, if accepted, requires its own contextual argument.

Al-An'am 6:121 says not to eat that over which God’s name has not been mentioned and describes it as wrongdoing. This prohibition cannot be ignored. Al-An'am 6:145, meanwhile, refers to dedication in the name of another beside God. Absence of mentioning God’s name and explicit invocation of another are not identical expressions. If their applications overlap, the connection requires evidence; if their scopes differ, their relationship also needs explanation. A quick slogan cannot settle the difficulty.

Al-An'am 6:119 asks why people should refrain from what bears God’s name when He has detailed the prohibitions, then criticizes those misleading others through desire without knowledge. Mentioning the name, specified restrictions, and uninformed disputation belong together. This is more than general table etiquette. The explicit prohibition in Al-An'am 6:121 cannot be reduced to an ineffective ethical sentiment. Equally, it cannot without evidence become a system in which every manufactured food of uncertain production history is treated as a devotional offering to another deity. Both conclusions require examination.

Saying bismillah (in the name of God) when eating can express remembrance and gratitude. But does the eater’s utterance change every prior circumstance of the meat? The passages do not state such an unrestricted rule. If food was dedicated as a devotional offering to another, saying a phrase at the table does not by itself settle its relation to the prohibition. Conversely, ordinary industrial production does not become proved dedication to another deity merely because the buyer did not hear God’s name spoken during it.

Al-Hajj 22:36 and Al-Hajj 22:37 place remembrance and sacrifice alongside the statement that neither flesh nor blood reaches God, but people’s moral devotion does. The practices remain meaningful while being protected from reduction to verbal magic. Mentioning God’s name in relation to food is serious. Taking it seriously does not mean inventing contradictory technical rulings from speculation and popularity.

Al-An'am 6:138 supplies further context: livestock subject to invented religious rules is described as not bearing God’s name, in an account of fabrication against Him. The omission belongs there to a ritual system, rather than simply a worker’s silence in a distant factory. Al-Hajj 22:28, Al-Hajj 22:34, and Al-Hajj 22:36 also connect mentioning God’s name with livestock and sacrifice. Al-Ma'idah 5:4 directly instructs hunters to mention His name, giving the believer an active responsibility. But it does not specify every timing and procedural detail in a way that resolves all later commercial uncertainties. Meat of unknown provenance therefore cannot be declared certainly forbidden without grounds, nor can remembrance be treated as a question settled by a context-free phrase.

5.13. Food from Scriptural Communities and Shared Human Life

Al-Ma'idah 5:5 permits the food of those given scripture to the addressees, and their food to those scriptural communities. Ahl al-kitab (people given scripture) is the relevant Qur’anic designation. The verse follows discussion of wholesome foods and hunting, making it important for shared meals across religious communities. Without evidence, its opening should not be narrowed to a piece of fruit or bread while leaving the substantive questions about meat unanswered.

Nor does the verse exempt everything sold in any market from every Qur’anic rule simply because of the seller’s religious identity. An explicitly prohibited substance must still be considered: a seller’s scriptural affiliation does not cancel the prohibition of pork. Actual spoilage, health conditions, and clear dedication in another’s worship remain separate questions. The permission opens social relationships; it does not remove judgment and responsibility.

Interpretation has understood this food both broadly and specifically in relation to slaughtered animals. Here the argument against restricting it to bread and fruit rests on the surrounding food and hunting passages and its practical role in shared life. Yet the word ta‘am (food) does not alone settle every slaughter technique. Neither a restrictive interpretation nor a broad interpretation may be imposed without evidence, and neither may disregard explicit prohibitions.

Modern goods pass through complex networks of production, transport, and sale. Buyers cannot know every detail. Treating every technical uncertainty or remote possibility as certain proof of divine prohibition would turn social openness into endless suspicion. Ignorance, however, does not excuse disregarding clear evidence of danger or prohibition. The claim must be assessed on appropriate evidence, preserving the difference between “I do not know” and “God has forbidden it.”

The Qur’an itself helps establish the range of “food.” Al-Imran 3:93 speaks of all food being lawful for the children of Israel. Al-An'am 6:145 uses the related expression for an eater eating in a passage listing animal foods and their prohibitions. There is therefore no linguistic basis here for confining Al-Ma'idah 5:5 to bread, fruit, or plants. A broad meaning still does not abolish the explicit conditions attached to particular foods.

5.14. Sea and Land Hunting: Rules That Depend on Circumstances

Al-Ma'idah 5:96 permits sea game and its food while prohibiting land hunting for those in pilgrimage consecration. Not every legal distinction therefore arises from a supposedly pure or impure essence in the animal. The action of hunting acquires a particular status in a particular condition. Al-Ma'idah 5:95 also addresses hunting in that state and the required compensation.

Permission for livestock in Al-Ma'idah 5:1 cannot therefore be inverted into prohibition of every other animal. Mentioning one permitted category does not exclude the rest; sea game is an explicit counterexample. This logical point alone does not prove all land animals unconditionally permitted. The connected passages must still be read. “These animals are permitted” and “Only these animals are permitted” are different statements.

The scope of “sea game and its food” also requires care. What of different species and conditions, spoiled or poisonous food, or destructive fishing practices? Al-Ma'idah 5:96 explicitly establishes an opening, but cannot without reading the other passages be made to settle every question of blood, carrion, corruption, and harm involving aquatic creatures. Permission is not indifference to health or justice.

5.15. Geography, Culture, and Personal Suitability

People live under different conditions of climate, income, food availability, farming, storage, disease, family custom, and experience. A food intolerable to someone because of allergy, illness, or strong aversion may be ordinary for another. Conversely, customary daily food can carry genuine risks through production or preparation. Cultural familiarity does not prove safety, just as cultural disgust does not prove religious prohibition.

Someone may be deeply disturbed by the thought of eating dog, donkey, reptiles, or other animals. That person may abstain. No command requires consumption merely because an animal’s name is absent from a familiar prohibition list. The separate question is whether an inward response can be declared God’s command for everyone. That requires revealed evidence. Even a majority sharing the feeling does not turn it into divine authorization.

A person who eats such food elsewhere cannot infer from familiarity that it is always safe. Parasites, infections, toxins, and environmental damage are possibilities requiring evidence specific to the animal, environment, storage, preparation, and consumer. Unsupported generalizations about everyone’s body are inappropriate. Health knowledge helps identify risks, and known risks demand responsible conduct. But science does not claim revelation authorizing an eternal ban on a species.

Choice and obligation must remain distinct. Lack of divine prohibition does not compel eating. Dislike requires no invented religious justification. A credible possibility of harm can warrant caution and evidence-based warnings. None requires assuming God’s legislative position.

An-Nahl 16:8 also deserves attention: horses, mules, and donkeys are described in relation to riding and adornment, whereas An-Nahl 16:5 previously mentions livestock and eating. An objection may infer that omitting eating from the later description forbids their meat. But stating a benefit does not declare it the creature’s only function. The verse does not say they are solely for riding and forbidden as food. Conversely, an absent prohibition label does not replace examination of all food conditions. Positive permissions and explicit restrictions must be read together without converting a stated use into an unstated ban.

5.16. Permission Is Not a Command to Participate

A recurrent mistake exchanges permission, approval, and obligation. “I find no Qur’anic evidence prohibiting this animal solely because of its species” does not imply “I must eat it.” The latter is a new command requiring evidence. People choose among permitted things according to need, taste, health, budget, environment, and ethical responsibility. Nobody must put something on the table to prove they have not forbidden it.

The taunt “If you do not consider it forbidden, eat it yourself” exposes the confusion. A logical response need neither invite everyone to eat unfamiliar animals nor defend every meat. It asks why rejecting a prohibition should establish a positive duty. Someone who dislikes an ordinary fruit, vegetable, or meat needs no prohibiting verse to decline it. The same rule applies to others.

Deep cultural feelings make discussion of dog, donkey, reptile, or other unfamiliar meat sensitive. Strong feeling does not change the evidence required. If the concern is a specific disease risk, explain that risk. If it is cruelty in hunting or trade, defend the rights involved. If it is family custom or personal aversion, say so. Without evidence, none becomes “God has forbidden this to the whole world.” The boundary protects personal abstention while preventing God’s name from being used to universalize it.

5.17. A Wholesome Table Includes Other People’s Rights

Wholesomeness cannot be settled by changing a product label. Al-Baqarah 2:267 criticizes giving inferior property. Al-A'raf 7:31 joins eating and drinking with avoiding excess. Al-An'am 6:141 connects agricultural produce, the due given at harvest, and restraint from waste. Al-Mutaffifin 83:1, Al-Mutaffifin 83:2, Al-Mutaffifin 83:3 and Al-Isra 17:35 address dishonest measures. An-Nisa 4:29 and Al-Baqarah 2:188 prohibit wrongfully consuming other people’s property.

Our food cannot therefore be separated from justice in earning, buying, and giving. This ethical connection does not establish that every unjustly obtained food is linguistically the precise referent of “corrupt things” in food passages. Wrongful appropriation must also be judged in its own right. Someone may scrutinize ingredient names obsessively while withholding workers’ rights, cheating in trade, or throwing away food extravagantly. Those actions can violate God’s commands even if the food’s species is not prohibited. Dietary classification must not excuse forgetting the ethics of the same table.

Today this responsibility can involve choosing safe food, reducing waste, avoiding needless cruelty, respecting producers’ rights, and preventing environmental destruction. Al-A'raf 7:56 and Ar-Rum Ar-Rum 30:41 address corruption on earth; Al-An'am 6:38 describes animals and birds as communities like human beings. These passages do not directly establish that only organic products are wholesome or that expensive food is necessarily more ethical. A family unable to afford a special label can still live honestly, avoid waste, and respect rights. A commercial claim of purity is not itself the Qur’anic standard.

Buying a better product does not automatically constitute zakat (the Qur’anic obligation associated with purification and giving). Charitable giving, spending for others, fair trade, and healthy choices are related but distinct. Collapsing Qur’anic concepts under the banner of benevolence repeats the methodological error criticized here: replacing their actual boundaries with personal definitions.

5.18. Ease in Religion: Removing Invented Burdens While Respecting Commands

The Qur’an speaks of ease, capacity, and the removal of undue hardship. This neither abolishes duties nor by itself determines the number of prohibited foods. It shows that human ability and responsible living matter within the commands. Al-Baqarah 2:185, discussing fasting, illness, and travel, identifies God’s intention with ease rather than hardship. Al-Baqarah 2:286 states that no person is burdened beyond capacity. Al-Ma'idah 5:6, after instructions on purification and its alternative when water cannot be used, denies an intention to impose hardship; Al-Hajj 22:78 likewise speaks of no constriction imposed in religion. These passages do not eliminate every difficulty or restriction. They establish the importance of actual capacities and circumstances.

The food passages’ allowance for necessity is a clear example. Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-An'am 6:145, and An-Nahl 16:115 recognize someone compelled by need, without willful transgression or exceeding the limit, and speak of forgiveness and mercy. Al-Ma'idah 5:3 addresses severe hunger without an inclination toward sin. Even explicit prohibitions take real inability into account. Someone with no other means of preserving life differs from someone pursuing pleasure in disregard of the command.

The allowance does not invalidate every boundary under any preferred circumstances. Its qualifications against transgression and deliberate wrongdoing remain in the text. They do not require a starving person to undertake impossible, obsessive calculations before surviving. They prevent necessity from becoming a pretext for willful excess. Understanding and action must be proportionate to real knowledge and ability.

The cow account in Al-Baqarah 2:67, Al-Baqarah 2:68, Al-Baqarah 2:69, Al-Baqarah 2:70, Al-Baqarah 2:71 illustrates another danger. The initial instruction was straightforward: slaughter a cow. Repeated, evasive questioning added descriptive details and made fulfillment harder. The account does not prohibit serious inquiry—the Qur’an repeatedly invites thought. It warns against turning a clear instruction into an exhausting network of details, particularly where questioning evades responsibility or burdens others.

Ease consistent with God’s oneness respects the limits He sets and declines to erect new barriers where He leaves room. It accords with reason, health, and responsibility, rather than indifference to divine commands or other people’s rights.

5.19. Intoxicants: Why the Absence of One Word Does Not Cancel a Prohibition

An objection asks whether rejecting unauthorized prohibitions makes khamr (wine and intoxicating drink) permissible. That changes the issue. The argument rejects inventing prohibitions, not the Qur’an’s explicit restrictions. Al-Ma'idah 5:90, Al-Ma'idah 5:91 commands avoidance and discusses defilement, hostility, hatred, and obstruction of God’s remembrance and salat (the Qur’anic practice of prayer and devoted worship). Al-Baqarah 2:219 describes great sin or harm alongside benefits. These passages must be read together rather than searching one verse for a particular label and denying its direct command.

Descriptions of harms can illuminate a command’s wisdom. They do not automatically create a new exception: “If I think it will not harm me, the rule disappears.” Recognizing a command’s wisdom in relation to risks differs from proving that an absence of observed harm cancels it. The latter claim requires explicit textual grounds.

Drinks in paradise receive different descriptions. As-Saffat 37:47 denies harmful effects; Al-Waqi'ah 56:19 denies headache and loss of awareness. Muhammad 47:15 speaks of rivers of wine delightful to drinkers, and Al-Insan 76:21 of a pure drink. Shared names do not prove identical earthly and afterlife composition or effects. These descriptions present rewards free of earthly corruption and impairment. Conduct in this life must nevertheless be judged by the passages addressing this life.

An-Nahl 16:67 also describes people obtaining intoxicating drink and good provision from dates and grapes. Reporting what people produce does not itself command consumption or endorse every effect. The explicit command to avoid must be read in Al-Ma'idah 5:90, Al-Ma'idah 5:91. An-Nisa 4:43 prohibits approaching prayer while intoxicated until one knows what one is saying, connecting intoxication with impaired awareness. Thus An-Nahl 16:67 cannot simply override the avoidance command; equally, not every substance or liquid becomes an intoxicant merely because it is called a drink. That classification requires its own linguistic argument.

5.20. Gold and Silk: Adornment, Excess, and Gendered Restrictions

Al-Hajj 22:23 and Fatir 35:33 describe gold ornaments, pearls, and silk clothing in paradise without dividing believers by sex in those descriptions. Gold and silk have a place in the Qur’an’s language of beauty and reward. That alone does not establish the absence of every earthly limitation on every use. Earthly conduct requires the passages addressing this world.

Al-A'raf 7:31, Al-A'raf 7:32 places adornment and wholesome provision alongside restraint from excess and asks who prohibited God’s adornment and good gifts. Al-Ma'idah 5:87 tells believers not to forbid the wholesome things He permitted. These passages place the burden on anyone prohibiting ordinary enjoyment of blessings for a category of people in God’s name.

They state no explicit gender-specific ban on men’s use of gold and silk. Anyone claiming such a divine command must independently establish its source and attribution. Reports of prophetic behavior or preferences, without analysis of reliability and scope, reopen the original authority question. Criticism must also avoid unsupported historical claims, such as asserting that the prohibition definitely came from Christian monasticism. Al-Hadid 57:27 describes monasticism people instituted, but does not trace a particular gold-and-silk report’s origin.

Adornment is also more than a choice of metal. Arrogance, humiliation of others, extravagance, and boasting are separate ethical issues. Someone can boast over an inexpensive ring; someone else can own an expensive object without violating anyone’s rights. Luqman 31:18 criticizes arrogance; Al-Isra 17:26, Al-Isra 17:27 criticizes wasteful dissipation. Attaching arrogance solely to gold while ignoring it in luxury watches, homes, fame, or status confuses the object with the conduct. High price alone also proves neither boasting nor injustice: judgment must consider actual behavior and circumstances.

An-Nahl 16:14 and Fatir 35:12 describe extracting ornaments from the sea for people to wear, without stating a religious gender prohibition. Az-Zukhruf 43:53, by contrast, reports Pharaoh invoking gold bracelets in a claim of superiority. The ornament and the arrogant behavior are different subjects. The same thing can be a blessing or an instrument of belittling others. None of these passages alone settles all clothing rules, but neither supplies an independent gendered prohibition. The standard remains divine authorization rather than a culture’s preferred evaluation of a metal or fabric.

5.21. Human Choice and Social Responsibility

No society can function without regulations. Selling contaminated food can be prohibited, slaughterhouses required to meet health standards, and hunting restricted to protect a species and its environment. Such rules can rest on knowledge, experience, public rights, and prevention of harm. A public-health regulation nevertheless differs fundamentally from declaring that God eternally prohibited a food species for every generation.

Individuals can likewise adopt diets, avoid ingredients, and prefer particular meat-production practices. Their responsibility is to describe their reasons honestly: health, culture, taste, ethics, or animal welfare. A claim of certain divine law requires revealed evidence. The greater the claim and the heavier its burden on others, the stronger the supporting argument must be.

God’s exclusive divine authority is therefore not hostile to reason or science. It gives each kind of knowledge its proper function. Science examines contamination and nutrition; ethical reasoning considers fairness and waste; communities take practical decisions against danger. None becomes God’s speech merely by adopting revelation’s name. Preserving the distinction both respects divine authority and returns people to responsibility for their own choices.

This responsibility also has Qur’anic foundations. An-Nisa 4:29 prohibits killing oneself or one another; Al-Baqarah 2:195, in a setting of spending and responsible action, warns against casting oneself into destruction. These should not be detached from context to manufacture a separate food list. They do, however, ground care for human life in responding to known danger. Reliable evidence of a serious hazard gives practical meaning to scientific warning and prudent avoidance. That differs from the claim that the substance is named in the Qur’an as eternally forbidden.

6. Questions and Responses

Question 1: If dog or donkey meat is not named as forbidden in the Qur’an, must someone who does not consider it intrinsically prohibited eat it? No. Permission and personal desire or obligation are independent. A person may abstain for reasons of taste, health, experience, or choice without declaring a divine ban for everyone. Nor does an animal’s absence from a familiar list settle every circumstance of consumption before all food and hunting conditions are examined. The issue is the claimant’s burden of proving a prohibition, not forcing anyone to eat.

Question 2: Must we eat every animal the Qur’an does not name? No. Abstention needs no proof of divine prohibition. We leave countless permitted foods uneaten because we do not need or prefer them. Evidence is required when that choice becomes a certain religious command for others. Species must also be considered alongside hunting, slaughter, carrion, blood, and dedication; a list of names is only part of the inquiry.

Question 3: Does a culture’s ordinary consumption of an animal prove it wholesome? No. Custom can reflect availability, experience, or culture, while physical safety requires empirical evidence. Real risks should be identified and managed. A demonstrated risk can justify a health warning and responsible restriction. Custom or cultural disgust alone does not establish divine prohibition for everyone.

Question 4: If something is corrupt or objectionable to me, is it so for everyone? Not necessarily. What is meant? Dislike of taste or appearance reports an individual experience. Microbial contamination requires examinable evidence. A universal divine prohibition requires legislative grounds. One word cannot perform all these functions without distinguishing them.

Question 5: Does the Prophet’s prohibition of corrupt things in Al-A'raf 7:157 suffice to prohibit every disliked food? The verse’s connection between his commission and prohibition is real. It does not make every person’s taste the universal legal definition of corruption. His commission, divine authorization, and the examples clarified in revelation must be examined. Using the verse for unsupported restrictions conflicts with Yunus 10:59 and An-Nahl 16:116.

Question 6: Can science replace hadith in identifying wholesome and corrupt food? Reliable knowledge and experience are essential for contamination, toxins, disease, spoilage, storage, and nutrition. A religious report is not needed to recognize spoiled food’s danger. Science also does not replace God or independently declare eternal divine prohibitions. Empirical inquiry identifies conditions; revelation states divine commands; people must responsibly use each in its proper role.

Question 7: Is a doctor’s advice to avoid a food an unauthorized religious prohibition? No. Within professional expertise, the doctor assesses risk or suitability for a patient. That is not the statement “God has forbidden this to everyone.” Following appropriate medical advice can be prudent and necessary for preventing harm, while its source and scope remain clear. Every practical restriction is not religious legislation.

Question 8: Do health regulations for restaurants, butchers, and food factories interfere with God’s law? Not when they are justified as public-health protection and supported appropriately, rather than presented as discoveries of eternal divine prohibitions. Temporarily restricting a contaminated product protects others’ lives. Responsible social regulation and permanent religious classification must remain distinct in language and practice.

Question 9: If we did not hear God’s name at slaughter, is the meat necessarily forbidden? Al-An'am 6:118 and Al-An'am 6:121 must be taken seriously, alongside their addressees, food and ritual context, dedication to another, hunting, and the food of scriptural communities. Not hearing an utterance does not itself prove dedication to another deity. Conversely, repeating a phrase later does not simply cancel the prohibition. All the relevant passages must be brought together.

Question 10: Is every meat sold in a non-Muslim country permitted? Al-Ma'idah 5:5 offers a clear opening for scriptural communities’ food, not unconditional permission for every substance and circumstance. The food’s kind, condition, dedication, and what is actually known about it matter. Equally, every small uncertainty in production should not be turned without evidence into proof of divine prohibition. The verse must retain priority over invented suspicions and unsupported fears.

Question 11: Does the Qur’an forbid only four things in all of life? No. The familiar fourfold food list is not the totality of Qur’anic prohibitions. Financial exaction, unjust killing, indecency, oppression, wrongful appropriation, and other conduct receive commands and restrictions too. Al-A'raf 7:33 and Al-An'am 6:151 clearly extend beyond food. The principal food categories and all Qur’anic prohibitions are different subjects.

Question 12: Must intoxicants be avoided even if someone thinks no harm will result? Al-Ma'idah 5:90 directly commands avoidance, and Al-Ma'idah 5:91 describes destructive consequences. “I think this occasion will be harmless” does not cancel the instruction. The stated wisdom and the rule are related but distinct. Descriptions of afterlife drinks, with their different qualities, do not repeal earthly guidance.

Question 13: Does gold and silk being rewarded in paradise prove they are unrestricted here? An afterlife reward alone does not establish every earthly rule. But Al-A'raf 7:31, Al-A'raf 7:32 and Al-Ma'idah 5:87 raise direct challenges to unsupported prohibitions of adornment and wholesome things. A male-specific ban requires particular divine evidence. Waste, arrogance, and boasting should be judged through the passages concerning those actions, rather than assumed to belong to a metal or fabric’s essence.

Question 14: Is personal religious caution itself unauthorized lawmaking? Personal caution differs from a universal prohibition. Someone may avoid a food while uncertain about its circumstances. That choice is not objectionable if it makes no unsupported attribution to God and compels nobody else. Turning caution into “God commands everyone to do this” reopens the question of divine permission. Caution cannot quietly become legislation.

Question 15: Is everyone following a mistaken religious opinion guilty of assigning partners to God? That conclusion does not follow. We can criticize unauthorized legislation and explain its dangers without deciding each individual’s knowledge, intention, and responsibility. Intellectual error, uninformed trust, interpretive uncertainty, and knowing opposition to a clear command are different conditions. Careful judgment matters more than hasty labeling.

Question 16: Why is every attributed report not binding if obedience to the Messenger is obligatory? “The Messenger must be obeyed” is not logically equivalent to “Every sentence attributed to him in a later book is certainly his and eternally binding.” The latter requires proof of attribution and scope. The dignity of his commission does not automatically guarantee narrators’ memories, chains, or later generations’ interpretations.

Question 17: Is unauthorized religious legislation a concern only in food law? No. Food is a particularly clear setting in which the Qur’an exposes it. The same question arises wherever unsupported divine commands are asserted concerning worship, life, property, family, freedom of belief, or people’s rights. This study develops shared principles, leaving detailed applications to specialized research.

Question 18: Can uncertainty justify prohibiting an unnamed food for others as a precaution? “Perhaps” cannot replace “God commanded this.” Someone may decline meat because its production is unclear without claiming to state divine law. Turning that uncertainty into everyone’s duty requires more than suspicion. Personal abstention is a choice; declaring others sinful for not sharing our doubts is a substantial religious claim requiring authorization.

Question 19: Does absence from a hadith list establish unconditional permission, and what about crocodiles or eagles? No absence establishes a duty to eat. Reports in recognized collections also use general categories such as fanged predators and birds with talons, so applying those categories to particular animals involves interpretation rather than simply checking names. The examples clarify a different logical point: lack of prohibition does not mean compulsory consumption. The critique concerns the authority of a divine ruling, not an invitation to try every animal.

Question 20: If science identifies harm in an unnamed food, are there no limits? This again conflates two questions. The Qur’an does not require indifference to life and rights. Reliable knowledge of harm can create practical and ethical reasons for individuals and communities to avoid unsafe food. The assessment rests on evidence and principles of preventing injury, rather than the claim that the species is explicitly named as prohibited in revelation. Preserving this distinction entails neither recklessness nor invented religion.

Question 21: Does “Obey God and obey the Messenger” require accepting al-Bukhari and Muslim exactly as the Qur’an is accepted? It establishes obedience to the Messenger, not exceptionless certainty for later attributions. An-Nur 24:54 and Al-Ma'idah 5:92 connect obedience with clear conveyance. Making a later collection equivalent to directly hearing that message requires additional evidence. Forqan’s studies of obedience and the clear message examine the distinction in detail.

Question 22: Does the clarification command in An-Nahl 16:44 make hadith an obligatory completion of revelation? The object of clarification is what was sent down to people. An-Nahl 16:89 itself calls the Book an explanation of all things within its guiding purpose. These verses do not deny the Messenger’s teaching; neither do they certify every later report or authorize independent legislation and cancellation of Qur’anic commands.

Question 23: Does “the Book and wisdom” identify hadith as a second source? Al-Isra 17:39 calls the preceding Qur’anic teachings part of revealed wisdom. Wisdom elsewhere also has a meaning broader than hadith collections. Naming Book and wisdom together establishes neither a second collection nor the reliability of its transmission.

Question 24: Why would the Messenger’s executive decisions not bind later generations? The Qur’an recognizes his judgments and connects them to revelation (Al-Ma'idah 5:48, Al-Ma'idah 5:49; An-Nisa 4:105). Their historical existence does not automatically establish the accuracy of a present report, the decision’s permanent religious character, or its application now. Whoever extends it must substantiate all three.

Question 25: Did God not teach the Prophet things beyond the recited verses? Passages including Al-Baqarah 2:143 and At-Tahrim 66:3 deserve examination without prejudgment. Even if extra-textual information was given, later hadith collections do not thereby become its preserved contents or an independent legislative authority. Possible disclosure and demonstrated authority for a historical collection remain different claims.

Question 26: Does calling a report widely transmitted resolve its attribution and authority? Widespread independent transmission must be demonstrated for the particular content. Establishing that a practice occurred still does not alone prove it divinely required forever. Nor does the Qur’an’s collective and written transmission confer the same confidence on every individual-chain report. These are different histories and kinds of evidence.

Question 27: Does one serious contradiction justify rejecting an entire collection? An established, unresolved contradiction defeats a claim that every part is guaranteed, error-free revelation. It does not prove all remaining reports false. Rejecting independent legislative authority does not require disproving every item. An-Nisa 4:82 speaks specifically of “much inconsistency,” wording we must preserve, and invites reflection on the Qur’an’s coherence. Contexts and interpretations must be examined before declaring a conflict.

Question 28: Why use stoning as an example? An-Nur 24:1, An-Nur 24:2 announces clear verses and one hundred lashes for unlawful sexual intercourse; An-Nur 24:6, An-Nur 24:7, An-Nur 24:8, An-Nur 24:9 addresses a husband’s accusation and averting the punishment from his wife; An-Nisa 4:25 describes a punishment that can be halved. Adding death in God’s name requires explaining compatibility with this network and the protection of life. The supposed stoning verse, abrogation, and related objections are examined in Stoning: A Qur’anic Ruling or a Rule Derived from Reports?.

Question 29: Why use poetry and history for vocabulary but not accept historical reports as sufficient for legislation? Examinable early Arabic usage helps us understand the verse. It does not make a poet or historian a divine lawgiver. A claim that God prohibited an action until the end of time requires revealed authority and proof of attribution and scope.
