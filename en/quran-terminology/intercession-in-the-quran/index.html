Qur’an Terminology

Shafāʿah in the Qur’an: Restoring the Meaning Behind “Intercession”

A note on wording: This study retains shafāʿah rather than treating “intercession” as its settled English meaning. It examines the term through joining in support. A shafīʿ is the one who joins in that support; “supporter” is used below as a readable gloss, with the role examined in each context. English renderings of the quoted verses follow this terminology.

Introduction

Shafāʿah (often mistranslated as “intercession”) is among the Qur’an’s most profound concepts, yet few concepts have suffered as much historical misunderstanding. A closer look at religious beliefs suggests that, over many centuries, people have displaced it from its proper setting and reduced it to a way of getting around God’s laws.

The extent of this misunderstanding becomes clearer when we consider other religions. Christian traditions include both the intercession of saints and Christ’s sacrificial death for the forgiveness of his followers’ sins. These are distinct doctrines, although both concern salvation. Some interpretations give a prominent saving role to intermediaries or to Christ’s redemptive act. Certain Hindu traditions also involve approaching deities or holy figures for assistance, nearness to the divine, or liberation. These beliefs are not interchangeable, however, and the title “Bhagwan” cannot simply be treated as another word for “intercessor.”

Among Muslims, understandings of intercession often reproduce this reliance on intermediaries in different sectarian forms. Many Sunnis accept that the Prophet Muhammad will intercede in the hereafter with God’s permission, benefiting some sinners. In parts of the Shi‘i tradition, intercession extends to the Prophet’s family, the Imams, and other holy figures. The problem arises when intercession becomes a guarantee of salvation or a form of personal influence. God’s just judgment is then imagined as a human courtroom in which a well-connected intermediary can circumvent the law and help someone escape punishment.

Whatever religious form they take, such interpretations directly contradict explicit Qur’anic statements. The Qur’an repeatedly rejects mediation, friendship, and bargaining as ways of changing the consequences of one’s actions on the Day of Resurrection. Al-Baqarah 2:254, for example, states:

«يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْفِقُوا مِمَّا رَزَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَأْتِيَ يَوْمٌ لَا بَيْعٌ فِيهِ وَلَا خُلَّةٌ وَلَا شَفَاعَةٌ ۗ وَالْكَافِرُونَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ»

2:254

“You who have believed, give from what We have provided for you before a day arrives on which there is no trading, no friendship, and no shafāʿah. Those who reject the truth are the wrongdoers.”

Elsewhere, the Qur’an rules out any supporter’s independent power or ownership. Al-Zumar 39:44 states:

«قُلْ لِلَّهِ الشَّفَاعَةُ جَمِيعًا ۖ لَهُ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۖ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ»

39:44

“Say: All shafāʿah belongs to God. His is the dominion of the heavens and the earth. Then to Him you will be returned.”

Before we ask what power or standing a supporter might possess, a more basic question requires an answer. Does the one God ever instruct His servants in the Qur’an to find intermediaries who will bargain with Him on their behalf—for access to Him, forgiveness, or salvation on the Day of Resurrection? Or does He instead describe a direct relationship, calling people to pray to Him, worship Him, ask His forgiveness, and seek His help? In 2:186, God describes Himself as near and responsive to His servants’ prayers; in 40:60, He directly commands them to call upon Him. By contrast, 39:3 and 10:18 report the claims of people who appoint other beings as intermediaries to bring them nearer to God or offer support before Him. This distinction matters. Even if shafāʿah is possible with God’s permission, that possibility does not itself establish a command to seek out supporters and invoke them to obtain what we want.

A superficial reading of idhn, the Arabic word commonly translated as “permission,” lies at the heart of this historical confusion. Because some Qur’anic passages explicitly make shafāʿah conditional on God’s permission, readers accustomed to human patronage have interpreted permission as a personal license or special privilege. On that reading, divine permission becomes a permit to lobby the court of the hereafter: selected individuals can exercise influence over human destinies and the allocation of Paradise and Hell. This projection of human relationships onto God overlooks the precise order of creation. It reduces permission within an established created order to a shifting decision governed by personal connections.

The Qur’an does, of course, speak of God giving permission to created beings. The difficulty is not that a servant may be authorized to act. It arises when that authorization is turned into personal leverage for bargaining, altering what is right, or establishing a necessary intermediary between God and His servants. Permission to act within God’s command is different from a right to exert influence over that command.

These historical contradictions and misunderstandings originate in a failure to understand shafāʿah in its own Qur’anic terms. Instead of allowing the Qur’an’s usage to explain the word, people import a transactional understanding of mediation and impose that meaning on the purposeful order of creation.

This study sets aside inherited historical and sectarian assumptions. By examining the Qur’an’s own statements and bringing them into relation with one another, we ask what shafāʿah actually is and how God situates it within the laws governing existence. Our method is to let the Qur’an explain itself.

The course of the inquiry

We begin with the Arabic root sh-f-ʿ and the understanding of shafāʿah among pre-Islamic Arabs. We then examine every Qur’anic use of the concept in context.

The key to the inquiry is divine permission understood within the laws God has established for creation. From there, we investigate shafāʿah’s relationship to divine knowledge, truth, the governance of creation, divine determination and measure, human freedom, and accountability.

Throughout, we also pursue a foundational question: does the Qur’an itself tell people to find supporters and intermediaries to reach God, or does it open a direct path of prayer, worship, seeking forgiveness, and seeking nearness to Him? We place passages describing direct access to God alongside those reporting and criticizing claims made for intermediaries. We also distinguish the possibility of authorized shafāʿah from a command to seek and invoke a supporter.

The study’s central questions

The preliminary question: Does the one God instruct people to seek intermediaries other than Him who will bargain with Him for them, or does He call them into direct relationship with Himself through prayer, worship, repentance, and seeking help? If shafāʿah is possible within divine permission, does that also authorize—or command—people to seek and invoke a supporter?

1. What is shafāʿah? What did the Arabic words shafʿ and shafīʿ originally mean, and how does the Qur’an use them?

2. Who—or what—is a shafīʿ, or supporter? Must shafāʿah be the activity of a conscious person, or can it take another form of joining, support, and effective participation within the created order?

3. How does divine permission operate? Within which established laws of God does shafāʿah take effect?

4. What is a supporter supposed to do for God? Tell Him something He does not know? Reveal a truth hidden from Him? Create mercy that was previously absent? Or change a judgment already made with perfect knowledge?

5. How is shafāʿah related to truth and knowledge? Why does the Qur’an speak of those who “bear witness to the truth, knowing it,” and how does that description relate to divine permission?

6. How is shafāʿah related to divine determination, measure, and human choice? If every effective action takes place within God’s established order, what grounds the responsibility of both the supporter and the person supported? Why can shafāʿah be either good or bad?

7. Why does the Qur’an sometimes deny shafāʿah and elsewhere make it conditional on permission? Are these descriptions of the same phenomenon under different conditions, or of different conceptions of shafāʿah?

8. How are shafāʿah in this life and in the hereafter related? Do they share a fundamental meaning? What roles do permission, approval, and covenant play on the Day of Accounting?

9. Where are the boundaries between shafāʿah, prayer, asking forgiveness for another person, testimony, and seeking a means of approach to God? Which practices does the Qur’an affirm, and which interpretations have been attached to them without Qur’anic support?

10. How can shafāʿah be consistent with justice and individual responsibility? Can anyone rely on a supporter to escape the consequences of their own actions?

The question the entire inquiry must ultimately answer is this:

Is Qur’anic shafāʿah a way of changing God’s laws and judgments, or does it acquire its meaning and effect within His knowledge, truth, justice, and established order?

These ten questions provide the study’s framework. We will seek their answers in the verses themselves and, at the end, bring the findings together into a formulation of shafāʿah grounded in Qur’anic usage.

Part One: The root sh-f-ʿ and the Qur’anic meaning of shafāʿah

From pairing and singleness to shafāʿah

Understanding shafāʿah—often mistranslated as “intercession”—begins with its Arabic root, sh-f-ʿ. The word shafʿ means “a pair” or “the even,” in contrast to watr, “the single” or “the odd.” Something joins another thing, making a pair where there was previously a single unit. This simple image underlies the related words shafʿ (pairing), shafīʿ (one who joins in support), and shafāʿah (joining in support).

The Qur’an uses this contrast in Al-Fajr:

وَالشَّفْعِ وَالْوَتْرِ

89:3

“By the even and the odd.” (89:3)

This verse does not directly concern mediation, forgiveness of sins, or salvation at the resurrection. It expresses the basic contrast between the paired and the single. To understand shafāʿah, we must ask how that image of joining develops into accompaniment and support within human relationships.

Nor does any creature act in isolation from the causes and conditions surrounding it. To live, understand, and act, human beings depend on capacities they did not create. Fatir 35:15 emphasizes humanity’s complete dependence on God and His freedom from all need. This offers a starting point for considering how pairing relates to creatures’ dependence on the means and laws God has established.

Nevertheless, not every connection between two beings can be called shafāʿah. The root meaning establishes a starting point; the word shafāʿah has a more specific social and religious use. To understand that use, we must consider its place in pre-Islamic Arabic language and culture.

The supporter in pre-Islamic Arabic society

In pre-Islamic Arab social relationships, shafāʿah meant joining another person’s request, position, or undertaking. Someone with a request drew on another person’s standing, speech, or ability to strengthen their case. The supporter was neither the original petitioner nor the final decision-maker. Their role was to join and reinforce the request.

Such support was not necessarily good or ethical. One person might intervene to support someone suffering injustice; another might join an oppressor and help advance a wrongful demand.

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani writes:

اتاک امرؤ مستبطن لی بغضة

له من عدو مثل ذلك شافع

“A man concealing hatred for me has come to you, with another enemy joining him in that hostility.”

Here, shāfiʿ—a related word for one who joins in support—describes someone joining another person’s hostility to strengthen his position. The significance of this historical example is that the designation carries no inherent holiness or moral merit. Being a supporter does not necessarily mean defending what is right. The moral worth of the support depends entirely on its purpose and direction.

In social use, then, shafāʿah means joining another person to strengthen their position, whether through words and requests or practical assistance. A supporter is not a passive bystander: their participation affects the course of events.

This distinction prepares us to understand one of the Qur’an’s most important statements about the nature of shafāʿah.

Good and bad shafāʿah: support entails responsibility

Al-Nisa states:

مَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً حَسَنَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ نَصِيبٌ مِنْهَا ۖ وَمَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً سَيِّئَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ كِفْلٌ مِنْهَا ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقِيتًا

4:85

“Whoever joins in good shafāʿah will have a share in it; whoever joins in bad shafāʿah will bear a share of it. God watches over all things.” (4:85)

This verse is foundational. Contrary to the common assumption that shafāʿah is necessarily holy and saving, it distinguishes good shafāʿah from bad shafāʿah. The act is not intrinsically good or bad; its value depends on what the person supports.

The preceding verse, 4:84, concerns fighting in God’s cause and encouraging the believers. In that setting, shafāʿah means joining, encouraging, and supporting others in a collective undertaking. Yet the general wording, “whoever joins,” is not confined to warfare. It encompasses support for good or bad actions more broadly.

The central point is the supporter’s responsibility. The Qur’an assigns a direct share and consequence to someone who supports either kind of action. No one can evade responsibility by saying that they were merely a supporter rather than the principal actor. Shafāʿah therefore extends responsibility instead of providing an escape from it: people are assessed not only for their direct actions but also for the words, encouragement, influence, and resources they contribute to others.

In this context, shafāʿah is neither a privilege reserved for holy people nor a guarantee of forgiveness for sinners. It is a conscious act carrying responsibility. This fits the underlying image of pairing: joining another person’s undertaking creates a connection whose consequences reach the supporter as well. The verse establishes both the supporter’s responsibility and shafāʿah’s two possible moral directions.

Shafāʿah in the religious beliefs addressed by the Qur’an

How did this idea of support enter religious belief? The Qur’an shows that claims to possess a supporter before God existed before Muslim theological systems developed.

Yunus states:

وَيَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَضُرُّهُمْ وَلَا يَنْفَعُهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ هَٰؤُلَاءِ شُفَعَاؤُنَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ ۚ قُلْ أَتُنَبِّئُونَ اللَّهَ بِمَا لَا يَعْلَمُ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

10:18

“They worship, apart from God, things that can neither harm nor benefit them, and say: ‘These are our supporters before God.’ Say: ‘Are you informing God of something in the heavens or the earth that He does not know?’ Glory be to Him! He is far above the partners they associate with Him.” (10:18)

The verse makes an important distinction. Those it criticizes did not necessarily deny God’s existence; they located their supporters “before God.” They had extended patterns of social patronage into the divine realm. Just as someone might approach an influential person to win the favor of a powerful ruler, they imagined beings who could secure support or salvation for them before God.

The Qur’an rejects that assumption. Appealing to God’s complete knowledge, it challenges the claim at its foundation: are you telling God about something in heaven or earth unknown to Him? Calling a being one’s supporter does not establish that being’s standing with God. Nor does adding the phrase “before God” make a religious belief legitimate.

Ya-Sin examines the same issue in terms of securing benefit and averting harm:

أَأَتَّخِذُ مِنْ دُونِهِ آلِهَةً إِنْ يُرِدْنِ الرَّحْمَٰنُ بِضُرٍّ لَا تُغْنِ عَنِّي شَفَاعَتُهُمْ شَيْئًا وَلَا يُنْقِذُونِ

36:23

“Shall I take gods other than Him? If the All-Merciful intends harm for me, their shafāʿah will be of no use to me, nor will they rescue me.” (36:23)

Here, shafāʿah concerns rescue and the prevention of harm, not merely forgiveness in the hereafter. The belief under criticism attributes to certain beings an ability to prevent God’s will from taking effect through their shafāʿah. The Qur’an categorically denies that power.

Together, these passages show that shafāʿah meant considerably more to the Qur’an’s original audience than forgiveness of sins. It included protection, preventing harm, and securing benefits. The issue is not the word itself but the standing attributed to the supporter. Human relationships cannot simply be projected onto divine sovereignty, granting others a right to intervene in God’s will and judgment. Supporting an action never amounts to owning its outcome or having final authority over it.

Shafāʿah, ownership, and authority

Al-Zumar addresses supposed supporters through the question of ownership:

أَمِ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ شُفَعَاءَ ۚ قُلْ أَوَلَوْ كَانُوا لَا يَمْلِكُونَ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ

39:43

“Have they taken supporters apart from God? Say: ‘Even though they possess nothing and have no understanding?’” (39:43)

قُلْ لِلَّهِ الشَّفَاعَةُ جَمِيعًا ۖ لَهُ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۖ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

39:44

“Say: All shafāʿah belongs to God. His is the dominion of the heavens and the earth. Then to Him you will be returned.” (39:44)

The first verse challenges the appointment of supporters apart from God, emphasizing their lack of ownership and understanding. The next declares that shafāʿah belongs entirely to God and connects this declaration to His absolute sovereignty over the heavens and the earth.

The connection is decisive. Instead of advising people to choose a stronger supporter, the Qur’an returns ownership of shafāʿah exclusively to God. In human society, an influential person may use power, wealth, or position to assist someone else. But that social model cannot simply be transferred to God’s order, in which no creature independently possesses sovereignty or final authority.

We can therefore distinguish the basic meaning of shafāʿah from the imagined ownership of shafāʿah. In ordinary social use, a shafīʿ joins in support of a person or undertaking. People may then go further, imagining that the supporter’s standing and influence give them ownership of other people’s destinies. The Qur’an rejects that unsupported assumption.

These verses do not yet explain every aspect of shafāʿah before God, but they establish a firm boundary: no shafāʿah can create an independent sphere of authority alongside His sovereignty.

When considering shafāʿah before God, we can distinguish the supporter, the person concerned, and the authority before whom shafāʿah is made. Yet every use of the word does not explicitly identify all three roles. Al-Nisa 4:85 discusses the act and the supporter’s share, whereas Al-Baqarah 2:255 speaks of shafāʿah “before Him.” Likewise, “All shafāʿah belongs to God” concerns ownership of shafāʿah itself. It follows that no creature acquires an independent right to impose its preferred effect or outcome.

The root’s range of Qur’anic uses

The root sh-f-ʿ occurs 31 times in 26 verses across 19 Qur’anic chapters. Its occurrences take five grammatical forms: five verbs, thirteen instances of the noun shafāʿah (joining in support), ten forms belonging to the shafīʿ family (“supporter”), two instances of shāfiʿīn (“supporters”), and one shafʿ (“the even” or “a pair”). Taken together, these uses occupy several related fields of meaning.

First comes the basic contrast between the even and the odd in 89:3. Second is the social function described in 4:85, which uses four forms of the root in parallel statements about good and bad shafāʿah.

Another group denies the presence of a supporter, the acceptance of shafāʿah, or its effectiveness: 2:48, 2:123, 2:254; 6:51, 6:70; 26:100; 30:13; 32:4; 40:18; and 74:48. These statements do not all have the same structure. Some deny acceptance, others benefit, others the existence of a supporter, and others the existence of a protector or supporter “apart from Him.” These distinctions matter. A verse rejecting one particular claim cannot be generalized to every possible form of shafāʿah without examining its context.

A further group reports people’s claims to have supporters, or their hopes of shafāʿah, and challenges the standing of those supposed supporters: 6:94; 7:53; 10:18; 36:23; and 39:43. Here we must distinguish a claim the Qur’an reports from a truth it affirms. Reporting an assertion does not endorse it.

Other passages connect shafāʿah with divine ownership, permission, covenant, approval, or truthful testimony: 2:255; 10:3; 19:87; 20:109; 21:28; 34:23; 39:44; 43:86; and 53:26. Shafāʿah is never separated from God’s sovereignty and complete knowledge. The conditions nevertheless differ: some verses emphasize permission; others approval or covenant; others informed testimony to the truth; and others God’s exclusive ownership. A dictionary definition alone cannot explain shafāʿah before God. We must examine its relationship to permission, knowledge, truth, approval, covenant, and divine governance.

The appearance of “supporter” alongside words meaning “protector” (walī), “helper” (naṣīr), and “close friend” (ḥamīm) in 6:51, 32:4, 40:18, and 26:100, 26:101 also places it within the language of support and assistance. These words are related without being exact synonyms.

A central finding emerges: the Qur’an does not confine shafāʿah to one scene in the hereafter or one narrow meaning. It can describe a human action that is good or bad, an unfounded claim about imagined intermediaries, or shafāʿah before God under specified conditions. We therefore cannot impose “a holy person requesting a sinner’s release from Hell” as the definition of every occurrence. Nor does this diversity imply unrelated meanings. We must identify the shared core while reading each context carefully.

From the basic meaning to God’s established order

We can now examine the root’s meaning against the wider background of creation. If its starting point is pairing, and its social use is joining in support of another person or undertaking, might joining and effective participation also have a place within the created order?

Human beings never act independently of causes. Decisions and actions take place within particular conditions, possibilities, and limits. We make our choices, but we do not create every factor that determines their results. A farmer, for example, performs the act of sowing; whether a crop grows and bears fruit does not depend on the farmer’s will alone. Al-Waqi‘ah 56:63, 56:64 distinguishes human cultivation from God’s causing plants to grow. We do not independently own every condition or outcome of our actions. Our work is always joined to causes we did not create.

This holds across human conduct: no action bears fruit outside the network of causes and possibilities governing the world. There is a conceptual connection here with the root image of joining and pairing. Yet precision requires a further question. Does the Qur’an call every connection between human action and causes “shafāʿah,” or is shafāʿah a particular kind of accompaniment and support within that order? Asking this prevents us from extending the word beyond its Qur’anic use.

So far, shafāʿah has emerged as a form of joining and support whose participant does not personally own the outcome. The Qur’an also connects shafāʿah before God to permission, knowledge, and truth. These connections open a path toward understanding its place within God’s established order.

This provides an initial linguistic and Qur’anic basis for the hypothesis developed here: human actions joining with the causes and conditions that enable them to take effect within God’s created order. The study calls this “causal coupling”: an action takes effect through its connection with other causes and conditions. The phrase names the proposed explanation of how shafāʿah operates; it is not a literal translation of the Arabic word. Establishing how such a relationship works requires further examination of shafāʿah, divine permission, and God’s governance.

Conclusions from Part One

The root, its pre-Islamic social use, and its Qur’anic occurrences show that shafāʿah does not fundamentally mean forgiveness of sins or salvation in the hereafter. Its starting image is joining and pairing, developed socially into support for a person, request, position, or undertaking.

The Qur’an does not treat this support as inherently holy or good. It may be good or bad, giving its supporter a share and a responsibility. Shafāʿah therefore creates responsibility rather than providing a way to avoid it. Nor does being a supporter confer ownership of the result: supporting another person does not give the supporter final control over the outcome.

When this concept entered religious belief, people sometimes assigned the role of supporter to beings they considered holy, imagining that their support could alter someone’s fate against God’s will. The Qur’an rejects such claims by testing them against God’s complete knowledge, ownership, and sovereignty. Its association of shafāʿah with permission, approval, covenant, and truthful testimony also shows that shafāʿah before God cannot simply mirror influence and patronage in human society.

These findings allow a preliminary definition:

Shafāʿah is joining in support of a person, request, position, or undertaking. It may be good or bad, gives the supporter a share and responsibility, and does not in itself confer ownership or independent control over the outcome.

This separates the basic meaning from later assumptions about a supporter’s power. It does not yet fully explain shafāʿah within God’s order.

How does this joining and effective participation occur within creation? If all causes, actions, and consequences fall within God’s knowledge and established laws, how does shafāʿah relate to them? Why does the Qur’an speak of good and bad shafāʿah while making shafāʿah before God conditional on His permission and approval?

The key is a careful understanding of divine permission. Part Two therefore examines permission in relation to the laws governing existence, the governance of affairs, human freedom, and how actions produce consequences, so that shafāʿah’s place within that order becomes clearer.

Part Two: Divine permission, the order of existence, and human agency

Part One established that shafāʿah fundamentally involves joining and support. Such support may be good or bad and gives the supporter a share and a responsibility. When the Qur’an speaks of shafāʿah before God, however, it makes it dependent on His permission. We must therefore first examine what “permission” means in Qur’anic usage.

In human relationships, permission usually means a person in authority agreeing to someone else’s request. If we simply transfer that model to God, we may imagine divine permission as a personal license granted because of someone’s connections, request, or closeness to Him. The Qur’an uses the word much more widely. It concerns not only responses to personal requests but also creation, the world’s order, events, death, guidance, belief, victory, and defeat.

In this setting, divine permission must be understood in relation to the order God has established: the conditions within which beings come into existence, act, and experience the consequences of their actions. The central question is therefore not merely who obtains permission, but what laws and limits God has established and how He has made effective action possible within them.

Permission in creation and the occurrence of events

Al-Hajj presents this within the order of the world:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ سَخَّرَ لَكُمْ مَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَالْفُلْكَ تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ بِأَمْرِهِ وَيُمْسِكُ السَّمَاءَ أَنْ تَقَعَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِالنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

22:65

“Do you not see that God has made what is on earth available to you, and the ships that sail upon the sea by His command? He holds the sky from falling upon the earth except by His permission. God is compassionate and merciful toward people.” (22:65)

Permission appears here alongside the earth’s availability, ships in motion, and the stability of the sky. No individual has first submitted a personal request for a ship to move or the sky to remain in place. People build and operate ships, but they did not make the sea, the properties of water, or the laws of buoyancy. Human action joins causes already established within creation. Divine permission thus concerns the possibility and limits of events taking place. Creatures possess no power independent of this order; their actions become effective only within God’s established laws.

Al-Taghabun extends this meaning:

مَا أَصَابَ مِنْ مُصِيبَةٍ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ۗ وَمَنْ يُؤْمِنْ بِاللَّهِ يَهْدِ قَلْبَهُ ۚ وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

64:11

“No affliction occurs except by God’s permission. Whoever believes in God, He guides their heart. God knows all things.” (64:11)

Affliction, too, lies within divine permission. This does not mean that God approves of wrongful conduct that causes suffering, or that suffering must be punishment for the person harmed. The verse emphasizes events’ dependence on God’s created order, then brings together belief, guidance, and His complete knowledge. Both an outward event and a person’s inward response belong within that order.

Ali ‘Imran likewise connects death with permission:

وَمَا كَانَ لِنَفْسٍ أَنْ تَمُوتَ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ كِتَابًا مُؤَجَّلًا ۗ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ ثَوَابَ الدُّنْيَا نُؤْتِهِ مِنْهَا وَمَنْ يُرِدْ ثَوَابَ الْآخِرَةِ نُؤْتِهِ مِنْهَا ۚ وَسَنَجْزِي الشَّاكِرِينَ

3:145

“No soul dies except by God’s permission, at a prescribed term. Whoever desires the reward of this world, We give them of it; whoever desires the reward of the hereafter, We give them of it. We shall reward the grateful.” (3:145)

Here, permission is connected to an appointed limit, while human intention and desire are acknowledged alongside it.

In these passages, divine permission is neither a formality nor a personal privilege. It expresses the operation of God’s will and created order in what can happen and what actually happens. That order is not confined to observable physical processes: it also encompasses death, life, guidance, knowledge, and accountability.

God’s established ways: order in the world and in human life

If permission concerns events becoming actual within creation, what does the Qur’an say about the stability of that order?

Fatir states:

اسْتِكْبَارًا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَكْرَ السَّيِّئِ ۚ وَلَا يَحِيقُ الْمَكْرُ السَّيِّئُ إِلَّا بِأَهْلِهِ ۚ فَهَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا سُنَّتَ الْأَوَّلِينَ ۚ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لِسُنَّتِ اللَّهِ تَبْدِيلًا ۖ وَلَنْ تَجِدَ لِسُنَّتِ اللَّهِ تَحْوِيلًا

35:43

“[They acted] arrogantly in the land and devised evil schemes. Yet evil scheming encompasses only those who practice it. Are they waiting for anything but the way that applied to earlier peoples? You will find no replacement for God’s established way, and you will find no diversion in God’s established way.” (35:43)

Here, God’s established way is connected to human behavior and its consequences. Arrogance and scheming result from human choices; their consequences belong to His judgment and unfailing order. The Arabic expression sunnat Allāh, “God’s established way,” therefore cannot be confined to material laws or the natural world. Alongside creation’s physical order, the Qur’an describes patterns governing individuals, societies, guidance, misguidance, and the moral consequences of conduct.

These established ways mean that the world is not merely a collection of arbitrary events and unprincipled decisions. The Qur’anic account maintains a firm relationship between action and consequence, choice and responsibility, truth and judgment.

Their stability does not mean that people can predict the exact timing and circumstances of every consequence. Someone may commit injustice without immediately suffering visible punishment. Indeed, 35:45 speaks of God granting a reprieve until an appointed time. We should therefore avoid reducing His established way to a simple mechanical formula.

What matters for this inquiry is that existence has an order and that every consequence falls within God’s complete knowledge and judgment.

Permission for something to happen is not moral approval

We must distinguish an event’s possibility from its moral value. This distinction is also essential to understanding good and bad shafāʿah.

Al-Baqarah 2:102 states that even harmful practices such as sorcery used to separate spouses have no effect without God’s permission. Yet the Qur’an calls such conduct harmful and does not endorse it. An action’s effectiveness within God’s created order therefore does not mean that He loves or approves of that action.

Al-Shura states:

أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ شَرَعُوا لَهُمْ مِنَ الدِّينِ مَا لَمْ يَأْذَنْ بِهِ اللَّهُ ۚ وَلَوْلَا كَلِمَةُ الْفَصْلِ لَقُضِيَ بَيْنَهُمْ ۗ وَإِنَّ الظَّالِمِينَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

42:21

“Do they have partners who have prescribed for them a religion that God has not authorized? Were it not for the word of final judgment, judgment would already have been made between them. The wrongdoers will have a painful punishment.” (42:21)

This verse rejects unauthorized religious legislation. It shows why we must ask what permission concerns in each passage. Sometimes it concerns something’s possibility and effectiveness within creation; elsewhere it concerns religious legitimacy and authority. An action occurring in a law-governed world does not place it outside God’s sovereignty over creation, but neither does its occurrence establish its moral legitimacy.

This makes the good and bad shafāʿah of 4:85 clearer. Both are human actions with real consequences, but they differ in moral worth and responsibility. The operation of God’s laws does not remove the need to judge whether an action is right.

This study distinguishes permission within creation—what God allows to occur in the world—from religious authorization—what He authorizes people to do as a matter of religious practice. The corresponding Arabic analytical terms are takwīnī (concerning creation) and tashrīʿī (concerning religious prescription). The verses themselves use the word “permission”; these labels must never replace attention to each verse’s wording and context.

The grammatical structures also differ. In 64:11 and 3:145, an event occurs “by God’s permission.” In 20:109 and 78:38, the wording “He has given permission to him” emphasizes a person receiving authorization. In the Throne Verse, 2:255, “by His permission” makes shafāʿah dependent on God. These constructions should not be treated as grammatically identical. Nevertheless, a person receiving authorization does not thereby step outside God’s knowledge, truth, or established order. Each instance must be understood in relation to its object, never as a license to disrupt divine governance.

Human freedom within God’s established order

If existence rests on the laws God has established, where does human freedom belong? Are people powerless instruments of destiny, or are their choices a real part of that ordered world?

Al-Ra‘d states:

لَهُ مُعَقِّبَاتٌ مِنْ بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ يَحْفَظُونَهُ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُغَيِّرُ مَا بِقَوْمٍ حَتَّىٰ يُغَيِّرُوا مَا بِأَنْفُسِهِمْ ۗ وَإِذَا أَرَادَ اللَّهُ بِقَوْمٍ سُوءًا فَلَا مَرَدَّ لَهُ ۚ وَمَا لَهُمْ مِنْ دُونِهِ مِنْ وَالٍ

13:11

“Each person has attendants in succession, before and behind them, guarding them by God’s command. God does not change the condition of a people until they change what is within themselves. When God intends adversity for a people, none can turn it back, and they have no protector apart from Him.” (13:11)

The verse directly connects a society’s outward transformation to an inward change among its members. People do not control every external condition, yet their decisions and inward dispositions play a decisive role in the course of their lives.

The same relationship appears in guidance and misguidance:

وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ يَا قَوْمِ لِمَ تُؤْذُونَنِي وَقَدْ تَعْلَمُونَ أَنِّي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ إِلَيْكُمْ ۖ فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا أَزَاغَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ ۚ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

61:5

“Remember when Moses said to his people: ‘My people, why do you hurt me when you know that I am God’s messenger to you?’ Then, when they turned away, God caused their hearts to turn away. God does not guide a defiantly disobedient people.” (61:5)

The sequence matters: their chosen deviation precedes God’s turning their hearts away. Misguidance is not imposed without relation to conduct; it becomes a real consequence of their own choice within the created order.

Al-Mutaffifin likewise states:

كَلَّا ۖ بَلْ رَانَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ مَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

83:14

“No! Rather, what they used to do has left a coating of rust upon their hearts.” (83:14)

Actions do not simply vanish. They leave real effects on the person who performs them. Through repeated choices, people shape their inner character and capacity to understand. Human freedom is therefore part of God’s created order, not a rival to it.

Permission in guidance: human choice and God’s response

The Qur’an directly connects permission with guidance and choice. Al-Ma’idah states:

يَهْدِي بِهِ اللَّهُ مَنِ اتَّبَعَ رِضْوَانَهُ سُبُلَ السَّلَامِ وَيُخْرِجُهُمْ مِنَ الظُّلُمَاتِ إِلَى النُّورِ بِإِذْنِهِ وَيَهْدِيهِمْ إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

5:16

“Through it, God guides those who pursue His approval along the ways of peace, brings them out of darkness into light by His permission, and guides them to a straight path.” (5:16)

Guidance and emergence from darkness by divine permission are connected here to pursuing God’s approval. Permission operates in a setting in which the person has taken a step toward what is right.

Yunus presents another side of the same relationship:

وَلَوْ شَاءَ رَبُّكَ لَآمَنَ مَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ كُلُّهُمْ جَمِيعًا ۚ أَفَأَنْتَ تُكْرِهُ النَّاسَ حَتَّىٰ يَكُونُوا مُؤْمِنِينَ

10:99

“Had your Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed, all together. Will you then compel people to become believers?” (10:99)

وَمَا كَانَ لِنَفْسٍ أَنْ تُؤْمِنَ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ۚ وَيَجْعَلُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

10:100

“No soul can believe except by God’s permission, and He places defilement upon those who do not use their reason.” (10:100)

Belief is neither coerced nor independent of God, yet it is connected to the exercise of human reason. Permission in guidance operates through an order in which reasoning and freely chosen orientation have a real role.

This is crucial for shafāʿah. If human growth, guidance, and salvation involve reasoning, seeking truth, and inward reform within God’s order, attachment to a supporter cannot be claimed to cancel those fundamental conditions and established laws.

Permission, divine determination and measure, and the limits of choice

The question of permission leads to qaḍāʾ and qadar: divine determination and the measures or limits established within creation. Al-Qamar states:

إِنَّا كُلَّ شَيْءٍ خَلَقْنَاهُ بِقَدَرٍ

54:49

“We have created everything according to a measure.” (54:49)

Here, “measure” describes creation as having limits, proportions, and structure. Events occur within an ordered setting. Al-Takwir places two decisive statements side by side:

لِمَنْ شَاءَ مِنْكُمْ أَنْ يَسْتَقِيمَ

81:28

“For whoever among you wishes to take a straight course.”

وَمَا تَشَاءُونَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَشَاءَ اللَّهُ رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

81:29

“Yet you do not will unless God, Lord of all worlds, wills.”

The first recognizes human willing; the second refuses to separate it from divine willing. Neither human choice nor its dependence on God’s order can be ignored. People choose, but they do not create the framework or the causes through which their choices become effective.

The same applies to shafāʿah. Deliberately joining a cause or supporting someone does not give us ownership of every condition and outcome. A person defending someone oppressed can use knowledge and ability to improve that person’s situation, without controlling every event or guaranteeing success. Supporting an oppressor also takes place within the same created order, but its possibility does not make it legitimate.

An ordered world therefore remains compatible with responsibility. People have agency; they do not own the system through which actions take effect or are judged. As 40:20 puts it, “God judges in truth.” No creature can use personal influence to make a final decision about another person’s destiny outside the standards of truth and divine knowledge.

The wider relationship between God’s prior knowledge, divine determination and measure, and human choice is examined in “Divine Decree and Human Choice”. For this inquiry, the essential point is that God’s knowledge and established laws do not abolish human responsibility or the reality of human action.

Permission and shafāʿah within divine governance

With this wider understanding of permission, we can return to a central passage in Yunus:

إِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ ۖ يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ ۖ مَا مِنْ شَفِيعٍ إِلَّا مِنْ بَعْدِ إِذْنِهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ فَاعْبُدُوهُ ۚ أَفَلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ

10:3

“Your Lord is God, who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then established Himself upon the Throne, governing all affairs. There is no supporter except after His permission. Such is God, your Lord: worship Him. Will you not take heed?” (10:3)

Notice the sequence: creation, the Throne, governance of affairs, shafāʿah conditional on permission, and finally the call to worship God alone. The following verses, 10:4, 10:5, extend the pattern through the return of everyone to God, just recompense, the ordered movement of the sun and moon, and the reckoning of time.

Shafāʿah belongs inside divine governance, not outside it. Within this framework, permission cannot be a license for an influential person to bypass that governance or nullify God’s established laws. The possibility and effectiveness of shafāʿah itself depend on His permission and will.

The causal-coupling hypothesis now becomes clearer. Just as shafāʿah socially means joining in support, no factor within creation acts independently of the conditions and laws God has established. Shafāʿah is thus a form of joining and support that has real effects whose possibilities and limits depend entirely on divine order.

To complete the argument, however, we must identify what kind of joining in support the Qur’an calls shafāʿah before God. The mere existence of a connection between two natural phenomena is insufficient to give it that name.

Nor does the general statement that all events occur by God’s permission explain the particular condition attached to shafāʿah. The Qur’an also speaks of its benefit and of approval. An action occurring within an ordered world is not automatically accepted or beneficial before God. This distinction prevents the condition of permission from becoming an empty repetition.

Permission and divine knowledge: why a supporter cannot have independent authority

The relationship between permission and knowledge is especially clear in the Throne Verse:

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ ۚ لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ ۚ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ ۗ مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ ۚ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ ۚ وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ ۖ وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا ۚ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ

2:255

“God—there is no deity but Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs everything in the heavens and everything on earth. Who can offer support before Him except by His permission? He knows what lies before them and behind them, while they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and preserving them does not burden Him. He is the Most High, the Magnificent.”

Al-Baqarah 2:255

Several truths form one structure here: God is living and sustaining; everything in heaven and earth belongs to Him; shafāʿah depends on permission; His knowledge is comprehensive; and everyone else’s knowledge is limited.

Their connection is essential.

A supporter supposedly revealing an unknown fact encounters God’s complete knowledge. One supposedly producing an outcome by independent power encounters His ownership and sovereignty. One supposedly acting outside His permission encounters the very condition the Qur’an attaches to shafāʿah.

The supporter’s role cannot therefore be founded on any deficiency in God’s knowledge, power, or self-sufficiency.

The verse also places permission immediately before its declaration of God’s comprehensive knowledge. This sequence situates shafāʿah within an order whose sovereign knows the whole truth from the outset.

Shafāʿah with God cannot mean informing Him of a servant’s hidden circumstances. Nor can it establish a power competing with His governance or judgment.

The same conclusion is reinforced in 20:109, 20:110 and 21:28, where permission or approval limits shafāʿah and divine knowledge appears in the same or the following verse.

God’s knowledge excludes any account of shafāʿah based on correcting His ignorance; His permission keeps its possibility and effectiveness within His own sovereignty.

This does not make every request, testimony, or act of support ineffective. The Qur’an also describes asking forgiveness for another person. The question is where such requests belong within God’s truth and mercy, which later parts will examine.

Shafāʿah and God’s laws: human action and the factors that support it

We can now connect this section’s findings to shafāʿah’s basic meaning.

Part One established that social shafāʿah involves supporting a person, request, or undertaking. Al-Nisa 4:85 showed that this support may be good or bad and gives the supporter a share in its consequences.

The passages on permission have now shown that no creature’s activity or effectiveness lies outside creation and God’s established order. People choose and participate, but they do not independently create the possibility or limits of their actions’ effects.

Together, these findings reveal a relationship central to the causal-coupling hypothesis.

An action is not an event isolated from the rest of the world. It takes place among particular causes, possibilities, limits, and consequences. A person chooses a course, and their action becomes effective through its connection with other conditions and factors.

That connection can serve either good or evil.

Someone supporting an oppressed person adds knowledge, ability, or resources to that person’s effort. Someone helping an oppressor also contributes to advancing an undertaking. Both provide support within human relationships, but their actions and responsibilities differ morally.

God’s established order does not remove either action from consequences and responsibility.

This helps explain why the Qur’an assigns a share to good and bad shafāʿah. Joining another person’s undertaking is itself a choice and an action, with implications for the supporter’s practical and moral life.

We must nevertheless distinguish shafāʿah’s meaning from the conditions of its effectiveness. Shafāʿah is joining and support; divine permission, when it concerns what can occur in creation, locates that activity within the world’s established laws and limits.

It does not yet follow that every natural force, law, or causal connection is automatically a supporter. That further claim requires examination of passages about the supporter’s nature, knowledge, truthful testimony, and shafāʿah in the hereafter.

What is already clear is that shafāʿah cannot be imagined outside God’s creation and governance.

Conclusions from Part Two: permission and the limits of effective action

The Qur’an’s use of permission extends beyond permission in human relationships to creation, events, death, guidance, belief, and actions taking effect. It concerns the order God has established for existence. Beings act within its limits, and no effect occurs outside His knowledge and governance.

This does not remove responsibility or deny human choice. Choosing, reasoning, and inward change have real roles in guidance, misguidance, and a person’s life. People choose within an ordered setting without independently owning their actions’ causes or results. Equally, an action’s possibility within creation does not mean moral endorsement. People can choose good or evil within this same order and bear responsibility for that choice.

These principles apply to shafāʿah. Supporting a person or undertaking never takes a supporter outside God’s established laws. The support may be effective without conferring independent power to determine the outcome. Shafāʿah’s dependence on “His permission” must therefore be understood alongside divine sovereignty, governance, and complete knowledge, rather than through human assumptions about personal influence over a ruler.

This gives us a working definition:

Within creation, divine permission expresses the possibility of events and effective action within God’s will and established laws—an order encompassing creation, action, choice, and consequences. No shafāʿah can acquire independent power or legitimacy outside that domain.

This strengthens the hypothesis of actions becoming coupled with supporting causes: human action and others’ support take place in an ordered world dependent on God’s governance, never in an independent sphere beyond His sovereignty.

The inquiry remains incomplete. What precisely does a supporter do before God? Why does the Qur’an connect shafāʿah with knowledge, truthful testimony, approval, and covenant? How can these be understood alongside permission and divine order without reducing shafāʿah to influence over God’s judgment?

Part Three addresses these questions: the nature of the supporter and the workings of shafāʿah in light of knowledge, truth, permission, and divine approval.

Part Three: The supporter, knowledge, truth, permission, and approval

Where does the supporter stand within creation?

The first two parts established the root’s connection to pairing and accompaniment, and the dependence of every being on God’s established order. Effects occur within His permission, knowledge, and governance. We now ask where a supporter stands within that order and how they affect another person’s circumstances or the outcome of an undertaking.

In human relationships, a supporter supports someone through standing, speech, or influence. This image cannot simply be transferred to God. He is not a ruler who needs advisers because He lacks information or changes His decisions under pressure from influential people. Yet the Qur’an does not deny the existence of agents operating within His command. The issue is their relationship to divine command, knowledge, truth, and permission. Does a supporter independently impose a preference upon God’s order, or does the role belong entirely within it?

Honored servants: standing without independence

Al-Anbiya explains their position while answering the claim that God has offspring:

وَقَالُوا اتَّخَذَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَلَدًا ۗ سُبْحَانَهُ ۚ بَلْ عِبَادٌ مُكْرَمُونَ

21:26

“They say: ‘The All-Merciful has taken a child.’ Glory be to Him! Rather, they are honored servants.” (21:26)

لَا يَسْبِقُونَهُ بِالْقَوْلِ وَهُمْ بِأَمْرِهِ يَعْمَلُونَ

21:27

“They do not precede Him in speech, and they act by His command.” (21:27)

يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يَشْفَعُونَ إِلَّا لِمَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ وَهُمْ مِنْ خَشْيَتِهِ مُشْفِقُونَ

21:28

“He knows what lies before them and behind them. They offer support only for those He approves, and they stand in fearful awe of Him.” (21:28)

وَمَنْ يَقُلْ مِنْهُمْ إِنِّي إِلَٰهٌ مِنْ دُونِهِ فَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِيهِ جَهَنَّمَ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الظَّالِمِينَ

21:29

“Whoever among them says, ‘I am a deity apart from Him,’ We will repay with Hell. That is how We repay wrongdoers.” (21:29)

These verses establish several connected points:

1. Being honored does not make a servant a partner in divinity. However elevated their standing, they remain servants of God.

2. Their speech and actions neither precede nor operate outside God’s command.

3. They possess no independent knowledge through which they could reveal something hidden from God. His knowledge encompasses what is before and behind them.

4. Their shafāʿah is not unrestricted: it depends on God’s approval of the person concerned.

5. They themselves stand in awe and fear before His greatness. They are not beyond law or responsibility.

A supporter can therefore have a real role and effect without independent authority. Their honored position does not make God’s order change to accommodate their personal wishes. The Qur’anic role remains wholly subject to His sovereignty; it establishes no power outside it.

This supports the causal-coupling hypothesis. Like any other agent, a supporter acts within the possibilities and laws God has established. The effect of that shafāʿah has meaning only within this order.

Angelic shafāʿah and the limits of its benefit

Al-Najm makes even the shafāʿah of angels dependent on permission and approval:

وَكَمْ مِنْ مَلَكٍ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ لَا تُغْنِي شَفَاعَتُهُمْ شَيْئًا إِلَّا مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ يَأْذَنَ اللَّهُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَرْضَىٰ

53:26

“How many angels there are in the heavens whose shafāʿah avails nothing, except after God gives permission for whom He wills and approves.” (53:26)

The surrounding passage criticizes invented beliefs about deities and the pursuit of conjecture and desire. Verses 53:24, 53:25 reject the assumption that people simply obtain whatever they wish and affirm that both this life and the next belong to God. Angelic shafāʿah is introduced against the fantasy that personal wishes can establish someone’s right to offer support and guarantee the desired result. Even angels do not possess guaranteed, independent shafāʿah merely because of their exalted standing.

“Avails nothing” is decisive. The question is not only whether shafāʿah occurs but whether it benefits or takes effect. An act does not guarantee its own result. In human relationships, too, support may occur while success depends on other conditions. Within God’s order, this dependence is more fundamental: no creature’s status enables it to produce an effect outside His permission and approval.

Saba presents the same point after denying other beings independent ownership, partnership, or a role as God’s supporting ally. Verse 34:22 says that the supposed deities own not even an atom’s weight in the world, share no part in its creation, and provide God with no assistance. It then states:

وَلَا تَنْفَعُ الشَّفَاعَةُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا لِمَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ ۚ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ قَالُوا مَاذَا قَالَ رَبُّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا الْحَقَّ ۖ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْكَبِيرُ

34:23

“Shafāʿah with Him benefits no one except those for whom He has given permission. When fear is lifted from their hearts, they ask: ‘What did your Lord say?’ They answer: ‘The truth.’ He is the Most High, the Great.” (34:23)

Placing shafāʿah after these denials shows that it is not a route to recovering the powers just denied. A being that is no partner in God’s sovereignty does not become one by receiving the title “supporter.” The verse’s ending also emphasizes truth. Permission does not authorize disregard for truth; every accepted effect belongs within it. Shafāʿah with God cannot bear fruit independently of His truth and approval.

Ghafir uses a particularly precise description of the supporter on the Day of Accounting:

وَأَنْذِرْهُمْ يَوْمَ الْآزِفَةِ إِذِ الْقُلُوبُ لَدَى الْحَنَاجِرِ كَاظِمِينَ ۚ مَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ مِنْ حَمِيمٍ وَلَا شَفِيعٍ يُطَاعُ

40:18

“Warn them of the approaching day, when hearts rise to throats as they suppress their anguish. The wrongdoers will have neither an intimate friend nor a supporter whose word is obeyed.” (40:18)

“Whose word is obeyed” deserves attention. The verse denies wrongdoers a supporter whose word is effective and heeded. We should not turn that into a literal denial of every form of shafāʿah for everyone. What it does invalidate in this setting is reliance on a powerful supporter whose intervention can be counted on. The following verses, 40:19, 40:20, emphasize God’s knowledge of the most hidden human states and His judgment in truth.

Informed testimony to truth: shafāʿah cannot oppose reality

Al-Zukhruf 43:86 is especially important for identifying the supporter’s nature:

وَلَا يَمْلِكُ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ الشَّفَاعَةَ إِلَّا مَنْ شَهِدَ بِالْحَقِّ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

43:86

“Those they invoke apart from Him do not possess shafāʿah, except those who bear witness to the truth and know it.” (43:86)

Immediately beforehand, 43:84, 43:85 calls God wise and knowing, declares His sovereignty over heaven and earth, and locates knowledge of the Hour with Him. Shafāʿah and truthful testimony follow in that setting.

The phrases “bear witness to the truth” and “know it” belong together. This is not merely uttering a correct sentence: testimony is tied to understanding and knowledge. “Truth” cannot therefore be a ceremonial formula or whatever the speaker happens to prefer. It concerns a reality known to the witness.

The same chapter records Jesus’ call to worship God in 43:64, while 5:116, 5:117, 5:118 describes his testimony concerning his people. That connection offers a useful example of truthful witness. Nevertheless, 43:86 alone does not name everyone included in its exception or specify each person’s exact relationship to shafāʿah. A possible example must not be fixed as the verse’s sole referent.

Testimony and shafāʿah remain different concepts, but the connection is clear: valid shafāʿah before God cannot rest on falsehood. No claim of closeness to Him can make falsehood true or rewrite a person’s actual record.

In human affairs, influential people sometimes distort judgment through false testimony, concealment, or pressure. That model has no place before God’s comprehensive knowledge. Neither a person’s actions nor their inward condition is hidden from Him, so a supporter cannot manipulate the information and change His judgment. Shafāʿah within God’s order is never founded on hiding reality or turning falsehood into truth. Its relationship to divine knowledge is therefore central to this inquiry.

God’s knowledge and the supporter’s limited knowledge

Complete knowledge needs no supplementary information

In the Throne Verse, 2:255, shafāʿah is immediately linked to God’s complete knowledge and His servants’ limited understanding. Ta-Ha makes the relationship especially clear:

يَوْمَئِذٍ لَا تَنْفَعُ الشَّفَاعَةُ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَرَضِيَ لَهُ قَوْلًا

20:109

“On that day, shafāʿah will not benefit anyone except one to whom the All-Merciful has given permission and for whom He has approved speech.”

يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِهِ عِلْمًا

20:110

“He knows what lies before them and behind them, while they do not encompass Him in knowledge.”

Benefit, permission, approved speech, and comprehensive divine knowledge appear together. The implication is fundamental: shafāʿah does not complete God’s knowledge. He already knows every intention, action, and circumstance. A supporter does not supply new information needed for a just judgment.

Nor does the phrase “for whom He has approved speech” name a particular speaker or spell out an unrestricted scope of approval. It must not silently become unlimited authority for a supporter or blanket endorsement of everything a person says. Al-Nisa 4:108 itself describes speech God does not approve. Speaking and speaking acceptably are different things.

If God knows everything, why does the Qur’an nevertheless describe speech and testimony at the resurrection?

The answer lies in distinguishing informing God from making truth manifest in judgment. Al-Mu’minun states:

وَلَا نُكَلِّفُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا وَلَدَيْنَا كِتَابٌ يَنْطِقُ بِالْحَقِّ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

23:62

“We burden no soul beyond its capacity. With Us is a record that speaks the truth, and they will not be wronged.” (23:62) Al-Jathiyah also states:

هَٰذَا كِتَابُنَا يَنْطِقُ عَلَيْكُمْ بِالْحَقِّ ۚ إِنَّا كُنَّا نَسْتَنْسِخُ مَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

45:29

“This is Our record, speaking truthfully about you. We were recording what you used to do.” (45:29)

These records do not speak to correct God’s ignorance. They make the reality of actions manifest in the accounting. Ya-Sin likewise attributes testimony to bodily limbs:

الْيَوْمَ نَخْتِمُ عَلَىٰ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَتُكَلِّمُنَا أَيْدِيهِمْ وَتَشْهَدُ أَرْجُلُهُمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

36:65

“Today We seal their mouths; their hands speak to Us, and their feet testify to what they used to earn.” (36:65)

Complete divine knowledge does not exclude testimony from the process of accounting. Authorized shafāʿah may likewise involve the expression of truth, informed testimony, or support for a position consistent with what is right. This does not make testimony and shafāʿah identical. It does show how a supporter’s speech can participate in truth becoming manifest without altering what God knows.

Abraham’s exchange concerning Lot’s people offers another example of a request in relation to divine knowledge:

فَلَمَّا ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ وَجَاءَتْهُ الْبُشْرَىٰ يُجَادِلُنَا فِي قَوْمِ لُوطٍ

11:74

“When Abraham’s fear subsided and the good news reached him, he began pleading with Us concerning the people of Lot.” (11:74)

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُنِيبٌ

11:75

“Abraham was indeed forbearing, deeply tenderhearted, and constantly turning to God.” (11:75)

يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ أَعْرِضْ عَنْ هَٰذَا ۖ إِنَّهُ قَدْ جَاءَ أَمْرُ رَبِّكَ ۖ وَإِنَّهُمْ آتِيهِمْ عَذَابٌ غَيْرُ مَرْدُودٍ

11:76

“Abraham, turn away from this. Your Lord’s command has come, and a punishment that cannot be turned back is coming upon them.” (11:76)

In another account of the same event, Abraham mentions Lot’s presence in the land. The messengers reply:

قَالَ إِنَّ فِيهَا لُوطًا ۚ قَالُوا نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَنْ فِيهَا ۖ لَنُنَجِّيَنَّهُ وَأَهْلَهُ إِلَّا امْرَأَتَهُ كَانَتْ مِنَ الْغَابِرِينَ

29:32

“He said: ‘But Lot is there.’ They replied: ‘We know better who is there. We will certainly save him and his family, except his wife, who will be among those left behind.’” (29:32)

Abraham’s plea is not presented as meaningless or blameworthy. Yet the exchange does not place God’s command or the people’s actual condition under Abraham’s control. Nor does the messengers’ statement about their knowledge imply knowledge or sovereignty independent of God. This example helps clarify the relationship between request, knowledge, and divine command, although these verses do not use the word “shafāʿah.”

Divine covenant and the failure of invented guarantees

Alongside permission, approval, and knowledge, the Qur’an connects shafāʿah with covenant—a binding assurance grounded in God, rather than a guarantee invented by a claimant. Maryam states:

يَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ الْمُتَّقِينَ إِلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ وَفْدًا

19:85

“On the day We gather those mindful of God to the All-Merciful as an honored delegation.”

وَنَسُوقُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ وِرْدًا

19:86

“And drive the guilty toward Hell, thirsty.”

لَا يَمْلِكُونَ الشَّفَاعَةَ إِلَّا مَنِ اتَّخَذَ عِنْدَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ عَهْدًا

19:87

“They do not possess shafāʿah, except one who has taken a covenant with the All-Merciful.”

A divine covenant cannot be manufactured at will. Its authority must come from God Himself. Al-Baqarah applies this test to a claim of guaranteed deliverance:

وَقَالُوا لَنْ تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ إِلَّا أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَةً ۚ قُلْ أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا فَلَنْ يُخْلِفَ اللَّهُ عَهْدَهُ ۖ أَمْ تَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

2:80

“They say: ‘The Fire will touch us only for a few numbered days.’ Say: ‘Have you taken a covenant with God—so that God will never break His covenant—or are you attributing to God what you do not know?’” (2:80)

Earlier in Maryam, the Qur’an reports someone’s claim that wealth and children will surely be granted to him:

أَفَرَأَيْتَ الَّذِي كَفَرَ بِآيَاتِنَا وَقَالَ لَأُوتَيَنَّ مَالًا وَوَلَدًا

19:77

“Have you seen the one who rejected Our signs and said: ‘I will certainly be given wealth and children’?” (19:77)

أَطَّلَعَ الْغَيْبَ أَمِ اتَّخَذَ عِنْدَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ عَهْدًا

19:78

“Has he gained access to the unseen, or taken a covenant with the All-Merciful?” (19:78)

The question appears in the very chapter that connects shafāʿah to a covenant with the All-Merciful. Covenant cannot therefore be reduced to personal hope or a self-issued guarantee about the future. In 19:87, too, the referent of “they do not possess” and the position of the person excepted must be examined within the gathering of the God-conscious and the guilty. The verse itself does not specify the covenant’s details.

Sectarian attachment or naming someone a supporter cannot create a personal covenant of salvation. Shafāʿah belongs within divine covenant, not imaginary religious contracts. Maryam 19:95 reinforces this:

وَكُلُّهُمْ آتِيهِ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَرْدًا

19:95

“Every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone.” (19:95) Each person’s individual return prevents the title “supporter” from becoming a means of evading personal responsibility.

Seeking forgiveness for another: spiritual support within divine knowledge and mercy

The practice of istighfār, asking God for forgiveness, is especially useful here when the request is made for someone else. The Qur’an does not call every such request shafāʿah. Nevertheless, these passages show how spiritual support can coexist with God’s comprehensive knowledge and mercy.

Ghafir states:

الَّذِينَ يَحْمِلُونَ الْعَرْشَ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهُ يُسَبِّحُونَ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ وَيُؤْمِنُونَ بِهِ وَيَسْتَغْفِرُونَ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا رَبَّنَا وَسِعْتَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ رَحْمَةً وَعِلْمًا فَاغْفِرْ لِلَّذِينَ تَابُوا وَاتَّبَعُوا سَبِيلَكَ وَقِهِمْ عَذَابَ الْجَحِيمِ

40:7

“Those who bear the Throne and those around it glorify their Lord with praise, believe in Him, and ask forgiveness for those who believe: ‘Our Lord, Your mercy and knowledge encompass everything. Forgive those who have repented and followed Your path, and protect them from the punishment of the blazing Fire.’” (40:7)

رَبَّنَا وَأَدْخِلْهُمْ جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ الَّتِي وَعَدْتَهُمْ وَمَنْ صَلَحَ مِنْ آبَائِهِمْ وَأَزْوَاجِهِمْ وَذُرِّيَّاتِهِمْ ۚ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ

40:8

“‘Our Lord, admit them to the lasting gardens You have promised them, together with those among their parents, spouses, and descendants who have become righteous. You are the Mighty, the Wise.’” (40:8)

These verses establish several connections between support, knowledge, mercy, and God’s order:

1. Reliance on divine knowledge and mercy. Before making their request, the bearers of the Throne acknowledge that God’s knowledge and mercy encompass everything. They are neither revealing an unreported fact nor producing new mercy in Him.

2. Connection to repentance and righteous conduct. The prayer concerns those who “have repented and followed Your path.” In the next verse, family relationship alone is insufficient: the condition is having become righteous.

3. Seeking fulfillment of God’s promise. They ask for nothing outside His wisdom and promise. Their request seeks the fulfillment of the mercy and promise He has already established.

Other beings’ requests do not oppose God’s will in this pattern; they rest on repentance, righteousness, mercy, and His promise. We must still preserve the distinction between seeking forgiveness and shafāʿah. These passages matter because they show that asking God to grant someone good can take place within God’s knowledge, mercy, and order without the petitioner owning the power to forgive.

Prophets as guides and witnesses, without ownership of human destinies

Prophetic activity belongs within the same framework. Prophets support people by conveying revelation, warning, bringing good news, and showing the path of truth. Consciously accepting their call and reforming one’s conduct creates a beneficial connection to that path. It never makes the prophet the owner of someone’s destiny.

At the end of Al-Ma’idah, the Qur’an depicts Jesus speaking with God in the setting of judgment. Having acknowledged God’s complete knowledge and described himself as a witness over his people while among them, Jesus says:

إِنْ تُعَذِّبْهُمْ فَإِنَّهُمْ عِبَادُكَ ۖ وَإِنْ تَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ فَإِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ

5:118

“If You punish them, they are Your servants. If You forgive them, You are the Mighty, the Wise.” (5:118)

Jesus claims no authority of his own to punish or forgive. He locates judgment in their relationship as God’s servants and in God’s might and wisdom. Prophetic standing plainly does not make someone an independent decision-maker in divine judgment.

By presenting the path of truth, prophets make it possible for people to connect their lives with guidance. Such support is fully consistent with God’s established order and absolute sovereignty. Yet conveying revelation, warning, witnessing, and guiding should not, without evidence, be treated as identical to every aspect of shafāʿah in the hereafter.

Causal coupling and the workings of shafāʿah

The findings of the first three parts can now be brought together:

Part One identified shafāʿah as joining in support of a person, request, or undertaking. It may be good or bad and gives the supporter a share and responsibility.

Part Two showed that no conduct or effect occurs outside the laws God has established. People have choice and responsibility, but they do not create every condition or limit affecting their actions.

Part Three connected shafāʿah before God to knowledge, truth, permission, approval, and covenant. Even honored servants and angels cannot use their standing to produce an effect independently of that order.

Together, these findings allow shafāʿah to be interpreted through the hypothesis of actions becoming coupled with supporting causes:

A person freely acts within a world governed by God’s causes and established laws. The action connects with those factors and produces consequences in outward life, inward character, social relationships, and the accounting of the hereafter. Other people’s support can be one effective factor within this order. Someone supports another through speech or action and shares in the result—the activity described as good or bad shafāʿah in 4:85.

Within divine judgment, further firm criteria apply. No factor can independently change the truth of an action or produce a judgment contrary to what is right. God’s complete knowledge excludes hidden facts; permission keeps effectiveness within His sovereignty; approval separates shafāʿah from arbitrary personal wishes; covenant makes any claimed right or privilege dependent on divine authority; and informed truthful testimony excludes conjecture and falsehood as foundations for valid speech. These are connected features of one order, not separate permits for exerting influence against God.

Shafāʿah in this framework is not an outside power arriving to break or cancel the consequences of actions. Any accompaniment, support, request, or testimony valid before God operates within His truth and established ways. Yet shafāʿah must not be equated with every cause-and-effect relationship: the Qur’an uses distinct terms for shafāʿah, testimony, seeking forgiveness, and guidance. The causal-coupling hypothesis offers a general framework for shafāʿah’s dependence on God’s order; it does not yet explain every detail of shafāʿah in the hereafter.

Conclusions from Part Three: shafāʿah within truth

We can now answer more clearly what a supporter’s role before God could be.

Would a supporter report an otherwise unknown fact? God’s comprehensive knowledge excludes that need.

Would a supporter produce fresh mercy in God? The prayers for forgiveness show the petitioners themselves relying on mercy that already encompasses everything.

Could a supporter use personal standing to alter judgment contrary to truth? Sovereignty, permission, approval, and truthful testimony exclude such independent power.

The role therefore rests on no deficiency in God’s knowledge, mercy, or justice. This does not imply that accompaniment, requests, and testimony have no effect whatsoever. The Qur’an describes good shafāʿah among people, prayer for others’ forgiveness, the speech of honored servants, and truthful testimony. The real question is how a supporter’s role and effect relate to God’s knowledge, truth, permission, and sovereignty.

No supporter possesses an independent domain before God. No creature’s standing or knowledge makes it any less a servant dependent on God. Within these limits, causal coupling offers a coherent account: human actions and the factors supporting them occur within an order governed by divine knowledge, truth, and established laws. Shafāʿah can never bypass that order.

A fundamental question remains. Some verses deny shafāʿah, while others condition it on permission and approval, particularly in resurrection passages. Is shafāʿah in the hereafter the manifestation of, and testimony to, the consequences of earthly actions and relationships? Can a new request or utterance also occur that day with permission? How can these possibilities fit individual responsibility and perfect justice?

Part Four brings together the denials and exceptions in resurrection passages and examines shafāʿah’s effectiveness within the accounting.

Part Four: Shafāʿah at the resurrection—accountability, justice, and permission

Here, shafāʿah (often mistranslated as “intercession”) remains the concept under examination: joining in support, with its meaning and limits read in context.

The Day of Accounting: actions revealed for what they are

The preceding parts established shafāʿah’s connection to accompaniment and support, including its good and bad forms among people. All beings act within God’s established order; no supporter can act independently of His knowledge, truth, permission, and absolute sovereignty.

How do these principles apply at the resurrection, where the Qur’an both denies shafāʿah and conditions its effectiveness on permission and approval? We must first understand the accounting itself. Is this a day when consultation, connections, and personal influence determine people’s destinies, or one when the truth of their actions and consequences becomes manifest within divine justice?

Al-Baqarah states:

وَاتَّقُوا يَوْمًا تُرْجَعُونَ فِيهِ إِلَى اللَّهِ ۖ ثُمَّ تُوَفَّىٰ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا كَسَبَتْ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

2:281

“Be mindful of a day when you will be returned to God. Then every soul will receive in full what it has earned, and they will not be wronged.” (2:281)

Three elements are joined: return to God, receiving the full consequences of one’s conduct, and the exclusion of injustice. The subject is what a person has done, not someone else’s power to decide their destiny. Each person faces the consequences of their own conduct under a judgment free of injustice.

Al-Anbiya makes this still more explicit:

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا ۖ وَإِنْ كَانَ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ أَتَيْنَا بِهَا ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ

21:47

“We shall set up the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul will be wronged in anything. Even if a deed weighs as little as a mustard seed, We shall bring it forth. We are sufficient as reckoners.” (21:47)

No action, however small, escapes the accounting. God is sufficient to reckon it, and no right is lost through ignorance or inability. This poses a fundamental challenge to shafāʿah conceived as personal influence changing judgment: if knowledge and justice are complete, what defect is the supporter repairing? God knows any violated right; any good action enters the account; no injustice is hidden. Shafāʿah’s role cannot rest on assumed ignorance or inability in God’s administration of justice.

A day when bargaining, friendship, and shafāʿah cannot secure an escape

Several Qur’anic passages expressly reject reliance on shafāʿah imagined as a power independent of God at the accounting.

Al-Baqarah states:

وَاتَّقُوا يَوْمًا لَا تَجْزِي نَفْسٌ عَنْ نَفْسٍ شَيْئًا وَلَا يُقْبَلُ مِنْهَا شَفَاعَةٌ وَلَا يُؤْخَذُ مِنْهَا عَدْلٌ وَلَا هُمْ يُنْصَرُونَ

2:48

“Be mindful of a day when no soul can discharge anything on behalf of another, no shafāʿah will be accepted from it, no ransom will be taken from it, and they will not be helped.” (2:48)

The warning returns with a slightly different order:

وَاتَّقُوا يَوْمًا لَا تَجْزِي نَفْسٌ عَنْ نَفْسٍ شَيْئًا وَلَا يُقْبَلُ مِنْهَا عَدْلٌ وَلَا تَنْفَعُهَا شَفَاعَةٌ وَلَا هُمْ يُنْصَرُونَ

2:123

“Be mindful of a day when no soul can discharge anything on behalf of another, no ransom will be accepted from it, no shafāʿah will benefit it, and they will not be helped.” (2:123)

Shafāʿah appears alongside ransom and outside help: the routes people use in worldly relationships to escape consequences. Money, influential patrons, or powerful protectors may secure an escape from human punishment. The Qur’an rejects that expectation at the resurrection. It does not advise choosing a better intermediary; it rejects reliance on something outside individual responsibility and the reality of one’s actions.

The same point is addressed directly to believers in 2:254:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْفِقُوا مِمَّا رَزَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَأْتِيَ يَوْمٌ لَا بَيْعٌ فِيهِ وَلَا خُلَّةٌ وَلَا شَفَاعَةٌ ۗ وَالْكَافِرُونَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

2:254

“You who have believed, give from what We have provided for you before a day arrives on which there is no trading, no friendship, and no shafāʿah. Those who reject the truth are the wrongdoers.” (2:254)

The opening command to give makes the contrast clear. This life offers an opportunity to act. People cannot waste that opportunity and assume that bargaining, friendship, or shafāʿah will secure the desired outcome afterward. Friendship may support cooperation in this world, but it cannot purchase an altered record of actions.

The denial in this verse contains no stated exception. We should not insert “without permission” into its wording. Yet the next verse, 2:255, makes shafāʿah conditional on permission. Both statements must be preserved: the first expressly rejects transactional reliance on shafāʿah; the next states permission as its condition. Reading them together closes imagined routes to bargaining over one’s destiny while locating valid shafāʿah within divine permission. Other passages are needed to clarify its nature.

Two further examples help explain this manner of expression. Concerning friends, the Qur’an states:

الْأَخِلَّاءُ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَعْضُهُمْ لِبَعْضٍ عَدُوٌّ إِلَّا الْمُتَّقِينَ

43:67

“Close friends will be enemies to one another that day, except those mindful of God.” (43:67)

Elsewhere it states:

يَوْمَ لَا يُغْنِي مَوْلًى عَنْ مَوْلًى شَيْئًا وَلَا هُمْ يُنْصَرُونَ

44:41

“A day when no ally can avail another in anything, nor will they be helped.” (44:41)

إِلَّا مَنْ رَحِمَ اللَّهُ ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الرَّحِيمُ

44:42

“Except those to whom God shows mercy. He is the Mighty, the Merciful.” (44:42)

These examples show that the Qur’an can make a broad denial and clarify an exception within the same context or another verse. But exceptions concerning friendship or mercy must not simply be substituted for shafāʿah’s exception. Each requires examination under its own conditions.

No one owns another person’s destiny

Al-Infitar describes ownership and authority on the Day of Recompense:

يَوْمَ لَا تَمْلِكُ نَفْسٌ لِنَفْسٍ شَيْئًا ۖ وَالْأَمْرُ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِلَّهِ

82:19

“A day when no soul possesses anything for another soul. All command that day belongs to God.” (82:19)

This is one of the clearest standards for evaluating claims about shafāʿah in the hereafter. No person independently owns or controls another; sovereignty belongs entirely to God. Any role played by a prophet, angel, or other honored servant cannot create independent ownership of someone else’s destiny. The Qur’an expressly excludes it.

Maryam also states:

وَكُلُّهُمْ آتِيهِ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَرْدًا

19:95

“Every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone.” (19:95)

Individual return does not deny all relationships or testimony at the resurrection—the Qur’an itself describes groups being gathered and witnesses testifying. It means that no one can take over another person’s identity and responsibility.

This fits 4:85. A supporter participates in another’s undertaking, but the original actor is not relieved of accountability. The supporter becomes responsible as well. Sharing in an action differs from taking someone else’s place in the judgment of their actions. Good and bad shafāʿah involve a chosen contribution to another’s conduct. Guaranteed shafāʿah instead imagines a patron changing the outcome of judgment through influence while the person’s actual record remains unchanged. The Qur’an provides no foundation for that assumption.

Religious wishes and affiliations do not determine salvation

Al-Nisa separates salvation from communal wishful thinking:

لَيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ وَلَا أَمَانِيِّ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ ۗ مَنْ يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا يُجْزَ بِهِ وَلَا يَجِدْ لَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

4:123

“It is neither according to your wishes nor the wishes of the People of the Book. Whoever does wrong will be recompensed for it and will find no protector or helper apart from God.” (4:123)

Although the word “shafāʿah” does not appear, the verse is central to the issue. Religious wishes do not determine the outcome. No community can bypass accountability merely by identifying with a scripture, prophet, or religious identity. The following verse, 4:124, identifies belief and righteous action as the criteria for entering Paradise without suffering injustice. “People of the Book” here refers to communities associated with earlier scripture.

Judgment is therefore grounded in belief, action, and justice, rather than sectarian wishes. The claim that belonging to a prophet’s community, a family, or a sect is sufficient for salvation conflicts with this Qur’anic principle. That does not close the door to forgiveness: other passages speak of repentance and God’s expansive mercy. The target is not mercy but invented guarantees of salvation based on wishful thinking.

Shafāʿah that offers no benefit: the consequences of a life

Al-Muddaththir contains an especially explicit statement about shafāʿah’s ineffectiveness for a particular group. It begins:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ رَهِينَةٌ

74:38

“Every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned.” (74:38)

After excepting the “companions of the right”—those in the favorable position at judgment—the passage depicts a conversation concerning the guilty. They are asked:

مَا سَلَكَكُمْ فِي سَقَرَ

74:42

“What brought you into Saqar?” (74:42) Saqar is a Qur’anic name for the punishment of Hell.

They reply:

قَالُوا لَمْ نَكُ مِنَ الْمُصَلِّينَ

74:43

“We were not among those who prayed.” (74:43)

وَلَمْ نَكُ نُطْعِمُ الْمِسْكِينَ

74:44

“And we did not feed the needy.” (74:44)

وَكُنَّا نَخُوضُ مَعَ الْخَائِضِينَ

74:45

“We joined those who indulged in falsehood.” (74:45)

وَكُنَّا نُكَذِّبُ بِيَوْمِ الدِّينِ

74:46

“And we denied the Day of Recompense.” (74:46)

حَتَّىٰ أَتَانَا الْيَقِينُ

74:47

“Until certainty came to us.” (74:47)

The passage concludes:

فَمَا تَنْفَعُهُمْ شَفَاعَةُ الشَّافِعِينَ

74:48

“So the shafāʿah of supporters does not benefit them.” (74:48)

The lack of benefit is directly connected to their way of life. They do not attribute their condition to lacking a supporter. They acknowledge neglecting prayer, disregarding the poor, joining falsehood, and denying the resurrection. The existence—or imagined existence—of a supporter cannot erase that record’s consequences.

“Them” refers to this group with these specified actions. It should not become an absolute denial of all shafāʿah for everyone. Equally, the limited audience does not prove that shafāʿah will necessarily occur for everyone else. What the verse establishes is that shafāʿah cannot cover over the truth of human conduct.

This also connects with causal coupling: actions are not isolated or without effect. Decisions and chosen associations take shape in a person’s eventual condition. “We joined those who indulged in falsehood” is a direct example of freely entering a course of conduct and encountering its consequences. The resurrection reveals the reality of the path taken; it is not an opportunity to bypass it through connections unrelated to truth.

Requests for shafāʿah at the resurrection and the limits of permission

Al-A‘raf depicts people confronting the final outcome of their actions:

هَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا تَأْوِيلَهُ ۚ يَوْمَ يَأْتِي تَأْوِيلُهُ يَقُولُ الَّذِينَ نَسُوهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ قَدْ جَاءَتْ رُسُلُ رَبِّنَا بِالْحَقِّ فَهَلْ لَنَا مِنْ شُفَعَاءَ فَيَشْفَعُوا لَنَا أَوْ نُرَدُّ فَنَعْمَلَ غَيْرَ الَّذِي كُنَّا نَعْمَلُ ۚ قَدْ خَسِرُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ وَضَلَّ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

7:53

“Are they waiting for anything but its fulfillment? On the day its fulfillment arrives, those who had previously neglected it will say: ‘Our Lord’s messengers did bring the truth. Do we have any supporters who will stand with us? Or could we be sent back to act differently from the way we used to act?’ They have lost themselves, and what they used to fabricate has deserted them.” (7:53)

The request for supporters appears beside the wish to return to earthly life and act differently. Together, they reveal a desire to escape the consequences of past conduct. These people now acknowledge the messengers’ truth, but reach for shafāʿah or a return only after the opportunity to choose and act has passed. The Qur’an presents neither wish as deliverance; it ends with their loss and the disappearance of their inventions. Their request therefore does not endorse their conception of shafāʿah. It expresses regret over failing to join the truth during their lives. A later request cannot make their previous choices as though they never occurred.

The permission exception: no independent power outside truth

Alongside the denials, other verses make shafāʿah’s benefit conditional on permission and approval. Ta-Ha offers one of the clearest examples:

يَوْمَئِذٍ لَا تَنْفَعُ الشَّفَاعَةُ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَرَضِيَ لَهُ قَوْلًا

20:109

“On that day, shafāʿah will not benefit anyone except one to whom the All-Merciful has given permission and for whom He has approved speech.” (20:109)

يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِهِ عِلْمًا

20:110

“He knows what lies before them and behind them, while they do not encompass Him in knowledge.” (20:110)

The resurrection setting—mountains transformed and voices hushed—shows that the permission exception is not confined to this world. Yet no independent authority is delegated to a supporter. “One to whom He has given permission” does not identify a particular person by name, and the next verse immediately sets God’s complete knowledge against His servants’ limited understanding.

Other passages reinforce the same structure:

In 53:26, angelic shafāʿah depends on divine permission and approval.

In 21:28, honored servants offer support only for those God approves.

In 19:87, the condition is a covenant with the All-Merciful.

In 43:86, informed testimony to truth is connected to shafāʿah.

Read together, the two groups describe one order, rather than two opposed forms of shafāʿah operating in separate domains:

The denials close the door to independent, transactional shafāʿah that supposedly owns an outcome, modifies God’s knowledge or judgment, or bypasses justice. The exceptions place any shafāʿah that occurs within the same order of truth, permission, and approval. Permission is never a loophole through which individual responsibility or God’s established ways can be evaded.

A further question remains:

Is authorized shafāʿah at the resurrection solely the manifestation of relationships and support previously established in truth during earthly life? Or may a new utterance, request, or testimony also occur on that day within divine permission?

The resurrection: past consequences revealed, and speech in the present

Under the causal-coupling hypothesis, earthly behavior connects with causes, choices, and circumstances to produce real consequences. The resurrection can reveal and account for these effects in full. This fits the passages about recorded actions, bodily testimony, and precise assessment. But does that agreement imply that no new speech or action occurs at the resurrection?

The Qur’an does not support that restriction. Al-Naba states:

يَوْمَ يَقُومُ الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ صَفًّا ۖ لَا يَتَكَلَّمُونَ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَقَالَ صَوَابًا

78:38

“On the day the Spirit and the angels stand in rows, none will speak except one to whom the All-Merciful gives permission and who says what is right.” (78:38)

This describes speech on that very day. Resurrection scenes cannot therefore be reduced to a silent display of a past record. Speech is nevertheless bounded by permission and correctness. The verse does not use “shafāʿah,” and not every authorized utterance should be given that name. It does clarify the conditions of speech at the resurrection.

Read beside 20:109, it makes the connection between permission and acceptable speech clearer. In both settings, authorization to speak or shafāʿah’s effectiveness remains within truth and divine sovereignty. Two matters can therefore be distinguished:

1. The resurrection reveals and accounts for actions already performed, whose truth has never been hidden from God.

2. Speech and testimony also occur at the resurrection with permission. If connected to shafāʿah, they cannot take effect outside truth and divine judgment.

These are compatible. Just as bodily testimony reveals past conduct, authorized speech may have a place in judgment without changing the truth of that conduct. Nevertheless, authorized shafāʿah should not be reduced to mere testimony about the past. What is certain is that no route opens to a result contrary to God’s knowledge and truth.

Individual responsibility and actions joined with others

Al-Najm helps explain shafāʿah’s relationship to personal responsibility:

أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ

وَأَنْ لَيْسَ لِلْإِنْسَانِ إِلَّا مَا سَعَىٰ

وَأَنَّ سَعْيَهُ سَوْفَ يُرَىٰ

ثُمَّ يُجْزَاهُ الْجَزَاءَ الْأَوْفَىٰ

53:38, 53:39, 53:40, 53:41

“No bearer of a burden bears another’s burden; a human being has only what they have striven for; their striving will be seen; then they will receive its fullest recompense.” (53:38, 53:39, 53:40, 53:41)

These verses insist on the connection between effort, action, and consequence. Individual responsibility does not make cooperation or others’ support ineffective. As 4:85 showed, good or bad shafāʿah gives the supporter a share because entering that undertaking was their own decision. Their assistance is their own responsible action.

Helping someone do good or commit injustice does not transfer guilt or merit from one person to another. Each answers for their own role and conduct. This distinction is central to causal coupling: actions connect with other factors and people’s behavior to produce a shared outcome, but the connection erases neither participant’s responsibility nor moral identity. The resurrection reveals those actions and relationships. Authorized shafāʿah therefore cannot mean personal responsibility automatically disappearing through association with someone else.

Defending wrongdoing in this life and at the resurrection

Al-Nisa distinguishes human advocacy from divine judgment. Verses 4:105, 4:106, 4:107, 4:108 emphasize judgment in truth, refusing to defend betrayal, and the impossibility of hiding reality from God. The passage then states:

هَا أَنْتُمْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ جَادَلْتُمْ عَنْهُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا فَمَنْ يُجَادِلُ اللَّهَ عَنْهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ أَمْ مَنْ يَكُونُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَكِيلًا

4:109

“Here you are, arguing on their behalf in this worldly life. But who will argue with God for them on the Day of Resurrection, or who will take charge of their case?” (4:109)

The term here is argument or defense, but the issue directly concerns mediation in the accounting. People may influence human judges through concealment or pressure. That model fails before complete divine knowledge: those who betray may hide from people, but not from God, as 4:108 states.

The next verse is particularly significant:

وَمَنْ يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا أَوْ يَظْلِمْ نَفْسَهُ ثُمَّ يَسْتَغْفِرِ اللَّهَ يَجِدِ اللَّهَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

4:110

“Whoever does wrong or wrongs themselves, then asks God for forgiveness, will find God forgiving and merciful.” (4:110)

After rejecting the defense of betrayal before God, the Qur’an directs the way out straight to His forgiveness. A sinner needs neither an influential advocate nor concealment. The path is repentance and seeking forgiveness from God Himself. Judgment is not only punishment; forgiveness has a central place. But forgiveness differs from deception or manipulating a case through influence. God forgives knowingly and judges justly without needing someone else’s advocacy.

Divine mercy: forgiveness within perfect wisdom and justice

Transactional shafāʿah wrongly sets mercy against justice. It imagines God first issuing a just judgment, then a supporter pressuring Him into forgiveness He had not wished to grant. Mercy becomes the product of the supporter’s leverage, while justice becomes its obstacle.

The Qur’an instead attributes mercy and forgiveness to God Himself. Al-Zumar states:

قُلْ يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

39:53

“Say: My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God’s mercy. God forgives all sins. He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.” (39:53)

This promise of mercy is immediately followed in 39:54 by a call to return and submit to the Lord. Mercy does not make action or responsibility meaningless. The Qur’an guards against both despair and heedlessness. Al-Nisa 4:48 also makes forgiveness of sins less grave than associating partners with God dependent on His will. We cannot therefore assign a certain outcome to every person and sin independently of that will.

God’s established order must not be reduced to a merciless mechanism with no place for forgiveness. Mercy belongs to His own attributes and actions and is not outside His wisdom and justice. Shafāʿah benefiting someone within divine mercy does not mean justice has lost to a supporter’s influence. Forgiveness itself arises from God’s knowledge, will, and encompassing mercy.

The resurrection and the causal-coupling hypothesis

We can now reconsider this part’s findings within the hypothesis of actions joining with supporting causes.

In this life, people freely decide and act. Their actions occur among causes, circumstances, others’ conduct, and God’s established laws. One person shares in a good undertaking by supporting it; another assumes responsibility by joining a wrongful course. Conduct also affects inner character, as 83:14 describes hearts becoming coated with rust through what people have done. Choice and divine law are not opposed: people choose their course, while its consequences develop within an order they neither created nor independently own.

The resurrection brings this reality fully into view. Actions, chosen associations, and resulting consequences appear in the accounting. Recorded deeds, bodily testimony, a record speaking truth, and scales of justice all express the reality of a human life laid bare. Shafāʿah cannot function here as a way of rewriting that history or concealing what happened.

Yet the hypothesis must not exceed explicit Qur’anic evidence. The Qur’an describes authorized speech and testimony at the resurrection and conditions shafāʿah’s benefit on permission and approval. We cannot categorically claim that shafāʿah there is only the direct outcome of earthly relationships, with no new activity or speech that day. Equally, authorized speech does not enable a supporter to change the past or produce a judgment contrary to divine knowledge and justice.

A more precise formulation follows:

The resurrection is the accounting and disclosure of actions and their connections within creation. Any authorized speech, testimony, or shafāʿah occurring that day belongs entirely within the same divine knowledge, truth, justice, and established order.

This preserves personal responsibility while taking seriously the explicit statements about permission and speech at the resurrection.

Conclusions from Part Four: shafāʿah remains subject to truth

The resurrection passages ground the accounting in knowledge, justice, and individual responsibility. People encounter the consequences of their conduct; no right is lost through divine ignorance or inability. Bargaining, personal connections, and self-appointed patronage cannot determine the result. Al-Baqarah 2:48, 2:123, and 2:254 expressly reject such reliance. Al-Muddaththir likewise states that shafāʿah will not benefit the people described as following deviation and denial.

The Qur’an also places shafāʿah under permission, approval, covenant, and right speech. A comprehensive reading must neither ignore these exceptions nor turn them into permits for personal influence. Five principles follow:

1. No concealment of reality. Shafāʿah cannot hide actions from God, whose knowledge is complete.

2. No transfer of sovereignty. Shafāʿah does not hand ownership of human destiny to a supporter. Authority on the Day of Recompense belongs exclusively to God.

3. Personal responsibility remains. Joining others in good or evil makes the supporter responsible without relieving the original actor of their own burden.

4. Mercy originates with God. Forgiveness comes from the Lord Himself, not pressure or influence applied to His decision.

5. Authorized shafāʿah is governed by truth. Speech and shafāʿah at the resurrection can be effective only within divine truth, knowledge, will, and justice.

Whatever its precise workings, shafāʿah cannot bypass divine accounting or cancel the reality of actions. Any benefit occurs within the Lord’s own permission, mercy, and established ways.

This agrees with causal coupling: human behavior connects with various causes and agents, and its effects do not disappear at judgment. The resurrection reveals those relationships; it is no marketplace for rewriting the past through influential intermediaries.

A final evaluation remains necessary. Can the hypothesis explain the entire range of shafāʿah—from good and bad shafāʿah in this life to permission and approval in the hereafter—without strained assumptions? We must also draw a clear boundary between explicit Qur’anic statements, analytical conclusions, and interpretive hypotheses.

Before that evaluation in Part Six, Part Five examines direct access to God and the search for supporters.

Part Five: Direct access to God—does the Qur’an command us to seek intermediaries?

A question prior to whether shafāʿah exists

We have examined shafāʿah’s meaning, divine permission, the supporter’s role, and the resurrection. Whenever the Qur’an discusses shafāʿah, it remains inseparable from God’s knowledge, truth, permission, and absolute sovereignty. We now return to a more basic question, one that comes before identifying supporters or describing their powers:

Does God command people to seek supporters who will petition or negotiate with Him for closeness, forgiveness, protection from harm, or salvation? Or does He call His servants to address Him directly, ask His forgiveness, serve Him sincerely, and work toward what is right?

These questions must not be confused. Saying that shafāʿah may occur with divine permission does not instruct people to find a supporter and request intervention in their destiny.

A phenomenon occurring within God’s order is not in itself a command to turn to it. A being’s ability to affect something does not legitimize every possible request for its assistance. There is a fundamental difference between what God describes as a reality within existence and what He instructs His servants to do.

To locate shafāʿah properly in religious practice, we must therefore examine the Qur’an’s direct instructions. To whom does God direct people when they are in need, repent, or seek deliverance?

God describes Himself as near and responsive

Al-Baqarah states:

وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِي عَنِّي فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ ۖ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ ۖ فَلْيَسْتَجِيبُوا لِي وَلْيُؤْمِنُوا بِي لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْشُدُونَ

2:186

“When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the caller’s prayer when they call upon Me. Let them respond to Me and believe in Me, so that they may find the right course.”

Although the question is addressed to the Prophet, the answer comes immediately in God’s own voice: “I am near.” He does not restrict this nearness to a group, family, or rank, nor does He make answering prayer conditional on approaching a supporter.

“I answer the caller’s prayer when they call upon Me” describes a person addressing God directly and God Himself responding. The Prophet is not made the exclusive channel through which prayers are heard, and no other being is placed between servant and Lord.

A divine answer does not necessarily mean immediate fulfillment exactly according to human wishes and schedules. God’s knowledge and wisdom exceed ours. But those limits do not undermine either the possibility or the legitimacy of addressing Him directly.

Nor is this a one-sided relationship. After promising a response, God calls people to respond to Him and remain faithful: “Let them respond to Me and believe in Me.” A person is not simply to submit demands and leave someone else responsible for their fulfillment. Prayer, belief, and responsibility belong together. This differs fundamentally from dependence on intermediaries. Since God calls Himself near, no intermediary is needed to fill a supposed gap before a prayer can be heard.

The command: “Call upon Me”

Ghafir states this direct relationship as an explicit command:

وَقَالَ رَبُّكُمُ ادْعُونِي أَسْتَجِبْ لَكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ عَنْ عِبَادَتِي سَيَدْخُلُونَ جَهَنَّمَ دَاخِرِينَ

40:60

“Your Lord says: ‘Call upon Me; I will answer you. Those too arrogant to worship Me will enter Hell humiliated.’”

“Call upon Me” is direct. God does not say to find supporters who will mention us to Him or entrust our requests to prestigious figures for delivery. The verse then describes turning away from this relationship as arrogance in refusing to worship. Prayer is therefore more than a device for meeting needs: it expresses devotion and conscious attention to the Lord.

Al-Jinn makes the point still clearer:

وَأَنَّ الْمَسَاجِدَ لِلَّهِ فَلَا تَدْعُوا مَعَ اللَّهِ أَحَدًا

72:18

“The places of prostration belong to God, so do not invoke anyone alongside God.”

Alongside the call to address God is an explicit prohibition on invoking another with Him in worship. “Anyone” allows no exception for elevated status. A person’s high standing with God does not authorize making that person an addressee of worship.

This does not prohibit ordinary mutual assistance. The Qur’an itself describes exchange, cooperation, and asking others to pray for good. The concern is invoking beings in worship or attributing unseen powers to them, rather than ordinary human interaction. We must distinguish asking a person for help within their abilities from invoking a being as an unseen power or a necessary intermediary to God.

Al-Fatihah places this orientation toward God alone in the servant’s own words:

إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ

1:5

“You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.”

The verse identifies the direction of worship and ultimate reliance. People use means and receive help from others without placing any means in the Lord’s position. Together, these passages clearly call people into direct relationship with the one God. A personal supporter is not made a condition of access.

Intermediaries sought as a way of drawing near to God

What, then, does the Qur’an say of those who invoke and worship other beings, not as the ultimate creator, but as a way of approaching God?

Al-Zumar states:

أَلَا لِلَّهِ الدِّينُ الْخَالِصُ ۚ وَالَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِهِ أَوْلِيَاءَ مَا نَعْبُدُهُمْ إِلَّا لِيُقَرِّبُونَا إِلَى اللَّهِ زُلْفَىٰ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَحْكُمُ بَيْنَهُمْ فِي مَا هُمْ فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي مَنْ هُوَ كَاذِبٌ كَفَّارٌ

39:3

“Surely, sincere devotion belongs to God. Those who take protectors apart from Him say: ‘We worship them only so that they may bring us nearer to God.’ God will judge between them concerning their disagreements. God does not guide someone who is lying and persistently ungrateful.” (39:3)

The key is that those being criticized do not present their objects of worship as the ultimate creator. They justify worshiping them as a route to the Lord. Deviation therefore need not involve denying God or believing in several creators. Someone may regard God as the final destination while worshiping others as a means of reaching Him.

The Qur’an rejects that justification. It begins with the requirement of sincere devotion and challenges the claim of approaching God through other protectors. Two directions must be distinguished: approaching Him through belief, righteous conduct, and following His guidance; and worshiping others in the expectation of approaching Him. Nearness itself is not rejected. Worship of others is not justified by the intention to attain it.

Yunus presents the same issue explicitly as shafāʿah:

وَيَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَضُرُّهُمْ وَلَا يَنْفَعُهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ هَٰؤُلَاءِ شُفَعَاؤُنَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ ۚ قُلْ أَتُنَبِّئُونَ اللَّهَ بِمَا لَا يَعْلَمُ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

10:18

“They worship, apart from God, things that can neither harm nor benefit them, and say: ‘These are our supporters before God.’ Say: ‘Are you informing God of something in the heavens or the earth that He does not know?’ Glory be to Him! He is far above the partners they associate with Him.” (10:18)

This verse directly examines a claim to supporters in connection with worship of beings other than God. The claimants describe their figures as intermediaries before God, rather than asserting independence from Him. The response appeals to complete divine knowledge: what do they know about these beings’ standing that God does not? “A supporter before God” is not a title that establishes its own legitimacy.

Both verses expose the same pattern. Someone appoints an intermediary, then treats the intention of drawing near as proof that the method is right. But the intention does not validate the chosen route. Anyone who regards God as the destination must also conform their prayer and worship to His instruction.

Fatir clarifies the issue through God’s ownership and the limits of the invoked beings’ hearing and response:

يُولِجُ اللَّيْلَ فِي النَّهَارِ وَيُولِجُ النَّهَارَ فِي اللَّيْلِ وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ ۖ كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ لَهُ الْمُلْكُ ۚ وَالَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ مَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِنْ قِطْمِيرٍ

35:13

“He causes night to enter day and day to enter night, and has subjected the sun and moon, each running to an appointed term. Such is God, your Lord; dominion belongs to Him. Those you invoke apart from Him do not possess even the thin skin of a date stone.” (35:13)

إِنْ تَدْعُوهُمْ لَا يَسْمَعُوا دُعَاءَكُمْ وَلَوْ سَمِعُوا مَا اسْتَجَابُوا لَكُمْ ۖ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يَكْفُرُونَ بِشِرْكِكُمْ ۚ وَلَا يُنَبِّئُكَ مِثْلُ خَبِيرٍ

35:14

“If you call them, they do not hear your prayer; even if they heard, they could not answer you. On the Day of Resurrection they will disown your association of them with God. None can inform you like One who knows.” (35:14)

The passage challenges more than independent ownership. It challenges invoking these beings and imagining that they hear and answer. Yet it must be read with reference to the particular objects of invocation and conduct under criticism, rather than extended without evidence to every ordinary request made to another person. Evaluating a devotional practice requires attention to the object, manner, and addressee of the appeal.

A means of approach: seeking nearness or relying on a personal intermediary?

Al-Ma’idah 5:35 is sometimes cited as requiring intermediaries:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَابْتَغُوا إِلَيْهِ الْوَسِيلَةَ وَجَاهِدُوا فِي سَبِيلِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

5:35

“You who have believed, be mindful of God, seek a means of approach to Him, and strive in His path, so that you may prosper.” (5:35)

The verse commands seeking a wasīlah, a means of approach. Its destination and context matter: the direction is “to Him,” and the command sits between mindfulness of God and striving in His path. The person is called to conscious restraint, movement, and effort. No individual or being is identified as a necessary intermediary. Although the verse does not list every possible means, its direction is clear: seek an approach to God through one’s own action and mindfulness.

Al-Baqarah describes practical ways of seeking help:

وَاسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ ۚ وَإِنَّهَا لَكَبِيرَةٌ إِلَّا عَلَى الْخَاشِعِينَ

2:45

“Seek help through patience and prayer. This is difficult except for the humble.” (2:45)

It repeats the instruction directly to believers:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

2:153

“You who have believed, seek help through patience and prayer. God is with those who are patient.” (2:153)

These passages direct people toward patience and prayer rather than dependence on revered individuals. Patience sustains perseverance through trials; prayer keeps attention directed toward God. Both are the servant’s own activity and reinforce responsibility. These verses use “seek help,” not the word wasīlah. Nevertheless, read alongside 5:35, they connect seeking nearness with patience, prayer, and personal conduct, rather than making another being an intermediary in prayer and worship.

Al-Isra makes the distinction clearer:

قُلِ ادْعُوا الَّذِينَ زَعَمْتُمْ مِنْ دُونِهِ فَلَا يَمْلِكُونَ كَشْفَ الضُّرِّ عَنْكُمْ وَلَا تَحْوِيلًا

17:56

“Say: Call those you claim apart from Him. They have no power to remove harm from you or to redirect it.” (17:56)

This is a challenge exposing the claim’s failure, not a devotional instruction to invoke them. The next verse states:

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ يَبْتَغُونَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمُ الْوَسِيلَةَ أَيُّهُمْ أَقْرَبُ وَيَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَهُ وَيَخَافُونَ عَذَابَهُ ۚ إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّكَ كَانَ مَحْذُورًا

17:57

“Those whom they invoke themselves seek a means of approach to their Lord, striving to be nearer; they hope for His mercy and fear His punishment. Your Lord’s punishment is indeed to be feared.” (17:57)

The very beings invoked are themselves seeking nearness, hoping for mercy, and fearing punishment. That defeats the idea of an intermediary so close to God that they no longer remain a servant who needs to seek Him. Even the nearest servants need His mercy and turn toward Him.

Reading 5:35 beside 17:56, 17:57 establishes a fundamental distinction. People are commanded to seek a means of approach, while the beings they invoke are themselves seekers of that means. The command “seek a means of approach to Him” therefore cannot by itself become an instruction to invoke other people. Seeking nearness to God differs from making another servant an addressee of prayer and worship.

The prophet’s role: directing people toward God

How do prophets fit this relationship? The Qur’an describes them as conveying revelation, inviting people to truth, warning, bringing good news, and providing guidance. Direct relationship with God never means dispensing with His message or His messengers’ guidance.

Ali ‘Imran states:

قُلْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تُحِبُّونَ اللَّهَ فَاتَّبِعُونِي يُحْبِبْكُمُ اللَّهُ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ذُنُوبَكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

3:31

“Say: If you love God, follow me; God will love you and forgive your sins. God is forgiving and merciful.” (3:31)

Following the Prophet is connected here to God’s love and forgiveness. But God alone forgives, and the command is “follow me.” It does not say to invoke the Prophet so that he can obtain forgiveness in our place, or to entrust salvation to a personal connection with him. The Prophet makes the way of guidance clear. Following his message is a path toward God; it does not make him the owner of forgiveness or God’s replacement.

The Qur’an also states the limits of the Prophet’s power:

قُلْ لَا أَمْلِكُ لِنَفْسِي نَفْعًا وَلَا ضَرًّا إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ ۚ وَلَوْ كُنْتُ أَعْلَمُ الْغَيْبَ لَاسْتَكْثَرْتُ مِنَ الْخَيْرِ وَمَا مَسَّنِيَ السُّوءُ ۚ إِنْ أَنَا إِلَّا نَذِيرٌ وَبَشِيرٌ لِقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

7:188

“Say: I possess no benefit or harm even for myself, except what God wills. If I knew the unseen, I would have acquired abundant good and no harm would have touched me. I am only a warner and a bearer of good news for people who believe.” (7:188)

If the Prophet does not independently control benefit and harm even for himself, on what explicit Qur’anic basis could he be given independent control over others’ benefit, harm, or salvation? Recognizing this does not diminish prophethood; it clarifies its nature. A prophet is God’s messenger, not a partner in His sovereignty. His task is to convey the message and direct servants to their Lord, not to become someone they must invoke before they can reach God.

We must distinguish following the Prophet to learn God’s way from making him a necessary intermediary in prayer and worship. The Qur’an expressly commands the first; the second does not follow from its shafāʿah passages.

Asking others to pray: support without control of forgiveness

If access to God is direct, why does the Qur’an describe asking others to pray and seek forgiveness?

In Yusuf, Jacob’s sons acknowledge their wrongdoing and ask their father to seek forgiveness for them:

قَالُوا يَا أَبَانَا اسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا خَاطِئِينَ

12:97

“They said: ‘Our father, ask forgiveness for our sins. We have indeed done wrong.’” (12:97)

قَالَ سَوْفَ أَسْتَغْفِرُ لَكُمْ رَبِّي ۖ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

12:98

“He said: ‘I will ask my Lord to forgive you. He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.’” (12:98)

The sons humbly acknowledge their guilt and ask for their father’s prayer. Jacob leaves forgiveness entirely to his Lord. He does not claim to forgive, and they do not evade responsibility. This is a clear example of spiritual support between people. Asking someone to pray is far removed from surrendering control of one’s destiny to that person.

Al-Nisa also states:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا مِنْ رَسُولٍ إِلَّا لِيُطَاعَ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ۚ وَلَوْ أَنَّهُمْ إِذْ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ جَاءُوكَ فَاسْتَغْفَرُوا اللَّهَ وَاسْتَغْفَرَ لَهُمُ الرَّسُولُ لَوَجَدُوا اللَّهَ تَوَّابًا رَحِيمًا

4:64

“We sent no messenger except to be obeyed by God’s permission. Had they, when they wronged themselves, come to you and asked God for forgiveness, and had the Messenger asked forgiveness for them, they would have found God accepting of repentance and merciful.” (4:64)

The wrongdoers themselves ask God’s forgiveness, and the Messenger’s request joins theirs. His prayer does not replace their repentance or their own request. The verse ends by attributing acceptance of repentance and mercy to God. Moreover, “come to you” concerns coming to the Prophet during his life. The wording does not in itself establish invoking him after death or a permanent role for him as an unseen intermediary who receives and answers appeals.

Al-Munafiqun supplies another important example:

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا يَسْتَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ لَوَّوْا رُءُوسَهُمْ وَرَأَيْتَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ وَهُمْ مُسْتَكْبِرُونَ

63:5

“When they are told, ‘Come, so that God’s Messenger may ask forgiveness for you,’ they turn their heads, and you see them turn away arrogantly.” (63:5)

سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَسْتَغْفَرْتَ لَهُمْ أَمْ لَمْ تَسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ لَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

63:6

“It is the same for them whether you ask forgiveness for them or do not ask: God will not forgive them. God does not guide the defiantly disobedient.” (63:6)

The first verse describes a particular invitation to come for the Messenger’s prayer and condemns the arrogant refusal. The next denies that prayer’s benefit for this same group. Asking the Prophet to pray is therefore neither intrinsically forbidden nor a guarantee of forgiveness. Neither verse gives a general command to establish a permanent intermediary in worship or transfer the authority to forgive to the Prophet.

In 9:103, God instructs the Prophet to pray for a group and describes his prayer as a source of reassurance. It has a real effect, but the verse concludes by calling God hearing and knowing, rather than dependent on the Prophet’s report or influence. Conversely, 9:80 shows that even repeated prophetic requests for forgiveness do not remove forgiveness from God’s control or guarantee it for the hypocritical group addressed.

Together, these passages affirm prayer and requests for others’ forgiveness without making the person praying the owner of mercy or a substitute for God. Spiritual support is good; it does not establish that God needs an intermediary to communicate with His servants.

Shafāʿah occurring is different from a command to seek a supporter

The Throne Verse, 2:255, conditions shafāʿah on permission; 21:28 and 53:26 likewise connect it to approval and permission. These passages describe its possibility and conditions.

Do they also command people to find supporters, invoke them, or demand salvation from them?

No such instruction appears in their wording. They neither identify a supporter to invoke nor teach a devotional method for approaching one. A statement that something may happen under specified conditions in God’s order does not authorize people to assign ownership of it to anyone they choose or invoke that person at will.

Divine permission is a condition of validity and effectiveness within God’s order, not a religious command to find influential intermediaries. The fact that God permits something to occur does not itself instruct anyone to seek its help. Moreover, 4:85 allows good and bad shafāʿah: occurrence alone cannot legitimize every kind of mediation.

To establish a devotional practice’s legitimacy, citing shafāʿah’s possibility is insufficient. The practice itself, what is requested, and the manner of approach must be assessed against explicit Qur’anic instructions.

The decisive principle is:

The possibility of shafāʿah with divine permission is never identical to a divine command to seek and invoke a supporter.

This preserves the place of authorized shafāʿah within creation while preventing a conditional possibility from becoming a self-invented devotional requirement.

Where does the path of repentance and forgiveness lead?

When someone sins, to whom does the Qur’an direct that person?

Ali ‘Imran states:

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا فَعَلُوا فَاحِشَةً أَوْ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ ذَكَرُوا اللَّهَ فَاسْتَغْفَرُوا لِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَمَنْ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

3:135

“Those who, when they commit an indecency or wrong themselves, remember God and ask forgiveness for their sins—and who forgives sins except God?—and do not knowingly persist in what they have done.” (3:135)

After wrongdoing, the person turns immediately to remembering God and asking His forgiveness. The question “Who forgives sins except God?” locates forgiveness exclusively with the Lord. The same passage requires people to stop knowingly persisting in wrongdoing. Seeking forgiveness is inseparable from reform and responsibility.

Al-Nisa states:

وَمَنْ يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا أَوْ يَظْلِمْ نَفْسَهُ ثُمَّ يَسْتَغْفِرِ اللَّهَ يَجِدِ اللَّهَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

4:110

“Whoever does wrong or wrongs themselves, then asks God for forgiveness, will find God forgiving and merciful.” (4:110)

This follows the discussion of defending betrayal and the impossibility of hiding it from God. In 4:109, the Qur’an asks who could defend those people before God at the resurrection; it then makes the path clear: the person asks God directly for forgiveness. They are not instructed to find an influential advocate to alter their record’s reality. This does not prohibit asking others to pray. It does establish that forgiveness is not conditional on a necessary intermediary.

In Al-Zumar, the Lord directly addresses His servants who have done wrong:

قُلْ يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِنْ رَحْمَةِ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

39:53

“Say: My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of God’s mercy. God forgives all sins. He is the Forgiving, the Merciful.” (39:53)

The next verse, 39:54, immediately calls them to return and submit to their Lord. Hope rests entirely on His encompassing mercy, not on finding someone who can pressure Him into pardoning. Even in sin and the search for deliverance, the relationship remains direct. God Himself forgives, opens the way back, and calls people to seek forgiveness. This undercuts the notion of an intermediary without whose intervention divine mercy cannot reach a servant.

Does direct access to God exclude all created means?

Emphasis on direct access might be mistaken for a prohibition on seeking anyone’s help or benefiting from guidance and practical means. That conclusion does not fit the Qur’an as a whole.

The Qur’an calls for cooperation in goodness and mindfulness of God in 5:2, describes prophets conveying revelation and guidance, and records requests for others’ prayers in 12:97, 12:98 and 4:64. Direct relationship with God does not deny the role of means and human contributions in life.

The essential distinction is between a means or helper and the ultimate authority. A teacher imparts knowledge, a physician treats illness, a person supports someone else, and a prophet shows the way. None thereby acquires independent ownership of benefit, harm, forgiveness, or human destiny.

The same distinction applies to shafāʿah:

1. Good shafāʿah among people does not authorize worship of the supporter.

2. Possible authorized shafāʿah in the hereafter does not itself command invoking a supporter in this life.

This avoids two fundamental errors:

Denying creatures any effective role in the world.

Turning their role into independent power or a necessary intermediary in worshiping God.

Within God’s established order, people make use of means while directing ultimate reliance, prayer, and worship exclusively to the one God.

Conclusions from Part Five: the direct relationship with the Lord

We can now answer the central question: does the Qur’an command people to seek supporters who will bargain with God for forgiveness, salvation, or nearness?

No such command appears in the passages examined. Instead, the Qur’an directs people to God in explicit terms:

In 2:186, God describes Himself as near and responsive to His servants’ prayers.

In 40:60, He commands directly: “Call upon Me.”

In 3:135 and 4:110, He directs the person who has sinned straight to seeking His forgiveness.

Conversely, 39:3 and 10:18 criticize worship of other beings justified by the hope of nearness or shafāʿah. The passages on a means of approach do not command invoking revered individuals: in 5:35, the destination is God Himself, while in 17:57 the supposed objects of worship are themselves seeking a means of approach to their Lord.

Requests for others to pray and seek forgiveness on someone’s behalf show that direct access to God leaves room for mutual goodwill and spiritual support. Such assistance does not make anyone the owner of mercy or forgiveness.

Three principles follow:

1. Direct access is fundamental. The Qur’an makes no personal intermediary a condition of prayer, worship, or seeking forgiveness.

2. Possibility differs from religious prescription. Authorized shafāʿah within God’s order does not command seeking a supporter. Its possible occurrence is fundamentally different from the legitimacy of invoking people as necessary intermediaries.

3. Created means remain within devotion to God alone. Prophets and righteous servants can guide and pray without assuming the authority that belongs to God. They act within His order and possess no independent control over mercy, benefit, harm, or destiny.

The practical implication is clear. Shafāʿah’s place within creation and judgment cannot be used to construct a devotional system dependent on intermediaries. The Qur’an does not require finding someone influential so that God will hear. He calls Himself near, invites direct prayer, declares mercy His own, and opens the way of return to everyone.

The issue is therefore deeper than whether a supporter exists or authorized shafāʿah is possible. When people stand in need and worship, whom do they address, whom do they invoke, and where do they place their ultimate hope? The Qur’an calls them directly to the one God, rather than toward a negotiator.

This neither denies created means nor ignores authorized shafāʿah. It locates each properly: causes and servants operate within God’s established order, while prayer, worship, seeking forgiveness, and ultimate hope return to the Lord Himself. Shafāʿah must not become a reason for inventing distance between servant and Creator—a distance the Qur’an itself removes by describing God as near and responsive.

Part Six: Testing the causal-coupling hypothesis against the Qur’an

Here, shafāʿah (often mistranslated as “intercession”) remains the concept under examination: joining in support, with its meaning and limits read in context.

From a word’s meaning to an account of how shafāʿah works

Having examined the root, social uses, permission and divine order, the supporter’s standing, and the resurrection, we can now assemble a coherent account. It must neither impose a later model of transactional mediation on the Qur’an nor discard passages simply to escape that model.

The central hypothesis is that human actions become coupled with supporting causes within God’s created order. No action is isolated from the world: conduct joins causes, conditions, and effective agents, and its outcome takes shape within God’s established laws. People choose freely without independently owning every cause or result of their choices.

Evaluating this requires distinguishing shafāʿah itself from the order within which it occurs. If every causal relationship becomes shafāʿah, the boundaries separating it from assistance, guidance, testimony, prayer, and natural law disappear. If it means only a holy person requesting forgiveness for sinners, much Qur’anic usage is excluded. We need a meaning faithful to the root image of pairing that can account for every use without altering the wording.

Shafāʿah joins in support without owning the result

The root and 4:85 establish the first firm finding. Pairing contrasts with singleness, while social shafāʿah joins a request, position, or undertaking. This is more than two objects standing alongside one another: it involves supportive participation. The supporter enters a situation through speech, standing, ability, or action to support something.

Such support is not inherently holy or saving. The Qur’an distinguishes good and bad shafāʿah and assigns the supporter a share of the consequences. Its moral value depends on the direction and object of the support.

This clarifies a further aspect of causal coupling. Joining another’s undertaking is itself a responsible action. Supporting good differs from assisting oppression. Accompaniment extends personal responsibility rather than negating it: the supporter answers for their contribution without taking over the original actor’s entire burden.

Shafāʿah is effective participation in support of a person, request, position, or undertaking. That participation does not itself establish ownership of the outcome.

Divine permission: effectiveness within God’s established order

The second finding concerns permission. Its Qur’anic uses—from affliction and death to causes taking effect, guidance, and belief—connect permission within creation to the order God has established for events to occur.

Yunus 10:3 makes the connection especially clear:

إِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ ۖ يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ ۖ مَا مِنْ شَفِيعٍ إِلَّا مِنْ بَعْدِ إِذْنِهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ فَاعْبُدُوهُ ۚ أَفَلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ

10:3

“Your Lord is God, who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then established Himself upon the Throne, governing all affairs. There is no supporter except after His permission. Such is God, your Lord: worship Him. Will you not take heed?” (10:3)

Shafāʿah follows creation, the Throne, and governance. It cannot therefore be an independent power opposing divine sovereignty. If all affairs belong to God’s governance, no supporter can establish a separate system for manipulating human destinies.

Permission is the condition of effectiveness within this order. A supporter does not first possess an independent power and then compel God to accept it. The possibility and limits of the role exist wholly within His governance. This rules out the human model of an influential courtier confronting a monarch.

Nevertheless, permission as a condition differs from shafāʿah as a concept. Divine laws establish the setting for effects, but not everything occurring through them—rain, plant growth, or a moving ship—is technically called shafāʿah. The hypothesis remains precise only if shafāʿah is a particular form of joining and support within that order, rather than a general name for all causality.

Must the supporter be a conscious being?

Could a supporter be an effective structure within existence, or must the term refer to a conscious agent?

The root image permits a general understanding of two factors joining. Yet passages explicitly discussing shafāʿah describe notable features of the actor. In 21:26, 21:27, 21:28, honored servants do not precede God in speech, act by His command, possess limited knowledge, and experience awe. In 53:26, shafāʿah is attributed to angels.

It is therefore impossible to reduce every Qur’anic supporter to an impersonal process or lifeless law. Some are described as knowing, speaking, and standing in awe. Their personal agency, however, does not imply independence from God’s laws. An action’s agent may be a conscious human or an angel while its possibility and effectiveness remain within divine permission.

A physician’s contribution to recovery and a teacher’s contribution to learning are real, though neither independently owns every condition and result. Likewise, causal coupling does not require eliminating supporters’ personhood. What must be rejected is the notion that personal connections give anyone independent power over God’s judgment.

The honored status of the servants in 21:26 also leaves open the possibility that authorized shafāʿah may express God’s honoring of a servant within His command. The verse does not explicitly define shafāʿah by that purpose. We should neither dismiss the possibility without evidence nor present it as the established mechanism. Independence is not the only issue: we must also ask under what conditions, and for whom, authorized shafāʿah can be beneficial.

What deficiency would a supporter repair?

We return to the foundational question: what could a supporter contribute before the Lord? Reveal an unreported truth, disclose a hidden condition, create new mercy, or change a judgment grounded in complete knowledge?

The repeated connection with divine knowledge supplies the answer. In 2:255, shafāʿah is immediately accompanied by comprehensive knowledge. In 20:109, 20:110, permission and approved speech are followed by the same emphasis. In 21:28, knowledge and shafāʿah for someone approved appear together. Shafāʿah never remedies ignorance in God. If someone has a right, has repented, or has become righteous, He already knows.

Likewise, the speaking record in 23:62 and 45:29, bodily testimony in 36:65, and the Throne-bearers’ prayer for the repentant in 40:7 do not add to God’s knowledge or create mercy in Him. They participate in truth being expressed and made manifest within the order of accounting.

A supporter repairs no deficiency in divine knowledge, mercy, or justice. No such defect exists in God or His governance. Valid shafāʿah can be understood only within His knowledge, mercy, and judgment, never as an external force overcoming His will.

Truth and approval: why support is not automatically accepted

Since 4:85 distinguishes good and bad shafāʿah, support alone establishes no moral merit. When shafāʿah before God is discussed, the Qur’an adds permission, approval, covenant, and truthful testimony.

Al-Zukhruf states:

وَلَا يَمْلِكُ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِهِ الشَّفَاعَةَ إِلَّا مَنْ شَهِدَ بِالْحَقِّ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

43:86

“Those they invoke apart from Him do not possess shafāʿah, except those who bear witness to the truth and know it.” (43:86)

Informed truthful testimony excludes conjecture, unsupported assertion, and false speech as foundations for valid shafāʿah. Divine approval in 21:28 and covenant in 19:87 also exclude self-created rights. Naming someone a supporter does not confer a rank or authority. The Qur’an expressly rejects guarantees of salvation founded on sectarian attachment in 2:80 and 4:123.

Within the causal-coupling hypothesis, support is valid before God only when directed toward truth. Bad shafāʿah and support for falsehood cannot become acceptable by receiving a sacred name.

Is the resurrection only the disclosure of past consequences?

One part of the hypothesis concerns earthly actions joining causes and divine laws, with their outcome revealed at the resurrection. This fits personal responsibility and actions becoming manifest in 53:38, 53:39, 53:40, 53:41 and 74:38. The real record does not become irrelevant while connections alone decide destinies.

Yet another part of the evidence must be preserved. The Qur’an explicitly describes shafāʿah and speech on that very day, each subject to permission, in 20:109 and 78:38. The resurrection cannot be imagined as wholly silent, with no new utterance or activity.

The key distinction is between authorized speech occurring and a power to alter reality. Like bodily testimony, speech at the resurrection can belong to making truth manifest and putting it into effect. It cannot fabricate a record or produce judgment contrary to divine knowledge and justice.

A balanced formulation is therefore:

The resurrection accounts for and reveals actions and their connections within creation. Any authorized speech, testimony, or shafāʿah occurring there belongs wholly within divine knowledge, truth, justice, and established laws. It never provides a route around reality.

Reading denials and exceptions together

Considering both groups of shafāʿah passages resolves their apparent tension:

1. Unqualified denials. Passages such as 2:48, 2:123, and 2:254 reject reliance on shafāʿah alongside trade, ransom, and friendship. They exclude human transactional mechanisms from divine judgment. Confidence in someone else’s influence cannot replace personal responsibility and accountability.

2. Exceptions and permission. Passages such as 2:255, 20:109, and 53:26 place shafāʿah under permission, approval, and divine knowledge. They confer no independent authority. Every effect remains subordinate to the Lord’s sovereignty and established ways.

Reading these together prevents two errors. We must not translate an explicit denial as merely “no independent shafāʿah,” inserting a qualification absent from the individual verse. Nor may we interpret the permission exceptions as authority to bargain or act independently. The combined contexts reject transactional reliance and establish authorized shafāʿah’s dependence on truth and God’s will. There is no need either to insert invented qualifications into the denials or to remove the exceptions. Both preserve absolute divine sovereignty and exclude any person’s independent right to determine an afterlife outcome.

Responsibility: participation differs from substitution

Al-Najm 53:38, 53:39, 53:40, 53:41—“No bearer bears another’s burden” and “a human being has only what they have striven for”—is fully compatible with good and bad shafāʿah in 4:85:

Participation extends responsibility. Freely supporting good or evil creates a new share and responsibility for the supporter. It does not transfer the original actor’s guilt or merit to them.

There is no substitution. A supporter cannot replace a person’s actual course of life or record. Al-Muddaththir 74:42, 74:43, 74:44, 74:45, 74:46, 74:47, 74:48 shows that hope in a supporter cannot make up for neglecting prayer, giving, seeking truth, and belief.

Personal responsibility is also compatible with mercy and forgiveness. God’s forgiveness does not arise from ignorance or a supporter defeating justice. He forgives in wisdom and mercy with complete knowledge of conduct, repentance, and inner condition.

Does authorized shafāʿah authorize invoking a supporter?

A central finding is the distinction between authorized shafāʿah being possible in the hereafter and permission to invoke supporters in this life. The first does not establish the second. It does not authorize asking a supporter to avert harm or entrusting one’s destiny to them:

Supposed supporters are challenged. In 10:18 and 39:43, 39:44, the Qur’an criticizes invoking other beings and naming them supporters before God. It declares that all shafāʿah belongs to Him.

Asking for prayer differs from attributing saving power. Jacob’s sons ask their living father to pray in 12:97, 12:98. This differs fundamentally from claiming that someone owns forgiveness or invoking absent and deceased beings to secure benefit and prevent harm.

Permission passages do not authorize assigning unseen powers to others, invoking them, or guaranteeing salvation through attachment to revered figures. The Qur’anic order recognizes created means and supportive relationships, while locating ultimate authority over benefit, harm, prayer, and judgment exclusively with the Lord.

Answers to the study’s central questions

We can now answer the initial questions through the root’s meaning, permission within creation, the laws God has established, resurrection accounting, and direct access to the Lord. The answers distinguish explicit statements from analytical conclusions and the proposed causal-coupling hypothesis.

The preliminary question: does God command the search for intermediaries or call people directly to Himself?

The passages examined contain no general instruction to find influential figures who will negotiate with God for prayer, forgiveness, or salvation. Specific requests for the Prophet’s prayer occur in 63:5 and 4:64, but neither transfers forgiveness to his control. Conversely, 2:186 describes God as near and directly responsive; 40:60 commands “Call upon Me”; and 3:135 and 4:110 direct the wrongdoer straight to asking God for forgiveness.

The means of approach in 5:35 leads “to Him,” while 2:45 and 2:153 identify patience and prayer as practical ways of seeking help. Nearness and help are connected to the person’s own conduct, mindfulness, and worship. Conversely, 39:3 and 10:18 expressly reject worship of other beings justified by nearness or shafāʿah.

Asking a living person to pray, as in 12:97, 12:98 and 4:64, is far removed from making that person a necessary devotional intermediary or owner of forgiveness. The possibility of shafāʿah within divine permission therefore supplies no automatic authorization or command to seek and invoke supporters.

1. What is shafāʿah?

The root image is pairing or making a pair, contrasted with singleness. Socially, this develops into joining in support of a person, request, position, or undertaking. Al-Nisa 4:85 establishes that the support can be good or bad and gives the supporter a share in its moral consequences. Shafāʿah’s basic linguistic and Qur’anic meaning is therefore not identical to forgiveness of sins or salvation in the hereafter. It is a form of joining in support that has real effects.

2. Who—or what—is a supporter?

In human society, a supporter supports someone or a cause through action, ability, or speech. The Qur’an attributes shafāʿah to honored servants in 21:28 and angels in 53:26, so the role cannot be confined to a human figure or reduced to lifeless natural laws. Nor does the root’s meaning of pairing make every natural force a supporter. Each referent must be identified through the particular passage’s context and evidence.

3. How does divine permission operate?

Permission appears in relation to creation, death, affliction, guidance, and causes taking effect. In these uses concerning creation, it expresses the order of laws and limits God establishes for events to be possible and actual. For shafāʿah, permission excludes an independent power confronting divine sovereignty and governance. Yet possibility within creation differs from moral legitimacy or religious authorization: God may permit an event to occur within creation without morally approving the action involved.

4. What is a supporter supposed to do for God?

A supporter cannot disclose a truth hidden from God, produce mercy absent in Him, or use influence to change judgment against what is right. Explicit statements of comprehensive divine knowledge exclude any need to remedy ignorance or susceptibility to pressure. Yet the Qur’an describes speech, prayer for forgiveness, and authorized testimony. Any possible role must therefore be understood entirely within God’s knowledge, mercy, and truthful judgment, rather than as an outside force overcoming His will.

5. How is shafāʿah related to truth and knowledge?

In 2:255, 20:109, 20:110, and 21:28, shafāʿah appears alongside permission, approval, and comprehensive knowledge. In 43:86, informed testimony to truth is a condition connected to shafāʿah. Valid shafāʿah therefore cannot depend on concealing reality, changing a record, or substituting falsehood for truth. Nevertheless, the Qur’an does not define every form of shafāʿah simply as testimony.

6. How does it relate to divine determination, measure, and choice?

People choose and act within God’s established order. Their conduct can join others’ support and environmental conditions to produce real consequences. Al-Nisa 4:85 shows that joining a good or bad undertaking is itself a chosen, responsible action. Divine sovereignty over causes does not negate choice or personal accountability. Causal coupling seeks to explain how choice, support, and consequences occur within one stable order.

7. Why does the Qur’an deny shafāʿah in some passages and condition it on permission in others?

Al-Baqarah 2:48, 2:123, and 2:254 describe shafāʿah as unavailable or unhelpful in warnings about the accounting. Passages such as 2:255, 20:109, and 53:26 instead state permission and approval as conditions.

The groups do not contradict one another:

The denials reject reliance on transactional, self-appointed shafāʿah independent of God as an escape from responsibility.

The exceptions locate shafāʿah within divine permission, knowledge, and established order.

People have no right to treat shafāʿah as a guaranteed instrument at their disposal for altering judgment. Nor do permission exceptions create autonomous power for a supporter.

8. How are shafāʿah in this life and in the hereafter related?

Both retain the fundamental meaning of joining and support, but their conditions differ. Social shafāʿah is a human action that may be good or bad and makes the supporter responsible. At the resurrection, it is wholly connected to permission, approval, divine knowledge, and truth becoming manifest. The Qur’an describes authorized speech and testimony there. Shafāʿah cannot therefore be reduced either to worldly influence or to a silent, speechless display of the past.

9. Where are the boundaries between shafāʿah, prayer, seeking forgiveness, testimony, and seeking a means of approach?

These concepts overlap, but the Qur’an uses distinct terms and structures. Duʿāʾ means prayer or calling upon; istighfār means seeking forgiveness; shahādah means testimony; wasīlah means a means of approach. Tawassul is the related term used for seeking such an approach, whose claimed forms must be examined rather than assumed:

Seeking forgiveness for another is not necessarily identical to shafāʿah as discussed in relation to the hereafter.

Testifying to truth does not exhaust shafāʿah’s meaning.

Seeking a means of approach, read through 5:35 and 17:56, 17:57, concerns seeking nearness through conduct and mindfulness of God, rather than invoking a personal intermediary.

The Qur’an teaches direct prayer to God and records requests for living servants to pray. Passages describing authorized shafāʿah do not establish the legitimacy of invoking the deceased, assigning independent unseen powers to them, or guaranteeing salvation through association with revered figures.

10. How can shafāʿah fit justice and individual responsibility?

Al-Najm 53:38, 53:39, 53:40, 53:41 connects responsibility and the outcome of striving to each person. Al-Nisa 4:85 gives a supporter a share in another’s undertaking. These principles agree because each person answers for their own role and voluntary choice.

Support can affect a life without transferring guilt or removing the original actor’s responsibility. At the resurrection, shafāʿah cannot destroy accountability by altering the truth of actions or producing a judgment contrary to justice. Mercy and forgiveness originate with the Lord, rather than pressure or influence on His decision.

The final assessment of the causal-coupling hypothesis

We can now evaluate the study’s principal hypothesis more precisely.

The direct textual findings are that the root connects to pairing, social shafāʿah may be good or bad and entails responsibility, and shafāʿah with the Lord remains inseparable from divine ownership, permission, approval, and knowledge. The Qur’an also directs prayer and requests for forgiveness to God and issues no general command to seek influential supporters.

Reading the passages together supports the conclusion that shafāʿah cannot remedy divine ignorance, impose forgiveness on God, or change judgment contrary to truth. Created beings’ support, requests for forgiveness, and testimony need not imply independent unseen power. Authorized shafāʿah also differs from requiring devotional dependence on intermediaries.

The hypothesis seeks to explain these findings in a wider framework. People freely act; their conduct joins effective causes and agents; and the results develop within God’s established laws. Resurrection accounting belongs to that same order of knowledge and justice.

This framework offers substantial explanatory value for shafāʿah, choice, responsibility, and divine order. Its every detail, however, is not directly established by Qur’anic wording. In particular, not every natural causal relationship can be called shafāʿah, nor can all afterlife shafāʿah be reduced to an automatic, impersonal process. The Qur’an expressly mentions angels, honored servants, speech, and testimony. It has not disclosed the full workings of authorized shafāʿah.

Causal coupling is therefore an interpretive framework open to examination and supported by Qur’anic evidence, rather than a definition whose every component is explicitly stated in a single verse.

With that boundary preserved, a defensible preliminary definition is:

Qur’anic shafāʿah is joining in support of another person, request, position, or undertaking. In human relationships it may be good or bad and entails responsibility. When discussed in relation to the Lord, no supporter independently controls the outcome. Its validity and benefit belong entirely within divine knowledge, truth, permission, and absolute sovereignty.

This preserves the underlying meaning without limiting it to sinners’ forgiveness or automatically naming every natural force a supporter.

Final conclusions

A central source of misunderstanding is the projection of worldly influence and mediation onto God. Human connections, standing, or leverage can alter a human judgment. The Qur’an never presents the Lord as a ruler needing fresh reports, influential advisers, or mediators to pressure Him into reluctant forgiveness.

God knows human conduct outwardly and inwardly. His judgment rests entirely on truth, while mercy and forgiveness originate in Him. No supporter’s standing or nearness can create independent power over another person’s afterlife destiny.

The Qur’an also leaves a real place for support, prayer, seeking forgiveness, and truthful testimony. Within their proper created and religious settings, these have meaning and effect. They must never be equated with ownership of forgiveness or power to change divine judgment.

Another central finding is the direct relationship between people and their Lord. The Qur’an does not require an intermediary to carry their voices to God. He describes Himself as near and responsive, inviting direct prayer, repentance, seeking forgiveness, patience, prayerful worship, and striving for truth.

Even if shafāʿah occurs within creation by divine permission, that possibility establishes neither a duty to seek a supporter nor the legitimacy of making revered figures necessary intermediaries in worship. Possible authorized shafāʿah and a command to seek mediation are fundamentally different.

Within God’s established laws, people freely choose, and their conduct connects with different agents, causes, and circumstances. Those connections create consequences and responsibilities. None displaces God’s absolute sovereignty and ownership over the accounting.

At the resurrection, the truth of actions is laid bare through the Lord’s knowledge and justice. Any accepted and effective speech or shafāʿah that day remains within His permission, knowledge, and judgment. The verses do not disclose every detail of how it works, and human assumptions must not be used to reconstruct what has not been given.

This study therefore does not deny support or authorized shafāʿah. It seeks their proper place within devotion to God alone: all ultimate ownership, authority, mercy, and judgment belong exclusively to Him.

Shafāʿah in the Qur’an is no way around the Lord or His established laws. No supporter owns anyone’s salvation, and no shafāʿah replaces personal responsibility, repentance, or a direct return to God.
