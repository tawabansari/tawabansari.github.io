Introduction: What does divine permission mean?

Several Qur’anic passages describe events as happening bi-idhn Allah (by God’s permission) or bi-idhnihi (by His permission). Plants grow by their Lord’s permission; people come to faith by His permission; one group defeats another by His permission. Even adversity does not occur outside divine permission.

But how does God’s permission operate?

Should we imagine God issuing a separate authorization for every plant to grow, every creature to move, or every person to find guidance? Or should we understand divine permission through the ordered system God has established in creation?

This question is central to understanding many passages about divine will, guidance, decree and destiny, human choice, and intercession (mediation on another person’s behalf).

1. The word family behind “permission”

The Arabic root ʾ-dh-n connects words associated with the ear, hearing, making something known, and granting permission.

Udhun means “ear”; adhan means an announcement in At-Tawbah 9:3; and idhn means permission in An-Nur 24:28, where the subject is entering someone else’s home.

In each case, the word’s form and its context determine whether it concerns hearing, announcing, or permitting. These distinctions are explored in the study The Qur’anic Root ʾ-dh-n.

When the Qur’an attributes an event to God’s permission, however, the question goes beyond one person granting another permission to act. It concerns how something becomes possible and takes effect within creation.

2. Divine permission: the conditions under which things become possible and occur

God has created a world in which phenomena arise and have effects within particular laws, patterns, relationships, and conditions.

Water, light, heat, gravity, the properties of matter, the structure of living organisms, and relationships of cause and effect all belong to this order. Human beings did not create these laws. We gradually discover them and learn how to make use of them.

Human conduct also has consequences in individual and social life. Thought, experience, upbringing, choice, repeated actions, prejudice, and the pursuit of truth can all shape a person’s life and understanding.

This study understands divine permission, where it concerns events and their effects, as expressing the operative laws, patterns, and conditions that God has established in creation, within which things do or do not come about.

This does not mean that “a law of nature” is the dictionary meaning of idhn (permission). It is an interpretation of divine permission in relation to the order of creation.

On this reading, God’s permission is neither a ceremonial authorization nor an arbitrary decision. Something occurs when the possibility and conditions for its occurrence are present within the divine order. Where the necessary conditions are absent, human wishes and claims alone cannot bring it about.

Fatir 35:43 speaks of the constancy of God’s way. Its context is human conduct and its consequences. The patterns God establishes should therefore not be restricted to the laws governing physical phenomena.

3. How does permission become evident in practice?

A few examples help make this principle concrete.

Al-A‘raf 7:58 states:

وَالْبَلَدُ الطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَالَّذِي خَبُثَ لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا

7:58

“Good land brings forth its vegetation by its Lord’s permission; what is poor brings forth only with difficulty.”

The verse speaks both of the Lord’s permission and of differences between the conditions of the land.

A suitable seed placed in fertile soil, with adequate water, light, and temperature, has the opportunity to germinate and grow. The same seed may fail to grow in unsuitable soil, without water, or at an unsuitable temperature.

Must we imagine that God allowed one seed to grow while withholding permission from another without any governing principle?

On the reading proposed here, the different outcomes should be examined through the conditions and laws of creation. Divine permission for growth is inseparable from the system God has established for plant life and development.

These laws also operate in reproduction, inheritance, adaptation, and changes in living organisms across generations. The emergence of biological diversity and species is a subject of scientific investigation; this verse does not explain its mechanisms. On this interpretation, however, such processes are also within God’s created order.

The movement of ships provides another example. Ash-Shura 42:33 refers to the wind falling still and ships becoming motionless.

The shipbuilder, the sailor, and natural conditions each play a part. A sailing vessel will not move without suitable wind, however much the sailor wishes to reach the destination. Wind alone is not enough either: the vessel’s condition, the sailor’s skill, and other circumstances also matter.

An event therefore depends on a combination of causes and conditions. People draw on the possibilities of creation by understanding these conditions; wishing and waiting do not change its laws.

The same principle can be seen in social life.

Al-Baqarah 2:249 attributes a small group’s victory over a larger one to God’s permission, while also speaking of perseverance in the same verse.

In Aal Imran 3:152, God likewise speaks of victory, but then identifies faltering resolve, disagreement, and disobedience as factors in the change in people’s circumstances.

Aal Imran 3:165 attributes adversity to the believers’ own conduct; Aal Imran 3:166 relates that same event to God’s permission.

Divine permission therefore does not render human conduct or the causes of events ineffective. Effort, preparation, sound decisions, and mistakes can influence outcomes within that very divine order.

4. Guidance: the patterns governing understanding and human choice

This principle has particular importance for human guidance.

After discussing people’s disagreements and the revelation of scripture to judge between them in truth, Al-Baqarah 2:213 says:

فَهَدَى الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لِمَا اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ مِنَ الْحَقِّ بِإِذْنِهِ

2:213

“Then The God guided those who believed, by His permission, to the truth about which they had disagreed.”

Why do some people find their way to truth amid disagreement, while others persist in conflict and wrongful rivalry even after clear evidence has reached them?

Do guidance and misguidance occur without any relationship to human thought, intention, or choice?

The Qur’an itself addresses this question.

Yunus 10:100 makes a person’s coming to faith dependent on God’s permission, and in the same verse speaks of those who do not use their reason.

In Al-Ma’idah 5:16, God guides those who seek His good pleasure along paths of peace and brings them out of darkness into light by His permission.

Al-‘Ankabut 29:69 connects striving in God’s cause with guidance. Conversely, As-Saff 61:5 describes the turning away of people’s hearts as following the deviation they themselves chose.

Consider two people.

The first grows up in a family with established religious beliefs. They value those beliefs, but examine the evidence when they encounter a new idea. If they discover that an inherited belief is mistaken, they are willing to correct it, even at the cost of opposition from their family or community.

The second grows up in the same circumstances but decides in advance to reject anything that conflicts with the beliefs of their ancestors and religious leaders. They dismiss opposing views before examining the evidence because they do not want to lose their reputation, position, or intellectual comfort.

Are these two people equally prepared to recognize truth?

No.

Reasoning, fairness, a commitment to truth, listening to different views, a willingness to correct mistakes, and letting go of prejudice help open the way to understanding. Pride, wrongful rivalry, blind conformity, and knowingly persisting in error can obstruct guidance.

Al-Baqarah 2:170 challenges adherence to ancestral beliefs without rational examination. Az-Zumar 39:18 praises those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it.

Guidance by God’s permission can thus be understood in connection with these patterns of understanding and choice. God has given human beings the capacity to think and the means to learn, and holds them responsible for how they use those capacities.

This does not mean that everyone who reaches a conclusion different from ours is avoiding the truth. People differ in upbringing, knowledge, opportunities to learn, and intention. God knows all these circumstances.

One principle nevertheless remains clear: guidance in the Qur’an is connected with human conduct and a person’s readiness to accept what is true.

This question is also examined in The Sealing of the Heart: Guidance and Misguidance.

5. Permission for something to occur does not mean God approves of it

A crucial distinction is needed here.

If something can happen within the created order, does that mean God is pleased with it?

Al-Baqarah 2:102 discusses harming others and states that such harm cannot take effect except by God’s permission. Yet the passage condemns the conduct in question.

Az-Zumar 39:7 also states that God does not approve of disbelief for His servants.

The possibility of an action occurring is therefore not the same as its being right or legitimate.

A person can use knowledge to treat illness or use that same knowledge to harm others. The laws and causes through which the action takes effect are real in both cases, but the moral value of the conduct differs.

No one can therefore justify injustice, corruption, or deception by claiming that because it happened, God must have approved of it.

In passages such as Yunus 10:59 and Ash-Shura 42:21, idhn (permission) concerns authorization to establish religious rules. The question there is whether a ruling has been authorized and is valid, rather than which laws govern the occurrence of natural phenomena. Each use of the word must therefore be understood in its own context.

Conclusion: What does this understanding clarify?

If we understand divine permission solely by analogy with one person granting another permission, we may imagine that events occur through separate, unknown authorizations, without any connection to actual laws and conditions.

The Qur’an, however, describes a world in which causes, conditions, limits, human choice, and the consequences of conduct matter. At the same time, it does not regard these processes or their effects as independent of God’s governance.

This study understands divine permission in creation as expressing the operative laws and patterns God has established in existence: an order in which the presence or absence of the necessary conditions determines whether something can or cannot occur.

The principle concerns more than plants and physical phenomena. Understanding, guidance, individual conduct, and social life are also shaped by real conditions and relationships.

When the Qur’an attributes an event to God’s permission, we should therefore neither erase the role of causes and conditions nor remove human choice and responsibility.

This interpretation does not claim to know every law of creation or every aspect of how God acts. It offers a way of understanding permission in relation to an ordered world and divine patterns, without attributing arbitrary decisions or behavior modeled on human relationships to God.

God has established the order of creation. Within it, human beings think, learn, choose, and act. Understanding divine permission calls us to study that order and act responsibly within it, rather than passively awaiting an unknown authorization.
