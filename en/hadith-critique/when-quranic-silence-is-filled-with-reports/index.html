Introduction: When Qur’anic Silence Is Filled with Reports

When one studies the heritage of hadith, tafsir, stories of the prophets, and Muslim religious culture with care, one encounters a phenomenon that cannot be ignored: many names, stories, rulings, predictions, and details that today are imagined to be part of “religious knowledge” do not exist in the Qur’an itself.

A portion of these materials are known in Islamic studies as Isra’iliyyat. Despite its name, in broad usage this term is not limited only to Jewish materials. It may include reports drawn from Jewish traditions, Christian traditions, oral tales, apocalyptic literature, local cultures, and other extra-Qur’anic sources.

The existence of Isra’iliyyat in the exegetical heritage has been acknowledged by Muslim scholars and by researchers in the history of religions. Even within the hadith tradition itself, reports exist that permit narrating from the Children of Israel; and this permission, in the early centuries, opened the way for the broad entry of stories and details attributed to the Children of Israel. The well-known study by M. J. Kister on the phrase “Narrate from the Children of Israel and there is no harm” shows that this idea circulated among Muslims in the early centuries and helped spread Jewish and Christian narrative materials. One can say with confidence that a vast and influential portion of extra-Qur’anic details, especially in stories of the prophets, angelology, eschatology, creation, the history of peoples, and some legal rulings, is connected with Jewish and Christian traditions and with the cultural atmosphere of Late Antiquity.

The main issue of this article is not to accuse every interpreter and narrator of deliberately inventing extra-Qur’anic concepts. Perhaps some of them did not properly distinguish between history, story, and revelation; and God knows best. But what is evident in the inherited legacy is that many of them did not have the courage to say “we do not know,” or the ability to accept the limits of their own knowledge.

Before the Qur’an speaks about the epistemic slips of the human being, it gives an instructive example in the story of human creation. When the Lord announced the placing of a khalifah on earth, the angels asked a question on the basis of the knowledge they had:

وَإِذْ قَالَ رَبُّكَ لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ إِنِّي جَاعِلٌ فِي الْأَرْضِ خَلِيفَةً ۖ قَالُوا أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَن يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ ۖ قَالَ إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ ٣٠

And when your Lord said to the angels, “I am placing a khalifah on earth,” they said, “Will You place in it one who will cause corruption in it and shed blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You?” He said, “I know what you do not know.”

Al-Baqarah 2:30

Then The One God gave Adam a knowledge that the angels did not possess:

وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ الْأَسْمَاءَ كُلَّهَا ثُمَّ عَرَضَهُمْ عَلَى الْمَلَائِكَةِ فَقَالَ أَنبِئُونِي بِأَسْمَاءِ هَٰؤُلَاءِ إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ ٣١

And He taught Adam all the names; then He presented them to the angels and said, “Inform Me of the names of these, if you are truthful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:31

When the angels were confronted with the limit of their knowledge, they did not fabricate an answer to preserve their rank. They did not take refuge in conjecture, and they did not fill what they did not know with stories and interpretation. They submitted and said honestly:

قَالُوا سُبْحَانَكَ لَا عِلْمَ لَنَا إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَا ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ ٣٢

They said, “Glory be to You; we have no knowledge except what You have taught us. Indeed, You are the Knowing, the Wise.”

Al-Baqarah 2:32

This answer of the angels is one of the clearest Qur’anic models for trustworthiness in knowledge:

لَا عِلْمَ لَنَا إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَا

We have no knowledge except what You have taught us.

The angels did not say: because we occupy a high station, we must have an answer for every question. They did not treat not knowing as shame, and they did not add anything to the speech of The One God in order to cover the limits of their knowledge. They accepted that their knowledge was only as much as had been given to them.

If the angels, before The One God, explicitly said, “We have no knowledge except what You have taught us,” why should a human interpreter or narrator be ashamed to say, “The Qur’an has not explained this, and we do not know”? Why must Qur’anic silence be filled with names, stories, predictions, and details about which there is no established knowledge?

The problem does not begin with asking; the angels also asked. The problem begins when the human being, after the limit of his knowledge becomes clear, is unwilling to accept that limit. The angels distinguished between asking and claiming knowledge; but a large part of our report-based heritage slipped precisely at this boundary.

At the same time, social and religious structures can turn “having an answer for everything” into a source of authority and power. When people turn to someone because they imagine he knows the answers to all mysteries, saying “I do not know” may threaten his social position. In such an environment, storytelling, interpretive construction, and presenting unknown details can become tools for gaining influence, even if we cannot prove the inner motive of every individual.

The fundamental question is this: what method does the Qur’an teach the human being when facing unknown matters?

Al-Imran 3:7 is not merely a marginal recommendation about interpreting the Qur’an. This verse clarifies the boundary between muhkam and mutashabih, knowledge and conjecture, rootedness in knowledge and deviation of the heart:

هُوَ ٱلَّذِيٓ أَنزَلَ عَلَيْكَ ٱلْكِتَابَ مِنْهُ ءَايَاتٌ مُّحْكَمَاتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ ٱلْكِتَابِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ ۖ فَأَمَّا ٱلَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ ٱبْتِغَآءَ ٱلْفِتْنَةِ وَٱبْتِغَآءَ تَأْوِيلِهِۦ ۗ وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُۥٓ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ ۗ وَٱلرَّاسِخُونَ فِي ٱلْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ ءَامَنَّا بِهِۦ كُلٌّ مِّنْ عِندِ رَبِّنَا ۗ وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّآ أُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَلْبَابِ ٧

He is the One who sent down upon you the Book. Some of it consists of clear and firm verses; they are the mother and reference of the Book, while others are mutashabihat. As for those in whose hearts there is deviation, they follow what is mutashabih from it, seeking fitnah and seeking its ta’wil, while no one knows its ta’wil except The One God. And those rooted in knowledge say, “We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord.” And none take reminder except people of pure understanding.

Al-Imran 3:7

The structure of the verse is very precise.

First, the Qur’an does not say that all verses are of one kind. It names a portion muhkamat and calls those verses umm al-kitab, the mother, foundation, and reference of the Book. The muhkamat must be the basis of understanding; they must not be pushed aside so that an ambiguous report, a dream, a story, or an external interpretation can rule over them.

Then the Qur’an speaks of mutashabihat. A mutashabih matter is not something upon which the human being is permitted to impose whatever meaning he wants. The existence of mutashabihat does not mean that the field is open for imagination and story-making.

Then the verse describes the behavior of those in whose hearts there is zaygh, crookedness and deviation. Instead of understanding the mutashabih in the light of the muhkam and accepting the limits of their knowledge, they pursue the mutashabih itself:

فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ

They follow what is mutashabih from it.

Their aim is also stated in the verse itself:

ٱبْتِغَآءَ ٱلْفِتْنَةِ وَٱبْتِغَآءَ تَأْوِيلِهِ

Seeking fitnah and seeking its ta’wil.

After that, the Qur’an makes the boundary of human knowledge clear:

وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُۥٓ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ

No one knows its ta’wil except The One God.

Those rooted in knowledge do not then say, “We also know its ta’wil.” Their answer is not a claim of ownership over the unseen. They say:

ءَامَنَّا بِهِۦ كُلٌّ مِّنْ عِندِ رَبِّنَا

We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord.

Therefore, in the logic of this verse, rootedness in knowledge is not recognized by the claim to know everything. Sometimes the sign of real knowledge is that the human being knows where he must stop. “We do not know” in the face of mutashabihat is not a sign of weak knowledge or weak faith; it may be exactly the answer the Qur’an expects from an honest human being.

This does not mean that every name or omitted detail in the Qur’an must technically be called mutashabih. But verse 3:7 gives a general epistemic principle: the human being is not permitted, where he has no demonstrable knowledge, to present his conjecture as religious truth.

The problem of many extra-Qur’anic reports begins exactly here. The Qur’an does not mention a name, but the interpreter assigns one. The Qur’an does not specify the location, but the storyteller builds a city, a cave, and a king. The Qur’an does not explain the form of an unseen matter, but the narrator provides shape, size, number of wings, time, and exact sequence. Then later generations forget that these details were not in the Qur’an and imagine them to be part of “Islam.”

Why Do We Treat Qur’anic Silence as a Gap?

The Qur’an is not a chronology, a historical encyclopedia, or a collection of complete biographies. When it recounts an event, it does not necessarily intend to answer every historical question we might ask. It gives what is needed for guidance, judgment, and understanding. Adam’s wife’s name, the kind of tree he was forbidden to approach, the exact number of the cave’s sleepers, their dog’s color, the personal name of the angel of death, and details of the Kaaba’s earliest construction are examples. Curiosity about them is legitimate; turning a guess into God’s word is not.

Apparently simple questions become dangerous when their answers carry an unwarranted claim to knowledge: What was the person’s name? Where did they live? How long? Who laid the House’s first stone? How many wings did that angel have? Which events must occur, and in what order, before the Resurrection? Sometimes the honest answer is simply that the Qur’an does not say and we lack reliable evidence for the details. Yet a mind accustomed to complete stories finds this unsatisfying. The storyteller steps into the silence, and a report fills the imagined gap.

Two different things then merge: a text that makes certain statements, and a tradition that makes statements about that text. A commentator’s words may illuminate intellectual history, but publication under the heading of Qur’anic interpretation does not make them a verse. Nor does popularity turn a report from a Companion of Muhammad or a member of the following generation into his established teaching. Even a chain of named transmitters does not, by itself, establish the truth of a report’s contents. We must first identify the speaker, examine the evidence, and ask how the claim relates to the Qur’an.

This does not prohibit historical research. Investigation may reject a report or corroborate it through independent evidence. The problem arises when something neither stated in the Qur’an nor established by reliable evidence enters people’s faith and law as an explanation of revelation. History can be read with care; what remains unknown must not be sold with the authority of God’s voice.

How Did Isra’iliyyat Enter Qur’anic Interpretation?

The first Muslim communities did not live in a cultural vacuum. Jews, Christians, followers of other religions, merchants, travelers, storytellers, and converts interacted. Stories of prophets, creation, angels, and the world’s end had circulated among them for centuries. Muslim territorial expansion intensified contact with Jewish, Syriac, Iranian, and Byzantine cultures. Converts brought their earlier religious memories and learning with them. Names and stories naturally passed between languages. But a natural process of cultural transmission does not establish the divine origin of what was transmitted.

A transmitter might say, “I found this in the Torah,” or “It is written in the earlier books.” Listeners could assume that everything introduced this way belonged to the revelation given to Moses. Yet some transmitters used “Torah” more broadly, including oral stories, religious writings, and interpretations circulating among scriptural communities. Later Jewish or Christian narratives could thus be presented to Muslim audiences under the name of a heavenly scripture. Material moved from story collections into Qur’anic commentary and, in some later transmissions, became attributed to revered figures or to Muhammad.

This account of transmission is not an anonymous hypothesis. Islamic sources identify people such as Abdullah ibn Salam and Ka‘b al-Ahbar, known for transmitting Israelite lore because of their background or knowledge of earlier books. Ka‘b had not met Muhammad, yet subsequent generations transmitted numerous accounts from him about creation, prophets, sacred places, and the end times. Wahb ibn Munabbih also appears repeatedly in collections of prophetic stories and reports from scriptural communities. Assessing his role does not require claiming certainty about every detail of his ancestry or life. These figures matter because they reveal actual channels through which religious stories circulated.

M. J. Kister’s research on transmitting Israelite reports and expressions such as “it is written in the Torah” examines this process. If an Islamic work recounts details about Solomon’s palace, Noah’s ark, or the world before creation, and comparable material has a history in outside traditions, the possibility of influence cannot be dismissed because the book bears the respected title of Qur’anic commentary. We must investigate the report’s history, earlier versions, intermediaries, and contents. Such inquiry is precisely how we distinguish an identifiable historical tradition from an unfounded tale.

Muslim scholars themselves recognized the problem. In his introduction to his commentary, Ibn Kathir does not treat Israelite reports as equally reliable. He distinguishes material acceptable by his criteria, material that conflicts with them, and much that is unnecessary for religious understanding. His examples include the sleepers’ names, their dog’s color, and the material of Moses’ staff. Criticism of Isra’iliyyat is therefore not merely an outsider’s verdict; the interpretive tradition itself recognized the issue.

A more sensitive question concerns these reports’ relationship to religious power. A scholar with an astonishing answer to every question could appear more knowledgeable than someone who honestly said, “I do not know.” A storyteller could expand a brief Qur’anic episode into a lengthy performance, supplying names, ages, homes, conversations, and precise endings. Repetition could turn yesterday’s “they say” into the next generation’s unquestioned religion. This process does not require every transmitter to have conspired deliberately. But the absence of conspiracy does not make a mistaken result correct.

The Qur’an affirms revelation to Moses and Jesus; it does not thereby affirm every story told centuries later among their followers. That boundary has nevertheless often been crossed. Even a historically interesting story requires clear identification: Is this the Qur’an? A reliably established saying of Muhammad? A commentator’s interpretation? Or another report drawn from communities’ religious memory?

Assessing Reports Before Calling a Story Knowledge

Every detail supplied from outside the Qur’an calls for separate questions. Does the Qur’an state it? If not, who first transmitted it? Is its attribution to Muhammad supported by reliable evidence? Is an earlier parallel known in another tradition? If two stories resemble each other, which particular elements do they share? Can a route of transmission be demonstrated? Above all, is the claim consistent with the Qur’an’s clear foundational passages, the relevant verses, reason, and justice?

Frequent transmission does not establish authenticity, just as lack of evidence does not prove that a past event never happened. But failure to establish a claim is sufficient reason not to present it as religious truth. Saying that the Qur’an does not name someone does not require proving that the person had no name. Saying that no sound prophetic report establishes a story does not require listing every weak version across several pages. Evidence for a religious claim must be proportionate to the claim itself.

Azrael: A Familiar Name the Qur’an Does Not Teach

The name Azrael is a particularly clear example. It is so firmly established in popular Muslim culture that many assume the Qur’an names the angel of death this way. Some religious teaching repeats it alongside other angelic names as though the list were explicitly Qur’anic. Yet consider the verse itself:

قُلْ يَتَوَفَّاكُم مَلَكُ الْمَوْتِ الَّذِي وُكِّلَ بِكُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ تُرْجَعُونَ

“Say: The angel of death entrusted with you will take you in full; then you will be returned to your Lord.” (Al-Sajdah 32:11)

The Qur’an says “the angel of death.” It does not use the name Azrael here or anywhere else. Nor does that name appear as the angel of death’s name in the canonical Torah or Gospels. Jewish writings outside the biblical canon contain extensive material about the angel of death and other angelic figures. But the existence of the concept is not evidence for a particular personal name. Popular understanding has lost this simple distinction.

Some early commentaries transmit a statement introduced by the words, “It has reached us that the angel of death’s name is Azrael.” That introduction itself tells us that this is neither a Qur’anic verse nor, in that form, a claim to direct instruction from Muhammad. Repetition in writings and sermons nevertheless made the name seem certain. No sound, reliable prophetic report establishes it as Muhammad’s explicit teaching.

Chronology must not be reversed. If a similar name appears in later Jewish mystical or angelological writings, we cannot claim without evidence that Muslims of the second Islamic century borrowed it from a book compiled several centuries afterward. Outside sources were diverse: writings, oral traditions, angelology, and Semitic cultures. This article does not depend on asserting an undocumented route of transmission. The clearer point is that a name absent from the Qur’an, without support from a sound prophetic report, became a widespread teaching treated almost as certainty.

The Qur’an describes the angel’s task without giving a personal name. Anyone calling that angel Azrael should identify the source and explain why the information is placed on the same footing as Qur’anic teaching. The burden of proof belongs to whoever adds the name, not to whoever follows what the Qur’an actually says.

The Kaaba: Adding a History Before Abraham

The same pattern appears in accounts of the Kaaba’s construction. Commentarial tradition frequently says that Adam built or established the House before Abraham, who merely restored an older structure. The Qur’an does not say this. It describes Abraham and Ishmael’s work as follows:

وَإِذْ يَرْفَعُ إِبْرَاهِيمُ الْقَوَاعِدَ مِنَ الْبَيْتِ وَإِسْمَاعِيلُ رَبَّنَا تَقَبَّلْ مِنَّا إِنَّكَ أَنتَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

“When Abraham and Ishmael were raising the foundations of the House, they said: Our Lord, accept this from us; You are the Hearing, the Knowing.” (Al-Baqarah 2:127)

Al-Hajj 22:26 also speaks of assigning Abraham the House’s site. Neither verse identifies Adam as its first builder. “Raising the foundations” does not, by itself, name an earlier builder, and the “first House” in Ali ‘Imran 3:96 does not name Adam either. Yet commentaries describe Adam’s construction, a heavenly house, angels, and extraordinary building materials without supplying a sound prophetic report establishing this particular claim.

In Al-Bahr al-Muhit, commenting on Al-Baqarah 2:127, Abu Hayyan al-Andalusi explicitly criticizes commentators for elaborating matters found neither in the Qur’an nor in sound hadith; he also notes contradictions among these stories. This criticism within the interpretive tradition shows that the problem did not go unnoticed. Nevertheless, the account entered popular understanding of the Kaaba’s history and is sometimes repeated as settled fact. For a study of how Qur’anic silence is filled, this example is sufficient. We need neither catalogue every historical opinion nor attribute unsupported information to the divine text.

Shu‘ayb: How a Name Entered Translations of Moses’ Story

Another example has moved beyond commentary into translations of the verses themselves: the identification of Moses’ father-in-law as the prophet Shu‘ayb. The Qur’an places Shu‘ayb’s story in Midian. It also says that Moses, after leaving Egypt, reached Midian’s watering place and met two women waiting to water their animals. Shared geography does not establish that two people are the same person. Midian names a place and a human community; it is not Shu‘ayb’s exclusive identity card.

The verse states:

وَلَمَّا وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ وَجَدَ عَلَيْهِ أُمَّةً مِنَ النَّاسِ يَسْقُونَ وَوَجَدَ مِن دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُمَا قَالَتَا لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

“When he reached the water of Midian, he found a group of people watering their animals, and apart from them two women holding theirs back. He asked, ‘What is the matter?’ They said, ‘We cannot water until the shepherds leave, and our father is an elderly man.’” (Al-Qasas 28:23)

The description is “our father is an elderly man.” The Qur’an neither calls him a prophet, names him Shu‘ayb, nor mentions an earlier mission. One daughter then invites Moses home, and the man offers him marriage to one of his daughters:

قَالَ إِنِّي أُرِيدُ أَنْ أُنكِحَكَ إِحْدَى ابْنَتَيَّ هَاتَيْنِ عَلَىٰ أَنْ تَأْجُرَنِي ثَمَانِيَ حِجَجٍ فَإِنْ أَتْمَمْتَ عَشْرًا فَمِنْ عِندِكَ وَمَا أُرِيدُ أَنْ أَشُقَّ عَلَيْكَ سَتَجِدُنِي إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

“He said, ‘I wish to marry one of these two daughters of mine to you on condition that you work for me for eight years. If you complete ten, that will be of your own accord. I do not wish to make things difficult for you. God willing, you will find me among the righteous.’” (Al-Qasas 28:27)

The verse begins simply, “He said.” Yet the Persian/Dari translation by Hossein Taji Kal displayed on Quran.com begins, “(Shu‘ayb) said.” The official Indonesian translation displayed there likewise reads “Dia (Syu'aib) berkata”—“He (Shu‘ayb) said.” The name is absent from the Arabic. A translator’s parenthesis places an interpretive conjecture precisely where readers turn to understand the Qur’an. After seeing it repeatedly, someone unable to read Arabic may stop asking which words come from the verse and which were supplied by the translator.

The Persian/Dari translation of Al-Qasas 28:27 and the Indonesian translation of the same verse make this transfer visible. Not all translations make the addition. The point is not that every translator behaves alike, but that an unestablished identification has crossed from transmitted tradition into the translation of the Qur’anic text.

Nor was this identification unanimously accepted or securely documented within commentary. In his discussion of Al-Qasas 28:25, Ibn Kathir explicitly says that the reports naming Shu‘ayb in this story are not sound in their chains of transmission and that no clear evidence identifies the elderly man. We need not catalogue commentators here. What matters is the change from acknowledged uncertainty in interpretation to apparent certainty in translation.

The Qur’anic narratives themselves also differ significantly. In Shu‘ayb’s story, Midian’s people suffer punishment for rejection and corruption (Hud 11:94, 11:95; Al-‘Ankabut 29:37). In Moses’ story, Midian has an active watering place, shepherds, and families. The Qur’an does not calculate the precise interval between the accounts, and it also describes Shu‘ayb and the believers with him being saved. A functioning settlement therefore does not, by itself, prove that contemporaneity is impossible. Nevertheless, these narrative and historical distinctions certainly prevent a shared place-name from proving a shared identity. Shu‘ayb’s warning in Hud 11:89 that Lot’s people are not far from his own also invites chronological investigation, but it does not alone supply a definitive timeline for people’s lifespans and family relationships.

Exodus 2:16–22 recounts a similarly structured encounter between Moses and a Midianite man’s daughters. Exodus 2:18 names Reuel, while Exodus 3:1 calls Moses’ father-in-law Jethro. This outside tradition requires historical examination in its own right. The comparison nevertheless shows that different identifications circulated, while the Qur’an names none of them. What the Qur’an leaves unstated must not be put into its mouth through an interpreter’s choice.

An unnamed man in the Qur’an was not necessarily nameless in life. We may ask questions, develop hypotheses, and investigate history. But without reliable evidence, no one may present his identification as Shu‘ayb as established Qur’anic teaching. The honest answer about Moses’ father-in-law’s identity remains: we do not know.

Stoning: When an Israelite Ruling Became Muslim Law

Stoning is one of the gravest examples because the issue extends beyond historical storytelling to punishment and human life. It existed before Islam in Israelite legal tradition. Deuteronomy 22:23–24 prescribes stoning for a particular sexual offense, while Leviticus 20:10 prescribes death for adultery. These texts document the punishment’s Torah precedent. The question here is not whether it belonged to that community’s law. It is how, although not prescribed for Muslims in the Qur’an, a ruling from that law came through reports to be presented as an “Islamic prescribed penalty.”

Surah Al-Nur states the punishment for a woman and man who commit unlawful sexual intercourse:

الزَّانِيَةُ وَالزَّانِي فَاجْلِدُوا كُلَّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا مِائَةَ جَلْدَةٍ وَلَا تَأْخُذْكُمْ بِهِمَا رَأْفَةٌ فِي دِينِ اللَّهِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَلْيَشْهَدْ عَذَابَهُمَا طَائِفَةٌ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“The woman and the man who commit unlawful sexual intercourse: lash each of them a hundred times. If you believe in God and the Last Day, do not let compassion for them prevent you from carrying out God’s ruling. Let a group of believers witness their punishment.” (Al-Nur 24:2)

The ruling does not divide its subjects into married and unmarried groups. It addresses the woman and man who commit the offense, without specifying marital status. Anyone replacing a hundred lashes with death by stoning for one group must show which Qur’anic verse establishes that change. A qualification absent from the verse cannot simply displace God’s explicit punishment.

Another passage helps clarify the structure of this ruling. Al-Nisa 4:25 discusses marriage to women living in slavery and then addresses punishment for a sexual offense:

وَمَنْ لَمْ يَسْتَطِعْ مِنْكُمْ طَوْلًا أَنْ يَنْكِحَ الْمُحْصَنَاتِ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ فَمِنْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ مِنْ فَتَيَاتِكُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِإِيمَانِكُمْ بَعْضُكُمْ مِنْ بَعْضٍ فَانْكِحُوهُنَّ بِإِذْنِ أَهْلِهِنَّ وَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ مُحْصَنَاتٍ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ فَإِذَا أُحْصِنَّ فَإِنْ أَتَيْنَ بِفَاحِشَةٍ فَعَلَيْهِنَّ نِصْفُ مَا عَلَى الْمُحْصَنَاتِ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ خَشِيَ الْعَنَتَ مِنْكُمْ وَأَنْ تَصْبِرُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

“Whoever among you lacks the means to marry free believing women may marry believing women held in your possession. God knows your faith best; you belong to one another. Marry them with their families’ permission and give them their bridal dues fairly, as chaste women, neither engaging in illicit sex nor taking secret lovers. Once married, if they commit a sexual offense, their punishment is half that prescribed for free women. This is for anyone among you who fears falling into hardship; patience is better for you. God is forgiving and merciful.” (Al-Nisa 4:25)

Halving a punishment clearly points to something divisible. A hundred lashes can become fifty; death by stoning cannot be halved. Traditional legal commentaries themselves recognize the difficulty and explain that the divisible punishment is flogging, not stoning. This is more than a verbal coincidence. An internal relationship between two Qur’anic rulings leaves no room for inventing “half a death.”

Some appeal to the report of two Jews being stoned. Even if that report is accepted, the case concerns Jews with their own prescribed law. The Qur’an says of their approaching Muhammad for judgment:

سَمَّاعُونَ لِلْكَذِبِ أَكَّالُونَ لِلسُّحْتِ فَإِنْ جَاءُوكَ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ أَوْ أَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ وَإِنْ تُعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ فَلَنْ يَضُرُّوكَ شَيْئًا وَإِنْ حَكَمْتَ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْقِسْطِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

“They eagerly listen to falsehood and consume illicit gain. If they come to you, judge between them or turn away. If you turn away, they cannot harm you at all. If you judge, judge between them fairly: God loves those who act justly.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:42)

وَكَيْفَ يُحَكِّمُونَكَ وَعِنْدَهُمُ التَّوْرَاةُ فِيهَا حُكْمُ اللَّهِ ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّوْنَ مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ وَمَا أُولَٰئِكَ بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“How do they appoint you as judge when they have the Torah, containing God’s ruling? Yet afterward they turn away. Such people are not believers.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:43)

The first verse allows Muhammad to decline their dispute and requires justice if he accepts it. The next explicitly refers to the ruling in their Torah. A judgment applying one community’s law to its members does not automatically become law for every other community. If a Torah ruling was applied to two Jews, why conclude that this act abolished the Qur’an’s own ruling on unlawful sexual intercourse?

After discussing the Torah and Gospel, the Qur’an describes its own role:

وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ مُصَدِّقًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ مِنَ الْكِتَابِ وَمُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ بِمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَاءَهُمْ عَمَّا جَاءَكَ مِنَ الْحَقِّ لِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مِنْكُمْ شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَجَعَلَكُمْ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ فِي مَا آتَاكُمْ فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ إِلَى اللَّهِ مَرْجِعُكُمْ جَمِيعًا فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

“We have sent the Book down to you in truth, confirming the earlier scripture and serving as its guardian and criterion. Judge between them by what God has revealed, and do not turn from the truth that has reached you to follow their desires. For each of you We have appointed a prescribed way and a path. Had God willed, He would have made you one community; instead, He tests you through what He has given you. So excel in good deeds. You will all return to God, and He will make clear what you differed about.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:48)

The description of the Qur’an as guardian and criterion cannot mean that another community’s law may govern its ruling without scrutiny. The Qur’an both confirms earlier revelation and recognizes differing prescribed ways. A punishment known in Israelite law does not become Muslims’ criminal law merely through that earlier recognition.

A still greater claim followed: a verse prescribing stoning had supposedly been revealed, but its wording was removed from the Qur’anic text while its ruling remained binding. Jurists describe this as “abrogation of recitation with retention of the ruling.” Giving it a technical name does not answer the question of its Qur’anic authorization. Can a person’s life be taken on the authority of a verse absent from the Book? If God removed its recitation, who was authorized to keep its lethal ruling enforceable outside the Book?

The Qur’an says of preserving the Reminder:

إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

“It is We who sent down the Reminder, and We will preserve it.” (Al-Hijr 15:9)

People are told both that the Qur’an is preserved and that a verse authorizing a person’s death is absent from its text, though its ruling must be recovered from reports. This no longer explains the Book. It substitutes a claim about a missing text for the ruling the Book contains. The Qur’an also asks who should judge:

أَفَغَيْرَ اللَّهِ أَبْتَغِي حَكَمًا وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ إِلَيْكُمُ الْكِتَابَ مُفَصَّلًا وَالَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ يَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّهُ مُنَزَّلٌ مِنْ رَبِّكَ بِالْحَقِّ فَلَا تَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْمُمْتَرِينَ

“Shall I seek a judge other than God, when He is the one who sent down to you the Book, set out in detail? Those to whom We gave scripture know that it has been sent down from your Lord in truth. So do not be among those who doubt.” (Al-An‘am 6:114)

It is sometimes said that stoning reports “specify” the Qur’anic ruling rather than change it. But what kind of specification removes a large group from those addressed by the verse and replaces a hundred lashes with death by stoning, although the verse makes no such division? Legal terminology does not resolve the contradiction. If a report can prescribe a new and harsher punishment for some of the Qur’an’s audience, the Qur’an no longer judges reports; reports become the authority for rewriting Qur’anic law.

Reaching this judgment does not require tracing every historical report to a particular author first. We have the punishment’s Torah precedent, the Qur’an’s explicit ruling, and the reports used to introduce stoning into Muslim law. The standard is clear: stoning is not the punishment prescribed in the verse on unlawful sexual intercourse. Neither earlier law nor an absent verse can annul the Qur’anic ruling. An unwelcome conclusion for inherited jurisprudence is not answered by finding it unwelcome.

The Hour: What Muhammad Was Commanded to Say

The Qur’an does more than withhold the exact date of the Resurrection. It identifies the limits of Muhammad’s knowledge of “the Hour,” the time of final judgment. People asked when it would arrive. His instructed response was not a timetable of future wars, governments, and personalities, or a count of the stages remaining. Its knowledge belongs to his Lord, and it comes suddenly.

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِنْدَ رَبِّي لَا يُجَلِّيهَا لِوَقْتِهَا إِلَّا هُوَ ثَقُلَتْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ لَا تَأْتِيكُمْ إِلَّا بَغْتَةً يَسْأَلُونَكَ كَأَنَّكَ حَفِيٌّ عَنْهَا قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it arrive? Say: Its knowledge is only with my Lord; none but He will disclose it at its time. It weighs heavily upon the heavens and the earth. It will come upon you only suddenly. They ask as though you had full knowledge of it. Say: Its knowledge is only with God, but most people do not know.” (Al-A‘raf 7:187)

Twice in one verse, knowledge of the Hour is assigned exclusively to God. Between those statements comes the declaration that it will arrive only suddenly. Why set aside this threefold emphasis and construct a calendar from a list of future events? Withholding the time differs from announcing that the Resurrection will occur. The Messenger must warn; he is not to claim access to the hidden schedule of the world’s end.

Al-Nazi‘at repeats the boundary:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا ۝ فِيمَ أَنْتَ مِنْ ذِكْرَاهَا ۝ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنْتَهَاهَا ۝ إِنَّمَا أَنْتَ مُنْذِرُ مَنْ يَخْشَاهَا ۝ كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَاهَا

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it arrive? What have you to do with declaring its time? Its ultimate knowledge belongs to your Lord. You are only a warner to those who fear it. On the day they see it, they will feel they stayed no longer than an evening or its morning.” (Al-Nazi‘at 79:42, 79:43, 79:44, 79:45, 79:46)

The question about declaring its time is clear: Muhammad is not commissioned to disclose hidden knowledge of the Hour. “You are only a warner” identifies his task. If warning were insufficient without a list of wars, banners, claimants to salvation, and ordered signs, why would the Qur’an describe his task within these limits?

Al-Zukhruf 43:66 likewise states:

هَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا السَّاعَةَ أَنْ تَأْتِيَهُمْ بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

“Are they waiting for anything but the Hour to come upon them suddenly, while they are unaware?” (Al-Zukhruf 43:66)

We cannot affirm both “suddenly” and “while they are unaware,” then construct a sequence of certain, recognizable, calculable signs through which event nine tells us that only two events remain. If such reports are said merely to warn rather than establish an exact time, their ordered schedules and identifiable signs still need to be reconciled with these verses. Calling an event a “sign” does not automatically remove the tension.

Muhammad 47:18 is often cited in response. It is the only passage using the word ashraṭuhā in relation to the Hour. The whole verse must be read:

فَهَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا السَّاعَةَ أَنْ تَأْتِيَهُمْ بَغْتَةً فَقَدْ جَاءَ أَشْرَاطُهَا فَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمْ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمْ ذِكْرَاهُمْ

“Are they waiting for anything but the Hour to come upon them suddenly? Its precursors have already come. Once it reaches them, what use will their recollection be?” (Muhammad 47:18)

The verse both speaks of sudden arrival and says that its precursors “have already come.” The verb jā’a is past tense, and qad emphasizes its realization. It does not say, “Its precursors will come in the future.” The Arabic dictionary Lisan al-‘Arab also uses ashraṭ of a thing’s beginnings or precursors, relating it to initial signs. A detailed later catalogue of “minor and major signs,” with named people and governments, therefore cannot be derived from this verse alone.

The argument is not merely a play on one verb. Resurrection brings accountability and judgment; before judgment, people must have received the case they are to answer. Prophetic calls, clarification of right and wrong, and warnings of return are intelligible preliminaries. In the Qur’an’s historical account, Muhammad is the final prophet, and his mission is presented as the concluding stage of conveying revelation to people. He recites this about himself:

مَا كَانَ مُحَمَّدٌ أَبَا أَحَدٍ مِنْ رِجَالِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ وَخَاتَمَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمًا

“Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but God’s Messenger and the seal of the prophets. God knows all things.” (Al-Ahzab 33:40)

Of the Book, it says:

وَيَوْمَ نَبْعَثُ فِي كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ شَهِيدًا عَلَيْهِمْ مِنْ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَجِئْنَا بِكَ شَهِيدًا عَلَىٰ هَٰؤُلَاءِ وَنَزَّلْنَا عَلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ تِبْيَانًا لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ وَهُدًى وَرَحْمَةً وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُسْلِمِينَ

“On the day We raise in every community a witness against them from among themselves, We will bring you as a witness against these people. We have sent down the Book to you as a clarification of all things, guidance, mercy, and good news for those who surrender.” (Al-Nahl 16:89)

Al-Ma’idah 5:3 also speaks of the completion of religion at a later stage of the mission. Its historical setting must not be equated with that of Muhammad 47:18. The argument is not that every stage of the mission, including the final declaration of completion, had already occurred when Surah Muhammad was revealed. Rather, the process of completing the case addressed to humanity began with the last prophet’s mission and reached completion within it; it did not depend on a future list of kings, rebellions, and legendary creatures. The arrival of the precursors in 47:18 is consistent with the beginning of this final stage of warning and guidance, whose significance becomes clearer through the mission’s eventual completion.

The verse ends by asking what use recollection will be once the Hour arrives. Its practical meaning is clear: the opportunity to listen, reflect, and reform conduct is now. At judgment, regret without action no longer helps. The verse addresses the person who can respond today; it does not teach a timetable of unknown events centuries ahead.

Anyone calling the Dajjal, the Mahdi, the Sufyani, black banners, final wars, and similar events “Qur’anic precursors” must show where those names occur in 47:18 and which Qur’anic words make them necessary conditions of the Resurrection. These are figures and motifs of later end-time narratives: a great deceiver, an expected guide, an opposing leader, banners, and battles. The verse names none of them. It speaks of precursors that have come and an Hour that comes suddenly. Using a Qur’anic word to impose contents it does not state is not an explanation of the Qur’an.

Later Islamic report literature, like other communities’ apocalyptic writing, accumulated accounts of wars, saviors, rulers, voices, and extraordinary events at the world’s end. The parallels call for historical investigation of earlier sources, routes of transmission, and each report’s credibility. Before that investigation, however, the Qur’an supplies a standard: Muhammad was commanded to warn that knowledge of the Hour belongs to God and that it arrives suddenly, not to turn God’s exclusive knowledge into a timetable traded in the marketplace of reports.

The People of the Cave: Filling a Gap with an Occasion-of-Revelation Story

A revealing example is the familiar account of questions put to Muhammad about the People of the Cave, Dhu al-Qarnayn, and the Spirit. In the popular account of the occasion of revelation, Quraysh consulted Jewish scholars, who proposed three questions to test Muhammad’s prophethood. Repetition in commentaries can leave readers thinking that the Qur’an itself identifies the questioners as Jewish. It does not name them in these passages. Concerning the Spirit and Dhu al-Qarnayn, it says, “They ask you”; it introduces the cave narrative differently. That all three questions were necessarily posed on one occasion at a Jewish group’s direction is not information supplied by the Qur’an. It is a claim made in a report about the occasion of revelation and must be assessed like any other report.

Assessing that attribution requires more than inspecting a chain of transmitters’ names. We must investigate the actual history of the subjects supposedly chosen to test a prophet. Who knew the sleepers’ story before Islam? Which communities told stories of a two-horned ruler and a barrier against Gog and Magog? In which religious settings were the Spirit, the Holy Spirit, and their relationship to Christ serious matters of dispute? The answers together provide a way to test the report itself.

First, the People of the Cave. Before Islam, Christian communities knew the story as the Sleepers of Ephesus. Syriac Christian writings describe young people who seek refuge from a ruler’s persecution, fall into a long sleep, and awaken years later. The connection is not a modern guess. Jacob of Serugh, a Syriac Christian poet and preacher who died in 521 CE, composed a verse homily about the youths of Ephesus in the late fifth or early sixth century. The text predates Muhammad’s mission. The story therefore belonged to Christian religious memory; its Christian background was not invented later by Muslim commentators.

If the sleepers’ story was used to test prophethood, a natural question follows: why would people who did not accept Jesus as a prophet make a story about Christian believers the test of another person’s prophethood? Of course, someone can hear about or ask about another religion. But the possibility of a Jewish questioner is not reliable evidence for that attribution. Where a story from Christian faith becomes the supposed test, it is reasonable first to investigate Christian participation in the setting of the question. The popularity of a report about “Jewish scholars” must not replace that inquiry.

Second, Dhu al-Qarnayn, the Qur’anic title usually rendered “the Two-Horned One.” The Qur’an gives no personal name, so we cannot arbitrarily identify him with a particular historical ruler. There is, however, important evidence about the literary setting of elements in his story. A Syriac Christian work known as the Syriac Alexander Legend brings together a ruler associated with two horns, journeys to the world’s limits, and a barrier against Gog and Magog. These parallels are more specific than a shared theme of a good king. Horns, journeys to the world’s edges, and the barrier are particular narrative elements that invite comparison.

Tommaso Tesei’s research shows that the dating of the Syriac work’s layers remains contested. He places a core of the story toward the end of Justinian’s sixth-century reign and identifies some additions as seventh-century material. Other scholars emphasize a seventh-century formulation. This disagreement must remain visible; it does not erase the existence of the related Christian tradition. Nor does this article seek a definitive personal name for Dhu al-Qarnayn. If the Qur’an leaves him unnamed, demonstrating literary connections must not require repeating the same unsupported identification we criticize. The necessary conclusion is narrower: this subject too has significant connections with the Christian narrative world of Late Antiquity.

Third, the Spirit. Its significance becomes clearer when we step outside later commentaries and examine Christianity’s pre-Islamic history. The Spirit and Holy Spirit were not marginal Christian concerns. Disputes involved the Holy Spirit’s relationship to Father and Son, Jesus’ humanity and divinity, the Word and incarnation, and what in Christ was human or divine. Some disagreements were consequential enough for bishops and rulers to convene councils to establish official doctrine. Their documents survive, so the existence of these debates is not an unsupported guess about earlier beliefs.

Constantinople, 381 CE: The Status of the Holy Spirit

The Council of Constantinople met in 381 CE, more than two centuries before Muhammad’s mission began. The Holy Spirit’s status was among the period’s controversies. Some groups rejected placing the Spirit on the same level as Father and Son; their opponents called them “fighters against the Spirit.” The creed associated with this council describes the Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life, proceeding from the Father and worshiped and glorified with Father and Son. This was not a private disagreement among a few individuals: a major church council acted to establish doctrine. Pre-Islamic Christian controversy about the Spirit therefore has clear historical documentation.

Chalcedon, 451 CE: How Were Christ’s Humanity and Divinity Related?

Seventy years later, in 451 CE, the Council of Chalcedon met near Constantinople during Emperor Marcian’s reign. Its principal concern was the relationship between Jesus’ human and divine natures, rather than a repetition of the earlier council’s particular dispute about the Holy Spirit. If Jesus was both human and divine in Christian belief, how were those realities united in one person? Was his humanity absorbed into divinity? Should there be two persons? If he was truly human, what place did his human body and soul have?

The council’s definition declared Christ complete in divinity and humanity, possessing a human body and rational soul, and recognized in two natures united in one person without confusion, change, division, or separation. This was the council’s official formulation, not a position every Christian accepted without dispute. Some Eastern churches, including communities in Egypt and Syria, opposed the Chalcedonian formulation. Disagreements about Christ’s nature, person, and later his will continued through the sixth and seventh centuries.

We therefore have two distinct but related historical witnesses: Constantinople in 381 on the Holy Spirit within the Trinity, and Chalcedon in 451 on Christ’s humanity and divinity. These issues must not be conflated. Nevertheless, the Holy Spirit, the Word, and Jesus’ humanity and divinity were subjects of active, documented Christian controversy. The councils preceded Muhammad, and the divisions they represented continued into Islam’s emergence. In that setting, a question about the Spirit was natural and highly sensitive.

Now consider the Qur’an itself. Al-Nisa 4:171 describes Jesus as God’s Word and a spirit from Him, while forbidding the assertion of “Three”:

يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ وَلَا تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ إِنَّمَا الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ وَكَلِمَتُهُ أَلْقَاهَا إِلَىٰ مَرْيَمَ وَرُوحٌ مِنْهُ فَآمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِ وَلَا تَقُولُوا ثَلَاثَةٌ انْتَهُوا خَيْرًا لَكُمْ إِنَّمَا اللَّهُ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ سُبْحَانَهُ أَنْ يَكُونَ لَهُ وَلَدٌ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ وَكِيلًا

“People of the Book, do not exceed the bounds in your religion or say anything about God except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, is only God’s Messenger, His Word conveyed to Mary, and a spirit from Him. Believe in God and His messengers, and do not say ‘Three.’ Desist; it is better for you. God is only one God, far above having a child. Everything in the heavens and earth belongs to Him, and God is sufficient as guardian.” (Al-Nisa 4:171)

Al-Baqarah 2:87 and 2:253, and Al-Ma’idah 5:110, also connect Jesus with the Holy Spirit. These terms are not detached from the Qur’an’s discussions of Jesus and scriptural communities. The verse concerning the question about the Spirit states:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الرُّوحِ قُلِ الرُّوحُ مِنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّي وَمَا أُوتِيتُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

“They ask you about the Spirit. Say: The Spirit belongs to my Lord’s command, and you have been given only a little knowledge.” (Al-Isra 17:85)

The verse does not identify the questioners. We do, however, have a historical setting in which the Spirit and Jesus’ status were prominent, documented Christian concerns. The possibility that Christians, or scriptural believers who accepted Jesus, raised such a question therefore has historical and Qur’anic grounds. This context offers a serious, testable alternative to the familiar account assigning all three questions to Jewish scholars.

It may be objected that Jewish tradition also contains the concept of spirit, and that Sahih al-Bukhari preserves Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud’s report of a Jewish group asking about the Spirit in Medina. The Jewish concept is undeniable, but it does not erase Christian controversy over the Holy Spirit and Christ’s nature. Nor does Ibn Mas‘ud’s report, even if accepted, establish that all three familiar questions were posed together at Jewish scholars’ direction. Moreover, relating Al-Isra 17:85 to a Medinan event creates a recognized issue in traditional commentaries because the surah is commonly classified as Meccan. That relationship requires explanation. One separate report must not be made to govern every element of another story without evidence.

The councils’ history nevertheless gives us no direct record naming or identifying the actual questioners. Its evidence establishes the historical background of Christian controversy. The verse establishes that a question about the Spirit was asked. Comparing them supports a strong hypothesis connecting the question with a Christian intellectual setting. We may state that hypothesis clearly and challenge the unsupported attribution of all three questions to Jews. The same Qur’an-centered method, however, forbids turning our own inference about the questioners into a new certainty of transmitted lore.

Concerning Dhu al-Qarnayn, the Qur’an likewise says:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنْ ذِي الْقَرْنَيْنِ قُلْ سَأَتْلُو عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْهُ ذِكْرًا

“They ask you about Dhu al-Qarnayn. Say: I will recite to you something of his account.” (Al-Kahf 18:83)

This is the relevant point: alongside the Christian sleepers’ story and debates about the Spirit, elements of Dhu al-Qarnayn’s narrative also have witnesses in Syriac Christian tradition. The collection of subjects is not disconnected from Christian culture. Anyone insisting that the questioners were certainly Jewish must do more than appeal to a familiar occasion-of-revelation report while setting that Christian history aside. The possibility that Jews asked these questions does not prove that they actually did.

The sleepers’ story itself supplies a second layer of the problem. Christian accounts of the youths of Ephesus offered an elaborate narrative, including names, city, ruler, reasons for flight, and events after awakening. The Qur’an does not reproduce every identity and detail. It presents what it considers necessary for guidance, then warns about their number:

سَيَقُولُونَ ثَلَاثَةٌ رَابِعُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ خَمْسَةٌ سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ رَجْمًا بِالْغَيْبِ وَيَقُولُونَ سَبْعَةٌ وَثَامِنُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ قُلْ رَبِّي أَعْلَمُ بِعِدَّتِهِمْ مَا يَعْلَمُهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ فَلَا تُمَارِ فِيهِمْ إِلَّا مِرَاءً ظَاهِرًا وَلَا تَسْتَفْتِ فِيهِمْ مِنْهُمْ أَحَدًا

“They will say, ‘Three, their dog the fourth,’ and ‘Five, their dog the sixth’—guessing at the unseen—and ‘Seven, their dog the eighth.’ Say: My Lord knows their number best; only a few know them. Do not dispute about them beyond what is apparent, and do not seek a ruling about them from any of those people.” (Al-Kahf 18:22)

The verse calls unsupported speculation “guessing at the unseen.” It directs Muhammad to refer knowledge of their number to his Lord and refrain from extended dispute or seeking details from others. Yet commentarial tradition supplied the youths’ names, a ruler, a cave location, a definite number, and the dog’s name, most famously Qitmir. Such details were available in outside stories and multiplied in commentaries. Why should the dog’s name become religious knowledge when the Qur’an itself warns against speculation about the sleepers?

Two layers are now visible. First, a popular but disputed report supplies the questioners’ identity. Second, known Christian material supplies details of the story itself. Where the Qur’an has not supplied those particulars, why should the machinery of reports fill the space with identities the text chose not to state?

The Sabians: A Familiar Qur’anic Name and Later Uncertainty

The Qur’an names the Sabians three times among religious groups, yet we have no clear, reliable teaching from Muhammad identifying them precisely. This is not merely the loss of a peripheral anecdote. The name appears alongside Jews, Christians, and other communities in three verses. Yet a tradition capable of long stories about the cave dog’s color or the Kaaba’s earliest construction cannot offer a consistent, well-established identification here.

Al-Baqarah states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالنَّصَارَىٰ وَالصَّابِئِينَ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا فَلَهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

“Those who believe, the Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does what is right—will have their reward with their Lord; they will neither fear nor grieve.” (Al-Baqarah 2:62)

Al-Ma’idah likewise states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالصَّابِئُونَ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا فَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

“Those who believe, the Jews, Sabians, and Christians—whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does what is right—will neither fear nor grieve.” (Al-Ma’idah 5:69)

Al-Hajj names a wider range:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالصَّابِئِينَ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ وَالْمَجُوسَ وَالَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدٌ

“Those who believe, the Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magians, and those who associate partners with God—God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection. God witnesses everything.” (Al-Hajj 22:17)

In each passage, the Sabians are named as though the audience could recognize the designation. The verse does not explain their location, detailed beliefs, or the meaning of the name, any more than it gives an encyclopedia entry for Jews or Magians. This is at least strong evidence that the name was recognizable in the mission’s first setting. Its use in such a context would be difficult to explain if the community had no idea whom it designated.

Later commentators disagree. Were Sabians closer to Jews or Christians? Were they star-worshipers, a separate community with distinctive beliefs, or people described as having left their ancestral religion? Various answers have been proposed. What matters here is the absence of clear, reliable prophetic instruction identifying the Sabians whom the Qur’an names three times. Disagreement among commentators does not turn any identification into Muhammad’s words.

Later history includes investigations of Mandaeans and other groups who adopted the Sabian name or received it from outsiders. We must not assume without grounds that the term designated exactly the same community with unchanging beliefs in every period. This historical complexity shows why a hurried definition from a later dictionary cannot replace understanding the Qur’an’s first audience.

Compare the patterns. Transmitters had extensive Jewish and Christian narrative material about some prophets, and Islamic commentary accumulated detailed names and stories. Yet for a group explicitly named three times in the Qur’an, there was no comparable, agreed stock of stories to reproduce. The result was silence about a precise identity, competing guesses, and contradictory identifications. If reports were Muhammad’s complete and independent explanation of the Qur’an, how should we explain this striking disparity between subjects rich in reports and subjects left unresolved?

The Disconnected Letters: No Definitive Explanation from Muhammad

Twenty-nine surahs begin with individual Arabic letters or combinations of letters: Alif-Lam-Mim, Alif-Lam-Ra, Kaf-Ha-Ya-‘Ayn-Sad, Ta-Ha, Ya-Sin, Ha-Mim, Qaf, Nun, and others. These “disconnected letters” are part of the Qur’anic text recited and memorized from the earliest days. Despite their conspicuous, recurring presence, we have no clear, sound hadith giving Muhammad’s definitive explanation of all of them.

If Muhammad explained every difficult part of the Qur’an outside the Book, and hadith preserved that explanation for later generations, these letters should be among the first subjects addressed. Everyone heard them at the openings of surahs. How could detailed reports survive about a dog’s name, the Kaaba’s materials, people’s biographies, and signs for distant future generations, yet no reliable, consistent explanation remain for letters Muhammad himself recited?

Commentators have offered different answers: names of surahs, names of God, oaths, initials referring to other words, or codes shared privately by God and the Messenger. Some derive chronological or historical secrets from numerical calculations and letter combinations. Multiple opinions do not make every proposal worthless. They do show that we lack a sound, definitive teaching from Muhammad that settles the disagreement. A proposed meaning must be presented as research or a possibility, not as something God has required us to accept.

Even “only God and the Messenger knew their meaning” requires evidence if asserted as certain knowledge. The Qur’an makes no such statement about these letters. The absence of a sound explanation does not prove that Muhammad knew their meaning and withheld it, just as it does not prove that he did not know. Our knowledge has a clear limit here. Instead of accepting it, a story of a private code can turn “we do not know” into a seemingly sacred formula.

The connection with parallel silence is clear. Unlike familiar prophetic stories, these particular opening-letter combinations had no ready-made, consistent outside narrative from which a detailed commentary could be drawn. Other subjects attracted thousands of reports, while explanations here remained scattered. A Qur’an-centered reader should attend to the letters and their place in the Book, rather than proclaim an unsupported guess a secret of revelation.

Dhu al-Kifl: A Qur’anic Name without an Established Biography

The Qur’an names Dhu al-Kifl twice. Al-Anbiya states:

وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِدْرِيسَ وَذَا الْكِفْلِ كُلٌّ مِنَ الصَّابِرِينَ ۝ وَأَدْخَلْنَاهُمْ فِي رَحْمَتِنَا إِنَّهُمْ مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

“And Ishmael, Idris, and Dhu al-Kifl: each was steadfast. We admitted them into Our mercy; they were among the righteous.” (Al-Anbiya 21:85, 21:86)

Surah Sad also states:

وَاذْكُرْ إِسْمَاعِيلَ وَالْيَسَعَ وَذَا الْكِفْلِ وَكُلٌّ مِنَ الْأَخْيَارِ

“Remember Ishmael, Elisha, and Dhu al-Kifl; each was among the chosen, the good.” (Sad 38:48)

The Qur’an tells us of Dhu al-Kifl’s patience, righteousness, and goodness, but does not disclose a personal name behind the designation, his people, location, period, mission, or life story. He appears alongside prophets, but these two passages do not explicitly call him a prophet. That boundary must remain. Placement among prophets does not automatically transfer every outside biography into the Qur’an.

Commentaries propose various identities. Some connect him with figures from Israelite history; others tell stories of his commitments and judgments, and they disagree about whether he was a prophet. Yet the simple question remains unanswered definitively: in which clear, sound report did Muhammad identify Dhu al-Kifl as the person being claimed? If these biographies formed part of his necessary teaching, how is their inconsistency explained?

The same tradition that offered lengthy accounts for figures with outside stories could not supply a consistent, documented identity for Dhu al-Kifl. There was no readily available narrative under that same name in familiar outside traditions that could easily become a Qur’anic biography. Like the Sabians and disconnected letters, this silence illustrates the tradition’s differing treatment of different subjects.

Parallel Silence: The Other Side of Isra’iliyyat

The article’s central argument should now be clearer. The problem is larger than a few commentators saying too much about a verse. Reports and commentaries behave differently across subjects. Where Jewish, Christian, and surrounding cultures offered abundant stories, religious details multiplied strikingly. Where no comparable, relatively consistent stock existed, reports fell silent or yielded scattered guesses and opinions. These are two sides of one pattern: abundant narration where stories were available, and silence where they were not.

Consider the examples. The Qur’an tells Adam’s story to teach human responsibility; reports supply his wife’s name, the tree’s identity, and details of creation and travels. The Qur’an identifies the angel of death by task; popular culture supplies “Azrael.” It describes Abraham and Ishmael constructing the House; commentary invents a prehistory of Adam and angels building it. It leaves the elderly Midianite unnamed; some translators write “(Shu‘ayb) said.” It recounts the sleepers for reflection and warns against guessing their number; commentary supplies names, an address, a ruler, and a named dog.

The same tradition displays numerous parallels with Jewish and Christian apocalyptic literature. Those traditions included returning saviors, final battles, tribulations, rulers, and cosmic signs. Islamic report literature developed detailed lists of minor and major signs, wars, voices, claimants, and sequences of governments. While the Qur’an describes a sudden Hour whose precursors have come, the marketplace of reports divides the future into recognizable stages. These parallels, alongside known channels of transmission, raise serious questions about the reports’ origins.

Now consider the other side. The Sabians appear three times: why has no clear, sound, consistent identification from Muhammad survived? Dhu al-Kifl is named twice: why no established prophetic biography? The disconnected letters are recited at the beginning of twenty-nine surahs: why no sound explanation that removes the need for so many guesses? If hadith’s role truly was to fill every Qur’anic silence through the Prophet’s independent instruction, why does the report tradition prove so limited on these important subjects?

This parallel silence cannot simply be dismissed as coincidence. A particular report might never have been recorded or might have been lost. But that general possibility is insufficient to explain a repeated pattern. If the distribution is accidental, why do subjects rich in outside stories attract similar, sometimes highly specific details in Islamic reports, while other subjects remain without reliable, coherent definitions? “Perhaps the reports were lost” is itself a hypothesis requiring evidence.

Nor does this article rely solely on resemblance between stories. Historical channels of transmission are known. Islamic works name transmitters of Israelite lore; early texts contain formulas such as “I found in the Torah” and “it has reached us from the People of the Book”; Muslim scholars themselves acknowledged the abundance and unreliability of much Isra’iliyyat. The sleepers’ story has pre-Islamic Christian textual witnesses. Dhu al-Qarnayn’s narrative has parallels in Syriac Christian tradition, with disputed dating of its layers. Shu‘ayb illustrates an unsupported identification entering contemporary translations. These different kinds of evidence together disclose an intelligible process.

The relationship between outside narrative resources and abundant Islamic reports is therefore not a random, groundless conjecture. This does not mean that every shared story was copied or every hadith with an outside parallel was fabricated. The more precise, and still firm, conclusion is that neither resemblance, popularity, nor a chain of transmissions can turn outside material into established teaching from God or the Messenger without reliable evidence and Qur’anic scrutiny. Patterns of abundance and silence, combined with known routes of transmission, make extensive Isra’iliyyat influence one of the strongest explanations for the formation of part of the interpretive tradition.

The familiar claim that “hadith explains everything the Qur’an leaves brief” now faces a serious difficulty. Who, then, was Dhu al-Kifl? Exactly which community were the Qur’an’s Sabians? What is the definitive meaning of the disconnected letters? Why are reliable answers scarce here, while commentarial reports abound about the cave dog’s name and the length of Adam’s strides? Treating commentary books as sacred cannot silence these questions.

The Difficulty of Saying “We Do Not Know”

Ready-made stories were not the only force behind Isra’iliyyat. Human responses to ignorance also mattered. A commentator or storyteller facing an unanswered question could say, “I do not know.” But where the ability to answer every mystery signals authority and power, that simple sentence can become the hardest one to say. Silence may look like ignorance, while a long story makes its teller appear learned and privy to secrets.

In that environment, every silence acquires a name: Adam’s wife, the tree, the sleepers’ ruler and dog, the angel of death, Moses’ father-in-law, the Kaaba’s earlier builder. Each disagreement attracts another story to cover it. One figure lives for centuries to join distant events; elsewhere a stone falls from heaven or an undocumented writing becomes God’s speech. Information moves from book to book until repetition substitutes for evidence. Readers eventually forget that what they read was absent from the Qur’an and may never have reached Muhammad at all.

The introduction began with the angels’ response in Al-Baqarah 2:31, 2:32. Faced with what they did not know, they did not insist on inventing an explanation. They said:

قَالُوا سُبْحَانَكَ لَا عِلْمَ لَنَا إِلَّا مَا عَلَّمْتَنَا إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

“They said: Glory be to You. We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are the Knowing, the Wise.” (Al-Baqarah 2:32)

If the angels were not ashamed to admit ignorance, why should a commentator be? If they did not fabricate answers to protect their standing, what right have human beings to sell conjecture and unsupported reports as God’s word? Acknowledging knowledge’s limits is not reason’s defeat; it begins intellectual honesty. The same honesty is needed for larger questions: Did Muhammad actually say this? Did the story exist centuries before Islam? Does the Qur’an teach this ruling or name? When evidence is absent, admitting its absence is a duty, not a disgrace.

The inability to say “I do not know” did not remain an individual failing. As a public habit, it shifts the boundary between God’s speech and human tradition. Stories enter commentary, commentary enters public teaching, reports govern how verses are understood, and eventually a commentator’s words become as binding as the Qur’an. The problem lies in that transformation: people bring their own learning close to revelation and hide their ignorance behind God’s name.

The Principle of Ali ‘Imran: Ambiguous Passages Are Not an Interpreter’s Property

Ali ‘Imran 3:7, quoted in full in the introduction, establishes an important principle for reading the Book. Its practical consequence must now be connected to the examples examined. The muhkamat—the clear, foundational passages—are called the Book’s mother, foundation, and reference point. The mutashabihat, passages open to uncertainty, also belong to the Book, but they are not a free field for imagination and invented reports. The verse describes people with a deviation in their hearts pursuing the uncertain parts for discord and the interpretation they seek. In contrast, those firmly grounded in knowledge say, “We believe in it; all is from our Lord.” The full verse appears in the introduction. Before treating an outside report as a verse’s definitive explanation, we must therefore turn to the foundational passages. When Al-Nur 24:2 specifies punishment, a story about a missing verse cannot create another ruling. When Al-A‘raf 7:187 assigns knowledge of the Hour to God, end-time reports cannot acquire authority over it. When Al-Kahf 18:22 calls speculation about the sleepers’ number “guessing at the unseen,” outside stories cannot impose names and numbers as articles of faith. When Al-Qasas 28:27 does not name the daughters’ father, inserting “(Shu‘ayb) said” blurs the boundary between the Qur’an’s words and a commentator’s interpretation.

This does not technically classify every silence or unnamed detail as mutashabih. The verse also supports a general principle about knowledge: the unknown must not displace the clear, and uncertainty must not be imposed as definitive interpretation. “This is how I understand the verse” must remain distinct from “This is what God has said.” The difference is between human inquiry and a claim to revelation.

Those firmly grounded in knowledge do not possess every secret. They understand how far evidence can take them and where they must acknowledge that they do not know. Uncertainty about the House’s earlier builder, the elderly Midianite’s identity, the dog’s name, or the disconnected letters’ definitive meaning is therefore not an evasion of interpretation. It is a refusal to manufacture certainty.

Keeping the Qur’an Distinct from Human Tradition

The Qur’an’s words must be presented as Qur’an. A saying attributed to Muhammad must remain identified as an attributed report. Statements by a Companion, a member of the next generation, a commentator, or a storyteller must not be equated with a verse. A historical report may deserve serious investigation; a Jewish or Christian text may illuminate ancient culture; an interpretation may be insightful. None of those merits turns a human work into God’s speech.

The practical distinctions are clear. The Qur’an does not name the angel of death Azrael, say that Adam built the Kaaba before Abraham, or identify Moses’ father-in-law as Shu‘ayb, although that name has entered some translations. It does not supply the sleepers’ names and a definitive number, and it warns against guessing. It describes a sudden Hour whose knowledge belongs to God. It prescribes a hundred lashes for unlawful sexual intercourse without including stoning in that penalty verse. It names the Sabians and Dhu al-Kifl and recites the disconnected letters, yet the report tradition offers no sound, decisive explanation for many questions about them.

These conclusions may trouble someone taught at home or school to regard certain names and rulings as sacred. But faith in the Qur’an means fidelity to the words it contains, not preservation of every addition accumulated around it. A report’s conflict with its clear foundations is not resolved by a transmitter’s fame or a book’s age. Whoever adds a name bears the burden of establishing it. Without reliable evidence, no one is obliged to believe what has not been established.

This boundary does not destroy history. It frees history from claims of divine authority. Let a story remain a story, a historical hypothesis be identified as a hypothesis, and a hadith report be assessed through its evidence and credibility. Let the Qur’an say what it actually says. It is not one of several equal authorities whose scope transmitters may restrict. The Qur’an should be the measure of reports, rather than reports becoming the measure by which the Qur’an is altered.

Conclusion

The phenomenon extends beyond a single name or story. From Adam and the angel of death to the Kaaba, Moses’ father-in-law, the sleepers, stoning, and the world’s end, interpretive and religious traditions contain material the Qur’an does not state. Sometimes earlier Jewish or Christian sources are identifiable. Sometimes attribution to Muhammad lacks sound evidence. Sometimes conflicting accounts have been repeated from book to book until readers forget they are encountering human tradition rather than Qur’anic text.

The Kaaba example shows the House’s history being filled with claims about Adam, although the Qur’an describes Abraham and Ishmael’s work. Moses’ father-in-law shows an unsupported identification crossing into translation as “(Shu‘ayb) said.” The Christian Sleepers of Ephesus tradition and Syriac parallels to Dhu al-Qarnayn’s narrative help us assess the familiar three-question account more carefully. The documented controversies over the Holy Spirit at Constantinople in 381 and Christ’s humanity and divinity at Chalcedon in 451 establish that these were serious, active Christian concerns before Islam. All the questions cannot be assigned exclusively to Jewish scholars without sufficient evidence.

With stoning, the issue moves from historical narrative to law and human life. A punishment with a Torah precedent encounters explicit Qur’anic rulings, yet reports established a place for it in jurisprudence, even invoking an absent verse. In the discussion of the Hour, the statement that its precursors had come and that it would arrive suddenly became buried beneath accounts of future events. Both cases raise the same question: will the Qur’an remain the standard, or will its clear meaning be changed to preserve reports?

Perhaps the most striking evidence is parallel silence itself. Where outside traditions offered abundant material, reports became expansive. Where no ready, reliable explanation of the Sabians, Dhu al-Kifl, or the disconnected letters was available, a system claiming to explain the whole Qur’an fell silent or disagreed. Together with known channels of Isra’iliyyat transmission, this pattern poses an unavoidable question: how much of what has been called an explanation of religion has actually filled Qur’anic silence with other communities’ learning?

The Qur’an offers another way. Its clear foundational passages are the Book’s reference point; its uncertain passages are not interpreters’ property. Guessing about the unseen does not produce knowledge. Knowledge of the Hour belongs to God; the Lord knows the sleepers’ number best. Where knowledge has not been given, the angels’ answer remains the standard of honesty: “We have no knowledge except what You have taught us.”

“We do not know” is not a failure of faith. Where the one God has not spoken, it is an expression of honesty. The failure comes when people call their conjecture knowledge, other people’s stories prophetic teaching, and human tradition revelation.

We seek refuge in the one God from the evil of the rejected Satan, from the domination of false and oppressive authority, and from the part of ourselves that presents conjecture as truth and human speech as revelation.
