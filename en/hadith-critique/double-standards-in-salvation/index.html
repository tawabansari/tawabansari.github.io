Hadith Critique

Blind to Ourselves, Quick to Judge Others

Double Standards in Salvation: A Qur’anic Critique of Doctrines Built on Religious Opinion and Hadith

نسخه فارسی

By name of The God The Merciful The Compassionate

Introduction A Mainstream Doctrine, Not a Marginal Belief When Wishful Thinking Becomes Doctrine When the Standard Is Applied to Someone Else The Qur’an Must Be the Standard for Reports Where Is the Covenant? Further reading Sources for the quotations

We are quick to spot other people’s faults and remarkably slow to recognize our own. The habit is old enough to have become proverbial: an error that seems obvious in someone else suddenly attracts a wealth of excuses when we make it ourselves. Psychologists call this the “bias blind spot”—our tendency to recognize other people’s biases more readily than our own assumptions and prejudices. Experiments have found that people identify self-serving judgments in others as biased, yet regard their own judgments, made on precisely the same basis, as reasonable and impartial.

Source

This weakness becomes more dangerous in religion. A religious belief is not simply an opinion: it is bound up with identity, family, fear, hope, and our expectations about life after death. We may immediately recognize a flaw in someone else’s religion, then abandon that standard when the same reasoning appears in the beliefs we inherited. What we dismiss elsewhere as wishful thinking or an unsupported assertion may acquire the authority of doctrine or sacred tradition when it belongs to us.

The Qur’an does not recount the failures of earlier religious communities so that we can mock them. Their histories offer guidance to its readers, warning us against following the same path. When God describes people of earlier scriptures replacing knowledge of the Book with conjecture, expectations, and assumptions, or attributing human words to the One God, the invitation is not to sneer at them centuries later. We are just as vulnerable. We can repeat their mistakes under different names and in different religious forms.

Yet the advocates of religious opinion and hadith have often fallen into precisely this blind spot. When judging those outside their own community, they may regard an entire lifetime of righteous deeds as worthless in the hereafter and consign the person to everlasting Hell. Within their own inherited system, however, the conclusion changes. Someone who accepts their religious order and remains a “monotheist” by their doctrinal definition may be corrupt, flagrantly sinful, or even a murderous criminal. That person may suffer punishment proportionate to the sins committed, but the final outcome is already settled: one day the Fire will give way to the lasting comfort of Paradise.

At that point, blindness to our own faults ceases to be merely a familiar social failing. It becomes a theological problem.

The Qur’an was not revealed so that we could catalogue the failures of earlier peoples while exempting ourselves from its scrutiny. Their stories hold up a mirror: they expose an error so that it will not be repeated. The problem begins when we condemn a practice in others, then sanctify the same practice under a new name in our own beliefs and exempt it from the very standard we applied to them.

In Al-Baqarah 2:80, the Qur’an records one such belief plainly:

وَقَالُوا لَن تَمَسَّنَا النَّارُ إِلَّا أَيَّامًا مَّعْدُودَةً ۚ قُلْ أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِندَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا فَلَن يُخْلِفَ اللَّهُ عَهْدَهُ ۖ أَمْ تَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ ٨٠

Al-Baqarah 2:80

“They say, ‘The Fire will touch us only for a few numbered days.’ Say, ‘Have you received a covenant from the One God? For the One God will never break His covenant. Or are you saying things about the One God that you do not know?’”

These people were not denying the Fire, accountability, or punishment. Their claim was that whatever punishment they received could not be their final destination. A few days would pass, and deliverance would follow. Such a belief naturally changes a person’s relationship to wrongdoing: the worst possible consequence has already been capped.

The Qur’an’s response, however, is not an argument over the number of days. It does not say that they expected three days but would receive ten, or that the punishment would last longer than they imagined. It challenges the claim itself: Who has guaranteed that outcome for you?

أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِندَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا؟

Al-Baqarah 2:80

Have you received a covenant from the One God?

If such a covenant exists, the One God will not break it. Without one, the error is more serious than miscalculating the duration of punishment: people are making a claim about God without knowledge. The central question is therefore present from the outset. Anyone who guarantees their own ultimate salvation must explain where that guarantee comes from.

A Mainstream Doctrine, Not a Marginal Belief

This belief is not confined to a small faction. Across mainstream Sunni creeds and hadith commentaries associated with different schools, a shared principle emerges: a person who dies with faith and belief in God’s oneness, and whose sins have not placed them outside those schools’ definition of Islam, may be forgiven without punishment or may enter the Fire because of those sins. If they enter it, however, they will not remain there forever. Paradise is understood to be their final destination.

The Creed of al-Tahawi states the principle explicitly:

«وَأَهْلُ الْكَبَائِرِ مِنْ أُمَّةِ مُحَمَّدٍ ﷺ فِي النَّارِ لَا يُخَلَّدُونَ، إِذَا مَاتُوا وَهُمْ مُوَحِّدُونَ، وَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُونُوا تَائِبِينَ... وَهُمْ فِي مَشِيئَتِهِ وَحُكْمِهِ، إِنْ شَاءَ غَفَرَ لَهُمْ وَعَفَا عَنْهُمْ بِفَضْلِهِ... وَإِنْ شَاءَ عَذَّبَهُمْ فِي النَّارِ بِعَدْلِهِ، ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُهُمْ مِنْهَا بِرَحْمَتِهِ وَشَفَاعَةِ الشَّافِعِينَ مِنْ أَهْلِ طَاعَتِهِ، ثُمَّ يَبْعَثُهُمْ إِلَى جَنَّتِهِ.»

Those members of Muhammad’s community who commit major sins will not remain in the Fire forever if they die as monotheists, even if they die without repentance. They remain subject to God’s will and judgment. He may forgive and pardon them through His grace, or He may punish them justly in the Fire. Afterward, however, He will bring them out through His mercy and the intercession of intercessors from among those who obey Him, and send them into His Paradise.

Source

Qadi ‘Iyad, discussing the sinner who professes the two testimonies of faith, likewise states:

«بَلْ هُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ، وَإِنْ لَمْ يُغْفَرْ لَهُ وَعُذِّبَ، فَلَا بُدَّ مِنْ إِخْرَاجِهِ مِنَ النَّارِ وَإِدْخَالِهِ الْجَنَّةَ.»

Such a person remains a believer; if he is not forgiven and is punished, he must nevertheless be brought out of the Fire and admitted to Paradise.

Source

Al-Nawawi expresses the same position even more starkly:

«فَلَا يُخَلَّدُ فِي النَّارِ أَحَدٌ مَاتَ عَلَى التَّوْحِيدِ وَلَوْ عَمِلَ مِنَ الْمَعَاصِي مَا عَمِلَ، كَمَا أَنَّهُ لَا يَدْخُلُ الْجَنَّةَ أَحَدٌ مَاتَ عَلَى الْكُفْرِ وَلَوْ عَمِلَ مِنْ أَعْمَالِ الْبِرِّ مَا عَمِلَ.»

No one who dies affirming God’s oneness will remain in the Fire forever, whatever sins he may have committed; just as no one who dies in unbelief will enter Paradise, whatever good deeds he may have performed.

Source

The Hanbali and Athari traditions maintain the same principle, and contemporary Salafi teaching repeats it unequivocally. ‘Abd al-‘Aziz ibn Baz says:

«الْمُوَحِّدُ الْمُؤْمِنُ لَا يُخَلَّدُ فِي النَّارِ، إِذَا دَخَلَهَا بِمَعْصِيَةٍ يُعَذَّبُ عَلَى قَدْرِ الْمَعْصِيَةِ، ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُهُ اللَّهُ مِنَ النَّارِ إِلَى الْجَنَّةِ.»

A believing monotheist will not remain in the Fire forever. If a sin causes him to enter it, he will be punished according to that sin, and God will then bring him out of the Fire into Paradise. The same principle is retained even for an intentional murderer. The punishment may be severe and prolonged, but because the person is a monotheist, the Fire is not treated as his final destination.

Source

There is no need to exaggerate or understate this doctrine. Sunni teaching does not say that sin has no consequences, or that every monotheist enters Paradise immediately and without accountability. Its precise claim is that a sinful monotheist may be punished, but his ultimate salvation is certain.

The same system reaches a different conclusion about someone who dies in unbelief. Al-Nawawi’s statement is explicit: however many good deeds that person has performed, he will not enter Paradise.

The hadith foundations of this doctrine are readily identifiable. In Sahih Muslim, Jabir reports:

«أَتَى النَّبِيَّ ﷺ رَجُلٌ فَقَالَ: يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ، مَا الْمُوجِبَتَانِ؟ فَقَالَ: مَنْ مَاتَ لَا يُشْرِكُ بِاللَّهِ شَيْئًا دَخَلَ الْجَنَّةَ، وَمَنْ مَاتَ يُشْرِكُ بِاللَّهِ شَيْئًا دَخَلَ النَّارَ.»

A man came to the Prophet and asked, “Messenger of God, what are the two things that make the outcome inevitable?” He replied, “Whoever dies without associating anything with God will enter Paradise, and whoever dies associating anything with God will enter the Fire.”

Source

The report from Abu Dharr is more explicit still:

«أَتَانِي جِبْرِيلُ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ فَبَشَّرَنِي أَنَّهُ مَنْ مَاتَ مِنْ أُمَّتِكَ لَا يُشْرِكُ بِاللَّهِ شَيْئًا دَخَلَ الْجَنَّةَ. قُلْتُ: وَإِنْ زَنَى وَإِنْ سَرَقَ؟ قَالَ: وَإِنْ زَنَى وَإِنْ سَرَقَ.»

“Gabriel came to me and gave me the good news that whoever among your community dies without associating anything with God will enter Paradise. I asked, ‘Even if he has committed unlawful sexual intercourse and stolen?’ He answered, ‘Even if he has committed unlawful sexual intercourse and stolen.’”

Source

Another report frequently used in this discussion concerns the last person to leave the Fire. In the report attributed to Ibn Mas‘ud, we read:

«إِنِّي لَأَعْلَمُ آخِرَ أَهْلِ النَّارِ خُرُوجًا مِنْهَا، وَآخِرَ أَهْلِ الْجَنَّةِ دُخُولًا الْجَنَّةَ، رَجُلٌ يَخْرُجُ مِنَ النَّارِ حَبْوًا، فَيَقُولُ اللَّهُ تَبَارَكَ وَتَعَالَى لَهُ: اذْهَبْ فَادْخُلِ الْجَنَّةَ. فَيَأْتِيهَا فَيُخَيَّلُ إِلَيْهِ أَنَّهَا مَلْأَى، فَيَرْجِعُ فَيَقُولُ: يَا رَبِّ، وَجَدْتُهَا مَلْأَى. فَيَقُولُ اللَّهُ تَبَارَكَ وَتَعَالَى لَهُ: اذْهَبْ فَادْخُلِ الْجَنَّةَ. فَيَأْتِيهَا فَيُخَيَّلُ إِلَيْهِ أَنَّهَا مَلْأَى، فَيَرْجِعُ فَيَقُولُ: يَا رَبِّ، وَجَدْتُهَا مَلْأَى. فَيَقُولُ اللَّهُ لَهُ: اذْهَبْ فَادْخُلِ الْجَنَّةَ، فَإِنَّ لَكَ مِثْلَ الدُّنْيَا وَعَشَرَةَ أَمْثَالِهَا. فَيَقُولُ: أَتَسْخَرُ بِي أَوْ أَتَضْحَكُ بِي وَأَنْتَ الْمَلِكُ؟»

“I know the last of the people of the Fire to leave it and the last of the people of Paradise to enter Paradise. A man comes crawling out of the Fire, and God, blessed and exalted, tells him, ‘Go and enter Paradise.’ He approaches it, but it appears to him to be full. He returns and says, ‘My Lord, I found it full.’ God, blessed and exalted, tells him again, ‘Go and enter Paradise.’ He approaches it, but again it appears to him to be full, and he returns, saying, ‘My Lord, I found it full.’ Then God tells him, ‘Go and enter Paradise, for you shall have the equivalent of the world and ten times as much.’ The man says, ‘Are You mocking me, or laughing at me, when You are the King?’”

Source

This report does not, by itself, establish the rule that all monotheists will be saved. Alongside other reports, however, it supplies one of the central images in the hadith account of release from the Fire.

Fairness requires acknowledging that this belief is not exclusive to Sunnis. A closely related position exists in Imami Shi‘i theology, although allegiance to the Imams and intercession add further layers to that doctrinal system. Shaykh al-Saduq states:

«فَأَمَّا الْمُوَحِّدُونَ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكَبَائِرِ فَإِنَّهُمْ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنْهَا بِالرَّحْمَةِ الَّتِي تَدْرِكُهُمْ وَالشَّفَاعَةِ الَّتِي تَنَالُهُمْ.»

Monotheists guilty of major sins will leave the Fire through the mercy that reaches them and the intercession that benefits them.

Source

The underlying belief is therefore clear. In mainstream Sunni thought, and in a related form in Imami theology, religious identity carries enormous weight in determining a person’s final destiny. The sinful monotheist may burn, but will ultimately be saved. Someone who dies in what those systems define as unbelief or association of partners with God has no equivalent assurance on the strength of good deeds.

When Wishful Thinking Becomes Doctrine

Let us now examine this belief by the same standard the Qur’an applies to others. The immediate response comes in the verses directly following Al-Baqarah 2:80:

بَلَىٰ مَن كَسَبَ سَيِّئَةً وَأَحَاطَتْ بِهِۦ خَطِيٓـَٔتُهُۥ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ أَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلنَّارِ ۖ هُمْ فِيهَا خَـٰلِدُونَ ٨١

Al-Baqarah 2:81

وَٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ أَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۖ هُمْ فِيهَا خَـٰلِدُونَ ٨٢

Al-Baqarah 2:82

“Indeed, whoever earns evil and is encompassed by his wrongdoing—those are the people of the Fire, remaining there. And those who believe and do righteous deeds—those are the people of Paradise, remaining there.”

Their claim was that their punishment would be limited. The Qur’an answers on precisely that point: the person who earns evil and is engulfed by wrongdoing belongs among the people of the Fire. On the other side stand those who believe and do righteous deeds.

The verse itself does not say “whoever associates partners with God” or “whoever is an unbeliever.” It speaks of sayyi’ah, an evil deed, and khati’ah, wrongdoing. Yet advocates of religious opinion and hadith effectively read it through the very logic the Qur’an challenged in the earlier community. Those people, too, claimed to possess monotheism and scripture. Despite distortion, concealment, and attributing human speech to the One God, they had reassured themselves that the Fire could not be their final destination.

Centuries later, the same assurance reappeared under a new name in Muslim doctrine. Now the claim was that anyone who died a “monotheist,” whatever sins he had committed, would never remain in the Fire forever. When the verse says that someone who earns evil and is engulfed by wrongdoing belongs in the Fire and remains there, the sinful monotheist must therefore be excluded. “Evil” is brought close to unbelief or associating partners with God, while being “encompassed by wrongdoing” is interpreted in a way that protects the doctrine already in place.

The resemblance is difficult to overlook. The Qur’an criticizes a community for treating its punishment as temporary because of a supposed special relationship with God. Advocates of religious opinion and hadith have constructed the same assurance for “the people of monotheism.” The names differ, but the structure is strikingly similar: we may sin and even enter the Fire, yet because we belong to the saved community, the Fire cannot be the end of our story. What is dismissed in others as wishful thinking and religious self-deception becomes, among ourselves, an article of faith.

We have to ask whether we derived the doctrine from the verse, or required the verse to accommodate a doctrine we already held.

The same danger appears a few verses earlier in the word amani. Al-Baqarah 2:78 reads:

وَمِنْهُمْ أُمِّيُّونَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ ٱلْكِتَابَ إِلَّآ أَمَانِيَّ وَإِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَظُنُّونَ ٧٨

Al-Baqarah 2:78

“Among them are unlettered people who have no knowledge of the Book beyond recitations and imaginings; they do nothing but conjecture.”

Amani comes from the Arabic root m-n-y, associated with wishes and mental imaginings, and in some older usages with reading or recitation. The verse places it between “they do not know the Book” and “they do nothing but conjecture.” They lack knowledge of the Book, but possess recitations, things they have heard, expectations, and imaginings—and base their judgments on those.

That is where the danger begins. Instead of reading the Book to discover what it says, we read it through beliefs we already hold. The verse must conform to our doctrine, rather than our doctrine to the verse. For a fuller discussion of this word and its context, see the reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:78 in the Qur’an Reflection section.

The Qur’an returns to the belief in “a few days of Fire” in Ali ‘Imran, this time explicitly describing its effect on religious life:

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ نَصِيبًۭا مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَىٰ كِتَـٰبِ ٱللَّهِ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَهُمْ ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّىٰ فَرِيقٌۭ مِّنْهُمْ وَهُم مُّعْرِضُونَ ٢٣

Al-Imran 3:23

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا۟ لَن تَمَسَّنَا ٱلنَّارُ إِلَّآ أَيَّامًۭا مَّعْدُودَٰتٍۢ ۖ وَغَرَّهُمْ فِى دِينِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَفْتَرُونَ ٢٤

Al-Imran 3:24

فَكَيْفَ إِذَا جَمَعْنَـٰهُمْ لِيَوْمٍۢ لَّا رَيْبَ فِيهِ وَوُفِّيَتْ كُلُّ نَفْسٍۢ مَّا كَسَبَتْ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ ٢٥

Al-Imran 3:25

“Have you not seen those who were given a share of the Book? They are called to the Book of the One God so that it may judge between them, yet a group of them turns away in rejection. That is because they say, ‘The Fire will touch us only for a few numbered days.’ What they used to invent has deceived them in their religion. How will it be when We gather them for a Day about which there is no doubt, and every soul is paid in full for what it has earned, and they are not wronged?”

We can no longer dismiss the connection between an assurance of temporary punishment and weakened responsibility as merely our own psychological speculation. The Qur’an itself says:

وَغَرَّهُمْ فِى دِينِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَفْتَرُونَ

Al-Imran 3:24

What they attributed falsely to God deceived them in their religion.

If someone is certain that the worst possible outcome is a period of punishment followed by guaranteed salvation, that belief affects conduct. Punishment ceases to be the dreadful end of the road and becomes a temporary stage that must eventually pass, however painful it is. The Qur’an identifies precisely this assurance among earlier peoples as a source of religious deception.

The passage ends by returning each person to what that person has earned:

وَوُفِّيَتْ كُلُّ نَفْسٍۢ مَّا كَسَبَتْ

Al-Imran 3:25

Every soul will receive in full what it has earned.

The subject is each soul and the consequences of its actions, not an immunity conferred by the name of a people or a religious community.

Al-Baqarah 2:111–112 approaches the same issue from another direction:

Al-Baqarah 2:112

وَقَالُوا۟ لَن يَدْخُلَ ٱلْجَنَّةَ إِلَّا مَن كَانَ هُودًا أَوْ نَصَـٰرَىٰ ۗ تِلْكَ أَمَانِيُّهُمْ ۗ قُلْ هَاتُوا۟ بُرْهَـٰنَكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَـٰدِقِينَ ١١١

Al-Baqarah 2:111

بَلَىٰ مَنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُۥ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌۭ فَلَهُۥٓ أَجْرُهُۥ عِندَ رَبِّهِۦ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ ١١٢

Al-Baqarah 2:112

“They say, ‘No one will enter Paradise unless he is a Jew or a Christian.’ Those are their wishes. Say, ‘Bring your proof, if you are truthful.’ Indeed, whoever submits his face to the One God and does good will have his reward with his Lord; no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve.”

The problem here is not merely that the wrong group name has been chosen for a monopoly on Paradise. The Qur’an does not say that Jewish or Christian identity is insufficient but that substituting another name would solve the problem. It calls the claim to communal salvation itself amani—wishful expectations—and demands proof.

If a Jew claims that being Jewish guarantees his final place in Paradise, he must provide evidence. A Christian is not exempt from that demand. How can a Muslim put the same question to others, then allow the designation “Muhammad’s community” to stand, by itself, in place of a covenant from God?

An-Nisa 4:123–124 closes off that escape still more firmly:

An-Nisa 4:124

لَّيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ وَلَآ أَمَانِىِّ أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ ۗ مَن يَعْمَلْ سُوٓءًۭا يُجْزَ بِهِۦ وَلَا يَجِدْ لَهُۥ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ وَلِيًّۭا وَلَا نَصِيرًۭا ١٢٣

An-Nisa 4:123

وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ مِن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌۭ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ يَدْخُلُونَ ٱلْجَنَّةَ وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًۭا ١٢٤

An-Nisa 4:124

“It is not according to your wishes, nor the wishes of the People of the Book. Whoever does evil will be requited for it and will find no protector or helper apart from the One God. And whoever does righteous deeds, whether male or female, while being a believer—those will enter Paradise and will not be wronged by so much as a speck.”

Commentaries differ over whom “your wishes” addresses, but the statement that follows is general:

مَن يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا يُجْزَ بِهِ

An-Nisa 4:123

Whoever does evil will be requited for it.

On the other side, faith and righteous action appear together. A religious label has not replaced action, and a claim to faith has not released anyone from responsibility for what they have done.

Al-Ma’idah 5:18 makes a further point:

وَقَالَتِ ٱلْيَهُودُ وَٱلنَّصَـٰرَىٰ نَحْنُ أَبْنَـٰٓؤُا۟ ٱللَّهِ وَأَحِبَّـٰٓؤُهُۥ ۚ قُلْ فَلِمَ يُعَذِّبُكُم بِذُنُوبِكُم ۖ بَلْ أَنتُم بَشَرٌۭ مِّمَّنْ خَلَقَ ۚ يَغْفِرُ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيُعَذِّبُ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَلِلَّهِ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ وَإِلَيْهِ ٱلْمَصِيرُ ١٨

Al-Ma'idah 5:18

“The Jews and Christians say, ‘We are the children of the One God and His beloved.’ Say, ‘Why, then, does He punish you for your sins? Rather, you are human beings among those He has created. He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. To the One God belongs the dominion of the heavens, the earth, and everything between them, and to Him is the return.’”

If a special relationship with God made people immune to the consequences of sin, the question “Why, then, does He punish you for your sins?” would make no sense. The verse then returns the decision to the One to whom it belongs:

يَغْفِرُ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيُعَذِّبُ مَن يَشَآءُ

Al-Ma'idah 5:18

He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills.

At this point we must return to An-Nisa 4:48 and 4:116, two of the principal verses invoked in support of the salvation of monotheists:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَغْفِرُ أَن يُشْرَكَ بِهِ وَيَغْفِرُ مَا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَن يَشَاءُ ۚ وَمَن يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ افْتَرَىٰ إِثْمًا عَظِيمًا ٤٨

An-Nisa 4:48

“The One God does not forgive the association of partners with Him, but forgives what is less than that for whom He wills. Whoever associates partners with the One God has fabricated a tremendous sin.”

And in verse 116:

An-Nisa 4:116

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَغْفِرُ أَن يُشْرَكَ بِهِ وَيَغْفِرُ مَا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَن يَشَاءُ ۚ وَمَن يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدْ ضَلَّ ضَلَالًا بَعِيدًا ١١٦

An-Nisa 4:116

“The One God does not forgive the association of partners with Him, but forgives what is less than that for whom He wills. Whoever associates partners with the One God has strayed far into error.”

The decisive words in both verses are li-man yasha’—“for whom He wills.” The Qur’an has not said that everyone who avoids associating partners with God will eventually be forgiven. It leaves the exercise of that will open and the judgment with God.

The later theological system takes a further step. A sinful monotheist may be forgiven and never enter the Fire, or may be punished. In either case, however, the final destination is already known: if he enters the Fire, he will certainly leave it one day.

That conclusion is not identical to “for whom He wills.” “God forgives whom He wills” and “God will ultimately save every member of this category” are different propositions. In the first, the decision remains with God. In the second, a final outcome has been guaranteed for an entire group of people.

One possible response is the theological distinction between wa‘d and wa‘id—promise and threat. God does not break His promise, but through grace and mercy He may refrain from carrying out a threatened punishment. Forgiving a sinner who has been threatened would therefore not amount to breaking a promise.

The possibility of pardon is not in dispute. The Qur’an itself says that God forgives whom He wills. Our question lies elsewhere: how have we moved from the possibility of forgiveness to a certain guarantee that every monotheist will be saved? If this guarantee is God’s promise, we must identify that promise in His own words. An-Nisa 4:48 does not say, “I will ultimately forgive every monotheist.” It says, “for whom He wills.” What remains open in the verse is the exercise of His will. What the theological system has settled in advance is the final destiny of an entire category of human beings.

Al-Baqarah 2:80 now becomes immediately relevant again. If you possess a genuine covenant from the One God, He will not break it. But the covenant must first be shown to exist.

The difficulty becomes sharper when we turn to verses threatening punishment. An-Nisa 4:93 says of intentional murder:

وَمَن يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًۭا مُّتَعَمِّدًۭا فَجَزَآؤُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ خَـٰلِدًۭا فِيهَا وَغَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُۥ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُۥ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًۭا ٩٣

An-Nisa 4:93

“Whoever kills a believer intentionally—his recompense is Hell, remaining there. The One God is angry with him, has cursed him, and has prepared a tremendous punishment for him.”

Hell, remaining in it, divine anger, a curse, and tremendous punishment appear together in a single verse. Yet once the principle “a monotheist does not remain in the Fire forever” is treated as settled, this verse, too, must be read in a way that excludes the monotheist murderer from its final consequence. Sometimes remaining is given a limited duration; sometimes the threat is treated as conditional; sometimes it is simply said that however much punishment the murderer receives, his monotheism means he must eventually leave.

When the Qur’an wishes to describe a way back from such sins, however, it has no difficulty doing so. Al-Furqan offers a particularly clear example:

وَٱلَّذِينَ لَا يَدْعُونَ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَـٰهًا ءَاخَرَ وَلَا يَقْتُلُونَ ٱلنَّفْسَ ٱلَّتِى حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ إِلَّا بِٱلْحَقِّ وَلَا يَزْنُونَ ۚ وَمَن يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ يَلْقَ أَثَامًۭا ٦٨

Al-Furqan 25:68

يُضَـٰعَفْ لَهُ ٱلْعَذَابُ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ وَيَخْلُدْ فِيهِۦ مُهَانًا ٦٩

Al-Furqan 25:69

إِلَّا مَن تَابَ وَءَامَنَ وَعَمِلَ عَمَلًۭا صَـٰلِحًۭا فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ يُبَدِّلُ ٱللَّهُ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِمْ حَسَنَـٰتٍۢ ۗ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًۭا رَّحِيمًۭا ٧٠

Al-Furqan 25:70

وَمَن تَابَ وَعَمِلَ صَـٰلِحًۭا فَإِنَّهُۥ يَتُوبُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ مَتَابًۭا ٧١

Al-Furqan 25:71

“Those who do not call upon another god alongside the One God, who do not kill the soul the One God has made inviolable except with right, and who do not commit unlawful sexual intercourse. Whoever does these things will meet a penalty. His punishment will be multiplied on the Day of Resurrection, and he will remain in it in humiliation—except for the one who repents, believes, and does righteous work. For those, the One God will replace their evil deeds with good deeds. The One God is forgiving and merciful. Whoever repents and acts righteously has truly turned back to the One God.”

Associating partners with God, killing, and unlawful sexual intercourse appear in the same sequence. After the threat, the Qur’an states its own exception: repentance, faith, and righteous action. The way to mercy has not been closed, even after sins of this gravity. But that way is not the same as claiming that monotheism alone, even without repentance, guarantees eventual salvation in advance.

Az-Zumar 39:53–55 preserves the same balance:

Az-Zumar 39:54 · Az-Zumar 39:55

قُلْ يَـٰعِبَادِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا۟ مِن رَّحْمَةِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ ٱلذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ ٥٣

Az-Zumar 39:53

وَأَنِيبُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَسْلِمُوا۟ لَهُۥ مِن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمُ ٱلْعَذَابُ ثُمَّ لَا تُنصَرُونَ ٥٤

Az-Zumar 39:54

وَٱتَّبِعُوٓا۟ أَحْسَنَ مَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكُم مِّن رَّبِّكُم مِّن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمُ ٱلْعَذَابُ بَغْتَةًۭ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ ٥٥

Az-Zumar 39:55

“Say, ‘My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of the One God. The One God forgives all sins; He is the Forgiving, the Merciful. Turn back to your Lord and submit to Him before punishment comes upon you, after which you will not be helped. And follow the best of what has been sent down to you from your Lord before punishment comes upon you suddenly, while you are unaware.’”

Read in isolation, verse 53 can be made to suggest that forgiveness is guaranteed without any return to God, whatever a person has done. But the Qur’an continues speaking in the following verse: return to your Lord, submit to Him, and follow what has been revealed before punishment arrives.

Az-Zumar 39:53 · Az-Zumar 39:54 · Az-Zumar 39:55

Mercy lifts a person out of despair so that they can turn back. Wishful assurance can make them comfortable staying exactly where they are.

Intercession can serve the same purpose when detached from its proper place. The Qur’an does not deny intercession itself, but never separates it from the One God’s permission and approval. Ta-Ha 20:109 says:

يَوْمَئِذٍۢ لَّا تَنفَعُ ٱلشَّفَـٰعَةُ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ وَرَضِىَ لَهُۥ قَوْلًۭا ١٠٩

Ta-Ha 20:109

“On that Day, intercession will not benefit anyone except one for whom the Merciful has given permission and whose speech He has approved.”

And An-Najm 53:26 says:

وَكَم مِّن مَّلَكٍۢ فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ لَا تُغْنِى شَفَـٰعَتُهُمْ شَيْـًٔا إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِ أَن يَأْذَنَ ٱللَّهُ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيَرْضَىٰٓ ٢٦

An-Najm 53:26

“How many angels there are in the heavens whose intercession avails nothing, except after the One God gives permission for whom He wills and approves.”

In both verses, intercession depends on God’s permission, will, and approval. No denomination or community can turn these verses into a deed of ownership granting intercession to all its members.

When the Standard Is Applied to Someone Else

The other half of this contradiction may be even more troubling. The same system that secures eventual salvation for the sinful monotheist reaches a different verdict when confronted with someone outside its doctrinal boundaries. That person may have spent an entire life doing good. If they die holding what the system defines as unbelief, however, their good deeds cannot open a path to Paradise.

Yet when the Qur’an speaks about the People of the Book, it does not place them all under a single judgment. Ali ‘Imran 3:113–115 says:

Al-Imran 3:114 · Al-Imran 3:115

لَيْسُوا۟ سَوَآءًۭ ۗ مِّنْ أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أُمَّةٌۭ قَآئِمَةٌۭ يَتْلُونَ ءَايَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ ءَانَآءَ ٱلَّيْلِ وَهُمْ يَسْجُدُونَ ١١٣

Al-Imran 3:113

يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ وَيَأْمُرُونَ بِٱلْمَعْرُوفِ وَيَنْهَوْنَ عَنِ ٱلْمُنكَرِ وَيُسَـٰرِعُونَ فِى ٱلْخَيْرَٰتِ وَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ ١١٤

Al-Imran 3:114

وَمَا يَفْعَلُوا۟ مِنْ خَيْرٍۢ فَلَن يُكْفَرُوهُ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌۢ بِٱلْمُتَّقِينَ ١١٥

Al-Imran 3:115

“They are not all alike. Among the People of the Book is an upright community who recite the verses of the One God in the hours of the night while prostrating. They believe in the One God and the Last Day, enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and hasten to good deeds; they are among the righteous. Whatever good they do will never be denied them. The One God knows those who are mindful of Him.”

The passage begins, “They are not all alike,” then describes this group among the People of the Book as believers in God and the Last Day, eager to do good, and among the righteous. Of their good deeds, it says:

وَمَا يَفْعَلُوا۟ مِنْ خَيْرٍۢ فَلَن يُكْفَرُوهُ

Al-Imran 3:115

Whatever good they do will never be denied them.

Anyone who wishes to restrict all these verses to people who subsequently entered their own sectarian definition must bring that restriction from somewhere outside the wording of the verses themselves.

Al-Baqarah 2:62 likewise says:

إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَٱلَّذِينَ هَادُوا۟ وَٱلنَّصَـٰرَىٰ وَٱلصَّـٰبِـِٔينَ مَنْ ءَامَنَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ وَعَمِلَ صَـٰلِحًۭا فَلَهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ وَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ ٦٢

Al-Baqarah 2:62

“Those who believe, those who are Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians—whoever believes in the One God and the Last Day and acts righteously will have their reward with their Lord. No fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve.”

The same criterion appears again in Al-Ma’idah 5:69:

إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَٱلَّذِينَ هَادُوا۟ وَٱلصَّـٰبِـُٔونَ وَٱلنَّصَـٰرَىٰ مَنْ ءَامَنَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ وَعَمِلَ صَـٰلِحًۭا فَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ ٦٩

Al-Ma'idah 5:69

“Those who believe, those who are Jews, the Sabians, and the Christians—whoever believes in the One God and the Last Day and acts righteously, no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve.”

The religious names are present in the verses, but the final criterion is not the name. It is faith in the One God, the Last Day, and righteous action.

The contradiction cannot be concealed. Inside our own community, the designation “monotheist” becomes so powerful that even grave sin and punishment in the Fire cannot prevent ultimate salvation. Outside our religious boundaries, however, a doctrinal classification can render a person’s entire record of goodness ineffective in the hereafter.

On one side, a murderer, someone who commits unlawful sexual intercourse, or a thief may eventually reach Paradise because he died a monotheist. On the other, a person who believes in the One God, the Last Day, and goodness is declared destined for Hell in advance, simply because that person does not belong to the sectarian category we recognize.

This is not an attempt to minimize shirk, the association of partners with God. The Qur’an itself condemns it severely. The question is who authorized us to turn a religious label into a definitive verdict on an individual’s destiny. How do we guarantee an eventual exit from the Fire for our own sinner while closing the reach of God’s mercy to someone else?

The blindness to ourselves with which this essay began has now entered theology. We call another community’s claim to privilege in the hereafter “wishful thinking,” but when the same logic enters our inherited tradition, we name it “sound doctrine.”

The Qur’an Must Be the Standard for Reports

The issue now extends beyond a disagreement about Hell and Paradise. When a report conflicts with the Qur’an’s clear reasoning, which is supposed to reshape the other?

Across much of the exegetical tradition, the doctrine drawn from reports remains fixed while the verse is restricted to fit it. Because monotheists must not remain in the Fire forever, “evil” in Al-Baqarah 2:81 is restricted to unbelief and associating partners with God. Because a monotheist murderer must not remain there, “remaining in it” in An-Nisa 4:93 must be understood in a way that preserves the principle. And because the doctrinal system has already ruled on the destiny of the People of the Book, verses such as Al-Baqarah 2:62 and Al-Ma’idah 5:69 are read with qualifications absent from their own wording.

The pattern becomes clear: doctrine stays in place, and the verse is moved toward it.

This is the danger encountered in the discussion of amani. The Qur’an’s words are still recited, but sometimes they are no longer permitted to convey a meaning at odds with the system of belief accepted in advance.

If the Qur’an is our standard, the direction must be reversed. We should bring the report before the Qur’an, rather than the Qur’an before the report. If a report agrees with the Qur’an, there is no conflict. If it addresses something about which the Qur’an is silent, it should retain the status of a human report, with its own degree of uncertainty. But if a statement attributed to Muhammad conflicts with the Qur’an’s clear teachings, the first question is straightforward: did Muhammad actually say it?

Muhammad delivered this very Qur’an to the people. It is difficult to accept that through it he would ask one community, “Have you received a covenant from the One God?”, then provide his own community, outside that same Qur’an, with a covenant guaranteeing that every sinful monotheist would eventually leave the Fire, even without repentance.

The same difficulty arises with “It is not according to your wishes, nor the wishes of the People of the Book.” How could a prophet deliver that verse, then be reported as offering a new set of wishes to another community as a guarantee of salvation?

The problem is similar in the case of intentional murder. The Qur’an speaks of Hell, remaining in it, divine anger, a curse, and tremendous punishment. Another report, however, ties the murderer’s ultimate destiny to Paradise, provided he retains the designation “monotheist.”

If that conflict is real, respect for Muhammad does not require interpreting the Qur’an away to rescue the report. It requires taking the possibility of error in human transmission seriously.

The Qur’an has been carried from generation to generation within Muslim society as a book, through recitation and memorization, and in public worship. Hadith consists of human reports attributed to Muhammad, whose major collections were compiled in later periods. The two do not occupy the same level of certainty, such that a human report should govern the One God’s words when they disagree.

Where Is the Covenant?

When a community claimed that the Fire would touch them for only a few days, the Qur’an did not ask which scholar had said so, or in which inherited religious text the doctrine appeared. It asked:

أَتَّخَذْتُمْ عِندَ اللَّهِ عَهْدًا؟

Al-Baqarah 2:80

Have you received a covenant from the One God?

When they claimed Paradise exclusively for their own community, the response was:

قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَـٰنَكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَـٰدِقِينَ

Al-Baqarah 2:111

Bring your proof, if you are truthful.

Those two questions apply to us with equal force. If we claim that every monotheist, whatever sins he has committed, must eventually leave the Fire even if he enters it, we must show the covenant behind that guarantee. If we claim that someone outside our doctrinal definition has no path to Paradise, however much good that person has done, that judgment also requires proof.

Bukhari and Muslim are hadith collections. The Creed of al-Tahawi is a theological text. Al-Nawawi, Qadi ‘Iyad, Ibn Baz, and Shaykh al-Saduq were human beings who spoke about religion. Respect for them does not make their words God’s covenant. They may have been right, and they may have been mistaken. The standard must be what we know with certainty to be the word of the One God.

We have returned to the problem with which we began. We see another person’s error quickly, but find a different language for our own. Their assurance of salvation becomes “wishful thinking”; ours becomes “doctrine.” We demand a covenant from them, but consider the attribution of a statement to the Prophet sufficient for ourselves. That double standard is what must be broken.

The Qur’an gives none of us immunity on the basis of group membership. Neither the wishes of the People of the Book nor our own wishes constitute proof. Salvation is not the possession of any people or sect, and final judgment has not been placed in our hands. The same God who says:

يَغْفِرُ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيُعَذِّبُ مَن يَشَآءُ

Al-Ma'idah 5:18

He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills,

also warns in the same Book:

أَمْ تَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

Al-Baqarah 2:80

Are you saying things about the One God that you do not know?

Mercy and punishment both belong to Him, as does judgment. We have no right to close His mercy to others or to claim it as our own possession. Declaring an individual certainly destined for Hell without a clear divine covenant is dangerous; writing a guarantee of Paradise for our own sinner is an equally momentous claim about God.

If we have a covenant, we must show it from Him. If we have proof, we must bring it from His Book. If we have neither, religious integrity may ask something simpler of us: before we call our wishes knowledge and issue definitive judgments about the destiny of God’s servants, we should have the courage to say:

We do not know.
