Introduction: Has God Made This World a Prison for Believers?

A saying attributed to Muhammad is widely repeated in Muslim religious life, especially within Sunni traditions of transmitted reports: “This world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever.” It is reported through Abu Hurayrah in Sahih Muslim, report 2956, a collection of hadith (reports attributing words or actions to the Prophet).

The sentence is short, but treating it as an absolute rule about human life has far-reaching consequences. On that reading, God has supposedly ordained poverty, illness, deprivation, and suffering for believers, while reserving comfort and abundance for those who reject faith. A believer’s religion would then appear more authentic the harder life becomes. The more deprived a community is, the more patiently it should endure and the less it should ask why it lives under those conditions.

This is more than a theoretical mistake. It can become a justification for poverty and oppression, silencing people who seek justice and a better life. Once misery is presented as the believer’s natural destiny, questions about economic corruption, discrimination, war, hoarding, the theft of public resources, and the responsibilities of those in power can be brushed aside. Those who suffer need only be told: “This world is your prison. Be patient.”

Is that the world the Qur’an describes? Has God forbidden believers a wholesome life and reserved earthly blessings for those who reject Him?

An-Nahl 16:97 promises a good and wholesome life to the person who believes and acts rightly. Al-Araf 7:96 connects the faith and moral responsibility of communities with blessings from heaven and earth. Hud 11:3 speaks of good enjoyment until an appointed time. Yunus 10:64 announces good news for believers in this life and the life to come.

Yet the Qur’an never promises that believers will escape every difficulty. Al-Baqarah 2:155 speaks of testing through fear, hunger, and losses of wealth, lives, and produce. Prophets and believers have faced threats, persecution, deprivation, and death.

The central question is therefore not whether believers can suffer. They plainly can. It is whether suffering and deprivation are intrinsic to a believer’s destiny, while worldly comfort belongs exclusively to the disbeliever.

To answer, we must distinguish several readings of the saying. One makes deprivation inevitable for every believer and a paradise-like existence inevitable for every disbeliever. Another compares this temporary life with the afterlife. A third concerns the believer’s refusal to pursue every desire at the expense of what is right and just. These are different claims and need separate examination.

Some commentators, including the medieval scholar al-Nawawi, emphasize moral restraint and the comparison with the afterlife. It would therefore be inaccurate to attribute the first, absolute reading to every commentator or to all Sunni Muslims. Equally, no commentary or explanation should take the place of what the Qur’an itself says.

This article tests those claims against the Qur’an. It does not undertake a historical determination of whether the report was authentically attributed or fabricated. The presence of a saying in a collection does not establish the validity of every interpretation built upon it.

Method: Reading the Claims Against the Qur’an as a Whole

The method here is to read the Qur’an through its own passages. We begin with verses about a wholesome life, earthly blessings, provision, faith, and right action. We then examine worldly enjoyment, beauty and attraction, temporary benefits, rejection of truth, and the consequences of human conduct. From there, we turn to testing, divine patterns, human choice, social responsibility, steadfastness, and good news.

For each passage, its audience, conditions, context, and the scope of its promise or warning matter. A promise to a community that believes and acts responsibly is not necessarily a guarantee that every individual will become wealthy. A description of some disbelievers enjoying prosperity is not a law that everyone who rejects faith will live comfortably.

Word meanings must also be distinguished from philosophical or ethical conclusions. Dunya (this present, nearer life) does not simply mean poverty or pleasure-seeking. Zinah (adornment or that which makes something attractive) does not necessarily describe actual well-being. Ibtila (being put to the test) is not a synonym for disaster: good fortune and adversity can both test us. Sabr (steadfastness and self-restraint under pressure) involves truth, responsibility, and conduct, extending beyond the endurance of pain.

In considering divine patterns, we must distinguish an event’s occurrence within creation from God’s moral approval of the person causing it. We must also distinguish the material consequences of an action from the moral responsibility of someone harmed by it. People can suffer from others’ conduct without having participated in it.

Finally, we should not extract more from a Qur’anic promise than it actually states. The good news is real; its form and timing cannot simply be set by our own expectations. These principles guide the inquiry throughout.

Part One: What Kind of Life Does the Qur’an Describe?

1. What Does “This World” Mean?

Dunya (the nearer or present life) belongs to an Arabic word family associated with nearness. In al-hayat al-dunya (the present, nearer life), it describes the life immediately available to human experience. The same adjective appears in the expression al-sama al-dunya (the nearest heaven) in As-Saffat 37:6 and Al-Mulk 67:5. In these uses, the word does not itself signify moral degradation or condemnation.

We cannot therefore derive from the word alone that this life is inherently a prison, a curse, or a place in which believers must be denied every good thing.

The Qur’an repeatedly describes its impermanence. Al-Hadid 57:20 speaks of play, diversion, adornment, boasting, and competition for greater possessions. Al-Ankabut 29:64 identifies the abode of the afterlife with true life. Al-Ala 87:16 and Al-Ala 87:17 address the preference for present life over what comes after it.

Two things must be distinguished: living here and enjoying legitimate blessings, and reducing one’s entire horizon to this life while preferring it to truth and the afterlife. The Qur’an criticizes the latter. An-Naziat 79:38 and An-Naziat 79:39 concern giving this life priority. Yunus 10:7 describes people who are content with it and settle into it as their horizon.

Simply being alive here, or enjoying what life provides, is not the central problem. The problem arises when this life becomes a person’s entire purpose, and truth and responsibility are abandoned to preserve an immediate advantage.

Its nearness does not make all its blessings worthless. Its impermanence does not justify preferring falsehood to truth.

2. Does the Attraction of This World Make It a Paradise for Disbelievers?

Al-Baqarah 2:212 is sometimes invoked in support of that claim:

زُيِّنَ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا وَيَسْخَرُونَ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا ۘ وَالَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا فَوْقَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ ۗ يَرْزُقُ مَن يَشَاءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

Al-Baqarah 2:212

“The life of this world has been made attractive to those who reject faith, and they ridicule those who believe. Those who guard against wrongdoing will be above them on the Day of Resurrection. And God provides without reckoning for whom He wills.”

Does this verse say that everyone who rejects faith enjoys a beautiful, comfortable, richly supplied life? It does not. It describes this life as having been made attractive in their eyes. That concerns perception and the value assigned to something, rather than giving a definitive account of bodily comfort, wealth, or happiness.

Something can seem attractive while being of lesser value, or even harmful. A person may live amid considerable suffering, deprivation, and ugliness and still prefer this life and its desires to truth and the good of the afterlife. Finding the world attractive does not mean that it has become a paradise.

Ibrahim 14:3 similarly describes people who prefer this life to the next. A person may choose something because desire, attachment, or judgment makes it appear superior, rather than because it actually is the best choice.

Al-Kahf 18:7 describes the earth’s adornment as a means of testing human conduct. Beauty and attraction do not guarantee happiness; they can provide the setting in which our choices become evident.

Conversely, Al-Araf 7:32 speaks of the adornment God has provided alongside wholesome provisions and challenges their unauthorized prohibition. The word “adornment” therefore carries no single, fixed moral judgment in every passage. God’s provision of beautiful things for His servants differs in context and purpose from the attraction this world holds for those who prefer it to truth.

To conclude from Al-Baqarah 2:212 that God has made earthly life a paradise for every disbeliever is to attribute to the verse something it does not say.

3. Comparing This Life with the Next Does Not Negate Life Here

Al-Ala 87:16 and Al-Ala 87:17 describe the afterlife as better and more enduring. Al-Ankabut 29:64 speaks of its true life. These comparisons remind us that our present existence is neither the whole of reality nor our final destination.

But the superiority of the afterlife does not logically require this life to be a prison for believers. A person can acknowledge what is better and more lasting while enjoying legitimate blessings, caring for the earth, raising a family, eating wholesome food, and working for public safety and justice.

Nor does life’s brevity make our actions unimportant. This is precisely where we act and bear responsibility.

If “prison” is used only to compare the limitations of temporary life with the abundance of the afterlife, it must be understood as an analogy. It cannot become a law condemning believers to earthly deprivation. The Qur’an expresses the comparison through more direct descriptions of impermanence, limited enjoyment, and the superiority of the life to come.

Part Two: The Qur’an’s Promises of Good in This Life

4. A Good and Wholesome Life for Those Who Believe and Act Rightly

An-Nahl 16:97 states:

مَنْ عَمِلَ صَالِحًا مِّن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَلَنُحْيِيَنَّهُ حَيَاةً طَيِّبَةً ۖ وَلَنَجْزِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْرَهُم بِأَحْسَنِ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

An-Nahl 16:97

“Whoever does what is right, whether male or female, while believing, We will surely give that person a good and wholesome life. And We will reward them according to the best of what they used to do.”

The verse connects faith and right action with hayat tayyibah (a good and wholesome life). Its promise is not restricted to one sex, people, or family. The reference to right action also makes conduct integral to the promise: faith is not detached from what a person does.

What makes a life wholesome? The Arabic word family examined in the root study of purity and wholesomeness encompasses what is pure, good, fitting, and agreeable. Al-Araf 7:32 places wholesome provisions alongside God’s adornment. Al-Maidah 5:87 and Al-Maidah 5:88 concern good things and the refusal to prohibit arbitrarily what God has permitted.

Without evidence, we should not restrict the promise to an inward feeling of contentment, as though actual living conditions, food, safety, human relationships, and access to blessings had no connection with it.

Equally, we should not reduce it to wealth, perfect health, or the fulfillment of every material desire. A wholesome life can involve integrity, peace, good provision, security, healthy relationships, and moral responsibility.

The timing of every aspect of the promise remains a matter for interpretation. Even if someone emphasizes an afterlife dimension in An-Nahl 16:97, other passages explicitly speak of good in this present life. The argument does not depend on this verse alone.

5. Explicit Promises of Earthly Good

Hud 11:3 connects seeking forgiveness and returning to the Lord with good enjoyment until an appointed time. The reference to an appointed limit concerns a life with a term, rather than solely a reward after resurrection.

An-Nahl 16:30 speaks of good for those who do good, explicitly placing this life within the sentence before recalling the superiority of the afterlife. Al-Imran 3:148 places a reward in this world alongside the good reward of the next.

An-Nahl 16:41 promises a good place in this world to people who leave their homes for God’s sake after suffering injustice, and then describes the reward of the afterlife as greater.

These passages cannot be dismissed in favor of the claim that goodness and relief begin for believers only after death. Al-Baqarah 2:201 also joins the request for good in this life with the request for good in the next. The Qur’an does not condemn that prayer.

Legitimate enjoyment of earthly life is compatible with faith and right action. What is condemned is allowing this life to replace truth and the afterlife as one’s ultimate concern.

6. Who Has Forbidden Believers Beauty and Wholesome Provision?

Al-Araf 7:32 addresses this question directly:

قُلْ مَنْ حَرَّمَ زِينَةَ الَّتِي أَخْرَجَ لِعِبَادِهِ وَالطَّيِّبَاتِ مِنَ الرِّزْقِ ۚ قُلْ هِيَ لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا خَالِصَةً يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ نُفَصِّلُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

Al-A'raf 7:32

“Say: Who has forbidden the adornment of God that He has brought forth for His servants, and the wholesome things of provision? Say: They are for those who believe during the life of this world, exclusively theirs on the Day of Resurrection. Thus We explain the signs for people who know.”

The verse concerns adornment and wholesome provisions that God has brought forth for His servants. It challenges their unauthorized prohibition and explicitly recognizes believers’ enjoyment of these blessings during this life.

How, then, can this world be described as a prison in which a believer must be denied legitimate good things?

The verse does not say that nobody else enjoys these provisions here. It addresses believers’ share in them in this life and their exclusive enjoyment on the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Maidah 5:87 likewise prohibits believers from declaring forbidden the wholesome things God has made lawful for them. Al-Maidah 5:88 directs them to eat lawful and wholesome provision.

Moral self-restraint is not the same as depriving oneself of every legitimate blessing. Anyone who turns religion into a rejection of beauty, good food, permissible comfort, and the earth’s provisions must explain which Qur’anic command requires that deprivation.

7. Blessings and Security: Social Promises, Not Guarantees of Individual Wealth

Al-Araf 7:96 connects the faith and taqwa (conscientious guarding against wrongdoing before God) of communities with blessings from heaven and earth. Its subject is the people of settlements, rather than an isolated individual.

An-Nur 24:55 also addresses a community of faith and right action in its promise of succession, stability, and security in place of fear.

These promises concern social life. A society that values justice, trust, integrity, and responsibility for people’s rights does not create the same conditions as one whose resources are plundered and whose institutions are corrupted.

Yet this relationship must not become the simplistic formula that every wealthy society is morally upright and every poor society is corrupt. These verses connect collective conduct with blessings. They do not authorize judging the moral standing of every nation solely by its wealth or economic power.

Nor does a promise of security to a community guarantee every individual physical protection from every incident. Distinguishing individual from social promises, and earthly from afterlife promises, allows us to take the verses seriously without inventing guarantees they never make.

8. Is Earthly Provision Reserved for Disbelievers?

Al-Isra 17:20 answers:

كُلًّا نُّمِدُّ هَٰؤُلَآءِ وَهَٰؤُلَآءِ مِنْ عَطَآءِ رَبِّكَ ۚ وَمَا كَانَ عَطَآءُ رَبِّكَ مَحْظُورًا

Al-Isra 17:20

“To each group—these and those—We extend the giving of your Lord. The giving of your Lord is not withheld.”

The preceding context matters. Al-Isra 17:18 describes someone seeking the immediate life. Al-Isra 17:19 describes a believer seeking the afterlife and making the effort appropriate to it. The next verse says that both groups receive from the Lord’s giving.

This is substantial evidence against assigning worldly blessings exclusively to one religious category. Material advantage does not necessarily establish that a person follows truth; material deprivation alone does not establish God’s displeasure.

In Al-Baqarah 2:126, Abraham asks for security and provision from fruits for the inhabitants of the land. God’s answer also mentions limited enjoyment for the person who rejects faith. Earthly provision is not confined to a single group.

Receiving a blessing and the ultimate value of one’s conduct are different matters. This distinction recurs throughout the Qur’an.

This world is neither the disbeliever’s exclusive prize nor the believer’s exclusive prison. Within God’s lordship, earthly gifts reach different people. Their responsibility concerns how they use what they receive.

Part Three: Is This Life a Paradise for Those Who Reject Faith?

9. Temporary Enjoyment Is Not Paradise

Al-Imran 3:196 mentions the movement of those who reject faith through the lands; Al-Imran 3:197 describes their advantage as brief enjoyment. Muhammad 47:12 also speaks of such people enjoying themselves and eating.

Do these verses say their lives are free of illness, fear, hunger, loss, and grief? They do not. Having access to temporary benefits is not the same as living in paradise.

Wealth can coexist with anxiety, illness, hostility, mistrust, and inward distress. Abundant food and facilities can coexist with an absence of safety or peace.

The Qur’an also speaks of some people being allowed time and being drawn gradually toward consequences they do not perceive, as in Al-Araf 7:182 and Al-Araf 7:183. This is often discussed as istidraj (being led gradually toward judgment without recognizing it). Al-Imran 3:178 warns against assuming that an extension of time is necessarily good for those rejecting faith.

Outward prosperity is therefore not sufficient evidence of well-being. But these passages also do not entitle us to declare that every wealthy nonbeliever is undergoing that process. A person’s final destiny cannot be read from visible possessions.

10. The Qur’an Also Describes Their Hardship

At-Tawbah 9:55 warns against being impressed by the wealth and children of a group whose rejection and hypocrisy are addressed in the surrounding passage. Those very possessions can become a means of suffering during this life.

Something that looks like a blessing can become a source of distress to its possessor. Even abundant wealth and numerous children do not amount to a paradise-like existence.

Ar-Rad 13:34 speaks of punishment in this life for a particular group, followed by a more severe punishment in the next. Taha 20:124 connects turning away from God’s remembrance with a constricted existence and then speaks of resurrection. The nature and stage of that existence warrant further inquiry, but the verse does not permit treating distance from guidance as a guarantee of happiness.

Al-Araf 7:130 describes Pharaoh’s people experiencing years of hardship and diminished produce. This is revealing: diminished produce is also one of the trials of believers in Al-Baqarah 2:155. Even the outward form of a hardship cannot be reserved for one religious category.

Al-Imran 3:140 similarly reminds believers that, if they have suffered a wound, their opponents have suffered a comparable wound. These passages do not describe a world in which believers alone suffer and disbelievers are protected against every calamity.

11. The Owner of Two Gardens and Qarun: When Wealth Becomes a Measure of Truth

Al-Kahf 18:32, Al-Kahf 18:33, Al-Kahf 18:34, Al-Kahf 18:35, Al-Kahf 18:36, Al-Kahf 18:37, Al-Kahf 18:38, Al-Kahf 18:39, Al-Kahf 18:40, Al-Kahf 18:41, Al-Kahf 18:42, Al-Kahf 18:43, Al-Kahf 18:44 tells of a man with gardens and considerable resources. In Al-Kahf 18:34 he boasts of his wealth and following. In Al-Kahf 18:35 he enters his garden while wronging himself.

His companion may possess less wealth and fewer children, but does not restrict what God can give to the resources presently in view; see Al-Kahf 18:39 and Al-Kahf 18:40. In Al-Kahf 18:42, the garden’s produce is overtaken by destruction.

The account acknowledges real economic differences. A person who acts wrongly may be wealthy while a person who acts rightly has fewer resources. But it does not turn that difference into a permanent law of happiness and misery. Abundant possessions establish neither their owner’s truthfulness nor the security of his future.

The account of Qarun, a wealthy man among Moses’ people, in Al-Qasas 28:76, Al-Qasas 28:77, Al-Qasas 28:78, Al-Qasas 28:79, Al-Qasas 28:80, Al-Qasas 28:81, Al-Qasas 28:82 likewise shows how wealth can encourage arrogance and a false assessment of one’s position.

These accounts do not sanctify poverty. They challenge judgments of human worth based on property, power, and outward appearances.

12. Wealth and Children Do Not Establish Closeness to God

Saba 34:34 describes the response of affluent people to prophetic warnings. In Saba 34:35, they treat their greater wealth and children as evidence that they will not be punished.

Saba 34:36 speaks of provision being expanded or restricted according to God’s will. Saba 34:37 then makes clear that wealth and children do not, in themselves, bring people close to God. Faith and right action matter.

A wealthy person can use possessions for good, while another makes the same resources instruments of corruption and oppression. Someone with little can remain truthful and upright. The amount a person owns cannot establish the final state of that person’s faith or moral responsibility.

In Al-Fajr 89:15 and Al-Fajr 89:16, people interpret abundance as proof that God has honored them and restricted provision as proof that He has humiliated them. Al-Fajr 89:17 rejects this superficial judgment.

Wealth does not prove that the world is paradise; poverty does not prove that it is prison.

Part Four: Why Do Good Fortune and Adversity Both Test Us?

13. Testing Is Not Confined to Calamity

Al-Anbiya 21:35 states:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَآئِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ ۗ وَنَبْلُوكُم بِالشَّرِّ وَالْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً ۖ وَإِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ

Al-Anbiya 21:35

“Every soul will taste death. We test you with adversity and good fortune as a trial, and to Us you will be returned.”

This passage is fundamental to understanding ibtila (being put to the test). Hunger, illness, and loss are not its only forms. Blessings can test us too.

In An-Naml 27:40, Solomon recognizes a favor from his Lord as a test of whether he will be grateful or ungrateful. Al-Kahf 18:7 connects the adornment of the earth with testing the quality of people’s actions. Al-Anfal 8:28 and At-Taghabun 64:15 describe wealth and children in terms of fitnah (a trial that puts a person’s response to the test).

Wealth and power are not always a final reward, just as deprivation is not always punishment. Poverty may test integrity and perseverance. Wealth may test justice, gratitude, generosity, and resistance to corruption.

For a closer examination, see the root study of testing. Shukr (recognizing and responding gratefully to a blessing) is examined in the root study of gratitude.

14. Does God Test People to Learn Something He Does Not Know?

Some passages connect testing with knowing or making known, including Al-Baqarah 2:143, Al-Imran 3:140, Al-Imran 3:142, and Muhammad 47:31. This raises a question: if God knows everything, why associate a test with knowledge?

Al-Imran 3:154 itself places the testing of what is within people’s hearts alongside God’s knowledge of their inward lives. The testing passages therefore do not establish that God was previously ignorant of His servants’ condition.

Human choice and conduct must occur in actual circumstances for practical responsibility to take shape. Knowing what someone would do in a hypothetical situation is not the same as that choice actually becoming part of the person’s lived history.

Testing can thus be understood as the setting in which human actions and commitments become actual and manifest, without supposing that God needs to discover something previously unknown.

This is an interpretation of the relationship among the passages. It is not a claim that every Qur’anic use of the word for knowledge means “becoming visible to other people.”

15. Al-Baqarah 2:155: Fear, Hunger, and Loss

Al-Baqarah 2:155 states:

وَلَنَبْلُوَنَّكُم بِشَيْءٍ مِّنَ الْخَوْفِ وَالْجُوعِ وَنَقْصٍ مِّنَ الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَنْفُسِ وَالثَّمَرَاتِ ۗ وَبَشِّرِ الصَّابِرِينَ

Al-Baqarah 2:155

“We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and produce. Give good news to those who remain steadfast.”

The verse brings together fundamental conditions of human life: security, food, possessions, lives, and produce.

Its expression bi-shay’in min (with something of, or a measure of) matters, but it does not establish that every test must be brief or insignificant. Al-Baqarah 2:214 describes hardships that shook believers profoundly. A difficulty can be limited in one respect and still be devastating to the person experiencing it.

There is also a distinction worth considering between fear and hunger, on the one hand, and losses of wealth, lives, and produce on the other. Fear and hunger are conditions that can subside: security may return and food may arrive. When severe or prolonged, however, they can leave damage that is difficult to repair.

Naqs (diminution or loss) concerns something that was present and has been reduced or taken away. Property can be destroyed, a season’s harvest lost, or a person killed. The end of a painful condition is not the same as the return of the very thing that was lost.

This matters in ordinary life. Someone may acquire new property or another garden may bear fruit, but that need not mean the return of the earlier possessions or harvest. When a person dies, someone else entering the survivors’ lives is not the return of that same person.

At the same time, the sentence’s grammar should not be made to carry a conclusive philosophical rule about every kind of loss and restoration. More than one grammatical analysis of the relationship between “loss” and the preceding phrase is possible. The verse does not specify when or how each loss will be made good.

What is clear is that testing involves tangible human realities. Its pain must not be trivialized.

16. Fear and Hunger: A Test, a Consequence, or a Need Relieved?

The Qur’an places fear and hunger in several different settings. Al-Baqarah 2:155 presents them as trials. An-Nahl 16:112 describes a community that once enjoyed security and abundant provision but experiences hunger and fear as a consequence of its conduct.

Quraysh 106:4, however, describes God as:

الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُم مِّن جُوعٍ وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنْ خَوْفٍ

Quraysh 106:4

“Who fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear.”

Here, relief from hunger and fear is a divine blessing. The comparison matters. The same phenomenon can be a setting for testing, a consequence of collective conduct, or a condition whose removal is a gift from God.

We cannot infer that every hungry person is being punished for personal wrongdoing, or that every secure person is thereby vindicated. People can help sustain one another’s security and provision; they can also destroy them.

Identifying the cause of a condition and judging people’s responsibility require attention both to actual circumstances and to the context of the verses.

Part Five: Divine Patterns, Human Choice, and Social Responsibility

17. Is Every Calamity the Victim’s Own Fault?

Ash-Shura 42:30 states:

وَمَا أَصَابَكُم مِّن مُّصِيبَةٍ فَبِمَا كَسَبَتْ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَيَعْفُوا عَن كَثِيرٍ

Ash-Shura 42:30

“Whatever calamity befalls you is because of what your hands have earned, and He pardons much.”

The verse emphasizes the connection between human conduct and its consequences. People cannot treat their actions as ineffective and attribute every harmful outcome to forces beyond their responsibility.

But does it follow that everyone struck by calamity must personally have committed a sin for which that particular calamity is the punishment? Such a generalization does not fit the Qur’an’s other passages.

Al-Anfal 8:25 warns of a trial whose effects will not be confined to wrongdoers. The consequences of some people’s actions can therefore reach others who did not participate in them.

Al-Anam 6:164, Al-Isra 17:15, and An-Najm 53:38, meanwhile, connect moral responsibility with each person’s own conduct and reject the transfer of one person’s burden of sin to another.

We must hold these two facts apart. A person can suffer the material consequences of someone else’s actions without becoming morally responsible for that person’s wrongdoing. Shared harm does not mean shared guilt.

Ash-Shura 42:30 connects calamity with human conduct. Understanding that relationship in social life requires us to consider others’ actions, collective conditions, and the verses on individual responsibility.

18. Human Responsibility and Divine Permission

Al-Imran 3:165 identifies the believers’ own role in a setback they experienced. Al-Imran 3:166 places the same event in relation to God’s permission. Together, these verses show human action and divine sovereignty operating within the account of one event.

An-Nisa 4:78 places events within God’s domain, while An-Nisa 4:79 connects the harmful consequence with the human being. At-Taghabun 64:11 also relates the occurrence of calamity to God’s permission.

Yet idhn (permission for something to occur) is not identical to moral approval of every agent involved. Al-Baqarah 2:102 describes a harmful practice having an effect only by God’s permission, while condemning the practice itself. Something can be possible within creation and remain morally wrong.

Consider fire. A person who uses it to cook draws upon a property of the natural world. A person who uses it to burn down a neighbor’s home commits an injustice. Combustion operates in both cases, but the actions do not have the same moral value.

This example illustrates the relationship between action and consequence. It does not reduce all divine patterns to physical formulas. Al-Anbiya 21:69 also speaks of God’s command to the fire in Abraham’s story. The orderliness of creation should not be taken to limit God’s power to our present knowledge of nature.

For human responsibility, the essential point is that an act’s occurrence within God’s knowledge and will does not exempt its human agent from answering for it. This relationship is explored further in Intercession in the Qur’an: Divine Permission and Human Responsibility.

19. Divine Patterns Within a World of Human Choice

Sunan (established divine ways or patterns) describes the order within which human choices and their consequences unfold.

Fatir 35:43 speaks of the constancy of God’s way. Ar-Rad 13:11 connects change in a people’s condition with change in what is within themselves. Al-Anfal 8:53 similarly relates a change in blessings to a change in people. Human conduct has consequences within the divine order.

We inhabit a world whose conditions we did not wholly create. The time of our birth, our families, the natural environment, many bodily characteristics, and our initial resources were not chosen by us. Yet we make real choices within those circumstances.

At-Takwir 81:28 speaks of the human will to follow a straight course. At-Takwir 81:29 places that will in relation to the will of the Lord. A human being is neither absolutely autonomous nor a will-less instrument whose actions occur without personal choice.

God creates the framework and the means through which actions have effects. Within their capacities, human beings make decisions and bear responsibility for them. This is essential to understanding moral and social consequences.

People who destroy a land’s resources cannot assign all the consequences to “fate” and deny their own responsibility. A society that respects rights, works for education and justice, and uses resources responsibly also makes choices that affect its life.

The constancy of divine patterns does not mean that we can predict the precise timing and form of every consequence. For the relationship between real human choice and unchosen circumstances, see Divine Decree and Human Choice.

20. Can an Upright Person Be Harmed by Widespread Corruption?

Human beings live together. One group’s decisions can affect the lives of others.

Imagine people destroying extensive forests for profit. Cutting and burning vegetation can contribute to soil erosion, habitat loss, and air pollution. In the same area lives a person who has spent years trying to protect those forests. This person warned, protested, and defended future generations’ interests, but could not overcome the power of those causing the destruction.

Now the defender of the forest suffers the consequences too. Should we conclude that pollution cannot affect an upright person’s body? Or that suffering proves this person must have sinned? Neither conclusion follows.

When pollution reaches a human body, physical processes do not issue a moral judgment distinguishing the person who destroyed the forest from the person who tried to protect it. Responsibility for the destruction belongs to its agents, while its damage can reach people who played no part in it.

Ar-Rum 30:41 connects corruption on land and sea with human actions. Al-Baqarah 2:205 describes corruption on earth and the destruction of crops and living generations. Al-Araf 7:56 prohibits spreading corruption on earth after it has been put right.

Human conduct thus affects more than an individual life. Land, resources, animals, and future generations can also be harmed. An upright person living amid social corruption may suffer its consequences without bearing moral responsibility for it.

Calling the experience a divine test must never become a means of absolving those who caused the damage.

21. Does God Want the Innocent to Suffer Simply to Hurt Them?

When the Qur’an speaks of testing, we should not immediately infer that God has brought about every particular calamity to torment, humiliate, or punish its victim.

Al-Buruj 85:4, Al-Buruj 85:5, Al-Buruj 85:6, Al-Buruj 85:7, Al-Buruj 85:8 describes people persecuting believers because of their faith. The oppression is attributed to the persecutors’ conduct. The victims’ faith does not make them deserving of that treatment. An-Nisa 4:40 also denies injustice on God’s part.

A larger philosophical question remains: why does God allow innocent people, in a world He created, to suffer from the consequences of others’ wrongdoing? A few sentences cannot honestly be presented as settling that question.

The Qur’an speaks of choice, testing, divine patterns, time allowed before judgment, accountability, and justice. These ideas help us consider the relationship between actions and consequences. They do not necessarily disclose every reason behind a particular calamity.

Al-Baqarah 2:216 and An-Nisa 4:19 recall the limits of human judgments about good and bad in certain circumstances. They do not authorize excusing every injustice or crime by invoking a “hidden good.” An unknown wisdom does not cancel a wrongdoer’s responsibility.

The account of Moses and his companion in Al-Kahf 18:60, Al-Kahf 18:61, Al-Kahf 18:62, Al-Kahf 18:63, Al-Kahf 18:64, Al-Kahf 18:65, Al-Kahf 18:66, Al-Kahf 18:67, Al-Kahf 18:68, Al-Kahf 18:69, Al-Kahf 18:70, Al-Kahf 18:71, Al-Kahf 18:72, Al-Kahf 18:73, Al-Kahf 18:74, Al-Kahf 18:75, Al-Kahf 18:76, Al-Kahf 18:77, Al-Kahf 18:78, Al-Kahf 18:79, Al-Kahf 18:80, Al-Kahf 18:81, Al-Kahf 18:82 also concerns the difference between an event’s appearance and what a human observer knows of its reality. But the conclusion in Al-Kahf 18:82 places those actions within a specific framework. No ordinary person may use that account to authorize harming others or to claim unlimited power over people’s lives.

We must recognize the limits of our knowledge while continuing to take responsibility in the face of oppression and corruption.

22. Poverty and Unjust Distribution: Is “Destiny” an Answer to Everything?

Sometimes deprivation reflects a genuine shortage of resources. Sometimes resources exist, but access and distribution are unjust. The Qur’an does not overlook this distinction.

Al-Hashr 59:7 addresses the prevention of wealth circulating only among the rich. At-Tawbah 9:60 specifies recipients of charitable funds. Al-Baqarah 2:273 directs attention to people in need whose circumstances prevent them from moving freely in pursuit of a livelihood.

The Qur’an does not leave poverty solely to the poor person’s patience. It assigns responsibilities concerning property, giving, the rights of those in need, and the community’s obligations.

Someone who plunders public wealth or blocks access to essential resources cannot be excused by calling the resulting deprivation a divine test. A religion that demands patience from the poor while remaining silent about the wealthy and powerful has set aside a significant part of the Qur’an’s commands.

Part Six: Steadfastness, Good News, and Restoration

23. Steadfastness Does Not Require Remaining Under Oppression

After describing fear, hunger, and loss, Al-Baqarah 2:155 directs that good news be given to those who remain steadfast. But sabr (steadfastness and self-restraint under pressure) must not be reduced to silently enduring injustice.

Ash-Shura 42:39 describes defending oneself against oppression among believers’ qualities. Ash-Shura 42:41 does not blame those who defend themselves after being wronged. Ash-Shura 42:43 also recognizes the value of patience and forgiveness. Seeking protection and justice can be legitimate, just as considered forgiveness can be valuable.

An-Nisa 4:97 questions those who remained in oppressive circumstances when they had the possibility of leaving. An-Nisa 4:98 makes an exception for people genuinely unable to find the means or a way out. The distinction matters: the Qur’an does not equate someone without options with someone who can leave and choose differently.

It is therefore wrong to tell all victims to remain in harmful conditions because this world is the believer’s prison. Steadfastness may involve resisting pressure, controlling a wrongful reaction, continuing the work of reform, or leaving an environment that obstructs truth and responsibility.

For a fuller discussion, see Sabr in the Qur’an: Steadfastness and What Makes It Worthwhile.

24. What Does the Good News Include?

Al-Baqarah 2:155 describes testing but ends with good news. Al-Baqarah 2:156 portrays those who, when calamity strikes, recall that they belong to God and return to Him. Al-Baqarah 2:157 speaks of salawat (blessings or gracious regard from the Lord in this context), mercy, and guidance upon them.

Within its immediate setting, the good news is therefore connected with divine mercy and guidance. This does not mean that the Qur’an never promises earthly relief to those who persevere.

An-Nahl 16:41 promises a good place in this world for those who leave after suffering injustice. Al-Araf 7:137 describes the children of Israel receiving deliverance and an inheritance after their endurance. Ash-Sharh 94:5 and Ash-Sharh 94:6 speak of ease accompanying hardship. At-Talaq 65:7, in a discussion of financial responsibilities, speaks of God bringing ease after difficulty.

These promises are real. They do not establish that every steadfast person will obtain every material desire at a personally chosen time. Al-Baqarah 2:154 even speaks of those killed in God’s cause. Divine good news cannot therefore be confined to physical immunity or immediate replacement of every loss during this life.

25. Does God Restore What Has Been Lost?

The Qur’an gives examples of restoration and renewed gifts. Al-Anbiya 21:84 speaks of Job’s distress being removed and his family being granted to him, together with their like. Sad 38:43 also addresses this. Joseph’s account describes reunion after years of separation in Yusuf 12:93 and the passages that follow. Al-Kahf 18:81 speaks, in a particular account, of a better replacement being intended for parents.

These examples prevent us from denying the possibility of restoration or a better gift. But they describe particular circumstances. They do not supply a universal guarantee that every bereaved or afflicted person will receive back precisely what was lost in this world.

Fear may give way to security and hunger to nourishment. New property may be acquired; a fresh harvest may replace a destroyed one. Restoration, however, does not always mean the return of the identical thing.

Bereavement especially must not be belittled through unsupported promises. Al-Baqarah 2:154 and Al-Imran 3:169 speak of life for those killed in God’s cause. Al-Imran 3:170 describes their joy and good news. These passages open a horizon beyond bodily death without identifying the quality of that life with ordinary earthly experience.

At-Tur 52:21 speaks of believers being joined by their believing descendants in the afterlife. Such passages belong to the Qur’an’s field of hope and promise. They do not give us authority to specify how every individual loss will be made good.

God may give something better. We have no right to determine its form and timing without clear textual evidence.

26. Does Perseverance Always End in Visible Earthly Victory?

Some Qur’anic accounts connect perseverance with relief and victory. Yusuf 12:90 brings moral responsibility and perseverance together. The children of Israel’s endurance is connected with liberation from oppression.

Yet Al-Imran 3:140 speaks of days of victory and defeat passing among people. Al-Imran 3:142 discusses testing and perseverance in that setting. Steadfastness does not always bring an immediate desired result.

A person may work for truth without living to see the full outcome. A community may strive for reform over many years, with change remaining slow. That reality does not make suffering the purpose of life or make worthwhile effort meaningless.

Steadfastness is the capacity to hold to what should be upheld and to restrain oneself from what should be avoided, despite pressure or attraction drawing one the other way. Its value must therefore be understood in relation to truth, justice, good conduct, and responsibility, rather than measured merely by the length of time a person endures pain.

Part Seven: Can the Famous Saying Govern Our Reading of the Qur’an?

27. If It Means Inevitable Deprivation for Believers and Comfort for Disbelievers

We can now place both halves of the saying alongside the collected passages. The claim that every believer is condemned to deprivation and misery conflicts with the promises of earthly good in Hud 11:3, Al-Araf 7:32, Al-Araf 7:96, An-Nahl 16:97, and other verses.

The claim that every disbeliever enjoys a paradise-like earthly life conflicts with passages describing hardship, constriction, worldly punishment, and the destruction of some such people’s possessions. These include At-Tawbah 9:55, Ar-Rad 13:34, and the account of the garden owner in Al-Kahf 18:32, Al-Kahf 18:33, Al-Kahf 18:34, Al-Kahf 18:35, Al-Kahf 18:36, Al-Kahf 18:37, Al-Kahf 18:38, Al-Kahf 18:39, Al-Kahf 18:40, Al-Kahf 18:41, Al-Kahf 18:42, Al-Kahf 18:43, Al-Kahf 18:44. Al-Isra 17:20 also shows worldly provision reaching two groups with different orientations.

This reading cannot be defended as a universal law of earthly destiny. Anyone who nevertheless attributes it to the Prophet must first explain how it can be reconciled with these passages. The saying’s popularity does not answer the question.

28. If It Compares This Life with the Afterlife

One might argue that this life is like a prison for believers relative to the blessings of the afterlife, and like paradise relative to the final fate of those who reject faith. This differs from asserting inevitable poverty for believers and inevitable prosperity for disbelievers.

The Qur’an itself repeatedly describes earthly enjoyment as brief and unstable, and the afterlife as superior; see Al-Imran 3:185, Al-Hadid 57:20, Al-Ankabut 29:64, and Al-Ala 87:17.

On this reading, however, prison and paradise must be relative comparisons, not literal and absolute descriptions of everyone’s earthly living conditions. They still cannot cancel the promises of a wholesome life, blessings, and earthly good.

If the intended point is that temporary enjoyment is slight beside the afterlife, the Qur’an already states that in its own language. There is no need to turn the comparison into a new rule distributing material prosperity among religious groups.

29. If It Means Restraint from Wrongful Desires

“Prison” may instead be interpreted as the moral limits a believer accepts. Here too, self-restraint must be distinguished from imposed deprivation.

An-Naziat 79:40 and An-Naziat 79:41 connect restraining wrongful desire with a good final outcome. That accords with moral responsibility. A person who refuses oppression, lying, aggression, and the theft of others’ property is not thereby required to forgo every legitimate blessing.

Al-Maidah 5:87 and Al-Maidah 5:88 reject arbitrary prohibitions on wholesome things. Al-Araf 7:32 recognizes God’s servants’ access to adornment and good provision. Restraining wrongful desires is not another name for poverty or for depriving oneself of earthly good.

Anyone using “prison” in this sense must therefore make clear that it is a moral analogy, not a sentence condemning believers to suffering and misery.

30. Do Religious Labels Determine Our Destiny?

In popular retellings, the saying sometimes becomes “This world is hell for Muslims and paradise for disbelievers.” That is not the wording of the report under examination.

We must also guard against reducing Qur’anic concepts to fixed sectarian labels. Iman (faith and trust), islam (submission to God), and kufr (rejection or concealment of truth, or ingratitude, according to context) concern more than a person’s declared group identity. The root study of rejection and ingratitude examines the last of these distinctions.

An-Nisa 4:123 does not make wishes and identity claims the decisive measure of a person’s outcome. An-Nisa 4:124 places faith alongside right action. Membership of a religious community alone guarantees neither salvation nor prosperity. Nor can the final judgment of an individual be extracted solely from the label others apply to that person.

The purpose of examining these verses is not to divide humanity into two fixed social classes, one happy and the other miserable. It is to understand how conduct, orientation, and responsibility relate to the Qur’an’s promises and warnings.

31. When Religion Becomes a Justification for Poverty and Oppression

The danger becomes especially clear when a religious expression is used to suppress social responsibility. If corruption has impoverished a country, calling this world the believer’s prison cannot erase the responsibility of those who stole public wealth.

If war and oppression have devastated a society, its victims cannot simply be told to endure while remaining silent about the perpetrators. If a region’s natural resources have been destroyed, naming the consequences a divine test cannot exempt those responsible from accountability.

Al-Maun 107:1 speaks of denying judgment or religion; Al-Maun 107:2 identifies mistreatment of the orphan; Al-Maun 107:3 criticizes the failure to encourage feeding the needy. These verses establish a direct relationship between religious truth and social conduct. Someone who neglects the rights of the deprived cannot establish fidelity to religion through slogans alone.

At-Tawbah 9:34 also describes some religious scholars and monks wrongfully consuming people’s wealth and obstructing God’s path. The Qur’an itself criticizes the abuse of religious standing to acquire wealth and obstruct what is right.

A religion that asks only for the poor person’s patience while refusing to hold an oppressive rich person accountable has discarded a fundamental part of the Qur’an’s message. God’s name cannot justify human oppression. Patience cannot cancel the responsibility to reform society.

Bringing the Argument Together

The Qur’an does not characterize earthly life itself as the believer’s prison and the disbeliever’s paradise. It presents a near and temporary life in which people experience, choose, and act. The problem arises when they make this life their entire purpose and abandon truth and the afterlife for its limited benefits.

It promises a wholesome life, earthly good, blessings, and good news to people of faith and right action. Those promises cannot be removed in the name of a report, ascetic practice, or inherited religious interpretation. Neither can they be converted into guarantees of wealth, health, and uninterrupted ease for every believer.

The enjoyment some disbelievers experience does not establish that their lives are paradise. The attraction of this world may concern preferring temporary advantage to lasting truth. The Qur’an also describes suffering, constriction, and harmful consequences among those who reject faith.

Testing is not identical with calamity. Good fortune and adversity can both test us. Wealth, power, knowledge, health, deprivation, and loss can each confront a person with responsibility.

Divine patterns do not abolish human choice. People live in an ordered world where their actions have consequences. An event occurring by God’s permission does not imply His moral approval of an oppressive agent.

The material effects of one group’s actions can reach others. An upright person may be harmed by social corruption without being morally responsible for it. The cause of harm, the perpetrators’ responsibility, and the affected person’s trial must be distinguished.

Steadfastness is not a general command to submit silently to oppression. Worthwhile perseverance may appear in seeking justice, resisting wrongdoing, controlling a harmful reaction, leaving an oppressive environment, pursuing reform, and continuing to do good.

Finally, the good news for the steadfast is real, but its form and timing must be learned from the passages. The Qur’an describes earthly restoration and promises for the afterlife. Nobody may invent a fixed formula and timetable for every human loss without Qur’anic evidence.

Conclusion

In the Qur’an, this life is a real setting for living, acting, choosing, receiving, and being tested. God does not present it solely as a place for believers to suffer, nor reserve its good things exclusively for those who reject faith.

A wholesome life, blessings from heaven and earth, good provision, and good news in this world belong to the Qur’an’s explicit promises. A famous report cannot erase them.

The same Qur’an also speaks of trials, calamities, oppression, and loss. Other people’s conduct affects our lives. An upright individual may suffer from the consequences of widespread corruption.

Divine patterns do not invalidate choice. They place our actions within a real world where choices have effects. The oppressor is responsible for the wrongdoing; the person who resists is tested along the chosen path.

Wealth is not conclusive proof of God’s approval. Deprivation is not proof that a person is contemptible before Him. Faith and right action cannot be measured by outward possessions and power.

Faith is not a sentence to misery. Rejection of faith is not a title deed to an earthly paradise. Steadfastness is not a command to remain silent before injustice.

God calls human beings to faith, right action, moral responsibility, gratitude, perseverance, and accountability. We must work in this life for truth, justice, the flourishing of the earth, and wholesome living, while recognizing that our final horizon extends beyond it.

A religion that asks people to endure corruption without requiring its perpetrators to answer for it cannot justify itself by repeating a saying. The measure must be the Qur’an that speaks of testing, human responsibility, and the promise of good and glad tidings together.

God knows best.
